# Topic 3.3 - Learning from others: Bandura's social learning theory

## A real classroom situation

A new teacher, Miss Sadia, takes over Class 6 in March. In her first week, a pupil gives
a wrong answer. She says, without thinking, "Good try - how did you get that?" and works
through the error with the class.

Nothing about that exchange was a lesson. But by April, pupils in that class answer
wrongly out loud, without the flinch the previous class showed, and when one pupil
struggles, another sometimes says "good try" to *him*. The class has learned how wrong
answers are treated, and the teacher never taught it.

That is the territory of this topic: the learning that runs through **other people**,
observed rather than experienced, and the psychologist who mapped it - Albert Bandura.

## Explanation

### Bandura: learning by observation

Albert Bandura's social learning theory holds that humans learn a great deal by
**observing others** - watching what they do and what happens to them - rather than only
by doing and being reinforced themselves (Seifert & Sutton, 2009; Spielman, Jenkins &
Lovett, 2020). The observation is not passive recording. Bandura described "specific
steps in the process of modeling that must be followed if learning is to be successful:
attention, retention, reproduction, and motivation" (Spielman, Jenkins & Lovett, 2020),
and the steps are a teacher's checklist.

**Attention.** The learner must notice the model. In a classroom, attention is
engineered by salience: the teacher's first response to a wrong answer is one of the
most attended-to events in the room, because every pupil is quietly checking what is
safe here.

**Retention.** The learner must remember what was seen, in a form that can be used
later. A vivid, structured episode - the "good try" exchange - retains better than a
rushed one.

**Reproduction.** The learner must be able to do the behaviour. A pupil who watched a
classmate defuse a quarrel may still fail to reproduce it if the words and moves are
beyond them; complex behaviours need practice and often a script.

**Motivation.** The expected outcome must make the behaviour worth doing. This is where
reinforcement re-enters - but with a twist Bandura made famous.

**Vicarious reinforcement.** The learner does not need to be reinforced personally;
watching someone else be reinforced works almost as well. Research repeatedly shows
that modelling desired behaviours is an effective way to teach new ones, especially
when the model is seen as important, similar, or warmly related to the learner (Seifert
& Sutton, 2009). The pupil who watches a classmate praised for politeness becomes more
likely to be polite. The reverse also holds: watching negative behaviour bring
attention - from peers or from a teacher who cannot look away - reinforces the negative
behaviour vicariously, in every pupil who saw it.

> **The misconception to clear now.** "Observing violence on a screen teaches nothing."
> The classic laboratory demonstrations of observational learning - Bandura's
> inflatable-doll studies, in which children who watched an adult behave aggressively
> toward the doll imitated what she did, "and even what she said" (Bandura, Ross, &
> Ross, 1961, as described in Spielman, Jenkins & Lovett, 2020) - led Bandura to
> conclude that "we watch and learn, and that this learning can have both prosocial and
> antisocial effects". What they show is not that every observer imitates everything;
> it is that the *capacity* is there, and that what is modelled, and what happens to the
> model, matter. The same mechanism that teaches a pupil to mock an error teaches the
> next one to say "good try". The theory is not a verdict on screens; it is a lens on
> models.

### What the observational-learning research showed, and what it means for models

Two findings from the research tradition matter directly to a teacher.

**Models are chosen, not assigned.** The strongest models are perceived as important
(the teacher, the head pupil), similar (the friend, the cousin), or warmly related (the
teacher who knows your name) (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). A teacher is a model in every
interaction whether or not they accept the office - the only choice offered is what is
modelled.

**The model's outcome is the lesson.** Pupils read what happens to the model, not what
the model says. When the loudest pupil gets the teacher's full attention and the quiet
reply that is right gets only a tick, the class has learned two things, and the second
one is not what the syllabus intended.

## Activity: Map the models

**In small groups, about 20 minutes.**

Take one classroom you all know. For each of the five model sources in the figure - the
teacher, peers, older pupils, family, screens - write one specific behaviour you believe
is being modelled there, and one piece of evidence for your claim (something you saw,
not something you assume).

Then answer the hard question: **which single model in that room is the teacher
reinforcing most often, and for what?**

## Check your understanding

1. Name Bandura's four observational-learning steps in order, with the classroom
   instance of each from this topic. *(Remember)*
2. Explain vicarious reinforcement in two sentences, with an example that is not from
   this topic. *(Understand)*
3. Why is the teacher's response to the *first* wrong answer of the year so powerful?
   Use at least two of the four steps. *(Apply)*
4. The loudest pupil gets the most attention; the quiet answer that is right gets only a tick.
   Name the two lessons the class has learned, and which one the syllabus intended. *(Analyze)*
5. A school bans phones and assumes screens no longer model anything. Using the model
   table, give two reasons why that assumption fails. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Bandura's social learning theory shows that much of what pupils learn arrives by
observation: attention, retention, reproduction and motivation must all be present, and
reinforcement can be vicarious - watched, not felt. Models are chosen by importance,
similarity and warmth, and the model's outcome, not the model's words, is what the
observers read. A teacher is the strongest model in the room by default; the only
question is what is being modelled, and what the room's attention trains.

## Try this at your practicum school

For one lesson, keep a two-column tally: on the left, every instance of pupil behaviour
the teacher visibly notices; on the right, every instance the teacher lets pass. Do not
judge the teaching. At the end, answer: what is attention being given for, and what is
a pupil watching this learning?

## Summative task

Write **250-300 words** on the new teacher's first week.

Miss Sadia's "good try" changed a class in six weeks. Using this topic, explain the
mechanism: which steps of observational learning her response served, what was being
vicariously reinforced, and why the effect spread to pupils she never spoke to. Then
name one behaviour you would *deliberately* model in your own first week, and what
outcome you would make sure the class observed for it.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong answer shows |
|---|---|
| Mechanism accuracy | The four steps and vicarious reinforcement are applied to the case, not just named. |
| Spread explained | The answer says why observers who were never addressed still learned. *(Analyze)* |
| Deliberate modelling | The chosen behaviour and its observed outcome are specific and realistic. |
| Plain, accurate writing | Terms used correctly; no padding; within the word count. |
