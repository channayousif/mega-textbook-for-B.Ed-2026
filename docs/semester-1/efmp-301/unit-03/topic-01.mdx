# Topic 3.1 - Learning as behaviour: Pavlov and Skinner

## A real classroom situation

Two things happen in Miss Farzana's Class 5 in a government school in Tando Allahyar,
and both happen every day.

First: the pupils can hear the head teacher's footsteps on the corridor before he appears.
By the time he opens the door, the room is already silent. Nobody taught them to fall
silent at footsteps; the sound simply arrived, many times, shortly before a scolding.

Second: Miss Farzana keeps a star chart. A pupil who completes the day's work gets a star
beside their name, and five stars mean a turn watering the class plants, the most wanted
job in the room. Work completion has risen since the chart appeared.

Both events are learning, and neither involved a lesson. The staff room has one word for
the first ("discipline") and another for the second ("incentives"), but they are two
mechanisms of the same family - and the family has a name.

## Explanation

### Learning as behaviour: the behaviourist view

<Glossary term="Behaviorism" /> is the view of learning that focuses on **changes in
observable behaviour** - changes in what people say or do (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). It
began as a deliberate decision by psychologists: thinking cannot be seen, so a science of
learning should start from what can. Whatever its limits, every teacher uses this
perspective daily. When you check whether pupils *can do* the sum, rather than whether
they can describe how to do it, you are measuring learning behaviourally.

The behaviourist family has two great mechanisms. The first explains behaviour that
pupils **cannot help** - the falling silent at footsteps. The second explains behaviour
pupils **choose** - the work done for a star. Confusing the two is the commonest error
in classroom management, because the two call for different teacher moves.

### Pavlov: classical conditioning

Classical conditioning is learning by **association**: a neutral signal comes to trigger
a response because it repeatedly arrived just before something that already triggered
it. Ivan Pavlov, a Russian physiologist, demonstrated it with dogs: a bell rang before
food; after repetitions, the bell alone made the dogs salivate (Seifert & Sutton, 2009).

The mechanism needs only four names. The **unconditioned stimulus** (food) already
triggers the **response** (salivation) without learning. The **neutral stimulus** (the
bell) triggers nothing - until it repeatedly precedes the food, at which point it
becomes a **conditioned stimulus** that triggers the response on its own.

Now read the Class 5 corridor scene with those four names. The scolding is the
unconditioned stimulus; fear and stillness are the response; the footsteps are the
neutral stimulus that became conditioned. The pupils did not decide to fall silent any
more than Pavlov's dogs decided to salivate. That is why telling them to relax is
useless: nobody can un-hear an association by being told to.

Classical conditioning also fades when the signal stops predicting: if the footsteps
repeatedly led to nothing, the silence would wear off. Psychologists call this
**extinction**, and it matters to a teacher in one specific way - associations the
school creates by accident (fear of the head teacher, dread of a subject) fade only when
the pairing stops, not when anyone announces it should.

> **The misconception to clear now.** Trainees often say conditioning is "for dogs, not
> children". But the corridor scene is conditioning in a Sindh government school, and so
> is the pupil who feels sick at the smell of the medicine she took during a hard school
> month. The honest objection is different: conditioning explains only part of human
> learning - the automatic part. It says nothing about understanding, and Topic 3.2 is
> where the course answers it.

### Skinner: operant conditioning

Operant conditioning is learning from **consequences**: behaviour that is followed by
good outcomes happens more often; behaviour followed by bad outcomes happens less often.
B. F. Skinner, a Harvard psychologist, mapped the process in painstaking detail with
laboratory rats and then argued, in book after book, that the same laws run through
human behaviour (Seifert & Sutton, 2009).

The key word is **consequence**, and it must be read precisely. A consequence is what
actually follows the behaviour - not what the teacher intends, and not what the teacher
says will follow. Miss Farzana's star is a consequence because it actually arrives. A
promised prize that never materialises is not a consequence; it is a broken signal, and
broken signals teach pupils to ignore the teacher's words.

<Figure id="fig-U3-2" src="/img/figures/efmp-301/unit-03/fig-U3-2.svg" alt="A flowchart of a behaviour and its consequence, branching into four quadrants: positive and negative reinforcement, which make behaviour more frequent, and punishment and response cost, which make it less frequent, each with a classroom example." />

### Reinforcement and punishment in the classroom

The four consequence types are worth naming exactly once and then using forever
(Seifert & Sutton, 2009).

**Reinforcement** always means one thing: the behaviour becomes **more** frequent. It
comes in two forms. **Positive reinforcement** adds something the pupil wants - praise,
a star, a turn at the plants. **Negative reinforcement** removes something the pupil
dislikes - the pupil who finishes seatwork and thereby stops the teacher's scolding has
been negatively reinforced for finishing. Both forms strengthen behaviour; only the
direction of the arrow differs.

**Punishment** always means the behaviour becomes **less** frequent. It too comes in two
forms: adding something disliked (the scolding) or removing something wanted (the star
taken away, called response cost).

The classic error is reading "negative" as "bad". Negative reinforcement is not
punishment; it is one of the two strengtheners. The pupil who learns to bring a
notebook because forgetting it brings a scolding, and who then stops being scolded once
the habit is in place, is being shaped by both mechanisms in sequence.

Three cautions belong in any classroom use.

**Reinforcement teaches what is rewarded, not what is meant.** The star chart rewards
completed work, and completed work rose. If neat copying completes work faster than
thinking, copying is what the chart teaches. The chart is not wrong; it is precise, and
its precision must be aimed.

**Punishment suppresses; it does not teach.** A scolded behaviour goes underground, not
away, and it carries a price: the pupil learns to avoid the scolding teacher, the
subject, and the risk of trying. Punishment has a place, but the place is narrow, and
the replacement behaviour must be taught and reinforced beside it.

**Attention is a reinforcer, and the weakest pupils get it for the wrong things.** A
pupil who is ignored when working and attended to when disrupting is on a schedule that
trains disruption. The remedy is not less attention but attention aimed - greeting the
pupil at the door, noticing the work, and letting the disruption meet a calm, small
response.

## Activity: Audit the day's consequences

**In pairs, about 25 minutes.**

List eight consequences that actually followed pupil behaviour in a school day you know
well - your own class, your practicum, or your own schooling. For each, name the
mechanism (classical conditioning, positive or negative reinforcement, punishment,
response cost) and say what behaviour it is training, whether or not anyone intended
that.

Then find the **one consequence you would change first**, and say exactly what you would
do instead and what behaviour your change would train.

## Check your understanding

1. Define behaviourism in one sentence, and say what it deliberately leaves out. *(Understand)*
2. Name the four elements of the corridor scene in conditioning terms: footsteps,
   scolding, falling silent, and the repeated pairing. *(Understand)*
3. A pupil feels sick at the smell of the school medicines. Explain the chain that
   produced this, and name the one thing that will make it fade. *(Apply)*
4. Explain, in two sentences, why negative reinforcement is not punishment, with one
   classroom instance of each. *(Understand)*
5. The star chart raised work completion but copying also rose. Using the precision
   warning in this topic, say what the chart is actually reinforcing and how to re-aim
   it. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Behaviourism defines learning as change in observable behaviour and offers two
mechanisms: classical conditioning, learning by association, which explains the
automatic responses no one decided to learn; and operant conditioning, learning from
consequences, which explains the behaviour that is strengthened or weakened by what
actually follows it. Reinforcement (positive and negative) makes behaviour more
frequent; punishment and response cost make it less. The classroom discipline is to read
consequences for what they actually train - not what they are meant to teach - and to
remember that attention itself is a reinforcer aimed by the teacher's choices.

## Try this at your practicum school

Choose one pupil everyone labels "difficult". For one day, note what behaviour of theirs
actually gets attended to, and what gets ignored. Do not intervene. At the end of the
day, name the schedule you have observed: what is that pupil being reinforced for, and
by whom?

## Summative task

Write **250-300 words** redesigning Miss Farzana's system.

The star chart raised completed work but you have noticed pupils rushing and copying.
Using this topic's precision rule, analyse what the chart actually reinforces, then
redesign it: what exactly will earn a star, what will be checked before it is given, and
what behaviour the redesigned chart trains. Name any risk your redesign carries.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong answer shows |
|---|---|
| Mechanism accuracy | Reinforcement is used precisely; the analysis names what is actually rewarded, not what was intended. |
| Redesign precision | The new rule states the exact behaviour, the exact check, and the trained outcome. *(Analyze)* |
| Risk awareness | At least one honest cost of the redesign is named. |
| Plain, accurate writing | Terms used correctly; no padding; within the word count. |
