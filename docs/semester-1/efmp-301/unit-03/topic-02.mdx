# Topic 3.2 - The cognitive turn: Piaget and Bruner

## A real classroom situation

Two pupils in a Sukkur school learned the same poem in the same week. Rashid learned it
the way poems are usually learned in his school: he recited it after the teacher, line
by line, until he could produce it without a single error. Sana learned it at home from
her grandmother, who first told her the story the poem tells, acted out the fisherman's
arrival with a scarf, and only then read the words.

A week later the teacher checked. Rashid's recitation was letter-perfect, but when she
asked him what the fisherman was doing in the second verse, he had no idea there was a
fisherman. Sana's recitation stumbled twice, but she corrected the stumbles herself,
because the words were hanging on a story she understood.

Both pupils learned. Only one of them learned a poem. The difference is the whole of
this topic.

## Explanation

### The cognitive turn: learning as mental activity

Behaviourism measured what learners do. The **cognitive** view asks what learners *think*,
and defines learning as change in thinking: new understandings, new connections, new
ways of representing the world (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). The turn was a correction, not
a rejection. Behaviourist mechanisms still run - Rashid's recitation is pure operant
conditioning, rehearsed and praised - but they cannot tell the teacher whether the
recitation carries any meaning.

The course guide names two theorists for this turn, and they divide the work neatly.
Piaget explains the **structure** a learner brings: the schemas that receive new
experience. Bruner explains the **route** teaching can take: the order from action to
image to symbol.

### Piaget: schemas, assimilation and accommodation

A **schema** is an organised packet of knowledge - "cat", "fractions", "how school days
go". Learning, in Piaget's account, is the constant adjustment of schemas to experience,
and it moves in two directions (Seifert & Sutton, 2009).

**Assimilation** fits new experience into an existing schema. The Class 1 pupil who
calls every four-legged animal "billi" (cat) is assimilating: dogs, goats and cows all
go into the cat-schema, because that is the schema he has. Assimilation is efficient and
mostly silent; it is how a learner absorbs the ordinary day.

**Accommodation** changes the schema to fit the experience that will not go in. When the
pupil finally separates dog from cat - a new category, a rebuilt schema - that is
accommodation. Accommodation is the expensive operation: it is effortful, it is where
misunderstanding and confusion live, and it is where real learning happens.

The two run together all day. The pupil who has a schema for "sharing sweets among
cousins" and meets the fraction 1/4 will assimilate it ("a quarter is my share when four
of us share") - and that assimilation is the teacher's ally, because it is what the
schema is *for*. The pupil who then meets 3/4 and cannot file it anywhere is being asked
for accommodation, and that is the moment the teaching must help.

Two consequences for a teacher. First, **a new topic lands on the schemas the pupils
already have**, so the teacher's first move is to find out what those schemas are - the
rote-learned poem landed on nothing and slid off. Second, **confusion is not failure**;
it is the signature of accommodation under way. A class that is quietly comfortable is
often a class being assimilated into its own existing ideas, and a class that is
productively puzzled is often a class rebuilding one.

> **The misconception to clear now.** "Piaget's stages mean a pupil cannot be taught a
> concept early." The stages (Topic 2.3) describe readiness, not permission. What the
> stage reading forbids is demanding the *symbolic* form of a concept before its
> concrete grounding - which is exactly what Bruner's route, next, supplies a way to
> avoid.

### Bruner: representation, discovery and the spiral curriculum

Jerome Bruner's contribution is the teaching-side of the cognitive turn, in three ideas.

**Three modes of representation.** Learners can hold knowledge as **enactive** (knowing
by doing - the halving of a roti), **iconic** (knowing by image - a drawing of the
roti), and **symbolic** (knowing by sign - the written 1/2). Bruner argued that any
subject can be represented in all three, and that teaching should move through them in
that order (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). Miss Shabana's roti lesson from Topic 2.4 is
Bruner's route exactly: break the roti (enactive), draw the roti (iconic), write 1/2
(symbolic).

**Discovery learning.** Bruner held that pupils learn best when they discover the
structure of a subject for themselves, with guidance, rather than receive it as finished
product. In practice this does not mean the teacher disappears; it means the teacher
arranges the experience so the structure shows up - the sink-and-float basin, the
unfair-sharing story that makes fractions necessary. The discovery is guided, or it does
not happen.

**The spiral curriculum.** Because any idea can be represented at several levels, a
curriculum can introduce an idea early in an intuitive form and return to it later in a
more formal one. Fractions meet Class 2 as sharing food, Class 4 as rotis and diagrams,
Class 6 as number operations. Nothing is wasted; the early round becomes the schema the
later round accommodates.

## Activity: Two lessons, one order

**In pairs, about 20 minutes.**

Below is one concept - "why wet clothes dry" - and two lesson sketches.

**Lesson A.** The teacher writes "evaporation" on the board, defines it, states that
heat makes water turn into vapour, and sets five questions whose answers are in the
notes on the board.

**Lesson B.** The teacher hangs a wet cloth at the window at assembly and a second one
in the store room, asks the class to predict which will dry first, records the
predictions, and returns at home time to compare. The word "evaporation" appears only
after the class has described what happened.

1. For each lesson, say where the enactive, iconic and symbolic representations appear -
   or note where one is missing.
2. For Lesson A, name what the pupils will most likely do with the definition
   (assimilate it into what schema, or fail to file it at all).
3. Decide: which lesson demands more from the teacher, and what exactly does it demand?

## Summary

The cognitive turn defines learning as change in thinking. Piaget gives it a structure:
schemas, adjusted by assimilation (new experience filed into what exists) and
accommodation (the schema rebuilt), with confusion as the signature of the second.
Bruner gives it a route: enactive before iconic before symbolic, discovery guided by
the teacher's arrangement, and a spiral that returns to each idea in a stronger form.
A new topic lands on the schemas the pupils already have; the teacher's first job is to
find out what those are, and the second is to order the experience so the structure can
show up.

## Try this at your practicum school

Take the next lesson you will teach and write its single most important new concept.
Before teaching it, ask two pupils what they already think about it - their words, not
the textbook's. Note whether your lesson's first activity meets their schemas or
ignores them, and what you would change.

## Summative task

Write **250-300 words** rebuilding Lesson A.

Using this topic's ideas, redesign the wet-clothes lesson so the concept of evaporation
is built the way Sana's poem was built. Your answer must name: what the pupils already
have (the schemas you will meet), where the enactive, iconic and symbolic moments sit,
and where you expect accommodation - the productive confusion - to appear. Close with
the one thing the redesigned lesson demands of you that Lesson A did not.
