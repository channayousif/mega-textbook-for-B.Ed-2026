# Topic 4.2 - Memory and information processing

## A real classroom situation

Every B.Ed trainee knows the October test. The Pakistan Studies chapter is learned on
Monday night - read, re-read, highlighted until the page glows - and the marks are
good. In December, before the exam, the same chapter is asked about again, and almost
nothing is there. The trainee concludes they "have a bad memory".

They do not. What they have is a study strategy - re-reading - that creates a
temporary familiarity and almost no durable retrieval route. The chapter was never
lost; it was never stored in a form that December could reach. The difference between
those two sentences is this whole topic.

## Explanation

### The information-processing model: sensory, working and long-term memory

The mind's handling of information is usefully drawn as a chain of three systems, each
with a different job (Spielman, Jenkins & Lovett, 2020).

**Sensory memory** holds the raw impressions - sights, sounds, tastes - "for up to a
couple of seconds". Almost everything held here is lost; its job is not storage but
decision: it keeps the world's input alive just long enough for attention to select
from it. This is the gateway of Topic 4.1 feeding the next stage.

**Working memory** is the workbench: the small, active space where thinking happens.
It holds only a handful of items at once - the classic estimate was seven, plus or
minus two (Miller); recent work puts the practical capacity nearer four (Cowan, 2010,
cited in Spielman, Jenkins & Lovett, 2020) - and it holds them for seconds to a
minute unless actively kept alive. Every demand of a lesson - the instruction, the
example, the question, the sentence being composed - runs through this one small
space. When a lesson overloads it, nothing is wrong with the pupils; the workbench is
simply full.

**Long-term memory** is the archive: "the continuous storage of information", with a
capacity believed to be unlimited (Spielman, Jenkins & Lovett, 2020). The assembly
routine a pupil has kept for years, the poem learned in childhood, the way home: all
live here, and all were once merely items on the workbench that survived the crossing.

Between the systems run three processes. **Encoding** is "the input of information
into the memory system" - and it is not passive: it takes "a lot of work and
attention" to encode material well (Spielman, Jenkins & Lovett, 2020). **Storage** is
the retention of what was encoded, cemented by connections to what is already known.
**Retrieval** is getting information "out of memory storage and back into conscious
awareness" - and retrieval is a skill: it strengthens with practice, which is the
single most practical fact in this topic.

> **The misconception to clear now.** "Memory is a recorder, and forgetting is
> malfunction." Memory is a system with jobs - select, hold, connect, recover - and
> forgetting is what the system does by design to almost everything, because storing
> all inputs would be useless. The October chapter was not deleted; it was never
> encoded with connections, and no retrieval route was ever practised. What feels like
> a bad memory is usually a strategy that asked the system for the one thing it does
> not do: durable storage from passive re-reading.

### Encoding, storage, retrieval - and why forgetting happens

If the systems are the hardware, the processes are where teaching lives.

**Encoding: connect, do not merely expose.** Material that connects to what the pupil
already knows is encoded; material that merely passes by is not. The fraction that
lands on a "sharing sweets" schema is stored; the fraction that lands on nothing
slides off. This is Unit 3's schema idea, now with a memory address.

**Storage: spaced, not massed.** Storage strengthens when material is revisited over
time rather than crammed together - the spacing of attempts is one of the factors that
improves the effectiveness of practice (Ariel & Karpicke, 2018). The chapter re-read
once a week for four weeks outlives the chapter re-read four times in one night, at any
total effort.

**Retrieval: practised, not assumed.** The act of pulling material back out - the
self-test, the closed-book attempt, the explanation to a partner - strengthens the
route more than re-exposure does: repeated retrieval practice is "a powerful learning
tool for promoting long-term retention", and its mnemonic benefits run "over a less
effective strategy (restudying)" (Ariel & Karpicke, 2018). "Active rehearsal is a way of attending to
information to move it from short-term to long-term memory" (Spielman, Jenkins &
Lovett, 2020). Re-reading creates recognition ("I have seen this"); retrieval
practice creates recall ("I can produce this"), and only recall is what an
examination, or a classroom question, asks for.

Three faces of **forgetting** follow. **Decay**: the trace fades with disuse - the
route grows over. **Interference**: newer material crowds older material - the
October chapter loses to the November chapter that shared its shelf. **Retrieval
failure**: the material is there but the route is not practised - the "it is on the
tip of my tongue" state. Each has a different remedy, and only the third responds to
"try harder": decay answers spacing, interference answers distinctiveness, retrieval
failure answers practice.

## Activity: The span test and the strategy audit

**In pairs, about 20 minutes.**

**Part 1 (5 minutes).** One partner reads a list of eight unrelated words at one word
per second; the other recalls them in any order. Repeat with a list of eight words
that form a connected story. Compare scores, and explain the difference using this
topic's terms.

**Part 2 (15 minutes).** Each partner lists the three strategies they actually used
for their last test. Place each on the strategy table: what does it ask of memory,
and what does the evidence say? Then agree on the single change to your own revision
that the evidence most supports, and write it as a sentence you will act on.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the three memory systems in order, with the job and rough capacity of each.
   *(Remember)*
2. A lesson gives a four-step instruction and then asks a question. Using working
   memory, explain why the question is often not heard. *(Apply)*
3. Define encoding, storage and retrieval in one sentence each. *(Understand)*
4. The October chapter: name the face of forgetting that best explains its loss, and
   the remedy that face answers to. *(Apply)*
5. Why does re-reading feel effective while it works? Use recognition versus recall.
   *(Understand)*

## Summary

The information-processing model draws the mind as a chain: sensory memory selects,
working memory - small, brief, the workbench of thought - holds and works, and
long-term memory keeps what encoding has connected. Storage strengthens with spacing;
retrieval strengthens with practice; forgetting has three faces - decay, interference
and retrieval failure - each with its own remedy. The study strategies the evidence
supports are the ones that ask memory to do its own work: spaced retrieval,
elaboration, and teaching it to someone. Re-reading, the culture's default, asks the
least and delivers it.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can name the three systems, their jobs and their rough capacities.
- [ ] I can define encoding, storage and retrieval and place a lesson's demands on
      them.
- [ ] I can name the three faces of forgetting with the remedy each answers to.
- [ ] I can evaluate any study strategy against the evidence, including my own.

## Try this at your practicum school

Introduce one five-question exit quiz into one lesson: three questions on today's
material, two on last week's. Say nothing about memory to the pupils. After two
weeks, ask the cooperating teacher whether they can see a difference in what the
class retains, and note the answer.

## Summative task

Write **250-300 words** redesigning a revision plan.

Choose one real topic you must teach (or were taught). Design a two-week revision
plan for it that uses the evidence of this topic: spaced retrieval, elaboration and
the teaching-it-to-someone structure, each appearing at a named point. Name what each
element asks of the memory systems, and concede honestly what your plan demands of
the timetable.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong answer shows |
|---|---|
| Evidence discipline | Every element of the plan maps to a strategy the evidence supports, not to effort. |
| System reasoning | Each element is justified by what it asks of encoding, storage or retrieval. *(Analyze)* |
| Timetable honesty | The plan's cost in minutes and periods is stated. |
| Plain, accurate writing | Terms used correctly; no padding; within the word count. |
