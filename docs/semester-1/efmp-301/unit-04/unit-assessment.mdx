# Cognitive Processes in Learning - assessment and review

## Unit summary

Attention is the gateway: a narrow spotlight, captured by novelty, contrast, meaning
and emotion rather than commanded, costly to divide, and depletable. A lesson competes
for the beam with structure or on the distractors' own terms, and the beam's design is
a teacher's decision.

Perception is active interpretation: what a pupil sees depends on what they already
know, ambiguous materials are read through expectation, and expert perception is
learned through named encounters.

Memory is a chain of systems with jobs: sensory memory selects, working memory holds a
handful of items for seconds and thinks with them, long-term memory keeps what
encoding has connected. Storage strengthens with spacing, retrieval with practice, and
forgetting has three faces - decay, interference, retrieval failure - each with its
own remedy. The strategies the evidence supports are the ones that make memory do its
own work: spaced retrieval, elaboration, teaching it to someone.

Thinking runs on concepts and named strategies - algorithm, heuristic, means-ends,
working backwards, analogy - against obstacles that belong to everyone: functional
fixedness, set effect, overload. Reasoning runs from cases to rules (inductive) and
rules to cases (deductive), and each fails differently.

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. Attention is best described as: *(Remember)*
   a) the storage of information for later use
   b) the mind's selection of what gets through to further processing
   c) the interpretation of what the senses deliver
   d) a fixed quantity a pupil has or lacks

2. "Copy this while I explain" tends to produce notes without understanding
   because: *(Understand)*
   a) copying is too easy to be engaging
   b) the two tasks are competing for one narrow workbench
   c) pupils cannot write and hear at the same age
   d) the explanation is always the weaker task

3. Sensation differs from perception in that sensation is: *(Understand)*
   a) interpretation and perception is detection
   b) detection by a receptor, while perception organises and interprets what was
      detected
   c) conscious and perception is automatic
   d) longer-lasting than perception

4. The practical capacity of working memory is closest to: *(Remember)*
   a) two items
   b) four items
   c) twelve items
   d) unlimited

5. In the classic study, many viewers counting basketball passes failed to notice a
   person in a gorilla costume walking through the scene. The finding shows: *(Understand)*
   a) the gorilla was too fast to see
   b) a mind occupied by one task closes the gateway to what it is not attending to
   c) counting is too difficult for most people
   d) attention is unlimited when motivation is high

6. Which study strategy has the strongest evidence for durable recall? *(Understand)*
   a) re-reading
   b) highlighting
   c) spaced retrieval practice
   d) listening once attentively

7. Re-reading feels effective because it creates: *(Understand)*
   a) recall
   b) recognition
   c) elaboration
   d) interference

8. Ayesha draws the rickshaw problem as two bars. The strategy is: *(Apply)*
   a) algorithm
   b) analogy
   c) means-ends analysis made visible
   d) deduction

9. "Every rickshaw fare I have checked ended in a zero" is: *(Understand)*
   a) deductive reasoning
   b) inductive reasoning
   c) a heuristic failure
   d) retrieval failure

10. Bilal's "use the numbers" habit is best described as: *(Apply)*
    a) functional fixedness
    b) a set effect: a habit that solved yesterday's problems
    c) an algorithm
    d) divided attention

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

1. Explain attention's three properties, with a classroom example of each. *(Understand)*
2. In two sentences, explain why the teacher's "it's obvious, look" fails, using
   perception. *(Understand)*
3. Describe the three memory systems in order, explaining the job of each. *(Understand)*
4. A lesson gives a four-step instruction and then asks a question. Explain why the
   question is often not heard, using working memory. *(Apply)*
5. Name three things that capture the attentional spotlight, with a classroom instance
   of each, and say why "pay attention" commands none of them. *(Understand)*
6. Name the three faces of forgetting and the remedy each answers to. *(Understand)*
7. Explain recognition versus recall, and why an examination asks for the second.
   *(Understand)*
8. Explain any three problem-solving strategies by giving an instance of each. *(Understand)*
9. Distinguish inductive from deductive reasoning with one classroom example of each.
   *(Understand)*
10. In three lines, design a class activity that works with attention and memory,
    naming the rule each line follows. *(Apply)*

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. **The timetable move.** Miss Hina moved the hardest listening to the first period
   and the copying to the noisy hour, and reading marks rose. Using attention's
   properties, analyse why it worked, what it cost, and name one further change using
   a different property. *(Analyze)*

2. **The October chapter.** A trainee learns Pakistan Studies by re-reading on Monday
   night, tests well, and has lost it by December. Using the memory model, analyse
   what was never done, and design the two-week revision plan the evidence supports,
   naming what each element asks of the memory systems. *(Analyze)*

3. **Bilal's 64.** Analyse Bilal's answer as a thinking problem rather than an ability
   verdict: the obstacle, the missing strategies, and what his habit says about the
   problems he has been set. Design the next lesson's first ten minutes and justify
   each element from the unit. *(Analyze)*

4. **The strategy audit.** Audit your own three study strategies against the evidence
   of this unit: what each asks of memory, what the evidence says, and the single
   change you would make. Honesty about your own habits is the criterion. *(Evaluate)*

5. **The whole unit in one lesson.** Design one lesson - any class, any subject - that
   works with the whole unit: the beam aimed and one demand at a time, the workbench
   load fitted and offloaded, a strategy named aloud, and retrieval spaced into the
   week. Justify every design choice by the rule it follows, and name the cost. *(Create)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### MCQ answer key

1. **b** - attention selects what reaches further processing; it is the gateway.
2. **b** - two attention-heavy tasks on one workbench: the copying wins, the
   explaining gets the remainder.
3. **b** - detection versus organised interpretation.
4. **b** - recent estimates put practical capacity near four items.
5. **b** - attention is selective: what is never attended is never encoded; the
   occupied mind literally did not see it.
6. **c** - retrieval practice, spaced, is the best-evidenced of the set.
7. **b** - re-reading creates recognition, which feels like knowing.
8. **c** - comparing where you are with where you must be, step by step: means-ends.
9. **b** - from cases to a rule: induction.
10. **b** - a set effect: the habit generalises from yesterday's same-shaped problems.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

Award marks for the substance below, not for matching wording.

1. Captured not commanded; costly to divide; depletable (1 for all three named); any
   apt examples (1).
2. The teacher's perception is expert: they have seen the diagram a hundred times, so
   its difficulty is invisible to them (1); the class reads it through expectation and
   missing concepts, so it is a puzzle - the remedy is naming the parts (1).
3. Sensory memory selects impressions for a second or two; working memory holds and
   works with a handful of items for seconds; long-term memory keeps what encoding
   connected (1 for all three; 1 for any two jobs accurate).
4. The four steps already occupy the workbench; the question arrives with no space to
   run in (2).
5. Any three of novelty, contrast, meaning and emotion, with apt classroom instances
   (1); "pay attention" is a command, and the beam is captured by properties of the
   task and the distractors, never commanded (1).
6. Decay answers spacing; interference answers distinctiveness; retrieval failure
   answers practice (1 for the three faces, 1 for matching remedies).
7. Recognition is "I have seen this"; recall is "I can produce this" (1); an
   examination asks for production, so only recall counts (1).
8. Any three of algorithm, heuristic, means-ends, working backwards, analogy, with
   instances (2: 1 for three named, 1 for instances).
9. Inductive: cases to rule, e.g. "this teacher always marks late"; deductive: rule to
   case, e.g. "if the test is Friday and today is Thursday..." (2).
10. Any three-line activity in which each line names its rule: e.g. question on the
    board first (aim the beam); givens written down (offload the workbench); strategy
    named aloud (concept formation) (2: 1 for the activity, 1 for the rules named).
