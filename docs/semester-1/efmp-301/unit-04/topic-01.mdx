# Topic 4.1 - Attention and perception

## A real classroom situation

Miss Hina's Class 5 room in Nawabshah sits on the main road. By eleven o'clock the
traffic is steady: rickshaws, a wedding band practising somewhere, the vegetable
seller's call. She has sixty pupils and one blackboard. Her colleague in the quiet
annex teaches the same lesson to the same syllabus, and her results are better, and
the staff room explains this with "better pupils".

Miss Hina tried one thing. She moved the reading lesson to the first period, before
the road woke up, and used the noisy period for copying and drawing. The reading
marks moved first, and the class did not.

No pupil in that room lacks attention. What they have is a gateway that the road is
competing for, at a time of day the road usually wins.

## Explanation

### Attention: the gateway to learning

<Glossary term="Attention" /> is the mind's selection: of all the information arriving
at the senses at any moment, attention is what gets through. It is the gateway to
learning because what is never attended is never encoded - the memory systems of Topic
4.2 receive only what attention lets past.

The most useful image is a **spotlight**. The beam is narrow: it lights one task, one
voice, one problem at a time. Everything outside the beam still exists - the road, the
heat, the neighbour's pencil - but it is not being processed for learning.

Three properties of the spotlight matter to a teacher.

**It is captured, not commanded.** Novelty, contrast, movement, meaning and emotion
pull the beam; instructions to "pay attention" push it nowhere. A teacher competing
with a wedding band for the beam by speaking louder is competing on the band's own
terms - loudness. A teacher who writes the day's question on the board before the band
starts, or moves the hardest listening to the quiet hour, is competing on structure,
which wins.

**It divides only at a price.** Divided attention - the split beam - gives each target
less. The pupil who copies the board while listening to the explanation is running two
tasks on one workbench, and the cost lands on the harder task. This is why "copy this
while I explain" reliably produces notes without understanding: the copying wins the
capacity and the explaining gets the remainder.

**It tires.** Attention flags with sustained use, and it is shortest exactly where
schools demand it most: in hot rooms, after lunch, in the last period.
The evidence on inattentional blindness makes the point uncomfortably: in the classic
study, nearly half of the viewers counting basketball passes failed to notice a person
in a gorilla costume walking through the scene (Simons & Chabris, 1999, cited in
Spielman, Jenkins & Lovett, 2020). A mind occupied is a mind with a closed gateway, and a Class 5 pupil
occupied with the road may simply not hear the lesson.

> **The misconception to clear now.** "Attention is a fixed quantity a pupil either has
> or lacks" - the "he cannot concentrate" verdict. Attention is a system with
> properties, not a trait with a size. The pupil who cannot concentrate for the
> double-period after lunch may concentrate beautifully for twenty minutes in the first
> period. The variable is the design of the demand, and the teacher owns most of the
> design.

### Perception: how the mind interprets what the senses bring

The senses deliver sensations; the mind delivers the world. **Sensation** is detection
by a receptor - light on the retina, sound on the ear. **Perception** is the
organisation and interpretation of what was detected: "the way sensory information is
organized, interpreted, and consciously experienced" (Spielman, Jenkins & Lovett,
2020). The eye receives light; the pupil sees the head teacher.

The classroom consequences of perception being *active* are easy to state and easy to
forget.

**What a pupil sees depends on what they already know.** A Class 4 pupil who reads
"قل" as one shape is not careless; the letters have not yet become, for them, the
parts that a fluent reader perceives automatically. The same is true of every expert
material: a chess board looks different to a player; a page of algebra looks different
to a mathematician.

**Ambiguous materials are perceived through expectation.** A diagram a teacher has
seen a hundred times is unambiguous to the teacher and a puzzle to the class. The
teacher's perception hides the diagram's difficulty - which is why "it's obvious, look"
is the least useful sentence in teaching.

**Perception can be taught.** What experts perceive quickly, novices can learn to
perceive: letter shapes, number bonds, the structure of a graph. Teaching perception
means arranging many encounters with the material, with the parts named, until the
pupil's interpretation becomes automatic. That is one of the quiet jobs of practice.

## Activity: The attention audit

**In pairs, about 20 minutes.**

Take one lesson you know well (your practicum class, or a lesson you remember being
taught). Audit it against this topic in three passes.

1. **The beam.** What was the lesson's single most important demand on attention? How
   long did it run without a break?
2. **The competitors.** List every real competitor for the beam in that room, in the
   order a pupil would notice them.
3. **The split.** Find every moment the lesson demanded two attention-heavy tasks at
   once (copying while listening; writing while watching).

Then agree on the **one change** that would recover the most attention for the
learning, and say whether it costs money.

## Check your understanding

1. Define attention in one sentence, and say why it is the gateway rather than the
   workbench. *(Understand)*
2. Name three things that capture the beam, with a classroom instance of each. *(Remember)*
3. Explain why "copy this while I explain" produces notes without understanding, using
   divided attention. *(Apply)*
4. Distinguish sensation from perception with the blackboard example, in two sentences.
   *(Understand)*
5. The teacher says "it's obvious, look" at a diagram. Using perception, explain why
   the diagram is obvious to the teacher and a puzzle to the class, and name the
   remedy. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Attention is the mind's spotlight: narrow, captured by novelty and meaning rather than
commanded, costly to divide, and depletable - which makes it a design problem before it
is a pupil problem. Perception is the active interpretation of what the senses deliver:
what a pupil sees depends on what they already know, ambiguous materials are read
through expectation, and expert perception is learned through named encounters. A
lesson that works with the gateway - one demand at a time, the beam aimed by structure,
the parts of the material named - beats a louder lesson competing on loudness.

      classroom examples.
- [ ] I can explain the cost of divided attention and find it in a real lesson.
- [ ] I can distinguish sensation from perception and say why the teacher's perception
      hides a material's difficulty.
- [ ] I can name three attention factors and one teacher move for each.

## Try this at your practicum school

Run one attention audit on your own lesson: the beam, the competitors, the split
moments. Then make one structural change (timing, order, one-demand-at-a-time) and note
what changes. Bring the before-and-after to the next session.

## Summative task

Write **250-300 words** on Miss Hina's timetable move.

Her change - hardest listening to the first period, copying to the noisy hour - raised
reading marks. Using attention's properties, explain exactly why it worked: which
property the road was exploiting, what the move did to the competition, and what it
cost. Then name one further change using a *different* property, and justify it.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong answer shows |
|---|---|
| Property precision | The explanation names the specific attention properties at work, not "noise is bad". |
| Cost honesty | The trade-off of the timetable move is named. *(Analyze)* |
| Second move | The further change uses a different property and is realistic in her school. |
| Plain, accurate writing | Terms used correctly; no padding; within the word count. |
