# Topic 8.3 - Managing discipline and behavior

## A real classroom situation

A head teacher in Tando Allahyar keeps an escalation ladder pinned inside her desk
drawer, and every teacher who has borrowed it says the same thing: it works because
it is boring. Proximity first - walk near, say nothing. Then the private word, at the
desk, not the room. Then the choice ("work now or at break - you decide"). Then the
consequence, stated before it is chosen, never invented in anger. The parent meeting
comes last, and arrives with a record, not a mood.

Two streets away, a teacher handles the same behaviour by naming the pupil to the
whole class. It works too, briefly, and the cost arrives on schedule: the pupil who
will not risk a wrong answer in that room again, and the class that has learned what
happens to the visible.

## Explanation

### Managing discipline: rules, routines and responsibility

Discipline is the class-level face of management: the standards a room holds and how
it holds them. Two findings carry most of the weight.

**Rules should be few and about responsibility.** Rules are not procedures (Topic
8.1's routines); they "express standards of behavior for which individual students
need to take responsibility", and they exist to encourage "students to be responsible
for learning and showing respect for each other" (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). A workable
set is short - five, not fifteen - because a rule nobody can remember is a rule nobody
can follow. The best sets are written with the class, in September, in the pupils'
own words; a rule the class wrote is defended by the class.

**Consequences should be natural or logical, never arbitrary.** Natural consequences
happen on their own: the pupil who arrives late misses the instructions. Logical
consequences "happen because of the responses of or decisions by others, but that
also have an obvious or 'logical' relationship to the original action" - the pupil
who takes a lunch replaces it (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). Both connect the outcome to
the act; the arbitrary consequence (miss the mathematics test for talking in Urdu)
teaches only that power exists.

### Managing individual behavior: responses and escalation

Individual behaviour needs the ladder, and the ladder needs its rules of use.

**Start at the bottom, every time.** Proximity - the walk past the desk - resolves
more low-level disruption than any raised voice, because it changes the situation
without announcing anything. The private word resolves most of the rest, because it
removes the audience that the behaviour was partly for (Unit 3's attention
reinforcement).

**Give the choice before the consequence.** "Work now or at break - you decide"
moves the decision to the pupil, which is where a consequence teaches anything at
all. The consequence is stated before it is chosen and is never invented in anger: a
consequence invented mid-anger is a punishment with a costume on (Unit 3's
distinction, now wearing a tie).

**Keep dignity at every step.** Nothing on the ladder requires an audience, a label
or a comparison. The pupil who is named to the room learns that visibility is
dangerous; the pupil who is corrected privately learns that the behaviour, not the
self, is the problem. The ladder's whole design is the difference between those two
lessons.

**Read behaviour developmentally before morally** (Unit 2's habit, now with a
ladder). The shouting pupil may be regulating badly (Unit 2's emotional domain); the
off-task pupil may be outside the zone (Unit 3); the refusing pupil may be protecting
(Unit 6's loop). None of this cancels the standard - the behaviour still meets the
ladder - but it changes which support is offered beside it, and it keeps the teacher
curious where the staff room is certain.

## Activity: Place it on the ladder

**In pairs, about 20 minutes.**

For each of the four behaviours in the figure: place the response you have most
often seen used on the ladder (which step was skipped?), then place the response you
would use, and name the principle that justifies it.

Then the hard one: write the private word you would have with the work-refusing
pupil - the actual sentences, no more than three - and check them against the
dignity rule before you show your partner.

## Check your understanding

1. Distinguish rules from routines, with one example of each. *(Understand)*
2. Explain why a workable rule set is short, and what its rules are about. *(Understand)*
3. Distinguish natural from logical consequences with one example of each. *(Understand)*
4. Name the ladder's five steps and the principle any one of them serves. *(Remember)*
5. The pupil refuses work. Give the developmental reading, the ladder response, and
   the support you would offer beside the standard. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Discipline is the room's standards and how it holds them. Rules should be few and
about responsibility, written with the class in their own words; consequences should
be natural or logical, connecting outcome to act, never arbitrary. Individual
behaviour meets a ladder that starts at proximity, keeps dignity at every step,
gives the choice before the consequence, and arrives at the parent meeting with a
record rather than a mood. Behaviour is read developmentally before morally - the
standard holds, and the support changes - and the ladder is tried a step at a time
because going up is a choice, not a slip.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can distinguish rules from routines and state what rules are about.
- [ ] I can distinguish natural from logical consequences with examples.
- [ ] I can name the ladder's five steps and the principle each serves.
- [ ] I can read one common behaviour for its function and choose the first
      response.

## Try this at your practicum school

Borrow the ladder for one week: proximity, private word, choice, consequence, parent
meeting - each tried before the next. Keep a tally of where behaviours actually
resolved. Bring the tally to the next session; the distribution will not be what the
staff room predicts.

## Summative task

Write **250-300 words** on the teacher who names to the room.

Analyse the naming strategy: what it achieves, what it costs, and on what schedule
the costs arrive (use Unit 3's reinforcement and Unit 6's anxiety). Then design the
same week with the ladder: what changes for the named pupil, for the room, and for
the teacher's own authority - which, notice, is the thing the naming was defending.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong answer shows |
|---|---|
| The costs are mechanistic | Reinforcement and anxiety explain the damage, not disapproval. |
| The ladder redesign is concrete | Each level of the week is placed, and dignity is visible in it. *(Analyze)* |
| Authority re-examined | The answer says what the ladder does for authority that naming undoes. |
| Plain, accurate writing | Terms used correctly; no padding; within the word count. |
