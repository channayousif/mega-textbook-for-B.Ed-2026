# Topic 8.2 - Creating positive learning environments

## A real classroom situation

A Class 6 room in Sukkur has one feature nobody planned: the pupils answer wrong out
loud. Ask the room a hard question and hands go up anyway; a wrong answer gets
"good try" from the pupil's neighbours without a glance at the teacher. New pupils
take about two weeks to start doing it too.

The feature was built, mostly by accident, in the first week of the year. The teacher
answered the first wrong answer with "good try - how did you get that?" (Unit 3's
Miss Sadia, now with a room around her), seated the quiet pupil where she could
always reach the board, and never once said "obviously". Two years later the room
still does it, and the teacher could not name the day it started.

## Explanation

### The parts of a positive learning environment

A positive learning environment is not a mood; it is a set of designed parts, each
of which can be built, starved or broken (Seifert & Sutton, 2009).

**Physical space.** Desks arranged so every pupil can see the board and the teacher
can see every pupil; wall space that shows the pupils' own work (not only the shop
bought charts); materials reachable without a cross-room expedition. Space is the
one part a teacher may share with forty others, but the desk grid and the wall are
usually negotiable, and both send a message about who the room belongs to.

**Routines.** Topic 8.1's principle, now as a part of the environment: the decided
answers that make the day's shape. In a positive environment the routines are
knowable by the pupils, not merely enforced on them - which is why the first week
teaches them rather than announcing them.

**Relationships.** The teacher knows the names before the first month ends; the
greeting at the door is routine; the pupil is seen as a person before a roll number.
This is Unit 6's relatedness with a timetable. It is also the part most tested by
the difficult pupil: the relationship has to exist before it is needed, which is the
whole argument for the greeting.

**Participation norms.** What the room does when someone speaks. Hands go up even
when unsure; wrong answers are worked with, not laughed at; the quiet pupil is
reached by design (a question they can answer, asked where only the teacher hears
it). Norms are taught by what the teacher does in the first week and maintained by
what the room does to itself ever after - the Sukkur room's "good try" is a norm
that outlived its author.

**Feedback.** At the centre, because it touches every part: the next step named,
not a verdict delivered (Unit 3's marking lesson, now spoken). Feedback that names
what to do next keeps the environment future-facing; feedback that ranks keeps it
fearful.

### The first-week moves

Each part has a first-week move, and the moves are cheap.

For space: seat for sight before anything else, and put one piece of pupil work on
the wall in week one - the room belongs to its users from the start. For routines:
teach the 8:05, the ending, the materials routine as content, with practice, not
announcement. For relationships: learn the names (a seating map on day one does
half of it), and greet at the door. For norms: answer the first wrong answer of the
year the way you want all wrong answers answered for the rest of it (Unit 3's
consequential moment). For feedback: mark the first work for the next step only.

The first week is not a soft start. It is the week with the highest management
leverage of the year: every routine taught then is a thousand decisions never made
again, and every norm set then is defended by the room itself thereafter.

## Activity: The room audit

**In pairs, about 20 minutes.**

Take one classroom you both know. Audit it against the five parts: for each, write
one line of evidence that the part is built, starved or broken. Do not judge the
teacher; describe the room.

Then, together: choose the **one part** whose repair would most change the room, and
write the first-week move for it - specific, cheap, and doable by one teacher.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the five parts of a positive learning environment. *(Remember)*
2. Explain how the Sukkur room's "good try" norm was built, in two sentences.
   *(Understand)*
3. Why must the relationship exist before it is needed? Answer with the difficult
   pupil in mind. *(Understand)*
4. Name the first-week move for any three parts. *(Remember)*
5. A room is silent and the work is wrong. Which part is most likely broken, and
   what does its repair look like? *(Apply)*

## Summary

A positive learning environment is designed, not felt: physical space that shows who
the room belongs to, routines the pupils can know, relationships built before they
are needed, participation norms taught by the teacher's first answers and then
defended by the room, and feedback that names the next step. Every part has a
first-week move, and the first week carries the year's highest management leverage -
the week that decides a thousand later answers once, and lets the room start
defending itself.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can name the five parts with evidence of each in a real room.
- [ ] I can explain how a norm is built and how it outlives its author.
- [ ] I can name the first-week move for each part.
- [ ] I can audit a room without judging its teacher.

## Try this at your practicum school

Audit one room against the five parts, one line of evidence each. Then make one
first-week move real now - a piece of pupil work on the wall, a greeting at the door
- and note what changes in a fortnight.

## Summative task

Write **250-300 words** on the first week of a difficult class.

You inherit the second Class 5 room from Topic 8.1 in a new year, with the same
pupils' reputations arriving before they do. Design the first week: the decisions
made once, the moves for each of the five parts, and the one norm you would build
first and how. Name what you would deliberately NOT do in that week.
