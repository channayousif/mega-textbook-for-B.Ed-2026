# Topic 9.1 - Types and purposes of assessment

## A real classroom situation

One Class 7 in Nawabshah, one year, three assessments. In April, before the
mathematics unit began, the teacher gave a ten-minute check on the previous year's
fractions work - and found half the class could not add unlike fractions, so the
first fortnight was re-planned. Every Tuesday since, a five-question exit quiz has
told her what to re-teach on Wednesday. In March, the board examination will rank
the class against the province.

Three assessments, three purposes, and only the third one looks like what the staff
room calls "a test". The April check and the Tuesday quiz are assessments too - and
arguably the ones that did the most teaching.

## Explanation

### Types of assessment and the decisions they feed

<Glossary term="Assessment" /> is the gathering of evidence about learning so that
somebody can decide something. The definition is deliberately empty of tests:
assessment is any systematic evidence - an observed task, a question, a portfolio, a
performance - gathered for a decision. The definition's value is that it makes the
**decision** the organising question, not the instrument. An assessment without a
decision to feed is paperwork.

Four purposes cover the classroom territory (Seifert & Sutton, 2009).

**Diagnose**: what do they bring? The April check is diagnostic - administered
*before* instruction, to find the starting point. Its decision: where to begin, and
what to repair first. A diagnostic finding (half the class missing a prior step) is
not a failure report; it is a planning input.

**Form**: what is landing now? The Tuesday quiz is formative - administered *during*
learning, to shape the next move. Its decision: re-teach, advance, or re-group.
Formative assessment is the only type whose purpose is to change teaching while
there is still teaching left to change.

**Sum**: what has been mastered? The December test is summative - "administered
after the instruction is completed", providing "information about how well students
mastered the material, whether students are ready for the next unit, and what grades
should be given" (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). Its decisions: readiness and grades.

**Certify**: what can this person do? The board examination is a certification
instrument - its decision extends beyond the classroom, into selection and
qualification. Certification is why standardisation exists (Topic 9.2).

Two families cut across all four purposes. **Teacher-made** assessments are written
by the teacher for this class, this week: free, fast, and exactly matched - at the
price of comparability. **Standardised** assessments are written for many classes at
once, administered and scored the same way everywhere: comparable and defensible -
at the price of fit. Neither family is better; they answer different questions, and
a school needs both.

> **The misconception to clear now.** "Assessment means marks at the end." Marks at
> the end are one purpose among four, and the least formative of them. The habit to
> build is the reverse question: not "is this a good test?" but "what decision is
> this feeding, and would a different instrument feed it better?" The April check
> fed its decision better than any end-of-unit test could have, because it arrived
> when the decision was still open.

## Activity: Name the decision

**In pairs, about 20 minutes.**

List every assessment you can remember from your own schooling - at least eight,
including the small ones. For each, name: the type (diagnose, form, sum, certify),
the decision it fed (or should have fed), and whether the instrument matched the
decision.

Then find the **mismatch** you remember best - the assessment whose instrument did
not fit its decision - and say what instrument would have fit.

## Check your understanding

1. Define assessment in one sentence, and say why the decision - not the instrument
   - organises it. *(Understand)*
2. Name the four purposes with the question each asks. *(Remember)*
3. Distinguish diagnostic from formative assessment by decision, not timing. *(Understand)*
4. What does a summative assessment provide information about, on the research
   summary? *(Remember)*
5. The April check re-planned a fortnight. Name its type, its decision, and why an
   end-of-unit test could not have done its work. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Assessment is evidence gathered for a decision. Its purposes are four: diagnose
(before, feeding where to start), form (during, feeding tomorrow's teaching), sum
(after, feeding mastery, readiness and grades) and certify (feeding selection and
qualification beyond the classroom). Teacher-made and standardised instruments cut
across all four, trading fit for comparability in opposite directions. The
discipline is to name the decision first and choose the instrument second - because
the best assessment is the one that arrives while its decision is still open.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can define assessment and name the four purposes with their questions.
- [ ] I can distinguish the purposes by decision, not by timing or instrument.
- [ ] I can name the decision any real assessment feeds - including the small ones.
- [ ] I can spot an instrument-decision mismatch and say what would fit.

## Try this at your practicum school

Take one week of the cooperating teacher's assessments - every question, quiz,
observation and mark. Name each one's type and the decision it fed. Count how many
were formative. The count is usually the week's most useful finding.

## Summative task

Write **250-300 words** on the fourth decision.

The Class 7 year had three assessments for three decisions - but a fourth sat
unaddressed: which pupils need which support next term. Using this topic, argue what
instrument should feed that decision, when it should run, and what the three
existing assessments cannot tell it. Name the type and defend the timing.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong answer shows |
|---|---|
| The decision is named precisely | Support allocation, not a vague "progress". |
| The instrument fits | The choice follows from the decision, with its type named. *(Analyze)* |
| The limits are real | What the three assessments cannot tell is argued, not asserted. |
| Plain, accurate writing | Terms used correctly; no padding; within the word count. |
