# Topic 9.2 - Psychological tests and measurement

## A real classroom situation

A Class 7 geography test asks pupils to "identify geography tools and their uses".
The class's newest arrival, eight months from a Sindhi-medium school, knows every
tool by sight and use - and cannot yet read the English sentence that asks. She
scores 40 per cent. The staff room reads the score as weak geography.

The score is not weak geography. It is a measurement of a mixture - some geography,
some English reading - wearing the name of one of them. The defect has a name
(validity), the mixture has a cure (knowing what you are measuring), and the whole
trouble begins with a question nobody asked: what, exactly, does an interpretation
of this score claim?

## Explanation

### Psychological tests: what they are and what they measure

A psychological or educational test is a standardised instrument: a set of tasks,
fixed administration conditions, and fixed scoring rules, built so that results can
be compared - across pupils, across classes, across years. "Standardised" names what
the test controls: the same items, given the same way, scored the same way,
everywhere (Seifert & Sutton, 2009).

Standardisation buys comparability at the price of fit. A teacher-made test written
for this class, this week, fits its decision exactly - and cannot be compared with
any other class's result. A standardised test compares across the province - and
asks this class's current question only by coincidence. The two families answer
different questions, and the choice between them is Topic 9.1's decision-first
discipline applied to instruments.

What no test measures is "geography" in the abstract. Every test measures a
performance on a sample of tasks, under conditions the test sets (Unit 5's lesson,
now with a purpose). The geography test measured the new arrival's English reading
as surely as her geography - which is why the interpretation of a score, not the
score, is where quality lives.

### Measurement quality: validity and reliability

Two properties decide whether an interpretation deserves trust, and they are
different properties (Seifert & Sutton, 2009).

**Validity** is "the evaluation of the 'adequacy and appropriateness of the
interpretations and uses of assessment results' for a given group of individuals".
The crucial word is *interpretations*: validity belongs to the claim made about a
score, not to the test itself. The same fractions test may support valid
interpretations for one group and invalid ones for another. The geography staff
room's claim - "she is weak at geography" - is the invalidity: a rival explanation
(English reading) fits the evidence just as well. A valid interpretation has ruled
out the rivals, or names the mixture honestly.

**Reliability** is "the consistency of the measurement". Would the scores be similar
on a Friday and a Monday? With different items from the same unit? If a different
teacher graded them? An unreliable measure produces different answers to the same
question - like a watch that gains ten minutes a day, which is useless even if it
was set correctly.

The two are independent, and the independence is the practical lesson. A test can be
reliable and invalid: consistently measuring the wrong mixture (the geography test,
given twice, would produce the same unfair 40 per cent). A test can aim at validity
and be unreliable: a rich project task that measures real understanding, scored
differently by every marker. The first needs a better question; the second needs a
better rubric - different repairs.

A third requirement completes the set: the **absence of bias** - no group of pupils
should be systematically disadvantaged by content or language irrelevant to what is
being measured (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). The new arrival's case is a bias case as
well as a validity case: the irrelevant English load fell on one group of pupils.

> **The misconception to clear now.** "A difficult test is a good test." Difficulty
> is not a property of quality. A test can be difficult and valid (it samples the
> unit's real demands), or difficult and invalid (it samples reading load the unit
> never taught), or easy and reliable. The quality questions are always the same
> two - does the interpretation fit what was measured, and would the measurement
> repeat? - and neither is answered by the pass rate.

## Activity: The same quiz, twice

**In pairs, about 20 minutes.**

1. One partner writes five one-mark questions on any topic the pair both know; the
   other answers them. Swap roles with a second topic.
2. Now each partner marks the other's answers - then marks them AGAIN, an hour
   later, without looking at the first marking. Compare the two markings.
3. Where they differ, name the cause: unclear rubric? ambiguous wording? marker
   mood? Each cause is a reliability repair waiting to happen.
4. Finally: name one interpretation each pair of scores could NOT support, and say
   why (the validity question).

## Check your understanding

1. What does a standardised test standardise? Name three things. *(Remember)*
2. Explain what a teacher-made test trades away, and what it gains. *(Understand)*
3. Define validity, and say why it belongs to the interpretation, not the test.
   *(Understand)*
4. Define reliability with the three consistency questions. *(Remember)*
5. The geography test is reliable and invalid. Explain both claims, and name the
   different repair each needs. *(Apply)*

## Summary

A test is a standardised instrument: fixed tasks, conditions and scoring, buying
comparability at the price of fit - the exact opposite trade to the teacher-made
test, and both trades are right for different decisions. No test measures a subject
in the abstract; every score is a performance on a sample, and quality lives in the
interpretation. Validity is the adequacy of that interpretation for this group -
ruling out rival explanations or naming the mixture. Reliability is consistency -
across days, items and markers. The two are independent: each has its own failure
and its own repair, and neither is answered by difficulty.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask the cooperating teacher for one recent test. Mark it yourself, then mark it
again the next day without looking back. Where your own markings differ, you have
found the test's reliability repairs - and you will never write an ambiguous
question the same way again.

## Summative task

Write **250-300 words** on the 40 per cent.

Evaluate the geography test: what the score measures, what interpretation it can and
cannot support, whether it is reliable, and where the bias sits. Then repair it in
two moves - one for validity, one for reliability - and say what each repair costs.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong answer shows |
|---|---|
| The mixture named | The score is read as geography-plus-English, with evidence. |
| Validity and reliability separated | Each is evaluated with its own criterion, and the repairs differ. *(Analyze)* |
| Bias located | The group-level disadvantage is named, not just the individual case. |
| Plain, accurate writing | Terms used correctly; no padding; within the word count. |
