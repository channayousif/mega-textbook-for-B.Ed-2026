# Topic 9.3 - Formative and summative evaluation

## A real classroom situation

In this authored classroom illustration, two teachers in the same corridor use the same five-question quiz. The first gives
it on Tuesday, reads the answers that evening, and re-teaches the gap on Wednesday;
the marks go nowhere except her planning book. The second gives the same quiz on
Tuesday and enters the marks in the term record the same night.

Same items, same day, same subject. The first use is formative because it informs
the next lesson; the second is summative because it contributes to a grade. This
illustration isolates the uses, but a teacher could use the evidence for both.
Pupils might respond differently to feedback and grading; the case does not
establish a fixed response or a one-month timescale.

## Explanation

### Formative evaluation: assessment that teaches

<Glossary term="Formative Assessment" /> is assessment whose purpose is to shape
learning while learning is still under way: "Using assessment to advance students'
learning, not just check on learning, requires viewing assessment as a process that
is integral to the all phases of teaching, including planning, classroom
interactions and instruction, communication with parents, and self-reflection"
(Seifert & Sutton, 2009).

The formative cycle has three steps, and the third is the one that makes it
formative.

**Elicit**: gather evidence of learning now. The instrument is whatever reaches the
evidence fastest - the exit question, the worked task on the board, the two-minute
talk with a group, the show-me card. The instrument should be small enough to read
and act on the same day.

**Interpret**: read the evidence against the goal. What landed, what did not, and
for whom? The interpretation is against the instructional goal, not against the
class average - the same stage-reading discipline as Unit 2, applied to evidence.

**Act**: use the evidence to choose the next teaching move: re-teach a gap, advance
the ready, or re-group pupils. Gathering and filing evidence alone does not make
its use formative. This unit's elicit-interpret-act cycle is an authored teaching
summary of assessment for learning (Seifert & Sutton, 2009, Chapter 11). Its pace
depends on when a useful decision can be made, sometimes within a lesson.

Seifert and Sutton (2009, Chapter 11) also explain that assessment type and the
feedback given influence motivation and confidence. In our illustration, useful
feedback might encourage revision, while an unexplained rank might discourage
some pupils. These are possible responses, not inevitable effects of grading.
Ask pupils about their responses and examine participation before concluding.

### Summative evaluation: assessment that certifies

<Glossary term="Summative Assessment" /> is assessment after instruction is
complete: it "provide[s] information about how well students mastered the material,
whether students are ready for the next unit, and what grades should be given"
(Seifert & Sutton, 2009). Its decisions are real - readiness, grades,
certification - and nothing in this unit argues against holding them.

A December result cannot change lessons that already happened in November. It can,
however, guide January support, re-teaching or the next unit. That is a formative
use of evidence first gathered for a summative decision. This is the unit's
application of the source's account of assessment across planning and instruction,
not a prohibition on using an instrument twice (Seifert & Sutton, 2009, Chapter 11).

Likewise, recording a Tuesday quiz for a grade does not stop a teacher from using
its errors to plan Wednesday. Explain both uses, give useful feedback, and check
that the evidence supports each decision fairly. **Name the decision first, and
let the decision choose the timing.** Purpose defines the use; timing matters
because evidence must arrive before the decision it is meant to inform.

## Activity: Design the cycle

**In pairs, about 25 minutes.**

Choose one real topic you could teach in a fortnight (any class, any subject).

1. Write the instructional goal in one sentence - what they should be able to DO by
   the end.
2. Design the fortnight's formative cycle: what you elicit (and when), how you
   interpret it (against what), and what you would change in the next lesson for
   each of three possible findings.
3. Then design the end-of-fortnight summative item - one question that would show
   the goal is met - and say why it is a summative decision and not a formative
   one.

## Check your understanding

1. State the formative-summative distinction in one sentence, as the unit draws it.
   *(Understand)*
2. Name the formative cycle's three steps, and say which one makes it formative.
   *(Remember)*
3. Explain how the same quiz became formative in one room and summative in the
   next. *(Understand)*
4. What does assessment influence besides achievement, on the research summary?
   *(Remember)*
5. Why can a December test not do the April check's work? Name the decision and the
   timing rule. *(Understand)*

## Summary

Formative use shapes ongoing or future learning through evidence, interpretation
and action; summative use reports mastery, readiness or grades after instruction.
The same evidence can serve both when its uses are explicit and justified. Results
cannot change past lessons but can guide future support. Assessment and feedback
can influence motivation and confidence; no fixed reaction or timescale follows.
Name the decision first and gather evidence in time to inform it.

## Try this at your practicum school

Introduce one formative cycle: a two-question exit slip on Wednesday, read that
evening, acted on Thursday. Keep the slips out of the record book. After two weeks,
ask the cooperating teacher what changed - and notice what the class does with the
slips once they learn where the marks do not go.

## Summative task

Write **250-300 words** designing a formative cycle.

Choose one real topic. Design its fortnight: the goal (one sentence, a DO), the
eliciting instruments (with days), the interpretations (against what), and the
teaching moves for three possible findings. Then name the one finding that would
send you back to the planning book rather than the next lesson, and defend the
summative item that closes the fortnight.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong answer shows |
|---|---|
| The cycle is real | Elicit, interpret and act are named with days and instruments. |
| The three findings have three moves | The teaching changes per finding, not one generic "re-teach". *(Create)* |
| The planning-book case is defended | It names the finding that exceeds a lesson-shaped repair. |
| Plain, accurate writing | Terms used correctly; no padding; within the word count. |
