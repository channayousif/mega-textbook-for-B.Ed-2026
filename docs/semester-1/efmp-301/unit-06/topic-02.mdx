# Topic 6.2 - Achievement motivation and emotions in learning

## A real classroom situation

Two pupils fail the same mathematics test. On the way out, Amna says: "I did not
practise enough. I will start the sums earlier next month." Zubair says: "I am just
not a maths person. My father was the same."

Neither statement is about mathematics. Both are **attributions** - explanations of
outcome - and they differ on the one dimension that decides what happens next. Amna's
cause is unstable and controllable: it can change, and she can change it. Zubair's is
stable and uncontrollable: it cannot change, and he cannot change it. Amna will
practise; Zubair will protect.

Six weeks later, Amna's marks have risen and Zubair has stopped attempting the
hardest section entirely - "because there is no point". The staff room calls this
attitude. The theory calls it the attribution loop running twice.

## Explanation

### Achievement motivation: why some pupils aim and some protect

Achievement motivation is the drive to do well against a standard - and the research
summary in Seifert & Sutton (2009) sorts its pupils by the **goals** they hold.
**Mastery goals** aim at competence: to get better. **Performance goals** aim at
appearance: to look good. And among performance goals sits the destructive member of
the family: **failure-avoidant goals**, whose aim is simply not to look bad.

The pupil who aims and the pupil who protect differ most after failure. The
mastery-oriented pupil reads failure as information: the attempt was wrong, not the
self. The failure-avoidant pupil reads failure as verdict, and the rational response
to a verdict is to avoid the courtroom - unattempted questions cannot be wrong.
Zubair's abandoned section is failure-avoidance doing its arithmetic.

Two things a teacher can do follow directly. **Make mastery visible**: progress the
pupil can see - this week's sums against last week's - feeds the mastery goal without
feeding the comparison. **Change what failure costs publicly**: the marking of a
wrong attempt as information (Unit 3's Miss Sadia and her "good try") lowers the
courtroom's price of appearing.

### The attribution loop

What connects an outcome to the next attempt is the explanation between them, and
attributions have three dimensions (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). **Locus**: is the cause
inside me (effort, ability) or outside (luck, task difficulty)? **Stability**: is it
fixed (ability, aptitude) or changeable (effort, strategy)? **Controllability**: can
I affect it?

The loop runs: outcome, explanation, expectation, attempt. A **changeable,
controllable** explanation ("I did not practise") sets an expectation that trying
differently can change the result - and the next attempt comes. A **stable,
uncontrollable** explanation ("I am not a maths person") sets an expectation that
nothing can change the result - and the rational next move is self-protection.

The teacher's leverage sits in box two, and it is not argument. Telling Zubair he is
wrong about himself is another courtroom appearance. What moves attributions is
**structured experience**: tasks sized so effort visibly pays, feedback that names
the controllable cause ("your method missed the carrying - here is the fix"), and the
class's own history of improvement kept in public record. Amna's sentence came from
somewhere; the somewhere can be built.

> **The misconception to clear now.** "He has a bad attitude." Attitude is the staff
> room's word for an attribution it has not listened to. The pupil who says "there is
> no point" is not describing his character; he is describing his expectation, and
> the expectation has a history that can be traced and changed. The theory does not
> excuse the abandoning of the hard section - it explains it, and explanation is the
> beginning of remedy.

### Emotions in learning: anxiety, interest and curiosity

Emotions are not the weather around learning; they are part of its machinery. Three
do daily work in a classroom.

**Anxiety** narrows. A little sharpens focus on a known task; a lot narrows the
workbench (Unit 4) until the pupil can hold only the fear. The pupil who freezes at
oral reading in English is not short of English; he is short of working memory. The
levers: familiarity (practise the format, not just the content), structure (known
steps reduce the unknown), and the calm naming of the feeling - a teacher who says
"nerves mean it matters" has already returned some of the workbench.

**Interest** widens. Situational interest - the spark of the demonstration jar - is
cheap and brief; personal interest - the pupil who reads about trains for years - is
expensive and durable (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). The teaching move is the bridge:
catch with the situational, keep with the personal, by returning to what caught and
letting the pupil go deeper into it.

**Curiosity** is the appetite for a specific gap - the question that opens. It
thrives where questions are safe, gaps are visible, and answers are findable. A
classroom that answers every question it did not ask is teaching curiosity out.

## Activity: Change the attribution

**In pairs, about 20 minutes.**

Below are four things pupils actually say after failing a test. For each: name the
attribution (effort, ability, luck, or task difficulty), place it on the three
dimensions, say which loop it feeds, and write the one sentence you would say back -
not to argue, but to move the explanation.

1. "The test was unfair."
2. "I am bad at this subject."
3. "I did not sleep last night."
4. "I studied the wrong chapter."

Then pick the one you found hardest to answer, and say what structured experience -
not what argument - would move it over a term.

## Check your understanding

1. Distinguish mastery goals from performance and failure-avoidant goals, with one
   classroom sign of each. *(Understand)*
2. Name the three attribution dimensions and place "I did not practise" on each.
   *(Remember)*
3. Explain why Zubair abandoned the hardest section, using the protect loop. *(Apply)*
4. Why does telling Zubair he is wrong about himself fail? Give the better lever, and
   say why it works. *(Analyze)*
5. Name the three emotions and one teacher move for each. *(Remember)*

## Summary

Achievement motivation sorts pupils by their goals - mastery, performance, and the
destructive failure-avoidance - and the aiming pupil and the protecting pupil differ
most after failure. Between outcome and attempt sits the attribution, on three
dimensions: locus, stability, controllability. Changeable, controllable explanations
feed the approach loop; stable, uncontrollable ones feed the protect loop; and what
moves them is structured experience, not argument. Emotions run through all of it:
anxiety narrows the workbench, interest widens and holds, curiosity opens gaps - and
each has a teacher move that costs attention, not money.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can distinguish mastery, performance and failure-avoidant goals with signs.
- [ ] I can name the three attribution dimensions and place real explanations on them.
- [ ] I can trace one pupil's after-test talk into the loop it feeds.
- [ ] I can name the three emotions' effects on learning and one move for each.

## Try this at your practicum school

For one week, write down what pupils say after wrong answers and failed tests -
verbatim, four or five sentences. Bring the list to the next session and place each
on the three dimensions. The list is the class's attribution profile, and it will
tell you more than the marks do.

## Summative task

Write **250-300 words** on Zubair's December.

Trace Zubair's path from the failed test to the abandoned section through the
attribution loop - outcome, explanation, expectation, attempt - and say where a
teacher could have intervened in October. Then design that intervention: the
structured experience, not the argument, that would have moved the explanation, and
name what would have made it fail.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong answer shows |
|---|---|
| The loop traced | All four stages are named with the evidence from the description. |
| The intervention is structural | Experience, feedback or visible progress - not persuasion. *(Analyze)* |
| The failure condition | What would make the intervention fail is named honestly. |
| Plain, accurate writing | Terms used correctly; no padding; within the word count. |
