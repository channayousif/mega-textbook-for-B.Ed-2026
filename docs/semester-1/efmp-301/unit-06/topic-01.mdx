# Topic 6.1 - Why learners try: theories of motivation

## A real classroom situation

A Class 8 in a Karachi school stops trying in October. The teacher's first response is
a prize chart - the weekly topper gets a cricket sticker - and for two weeks the marks
rise. By December the chart is ignored, the marks are back down, and a pupil who used
to write stories for pleasure now writes nothing at all, "because there is no point
unless it counts".

Meanwhile the school's midday meal has quietly changed Class 3's afternoons, and a
project in which Class 9 chose their own topics has pupils working past the bell. The
staff room has three explanations - prizes work, food works, freedom works - and no
way to choose among them.

The theories in this topic are the way to choose.

## Explanation

### What motivation is: intrinsic and extrinsic

"Motivation describes the wants or needs that direct behavior toward a goal" (Spielman,
Jenkins & Lovett, 2020). The definition's value is that it puts the cause of behaviour
inside the pupil - in wants and needs - rather than in the pupil's character. An
unmotivated pupil is not a defective pupil; they are a pupil whose wants are pointed
somewhere else. The teacher's question changes from "why won't he?" to "what is he
motivated by, and can the lesson meet it?"

The two great forms are named by their source. **Intrinsic motivation**: doing a thing
"because of the sense of personal satisfaction that it brings" - the pupil who writes
stories for pleasure. **Extrinsic motivation**: doing it "in order to receive something
from others" - the pupil who writes for the sticker (Spielman, Jenkins & Lovett,
2020). Both are real and both move pupils; the trouble starts when they mix.

The trouble has a name: the **overjustification effect** - "intrinsic motivation is
diminished when extrinsic motivation is given" (Spielman, Jenkins & Lovett, 2020). The
prize chart paid the class for what some of them were already doing for free, and the
payment re-told them what the writing was *for*. The stories, now work, stopped. The
effect does not make rewards forbidden; it makes them a scalpel. Reward effort and
progress where no intrinsic motive exists yet; do not buy what the pupil already owns.

> **The misconception to clear now.** "Punishment motivates." What punishment does is
> suppress - Unit 3's operant lesson - and it carries the price Unit 3 named: avoidance
> of the punisher, the subject and the risk of trying. A pupil working to avoid a
> scolding is motivated, technically, by the removal of fear; the staff room calls
> this motivation and the theory calls it a debt that compounds.

### Maslow: the hierarchy of needs

Abraham Maslow proposed "a hierarchy of needs that spans the spectrum of motives
ranging from the biological to the individual to the social" (Spielman, Jenkins &
Lovett, 2020). The levels, in order: physiological needs; safety and security; love
and belonging; esteem; and at the apex self-actualization - "reaching one's full
potential", attainable only once the lower needs are met. Maslow also distinguished
**deficit needs** (the lower four, which demand relief) from **growth needs** (the
top, which pull rather than push) - and held that the deficit needs must be satisfied
before growth needs can influence behaviour (Seifert & Sutton, 2009).

For a teacher the hierarchy is a **triage order**. The Class 3 whose afternoons
changed when the midday meal arrived is the hierarchy working exactly as Maslow drew
it: a hungry pupil cannot learn, and no pedagogy competes with an empty stomach. The
pupil who cannot learn while afraid is on the second step; the isolated pupil, on the
third; the pupil who is never noticed, on the fourth. The teacher who demands
self-actualization - curiosity, ambition, growth - from pupils whose deficit needs are
unmet is demanding the one thing the theory says cannot be demanded first.

The honest caution: the hierarchy's strict order does not hold in every case - people
do fast for belief and create in despair. Use it as a checklist of what might be in
the way, not a ladder with a lock on it.

### Herzberg: the two-factor theory

The course guide names Frederick Herzberg, whose two-factor theory came from studies
of workplace satisfaction (Herzberg is standard history in management psychology; the
guide names him, and this topic states his theory at that level).

Herzberg's finding was asymmetrical: the things that make people **satisfied** at work
are not the same things whose absence makes them **dissatisfied**. **Motivators** -
achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, growth - drive
satisfaction. **Hygiene factors** - pay, working conditions, policy, supervision,
relationships - do not motivate when present; their absence demotivates. Removing
dissatisfaction and creating satisfaction are two different jobs.

Applied carefully to schools, the theory explains the staff room's three explanations.
The two schools of Unit 2's Nawabshah scene - one with functioning fans and clean
water, one without - differ in **hygiene**: the second school cannot motivate anyone
until the fans work, and the first school has not thereby motivated anyone either.
The Class 9 project is a **motivator**: achievement, responsibility and the work
itself. A school that fixes hygiene and stops there has a comfortable building full of
pupils with no reason to try; a school that adds motivators on top of broken hygiene
is pouring water into a cracked pot.

The caution is the mirror of Maslow's: the theory was built on adult workers, and a
classroom is not a workplace. Pupils did not apply for the job and cannot resign from
it. Use the two-factor distinction as a diagnostic - *is this a satisfaction problem
or a dissatisfaction problem?* - not as a timetable.

### Self-Determination Theory: autonomy, competence, relatedness

Self-determination theory, proposed by Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, locates the
wants that matter in three needs: **autonomy** (being the origin of one's actions),
**competence** (experiencing mastery), and **relatedness** (being connected to
others) (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). Where Maslow stacks needs in order, SDT says these
three feed intrinsic motivation together - and a classroom can supply them or starve
them one by one.

The Class 9 project worked past the bell because it fed all three: pupils chose their
topics (autonomy), could actually do the work (competence), and did it in teams
(relatedness). The prize chart starved all three: it took the choosing away, measured
only the topper's competence, and set pupil against pupil.

SDT's classroom translations are concrete: give choice inside a frame (which of these
three questions); make mastery visible (progress the pupil can see, not only ranks);
and build connection deliberately (the greeting at the door, the group that stays a
group). None costs money. All three cost the teacher's attention, which is the
currency this whole unit trades in.

## Activity: Three theories, one school

**In small groups, about 25 minutes.**

A school you all know has: a broken water cooler; a Class 7 that will not try; a star
chart that worked for two weeks and stopped; and one teacher whose class always works.

1. Read the school through **Maslow**: which deficit needs are unmet, for whom?
2. Read it through **Herzberg**: which problems are hygiene, which are motivators?
3. Read it through **self-determination**: where are autonomy, competence and
   relatedness being fed or starved?

Then, together: name the **one change you would make first**, which theory justifies
it, and what that theory's limit says about what the change cannot do.

## Check your understanding

1. Define motivation in one sentence, and say why the definition moves the teacher's
   question from "why won't he?" to "what is he motivated by?" *(Understand)*
2. Explain the overjustification effect with the prize chart, in three sentences.
   *(Apply)*
3. Name Maslow's five levels in order, with the classroom instance of each from the
   figure. *(Remember)*
4. Explain the difference between motivators and hygiene factors, with one school
   example of each. *(Understand)*
5. Name SDT's three needs and say how the Class 9 project fed each. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Motivation is the wants or needs that direct behaviour toward a goal. Intrinsic
motivation runs on personal satisfaction, extrinsic on what others give, and paying
for what a pupil already owns can eat it - the overjustification effect. Maslow's
hierarchy orders the deficit needs before growth and gives the teacher a triage list.
Herzberg separates satisfaction from dissatisfaction: motivators drive the first,
hygiene's absence causes the second, and a school needs both jobs done. Self-
determination theory names the three needs that feed intrinsic motivation - autonomy,
competence, relatedness - and each has a classroom translation that costs attention
rather than money.

## Try this at your practicum school

Take one class that "will not try". Before changing anything, spend two lessons
noting: which pupils arrive hungry or tired; who is isolated; whose work is never
noticed; and where any real choice exists in the lesson. You are reading the class
through this topic's theories. Bring the notes to the next session.

## Summative task

Write **250-300 words** on the prize chart's two months.

The chart raised marks for two weeks and then stopped, and the story-writer stopped
too. Using this topic's theories, analyse what happened - what the chart supplied,
what it starved, what it re-told the writing was for - and design what you would do
in January instead, naming the theory behind each element.

| Criterion | What a strong answer shows |
