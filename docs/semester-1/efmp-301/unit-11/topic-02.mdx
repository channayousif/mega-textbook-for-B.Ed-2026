# Topic 11.2 - The school's and the teacher's role in well-being

## A real classroom situation

A Class 8 tutor in Hyderabad keeps a private map of her form: which pupils went
quiet after the floods, which one's marks fell when his father's shop closed, which
one has stopped eating at break. She does nothing dramatic. She greets each pupil by
name at the door, keeps the morning routine unbroken, and asks one quiet question a
week - "how is the week going?" - of the two pupils she is watching.

In March, one of them tells her something she cannot carry alone. She writes down
what was said, word for word, and walks it to the head teacher the same hour. That
sentence - written, dated, handed over - is the most professional act of her year,
and it took four minutes.

## Explanation

### The school environment and well-being: demands and supports

Topic 11.1 drew the balance; this topic walks it through the day. Every station of
a school day is simultaneously a demand and a support, and **the school designs
which it becomes**.

**Assembly** demands public standing and delivers public comparison; it can also be
the day's predictable start - the routine that Unit 8 built, which is a well-being
support wearing a timetable. **Lessons** demand new material at pace; they can also
be the place where steps are made external (Unit 4) and wrong answers are safe
(Unit 8's norm) - the two practices that most reduce classroom fear. **Break** is
the social test; the peer group that includes (Unit 2's seating map, Unit 7's
inclusion) is the support that meets it. **The last period** meets spent attention
(Unit 4's depletion); the support is the one-demand rule. **Home time** carries the
home load out of the school's sight; the support is the goodbye that says tomorrow
is expected.

Reading the day this way converts the vague duty "we must support well-being" into
an auditable list: which stations currently work as demands without supports, and
what would flip them. The audit costs one walk of the timetable and repays it in
the same coin as Unit 8's room audit.

### The teacher's role and its limits: notice, respond, refer

The teacher's role in well-being has exactly three parts, and the third is where
the profession lives.

**Notice.** The signs of Topic 11.1, read as patterns, kept in a private map. The
map is not surveillance; it is attention with a memory - the difference between
"he seems off today" and "this is the third Monday running".

**Respond within the classroom.** The moves are the course's own, now seen as
well-being machinery: greet by name (the relationship that exists before it is
needed, Unit 8); keep the routine unbroken (the predictability that fear cannot
survive without); reduce the demand you control (the extension, the smaller list);
name feelings calmly ("nerves mean it matters", Unit 6); and keep the pupil
included by role (Unit 7's deliberate inclusion). None of these is therapy. All of
them are protective, and every one is inside the teaching role.

**Refer beyond it.** The line is exact: the teacher escalates to the head teacher
or the designated person - with the words written down, dated, and handed over the
same day - when the signs exceed the classroom's reach: any talk of self-harm; a
sudden calm in visible distress; a pattern that deepens despite the classroom
supports; or anything the teacher cannot keep safe. The referral is not a failure
of care; it IS the care, delivered by the right hands. And the boundary protects
the pupil twice over: a teacher who counsels beyond training can do harm no
kindness repairs, and a pupil who confides in a teacher who then keeps the
confidence is left carrying it alone.

<Figure id="fig-U11-4" src="/img/figures/efmp-301/unit-11/fig-U11-4.svg" alt="A table of the teacher's five well-being moves, each against what it supports and its limit, with the referral's limit named as the profession, not a failure." />

> **The misconception to clear now.** "Referring is giving up on the pupil." The
> reverse is true: the teacher who keeps a confidence that should have been
> escalated has chosen their own comfort over the pupil's safety, and the pupil
> pays. The tutor's four-minute walk to the head teacher - the sentence written,
> dated, handed over - is not the end of her care for that pupil; it is the
> moment her care became effective. What ends at the handover is the teacher's
> RESPONSIBILITY for treatment, never the relationship: the greeting, the routine
> and the quiet question continue, and they matter more after a referral than
> before it.

## Activity: The walk of the timetable

**In small groups, about 20 minutes.**

Take one school you all know and walk its day, station by station: assembly,
lessons, break, the last period, home time. For each station, write the demand as
the pupils experience it and the support as it currently stands - including
"none", where that is the truth.

Then, together: choose the **one station whose flip would protect the most
pupils**, and design the flip - specific, cheap, and inside one teacher's or one
school's power this term.

## Check your understanding

1. Explain how one station of the day can be both a demand and a support, with an
   example. *(Understand)*
2. Name the teacher's role in three parts. *(Remember)*
3. Name any four classroom responses and the unit each came from. *(Remember)*
4. When does the classroom's reach end? Name any three triggers for the same-day
   referral. *(Understand)*
5. Why does the boundary protect the pupil twice over? *(Analyze)*

## Summary

Every station of the school day is both a demand and a support, and the school
designs which: the audit is one walk of the timetable. The teacher's role in
well-being has three parts - notice (the signs read as patterns, kept in a private
map), respond within the classroom (the course's own moves, now seen as well-being
machinery), and refer beyond it (the words written, dated, handed over the same
day, when the signs exceed the classroom's reach). The boundary protects the pupil
twice: from a teacher counselling beyond training, and from a confidence that
leaves them carrying it alone. What ends at the handover is the responsibility for
treatment, never the relationship.

## Try this at your practicum school

Start the private map: one page, one form, the pupils you are quietly watching and
the one thing you noticed about each this week. Keep it private, keep it factual,
and keep the quiet question going. The map is the notice part of the role, and it
is entirely yours.

## Summative task

Write **250-300 words** on the four-minute walk.

The tutor's pupil disclosed something she could not carry alone. Write what she
did next as a plan: what she wrote down and why the words matter, who she walked
it to, what she said to the pupil before she went, and what she did NOT do. Close
with what continues after the handover - and why that continuation is the
profession's point.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong answer shows |
|---|---|
| The record is understood | Why verbatim, dated, written - not a summary from memory. |
| The pupil is prepared, not ambushed | What was said before the walk, and why it was said. *(Analyze)* |
| The not-do list is exact | No diagnosing, no promising secrecy, no carrying it alone. |
| Plain, accurate writing | Terms used correctly; no padding; within the word count. |
