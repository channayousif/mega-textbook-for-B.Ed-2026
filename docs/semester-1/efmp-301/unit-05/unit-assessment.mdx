# Intelligence and Creativity - assessment and review

## Unit summary

Theories of intelligence arrange along an axis: Spearman's one general factor,
measurable and comparable, at one end; Gardner's at-least-eight intelligences at the
other; most working psychologists between. Gardner's theory carries a real classroom
appeal - abilities are wider than the two schools examine - and a real evidence
caution: the eight have not demonstrated independence in measurement. The
learning-styles claim, often confused with it, is weaker still; what survives is
pupil variety and multimodal teaching.

The tests are standardised - mean 100, deviation 15, the 85-115 average band - and
their score summarizes performance on the sampled tasks, predicts school work
moderately and little else, and inherits a history that argues for humility.

Creativity is the ability to generate the new, and it is observable: fluency,
flexibility, originality and elaboration, powered by divergent thinking and
disciplined by convergent. Classrooms grow it with open questions, generation before
evaluation, safe wrong answers and real audiences. Giftedness is an unusual level of
skill or knowledge - an educational need, not a prize: gifted pupils come from every
group and languish, into boredom and misbehaviour, without accommodation.

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. Spearman's g is the claim that intelligence is: *(Remember)*
   a) eight relatively independent abilities
   b) one general factor, measurable and comparable among individuals
   c) whatever the school examines
   d) a fixed quantity set at birth

2. Gardner's theory names at least: *(Remember)*
   a) two intelligences
   b) four intelligences
   c) eight intelligences
   d) one general intelligence with strands

3. The evidence caution about multiple intelligences is that: *(Understand)*
   a) teachers reject it
   b) among cognitive psychologists it has been criticised for lacking empirical
      evidence
   c) it has been proven in every classroom study
   d) Gardner withdrew it

4. On most modern IQ tests, the mean score and one standard deviation are: *(Remember)*
   a) 100 and 10
   b) 100 and 15
   c) 85 and 115
   d) 50 and 25

5. An IQ-style score predicts best: *(Understand)*
   a) lifelong income
   b) happiness
   c) school performance
   d) practical problem-solving in real settings

6. "She is a visual learner, so I teach her visually." The evidence supports: *(Apply)*
   a) matching teaching to a diagnosed style
   b) presenting new ideas in more than one mode, said, shown and done
   c) grouping pupils by style
   d) none of it; one mode for all is fine

7. Creativity, as defined in this unit, is: *(Remember)*
   a) a mysterious gift possessed by few
   b) the ability to generate, create or discover new ideas, solutions and
      possibilities
   c) artistic talent only
   d) the absence of convergent thinking

8. The pupil who lists seventeen uses for a brick, of many different kinds, is
   showing: *(Apply)*
   a) fluency and flexibility
   b) convergent thinking
   c) elaboration only
   d) giftedness

9. Gifted pupils, on the research summary in this unit: *(Understand)*
   a) are usually socially awkward and narrow in their interests
   b) come from all economic and cultural groups and are not necessarily awkward
   c) manage fine without accommodation
   d) are best given more of the same work, faster

10. The pupil who finishes early and disturbs others is best first read as: *(Apply)*
    a) a discipline case
    b) an unmet educational need: boredom from lack of accommodation
    c) a pupil testing the teacher
    d) a pupil with an attention problem

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

1. State Spearman's claim and Gardner's, in one sentence each, and name what each
   predicts about Salman and Kiran. *(Understand)*
2. State the multiple-intelligences evidence caution fairly, in two sentences,
   keeping its classroom appeal. *(Understand)*
3. Explain what an IQ-style score is (mean, deviation, average band) and what it does
   not show, in three lines. *(Understand)*
4. Explain creativity's four observable parts with a one-line example of each.
   *(Understand)*
5. Explain divergent and convergent thinking with one classroom example of each.
   *(Understand)*
6. Name two classroom conditions that grow creativity and say which part each grows.
   *(Apply)*
7. State the two facts about gifted pupils that carry the classroom weight.
   *(Understand)*
8. The essay reusing memorised phrases earns the top mark. Explain what the way marks
   are given lacks, using this topic's terms. *(Apply)*
9. The early finisher is disturbing the class. Give the unmet-need reading and one
   response that follows from it. *(Apply)*
10. In three sentences, separate the learning-styles claim from what the evidence
    supports. *(Analyze)*

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. **The staff room's two words.** Salman is "intelligent" and Kiran "weak in
   studies". Analyse what each label implicitly assumes, what evidence fits and does
   not, and what each leads the school to do and stop doing. Close with the label you
   would use instead, defended. *(Analyze)*

2. **The fifth box.** The way marks are given has no box for originality. Argue for adding
   one: what it would measure (using creativity's parts), what it would change in the
   class's writing, and what it risks. Defend the weight you would give it. *(Analyze)*

3. **The talent show.** A Class 5 talent show displays abilities the examinations
   never sample. Analyse what the show demonstrates - pupil difference, or the theory
   of multiple intelligences? - using the evidence caution, and say what the school
   should change if your reading is right. *(Analyze)*

4. **The early finisher.** Design a fortnight's provision for the pupil who finishes
   twenty minutes early: the tasks, the structure, the check that the provision is
   working. Justify each element from this unit, and name the one thing you would not
   do and why. *(Create)*

5. **One task, both needs.** Design one task that serves both the early finisher and
   the pupil whose essay reuses memorised phrases. Name what each needs in this
   unit's terms, how the task meets both at once, how it is marked, and what it
   cannot do. *(Create)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### MCQ answer key

1. **b** - one general factor, g, measurable and comparable.
2. **c** - at least eight, from linguistic to naturalistic.
3. **b** - the criticism is empirical: the eight do not show as separable factors.
4. **b** - mean 100, one standard deviation 15; the 85-115 band is average.
5. **c** - school performance is what the tests were built to predict.
6. **b** - multimodal presentation is the supported residue of the styles claim.
7. **b** - the definition makes creativity observable, not mysterious.
8. **a** - many ideas (fluency) of many kinds (flexibility).
9. **b** - the stereotype is the defect; gifted pupils come from every group.
10. **b** - boredom from lack of accommodation, on the research summary.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

Award marks for the substance below, not for matching wording.

1. Spearman: one general factor, measurable (1); Gardner: at least eight
   intelligences (1); any accurate application to Salman and Kiran (1).
2. Among cognitive psychologists the theory is criticised for lacking empirical
   evidence (1); yet it names abilities schools ignore, which is its classroom appeal
   (1).
3. Mean 100, one standard deviation 15, 85-115 the average band (1); the score
   summarises performance on the sampled tasks (1); it is one source of evidence,
   never a verdict (1).
4. Fluency, flexibility, originality, elaboration (1 for all four); any apt one-line
   examples (1).
5. Divergent: many possible answers (any apt example); convergent: the one
   well-established answer (any apt example) (2).
6. Any two of open questions, generation before evaluation, safe wrong answers, real
   audiences (1 for two); correctly matched to the part each grows (1).
7. Gifted pupils come from all groups and are not necessarily awkward or narrow (1);
   without accommodation they become bored, and the boredom can become behaviour
   problems (1).
8. The scheme lacks a box for originality - what could not have been written by
   anyone (1); it rewards convergent correctness only, training the divergent half
   away (1).
9. The unmet need: unusual skill without accommodation, boredom as its symptom (1);
   any response that follows: real problems, choice, high expectations - enrichment
   rather than more worksheets (1).
10. The styles claim (matching teaching to a diagnosed style) has weak evidence (1);
    what survives is pupil variety and multimodal teaching (1); the two claims are
    different, whatever the staff-room shorthand says (1).
