# Intelligence and Creativity - assessment and review

## Unit summary

Theories of intelligence arrange along an axis: Spearman's one general factor,
measurable and comparable, at one end; Gardner's at-least-eight intelligences at the
other; most working psychologists between. Gardner's theory carries a real classroom
appeal - abilities are wider than the two schools examine - and a real evidence
caution: cognitive psychologists criticize it for lacking empirical evidence. The
learning-styles claim, often confused with it, lacks empirical support for matched
instruction; while rejecting it does not prove multimodal presentation is superior,
varying classroom tasks prevents reliance on any single mode.

The tests are standardised - mean 100, deviation 15, the 85-115 average band - and
their score summarizes performance on the sampled tasks without capturing every
ability, and inherits a history that argues for humility.

Creativity is the ability to generate the new, and it is observable: fluency,
flexibility, originality and elaboration, powered by divergent thinking and
disciplined by convergent. Classrooms grow it with open questions, generation before
evaluation, safe wrong answers and real audiences. Giftedness is an unusual level of
skill or knowledge - an educational need, not a prize: gifted pupils come from every
group and languish, into boredom and misbehaviour, without accommodation, where
early finishing serves as a working hypothesis of unmet challenge rather than a simple label.

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. Spearman's g is the claim that intelligence is: *(Remember)*
   a) eight relatively independent abilities
   b) one general factor, measurable and comparable among individuals
   c) whatever the school examines
   d) a fixed quantity set at birth

2. Gardner's theory names at least: *(Remember)*
   a) two intelligences
   b) four intelligences
   c) eight intelligences
   d) one general intelligence with strands

3. The evidence caution about multiple intelligences is that: *(Understand)*
   a) teachers reject it
   b) among cognitive psychologists it has been criticised for lacking empirical
      evidence
   c) it has been proven in every classroom study
   d) Gardner withdrew it

4. Elaboration, one of creativity's four observable parts, is: *(Understand)*
   a) how many ideas the pupil lists
   b) how many kinds of idea the pupil lists
   c) how far a single idea is worked out
   d) how new the ideas are in this room

5. An IQ-style score primarily reflects: *(Understand)*
   a) lifelong income
   b) personal happiness
   c) performance on the specific cognitive tasks sampled by the test
   d) practical mechanical skill in everyday settings

6. "She is a visual learner, so I teach her visually." Educational research indicates that: *(Apply)*
   a) matching instruction to a diagnosed learning style lacks empirical support
   b) visual learners cannot comprehend verbal explanations
   c) grouping pupils by sensory preference improves learning outcomes
   d) learning styles are identical to Gardner's multiple intelligences

7. Creativity, as defined in this unit, is: *(Remember)*
   a) a mysterious gift possessed by few
   b) the ability to generate, create or discover new ideas, solutions and
      possibilities
   c) artistic talent only
   d) the absence of convergent thinking

8. The pupil who lists seventeen uses for a brick, of many different kinds, is
   showing: *(Apply)*
   a) fluency and flexibility
   b) convergent thinking
   c) elaboration only
   d) giftedness

9. Gifted pupils, on the research summary in this unit: *(Understand)*
   a) are usually socially awkward and narrow in their interests
   b) come from all economic and cultural groups and are not necessarily awkward
   c) manage fine without accommodation
   d) are best given more of the same work, faster

10. A pupil finishes class exercises twenty minutes early and disturbs neighbours.
    An evidence-informed teacher should first treat this observation as: *(Apply)*
    a) proof that the pupil must be retained in the same grade
    b) a working hypothesis of unmet educational need and boredom from lack of
       challenge to investigate
    c) definitive confirmation of exceptional general intelligence
    d) a disciplinary infraction requiring punitive detention

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

1. State Spearman's claim and Gardner's, in one sentence each, and name what each
   predicts about Salman and Kiran. *(Understand)*
2. State the multiple-intelligences evidence caution fairly, in two sentences,
   keeping its classroom appeal. *(Understand)*
3. Explain what an IQ-style score is (mean, deviation, average band) and what it does
   not show, in three lines. *(Understand)*
4. Explain creativity's four observable parts with a one-line example of each.
   *(Understand)*
5. Explain divergent and convergent thinking with one classroom example of each.
   *(Understand)*
6. The history of IQ testing includes the eugenics movement and the Buck v. Bell case.
   What did that history lead the profession to restrict, and what does it ask of a
   teacher who reads a score? *(Understand)*
7. State the two facts about gifted pupils that carry the classroom weight.
   *(Understand)*
8. The essay reusing memorised phrases earns the top mark. Explain what the way marks
   are given lacks, using this topic's terms. *(Apply)*
9. A pupil consistently finishes assignments early and disrupts neighbours. Frame
   this behaviour as a hypothesis to check, and give one instructional response that
   follows. *(Apply)*
10. In three sentences, separate the learning-styles claim from what the evidence
    actually supports regarding instruction. *(Analyze)*

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

Respond to each prompt in **250-300 words**. Each question is marked out of 20 using the analytic rubrics below.

1. **The staff room's two words.** Salman is "intelligent" and Kiran "weak in
   studies". Write **250-300 words** analysing what each label implicitly assumes,
   what evidence fits and does not, and what each leads the school to do and stop
   doing. Close with the label you would use instead, defended. *(Analyze)*

2. **The fifth box.** The way marks are given has no box for originality. Write
   **250-300 words** arguing for adding one: what it would measure (using
   creativity's parts), what it would change in the class's writing, and what it
   risks. Defend the weight you would give it. *(Analyze)*

3. **The talent show.** A Class 5 talent show displays abilities the examinations
   never sample. Write **250-300 words** analysing what the show demonstrates - pupil
   difference, or the theory of multiple intelligences? - using the evidence caution,
   and say what the school should change if your reading is right. *(Analyze)*

4. **The early finisher.** Write **250-300 words** designing a fortnight's
   provision for the pupil who finishes twenty minutes early: the tasks, the
   structure, the check that the provision is working. Justify each element from
   this unit, and name the one thing you would not do and why. *(Create)*

5. **The integrative pupil profile.** A Class 6 teacher describes Zainab: her
   standardized test score sits in the average band (96), she struggles with
   speeded arithmetic worksheets, yet during science projects she invents novel
   apparatus from scrap materials, and in group discussions she synthesizes divergent
   peer viewpoints with remarkable fluency. Write **250-300 words** reading Zainab's
   profile through the unit's intelligence theories (Spearman, Gardner, CHC),
   explaining what her test score does and does not measure, and designing a
   justified instructional plan that fosters her creativity while addressing her
   academic needs. *(Create)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### MCQ answer key

1. **b** - one general factor, g, measurable and comparable.
2. **c** - at least eight, from linguistic to naturalistic.
3. **b** - cognitive psychologists criticize Gardner's theory for lacking empirical
   evidence (Spielman, Jenkins & Lovett, 2020).
4. **c** - elaboration is the working-out: the village built around the absent bus,
   not the count of ideas.
5. **c** - an IQ score reflects performance on the tasks sampled by the test, with
   open questions about measuring broader intelligence (Spielman, Jenkins & Lovett, 2020).
6. **a** - research reviews find no empirical support for style-matched instruction
   (Rohrer & Pashler, 2012).
7. **b** - the definition makes creativity observable, not mysterious.
8. **a** - many ideas (fluency) of many kinds (flexibility).
9. **b** - the stereotype is the defect; gifted pupils come from every group.
10. **b** - early completion suggests a hypothesis of under-challenge and boredom
    requiring investigation and adapted tasks (Seifert & Sutton, 2009).

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

Award marks for the substance below, not for matching wording.

1. Spearman: one general factor, measurable (1); Gardner: at least eight
   intelligences (1); any accurate application to Salman and Kiran (1).
2. Among cognitive psychologists the theory is criticised for lacking empirical
   evidence (1); yet it names abilities schools ignore, which is its classroom appeal
   (1).
3. Mean 100, one standard deviation 15, 85-115 the average band (1); the score
   summarises performance on the sampled tasks (1); it is one source of evidence,
   never a verdict (1).
4. Fluency, flexibility, originality, elaboration (1 for all four); any apt one-line
   examples (1).
5. Divergent: many possible answers (any apt example); convergent: the one
   well-established answer (any apt example) (2).
6. Only professionals trained in psychology may administer IQ tests, and purchasing
   most requires an advanced degree in psychology (1); a teacher who knows the history
   treats every score as one source of evidence, never a verdict (1).
7. Gifted pupils come from all groups and are not necessarily awkward or narrow (1);
   without accommodation they become bored, and the boredom can become behaviour
   problems (1).
8. The scheme lacks a box for originality - what could not have been written by
   anyone (1); it rewards convergent correctness only, training the divergent half
   away (1).
9. Treat early finishing as a hypothesis of unmet challenge or boredom to investigate
   rather than a conclusive label (1); provide differentiated challenge or enrichment
   tasks (e.g. real problem-solving or choice) rather than repetitive worksheets (1).
10. Style-matched instruction (tailoring teaching to a diagnosed style) lacks
    empirical support in research reviews (1); rejecting style-matching does not
    prove multimodal teaching superiority, though varying presentation remains common
    classroom practice (1); learning styles and multiple intelligences are separate
    claims that should not be conflated (1).
