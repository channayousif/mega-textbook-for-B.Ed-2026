# Topic 5.2 - Creativity and giftedness

## A real classroom situation

Two Urdu essays arrive on the same topic, "my village". The first reuses the phrases
every essay on this topic has reused for a decade: the green fields, the simple
people, the fresh air. It is correct, complete, and could have been written by anyone.
The second begins with the bus that no longer stops at the village, and builds the
whole village around what that absence changed.

The first essay earns more marks under the usual rules - no errors, all parts
present. The second is the creative one, and the way marks are given has no box for
what it did. Meanwhile, at the back of the same class, a pupil finished the work twenty
minutes ago and is now disturbing his neighbours, and has been doing exactly this
since March.

## Explanation

### Creativity: what it is and how classrooms grow it

"Creativity is the ability to generate, create, or discover new ideas, solutions, and
possibilities" (Spielman, Jenkins & Lovett, 2020). The definition's value for a
teacher is that it makes creativity **observable** - not a mysterious gift but a set
of moves that can be counted, named and practised.

Four parts are usually distinguished. **Fluency**: how many ideas - the pupil who
lists seventeen uses for a brick. **Flexibility**: how many kinds of idea - the same
pupil's list includes building, a paperweight, a story prop and a boundary marker.
**Originality**: how new the ideas are in this room - the bus that no longer stops.
**Elaboration**: how far an idea is worked out - the village built around the absent
bus.

Behind the parts sit the two thinking modes of Unit 4, now with names.
**Divergent thinking** "allows an individual to arrive at unique, multiple solutions
to a given problem"; **convergent thinking** "describes the ability to provide a
correct or well-established answer or solution" (Spielman, Jenkins & Lovett, 2020).
Creativity needs both: the divergent flood, then the convergent choice. A school that
examines only convergent thinking - one right answer, found fast - trains half the
pair and calls the other half misbehaviour.

The classroom conditions that grow the observable parts are ordinary and cheap:
questions with more than one defensible answer; time to generate before anyone
evaluates (the flood before the choice); wrong answers safe in public, because
originality is a species of risk-taking; and real audiences - a poem read to the
class, a poster that actually hangs - because elaboration is effort, and effort needs
a reason.

> **The misconception to clear now.** "Creativity is only for art lessons." The
> essay's absent bus is creative writing, but so is the mathematics pupil's
> unrequested second method, the science pupil who tests the control twice, and
> Kiran's wire-diagnosis from Topic 5.1. Creativity is a property of thinking, not of
> subjects, and every subject's way of giving marks either has a box for it or trains it
> out.

### Giftedness: recognizing and teaching gifted learners

The guide pairs creativity with giftedness, and the pairing is deliberate: both are
abilities the standard examination undersamples.

**Giftedness** means an unusual level of skill or knowledge in some domain. The
traditional reading was narrow - unusually high verbal and test performance - and the
modern reading is wider, "unusual talents in a range of activities", which is why
educators often say "gifted and talented" (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). The qualities
observed are consistent: such pupils "learn more quickly and independently than most
students their own age", often show advanced language, are strongly motivated by
challenge, and hold themselves to high standards (Seifert & Sutton, 2009).

Two facts carry the classroom weight. First, gifted pupils "are not necessarily
awkward socially, less healthy, or narrow in their interests - in fact, quite the
contrary", and they come from all economic and cultural groups (Seifert & Sutton,
2009). The stereotype of the gifted misfit is a story, and a harmful one: it tells a
school to look for giftedness only in pupils who look a particular way.

Second, and harder: gifted pupils **languish without accommodation**. "Without
accommodation to their unusual level of skill or knowledge, students who are gifted
or talented can become bored by school, and eventually the boredom can even turn into
behavior problems" (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). The pupil who finished twenty minutes
ago is not a discipline case; he is an unmet educational need wearing a discipline
case's clothes. The support that fits is a mixture of challenge and structure:
real problems rather than more worksheets, some say in what is studied, and
expectations kept high - enrichment rather than mere acceleration, which only moves
the boredom forward a year.

## Activity: Marking for originality

**In pairs, about 20 minutes.**

Take the two "my village" essays as described (write each in five lines if you have
not read them).

1. Mark both under the usual scheme: content, accuracy, organisation, presentation.
2. Now add a fifth box: originality - "what in this essay could not have been
   written by anyone?" Mark both again.
3. Compare the two orderings. Decide together what the fifth box should be worth,
   and defend the weight you give it.

Then: design the ten-minute task you would set the whole class tomorrow that has
more than one defensible answer - the task whose marking needs the fifth box.

## Check your understanding

1. Define creativity in one sentence, and name its four observable parts. *(Remember)*
2. Explain divergent and convergent thinking with one classroom example of each.
   *(Understand)*
3. Name three classroom conditions that grow creativity, and say which part each
   grows. *(Apply)*
4. State the two facts about gifted pupils that carry the classroom weight, in two
   sentences. *(Understand)*
5. The early finisher is disturbing the class. Give the unmet-need reading and one
   response that follows from it. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Creativity is the ability to generate the new, and it is observable: fluency,
flexibility, originality, elaboration, powered by divergent thinking and disciplined
by convergent. Classrooms grow it with open questions, generation before evaluation,
safe wrong answers and real audiences - and every way of giving marks either has a box for
originality or trains it out. Giftedness is an unusual level of skill or knowledge, an
educational need rather than a prize: gifted pupils come from every group, mostly
thrive socially, and languish - into boredom and misbehaviour - without accommodation.
The support is enrichment and real challenge, not more of the same worksheet.

## Try this at your practicum school

Add the fifth box to one piece of marking this week: "what here could not have been
written by anyone?" Do not change the total marks. Note what the box reveals, and
what the class does when it discovers the box exists.

## Summative task

Write **250-300 words** designing for the two pupils.

Design one task - any subject, any class - that serves both the pupil who finished
twenty minutes ago and the pupil whose essay reuses memorised phrases. Name what each
pupil needs from the task (using this topic's terms), how the task meets both needs
at once, and how it would be marked. Concede the one thing the task cannot do.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong answer shows |
|---|---|
| Needs named precisely | Each pupil's need is stated in this topic's terms, not as "bored" and "weak". |
| One task, both needs | The design genuinely serves both, and the mechanism is explained. *(Create)* |
| Marking thought through | The way marks are given includes the fifth box or its equivalent. |
| Honest concession | The task's limit is named, not hidden. |
