# Topic 5.1 - Intelligence: theories and measurement

## A real classroom situation

Two pupils sit in the same Class 6 in a Hyderabad school. Salman solves the
mathematics textbook's hardest problems before the teacher finishes writing them, and
cannot hold a conversation with the group beside him. Kiran struggles with every
written test she has ever sat, and when the school's fan broke last June, it was Kiran
who diagnosed the loose wire while three teachers watched.

The staff room calls Salman intelligent and Kiran "weak in studies". Both words are
doing the work of a theory. Which theory - and what it entitles a school to conclude -
is this topic's business.

## Explanation

### Theories of intelligence: one ability or many

What is intelligence? The answers arrange themselves along one axis (Spielman,
Jenkins & Lovett, 2020).

At one end, **one general ability**. Charles Spearman, working from the observation
that pupils who do well on one kind of test tend to do well on others, "believed
intelligence consisted of one general factor, called g, which could be measured and
compared among individuals" (Spielman, Jenkins & Lovett, 2020). On this view Salman's
mathematics marks predict his conversation skills, and Kiran's test marks predict her
hands - and the staff room's single word for both pupils is, strictly, Spearman's
word.

At the other end, **many abilities**. Howard Gardner argued that "each person
possesses at least eight intelligences" - linguistic, logical-mathematical, musical,
bodily kinesthetic, spatial, interpersonal, intrapersonal, and naturalistic (Spielman,
Jenkins & Lovett, 2020). On this view Salman's logical-mathematical strength and
Kiran's practical-spatial diagnosis are different intelligences, and a single score
that ranks them against each other is measuring one slice of each and calling it the
whole.

Between them stand the middle positions - theories that keep a general factor while
admitting distinct strands, a few primary abilities among them. Most working psychologists stand somewhere here: there is something general,
and there are strands.

For a teacher, the axis is not a quiz to pass but a position to notice. **Where you
stand decides what your words mean.** A staff room on Spearman's end has one ladder,
with every pupil on a rung; a staff room at Gardner's end has a profile, with every
pupil strong somewhere. Neither end is free: the ladder misses Kiran's wire, and the
profile, unchecked, can flatter every pupil into a strength no one can name.

> **The misconception to clear now.** "The multiple-intelligences theory is proven
> science, and anyone who doubts it doubts the pupils." The honest position is more
> interesting. Among cognitive psychologists, Gardner's theory "has been heavily
> criticized for lacking empirical evidence" (Spielman, Jenkins & Lovett, 2020) - the
> eight intelligences have not demonstrated their independence in measurement. And yet
> educators use it, because it names abilities schools have historically ignored.
> Both halves are true at once, and a teacher who can hold both is better armed than
> one who has picked a side.

### Multiple intelligences: the claim and its evidence

The claim, stated fairly: human abilities are wider than the linguistic and
logical-mathematical skills schools examine, and a pupil weak on those two may be
strong elsewhere. The classroom appeal is real, and Vosniadou (2001) makes the
adjacent point carefully: Gardner has argued there are "many dimensions of human
intelligence other than the logical and linguistic skills that are usually valued in
most school environments... Schools must create the best environment for the
development of children taking into consideration such individual differences."

The evidence caution, stated just as fairly: the eight intelligences have not
demonstrated independence in measurement. What has survived scrutiny is narrower and
still useful - abilities are multiple enough that a single score is a poor summary of
a pupil, and the school-valued two are not the only two that matter.

A third claim circulates beside these and must be separated from both: the
**learning-styles** claim that pupils learn best when taught in their preferred style
(visual, auditory, kinaesthetic). The evidence for matching teaching to a diagnosed
style is weak - "a thoughtful review of the data provides no support for style-based
instruction" (Rohrer & Pashler, 2012) - and the theory is not the same as multiple
intelligences, whatever the staff-room shorthand says. What survives is humbler and free: pupils differ, and a
teacher who presents a new idea in more than one mode - said, shown, done - reaches
more of them than one who only says it.

### Measurement of intelligence: what tests measure, and what they miss

The measurement story, briefly. "IQ stands for intelligence quotient and describes a
score earned on a test designed to measure intelligence" (Spielman, Jenkins & Lovett,
2020). The tests are standardised: on most, "the average (or mean) IQ score is 100",
and "one standard deviation is 15 points", with scores from 85 to 115 counting as the
average band - about two-thirds of the population.

Three honest limits belong beside those facts.

**A score summarises test performance, not a pupil.** It samples the abilities the
test samples, under the conditions the test sets. Kiran's wire-diagnosis is not in
the sample.

**The tests were built by people, and history shows it.** IQ tests "have sometimes
been used as arguments in support of insidious purposes, such as the eugenics
movement", and the Supreme Court case Buck v. Bell legalized the forced sterilization
of people deemed "feeble-minded" through this type of testing (Spielman, Jenkins &
Lovett, 2020). A teacher who knows this history treats every score as one source of
evidence, never a verdict.

**The measurement question is itself open.** Researchers still debate "the degree to
which any test can truly measure an individual's intelligence" (Spielman, Jenkins &
Lovett, 2020). A score predicts school performance - that is what it
was built for - and "the degree to which any test can truly measure an individual's
intelligence" remains an open question (Spielman, Jenkins & Lovett, 2020).

## Activity: The talent show

**In small groups, about 20 minutes.**

A Class 5 talent show runs for one hour: singing, cricket skills, story-telling,
mimicry, a pupil who can name every district of Sindh and its crop, a pupil who
repairs the head teacher's torch.

1. For each act, name the ability it displays - using Gardner's names where they fit
   and plain words where they do not.
2. Now the hard part: which of these abilities does the school's examination system
   sample? What follows from your answer?
3. Finally: has the talent show demonstrated the theory of multiple intelligences,
   or has it demonstrated that pupils differ? Explain the difference, using this
   topic's evidence caution.

## Check your understanding

1. State Spearman's claim in one sentence, and say what it predicts about Salman and
   Kiran. *(Understand)*
2. Name any five of Gardner's eight intelligences. *(Remember)*
3. State the evidence caution about multiple intelligences fairly, in two sentences,
   without dismissing its classroom appeal. *(Understand)*
4. What does an IQ-style score predict moderately well, and what does it not
   predict? *(Understand)*
5. A colleague says "she is a visual learner, so I teach her visually." Separate the
   learning-styles claim from what the evidence supports, in three sentences. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Theories of intelligence arrange along an axis from one general factor (Spearman's g)
to many relatively independent abilities (Gardner's eight), with most working
psychologists between. Gardner's theory carries a real classroom appeal and a real
evidence caution, and holding both is the analytic skill. The tests measure what they
sample - mean 100, deviation 15, the 85-115 average band - predict school performance
moderately and little else, and carry a history that argues for humility. What
survives every caution is this: pupils differ in more ways than examinations sample,
and a single score is a poor summary of a person.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask the cooperating teacher to name the three pupils the examinations rate highest,
and the three the teacher would most want beside them if the school bus broke down on
a hot road. Compare the two lists, and note what the comparison shows about what the
tests sample.

## Summative task

Write **250-300 words** on the staff room's two words.

The staff room calls Salman "intelligent" and Kiran "weak in studies". Using this
topic, analyse what each label assumes (which theory, implicitly), what evidence in
the description fits and does not fit, and what each label leads the school to do and
stop doing. Close with the label you would use instead, and defend it.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong answer shows |
|---|---|
| Theory identification | Each label is read for its implicit theory, not just contradicted. |
| Evidence discipline | The fit and misfit of each label uses the described facts only. *(Analyze)* |
| Consequence analysis | What each label leads the school to do and stop doing is named. |
| Plain, accurate writing | Terms used correctly; no padding; within the word count. |
