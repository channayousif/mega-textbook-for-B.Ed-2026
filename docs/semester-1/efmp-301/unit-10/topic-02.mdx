# Topic 10.2 - Instructional design and teacher effectiveness

## A real classroom situation

Two trainees plan the same lesson on fractions. The first writes the activity list:
warm-up, explain, worksheet, homework. It fills the forty minutes neatly. The
second writes one sentence first - "by the end, every pupil can add unlike
fractions using a diagram" - then asks what evidence would show it, then chooses
the activities that produce that evidence. Her list looks almost the same: warm-up,
explain, worksheet. But the worksheet has different questions on it, chosen because
they would show the sentence, and she knows before she starts what she will accept
as proof.

The difference is invisible in the plan book and decisive in the room. "If you
don't know where you're going, you could end up someplace else" - the baseball
coach's warning that opens Seifert and Sutton's planning chapter, and the whole of
instructional design in one sentence (Seifert & Sutton, 2009).

## Explanation

### Instructional design: planning backward from outcomes

Instructional design is the discipline of deciding, before teaching, what success
will look like - and building the lesson to produce it. Its order is backward:
**outcome first, evidence second, activity third.**

**The outcome** comes first, and it names a behaviour. In Mager's classic
formulation, a good objective "should specify a behavior that can in fact be
observed" - something a student does or says, "not something a student thinks or
feels" - with conditions of performance and a criterion for judging it (Seifert &
Sutton, 2009). "The student will understand fractions" fails the test; "the pupil
will add unlike fractions using a diagram, in five out of six sums" passes it. The
behavioural wording is not bureaucracy: it is Unit 2's stage reading and Unit 9's
validity discipline applied to planning, and it is what makes the next step
possible.

**The evidence** comes second: what would show the outcome is met? This is Unit 9
wearing a planner's hat - the assessment designed before the activity, so that it
tests the outcome rather than the activity. The first trainee's worksheet tested
whatever the worksheet happened to ask; the second's tested the sentence she had
written.

**The activity** comes third, chosen to produce the evidence: warm-up, explain,
practise, close - each step earning its minutes by its contribution to the
outcome. The design then runs its **forward check**: does this activity, as
planned, produce that evidence for that outcome? If not, the activity changes -
the outcome does not.

The benefits run through every phase: "If students know precisely what they are
supposed to learn, they can focus their attention and effort more effectively. If
the teacher knows precisely what students are supposed to learn, then the teacher
can make better use of class time and choose and design assessments of their
learning that are more fair and valid" (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). Clarity is not
the enemy of creativity; it is what creativity spends its budget on.

> **The misconception to clear now.** "A lesson plan is a script to be followed."
> A plan built backward is a promise about learning, not a timetable about
> activities - and the promise survives deviation. The teacher who departs from
> the plan because the evidence is arriving early (or not arriving) is using the
> design, not breaking it: the formative cycle (Unit 9) outranks the plan. The
> script-follower and the plan-abandoner both miss the point: the plan exists to
> be checked against, not obeyed.

### Teacher effectiveness: what it is and how it grows

What makes a teacher effective? The research answer of this whole course is
unsentimental: effectiveness is a set of **observable practices**, each of which
this course has already taught you, and none of which is charisma.

The effective teacher **plans backward** (this topic) and **runs the formative
cycle** (Unit 9). She **aims attention by design** (Unit 4) and **fits the load to
the workbench** (Unit 4 again). She **reads pupils developmentally** (Unit 2),
**gives feedback that names the next step** (Units 3 and 8), and **builds the
environment's five parts** (Unit 8). She **feeds autonomy, competence and
relatedness** (Unit 6) and **holds the ladder's dignity** (Unit 8). Every one of
those is a practice: visible in a classroom, nameable in a observation, and
improvable by deliberate work - which is what "growable" means.

The two remembered teachers exercise makes the point personally. The teacher you
remember as brilliant was doing some subset of these things - find which. The
teacher you remember as poor was failing some subset - find which. The difference
between them was not a gift; it was a repertoire. And a repertoire, unlike a gift,
can be built: by watching for the practice, naming it, trying it, and checking
what the evidence says (the formative cycle, applied to your own teaching).

## Activity: Two teachers, one inventory

**In pairs, about 20 minutes.**

1. Each partner names two teachers from their own schooling: the most effective
   and one who struggled.
2. Place each teacher against the five practices in the figure: which practices
   were present, absent or visible-but-failing?
3. Compare placements. Where you disagree, look for the evidence each of you is
   using - the placement disagreement is usually an evidence disagreement.
4. Then the hard one: name the ONE practice whose absence most damaged the
   struggling teacher's room, and the first step of building it.

## Check your understanding

1. State the backward order, and say what each step produces. *(Remember)*
2. What are Mager's features of a good objective? Give a pass and a fail example.
   *(Understand)*
3. Why does the evidence come before the activity? Answer with Unit 9's validity
   idea. *(Understand)*
4. Explain the forward check and what it can change. *(Understand)*
5. Name any four observable practices of effective teaching and the unit each came
   from. *(Remember)*

## Summary

Instructional design is planning backward: the outcome first - a behaviour that can
be observed, with conditions and a criterion, in Mager's tradition - the evidence
second, the activity third, and a forward check that changes the activity, never
the outcome. Benefits run through every phase of teaching: attention aimed, class
time spent, assessments fair and valid. Teacher effectiveness is the same
discipline made habitual: a repertoire of observable practices - planning backward,
the formative cycle, attention and load, the three needs and dignity, the
developmental reading - each nameable, watchable and growable. The plan exists to
be checked against, not obeyed; the repertoire exists to be built, not born with.

## Try this at your practicum school

Write your next lesson's outcome sentence first - one line, a verb, a criterion -
then the evidence, then the activity. Teach it. Afterwards, check: did the
evidence arrive? The answer is the formative cycle applied to your own design.

## Summative task

Write **250-300 words** on the more effective of your two teachers.

Analyse the teacher against the practice inventory: which practices were present,
what each looked like in the room, and which single practice carried the most
weight - with evidence, not affection. Then name the practice the struggling
teacher most needed, and the first step of building it.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong answer shows |
|---|---|
| The inventory is used precisely | Practices are named, not vibes. |
| Evidence over affection | Every claim rests on something seen in the room. *(Analyze)* |
| The weight judgement is defended | One practice is argued to carry the most, with a reason. |
| Plain, accurate writing | Terms used correctly; no padding; within the word count. |
