# Topic 10.1 - Teaching methods and strategies

## A real classroom situation

One topic - the water cycle - goes to three Class 6 sections in a Hyderabad school.
Section A gets the lecture: the teacher explains evaporation, condensation and
precipitation, writes the diagram on the board, and sets the textbook questions.
Section B gets the demonstration: a kettle, a cold plate, and the class watching
steam become water. Section C gets the group investigation: each group gets a
sealed jar with a little water, a mark at the water line, and a fortnight of
observations to keep.

By December, all three sections can define the water cycle. Section B can also
explain why the plate had to be cold. Section C can also tell you the water level
in the jar fell, argue about where it went, and design a test for their answer. The
examination will not ask any of that - but a teacher choosing a method is choosing
what the examination does not ask.

## Explanation

### Teaching methods: what each is good for

A teaching method is a tool, and a tool is known by its job. The guide names five
strategies - lectures, interactive discussions, question-answer sessions,
demonstration, and case study analysis - and each earns its place by doing
something the others do not.

**Lecture** covers new ground fast and gives a class a shared framework. Its
weakness is the passive listening it demands - attention's spotlight (Unit 4) tires,
and the workbench holds only so much - so a lecture earns its length by pausing,
recapping and being short.

**Demonstration** shows a process: the kettle and the cold plate make
evaporation an event rather than a word. Its demands are materials and visibility -
and its gift is the "why does the plate have to be cold?" question, which is
curiosity (Unit 6) made of steam.

**Interactive discussion** makes thinking audible and gives the teacher a window
into the room's ideas. It demands a question worth discussing and the norms (Unit
8) that make talk safe; without them it is a recitation with longer sentences.

**Question-answer** is the guided practice of thinking: the teacher's questions
carry the steps, and the answers show where the steps break. Its craft is in the
questions - reaching past yes and no, and waiting (the pause after a question is
where the thinking happens).

**Group work** rehearses ideas socially - relatedness (Unit 6) and the social
construction of understanding (Unit 3) with a timetable. It demands a task that
genuinely needs the group, roles so no one hides, and monitoring so no one drifts;
without these it is seating rearranged.

**Case study analysis** puts the class on real material and demands a judgement -
this course has run you through Allah Dino, the two schools, the prize chart. It
demands a real case and time; its gift is transfer, the test of understanding
(Unit 3's analogy strategy, now a method).

> **The misconception to clear now.** "One method is best." Every method in the
> table is best for something and worst for something else: the lecture that covers
> ground fast cannot also check the room's ideas; the investigation that builds
> understanding cannot also cover the syllabus in a fortnight. The teacher who
> marries one method has stopped asking what the lesson is for. The professional
> question is always the match: this purpose, these constraints, which method?

### Choosing strategies: matching method to purpose

Choosing is one decision with three parts, not three decisions.

**The purpose**: what must the pupils be able to DO by the end - recall the map,
see the process, argue the case, build the understanding? The purpose names the
verb, and the verb names the method family.

**The constraints**: sixty pupils, one blackboard, forty minutes, this room's
noise, this class's norms. Constraints are not excuses; they are the material the
design works in. A demonstration impossible to see from the back row is a
mis-chosen demonstration, not a bad class.

**The method**: chosen last, to fit the purpose inside the constraints. The same
teacher who lectures the map on Monday demonstrates the process on Wednesday and
runs the investigation over the fortnight - not because variety is a virtue, but
because the water cycle has three purposes: know it, see it, and test it.

## Activity: One topic, three methods

**In small groups, about 25 minutes.**

Take one topic you could teach next week (any class, any subject).

1. Write the topic's three purposes: what pupils should KNOW, SEE and TEST by the
   end.
2. Match a method to each purpose, naming the constraint each choice works within.
3. Choose the one lesson you would actually teach first, and defend the order.

Then compare across groups: where two groups chose different first methods for the
same topic, find the purpose difference that explains the choice.

## Check your understanding

1. Name any four methods with the job each is best at. *(Remember)*
2. Explain what a demonstration gives that a lecture cannot, and what it costs.
   *(Understand)*
3. What does group work demand beyond rearranged seating? Name three things.
   *(Understand)*
4. State the choosing frame's three parts in order, and say why method comes last.
   *(Understand)*
5. The water cycle went to three sections. Name each section's method, its purpose
   and the learning the examination would not see. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Teaching methods are tools known by their jobs: lecture covers ground and builds a
shared framework; demonstration shows process and earns the why-question;
discussion makes thinking audible; question-answer carries guided practice; group
work rehearses ideas socially; case study demands judgement on real material. None
is best; each is best for something. The professional choice is one decision in
three parts - purpose, constraints, method, in that order - with a check that the
method chosen still serves the purpose it was chosen for.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can name the methods with the job each is best at.
- [ ] I can state what each method demands, not only what it gives.
- [ ] I can run the choosing frame on a real topic, in order.
- [ ] I can defend a method choice against an alternative for the same topic.

## Try this at your practicum school

Teach one topic twice, by two methods, to two comparable groups - or watch the
cooperating teacher do it. Note what each group can do a week later, not on the
day. The comparison is the choosing frame made visible.

## Summative task

Write **250-300 words** choosing the first lesson.

Choose your topic and defend the method you would teach FIRST: the purpose it
serves, the constraints it works within, and what it deliberately leaves for the
second lesson. Name the method you rejected and the purpose difference that
justifies the rejection.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong answer shows |
|---|---|
| The frame runs in order | Purpose, constraints, method - and the check. |
| The rejection is argued, not dismissed | The rejected method's purpose is named fairly. *(Analyze)* |
| The constraints are real | Sixty pupils, one blackboard, forty minutes - not a wish list. |
| Plain, accurate writing | Terms used correctly; no padding; within the word count. |
