# Topic 2.4 - Developmental stages and the classroom

## A real classroom situation

Miss Shabana teaches Class 4 in a girls' primary school in Sukkur. Her fractions lesson
was not working. She had drawn the fractions on the board as symbols - one half as 1/2,
one quarter as 1/4 - explained the notation, and set sums. Half the class copied without
understanding, and the other half did not even copy correctly.

A colleague from the B.Ed programme she attends at weekends suggested she start
differently: bring rotis to class, actually break them, and let the pupils see and hold
the halves and quarters before a single symbol appeared. She tried it. The lesson was
noisier, slower, and - by the end - the pupils who could not previously touch fractions
were arguing about whether two quarters really equalled one half, using pieces of roti
as evidence.

The difference between the two lessons is not effort, discipline or ability. It is
whether the teaching met the pupils at the stage their thinking had reached.

## Explanation

### Developmental stages: reading age in stages

A **developmental stage** is a pattern of functioning that holds for a period and then
gives way to the next in a fixed order. Topic 2.3 met Piaget's cognitive stages; other
domains have their own (this course meets Erikson's social stages and Kohlberg's moral
stages in later reading). What they share, and what earns the word "stage", is the
package from Topic 2.1: same order for everyone, no skipping, each stage a real
transformation of the last.

For a teacher, the stage idea is a **reading tool**, and it works in one direction only.
Given a pupil's work or behaviour, you can read which stage the work is consistent with.
You cannot run it backwards - from age to expectation - without breaking the rate
principle, because ages are approximate and the spread inside any class is wide. The
correct comparison for a pupil's work is **the stage descriptions, not the class
average**.

Watch what that single change does to a staff-room judgement. "Bilqis is behind the
class" compares Bilqis to sixty other pupils and says nothing actionable. "Bilqis's
answers are still led by how things look - she cannot yet hold the rule that pouring
does not change the amount" locates her on a road everyone travels, names what she will
be able to do next, and tells the teacher what to teach meanwhile. Same pupil, same
work; the second reading has somewhere to go.

The stage reading also protects the teacher from the two classic errors. **Demanding the
next stage early** - abstract symbols before concrete experience, formal argument from
ten-year-olds - produces copying without understanding, exactly the failure in Miss
Shabana's first lesson. **Resting at the current stage forever** - concrete materials
with a class ready for rules - produces boredom and misbehaviour from pupils whose
thinking has moved on. The match is two-sided: the task must meet the stage, and the
stage must be re-read as pupils move.

> **The misconception to clear now.** "Stages are deadlines - a pupil who misses one is
> broken." No stage is ever skipped, and no pupil is ever stuck in one permanently
> (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). A Class 4 pupil answering like a Class 2 pupil is not
> damaged; the reading is that the concrete-experience side of the lesson is missing,
> and the remedy is to supply it. The stage vocabulary describes position on a road, and
> every road in this unit runs in one direction: forward.

### Educational implications: matching teaching to development

The classroom consequences of this unit collect into a short list of moves a teacher can
actually make.

**Start concrete, then move to symbols.** For any new concept, the order that matches
development runs from handling or seeing the thing (rotis, water, counters), through
pictures of the thing, to the symbols for the thing. Miss Shabana's second lesson is the
pattern: break the roti, draw the roti, then write 1/2. The order can be compressed for
older pupils; it cannot be skipped for younger ones.

**Check multi-step instructions against reversibility.** A class that is not yet
concrete operational can follow steps only in the order given, and loses the thread the
moment a task asks them to double back (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). The remedy is external:
numbered steps on the board, a reminder to return to the story, a partner whose job is
to track the place. The remedy is not repetition of the instruction at higher volume.

**Give the fine-motor task a fine-motor page.** Topic 2.2's motor order meets its
classroom consequence here: wide-spaced lines and large letter boxes for the youngest,
narrowing only as the control arrives. The page is cheaper than the pupil's confidence.

**Read behaviour developmentally before reading it morally.** The pupil who shouts when
the group complains (Topic 2.3's activity), the pupil who cannot sit still after lunch,
the pupil who cries at wrong answers: each has a developmental reading, and the
developmental reading changes the response from correction to design. This is not
permissiveness - the response still includes teaching the better behaviour - but it
starts from "where is this pupil?" instead of "what is wrong with this pupil?"

**Use the peer group, do not just police it.** Group work is not a classroom technique
only; it is a developmental opportunity, because negotiating a shared task is precisely
the social rehearsal play used to provide. The isolated pupil the seating map reveals
is the one for whom "work in groups" needs deliberate structuring - a role, a partner,
a turn - rather than a general instruction.

**Expect unevenness, and say so to the class.** A class of sixty contains a spread of
stages and rates. The teacher who names this openly - "some of you will get this today,
some next week, and both are normal" - removes the shame that turns developmental
difference into ability belief. Unit 6 of this course returns to what that shame does
to motivation.

## Activity: Match or mismatch

**In pairs, about 20 minutes.**

For each lesson choice below, decide whether it is **matched** or **mismatched** to the
stage of its class, and name the principle from this unit that decides it.

1. A Class 3 teacher introduces multiplication with bundles of sticks before the times
   table.
2. A Class 2 teacher sets a comprehension worksheet with five instructions, the fifth of
   which says "go back to the passage and underline any word you used in answer 2".
3. A Class 6 teacher refuses to let pupils use fraction strips "because they are big
   enough to know fractions now".
4. A Class 1 teacher ends every writing period with a display of the neatest three
   books.
5. A Class 5 teacher gives the isolated pupil the job of materials manager for group
   work.

Then write the **one** you would change first, and the exact change you would make.

## Check your understanding

1. Define a developmental stage in two sentences, naming the two properties that make a
   sequence of changes a stage sequence. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why a pupil's work should be compared to stage descriptions rather than to
   the class average. *(Understand)*
3. Name the two classic errors a stage reading protects a teacher from, with one
   classroom example of each. *(Understand)*
4. Miss Shabana's first lesson failed and her second worked. Using this topic's terms,
   explain exactly what changed and why it mattered. *(Apply)*
5. A colleague says "my Class 2 pupils are just careless - they lose track of the
   worksheet steps every time." Give the developmental reading and one matched response.
   *(Apply)*

## Summary

A developmental stage is a period-bound pattern of functioning, occurring in a fixed
order, never skipped. Read against stage descriptions rather than class averages, a
pupil's work yields position and next steps instead of verdicts. The teaching that
follows is matched rather than accelerated: concrete before symbolic, steps made
external until reversibility arrives, pages that fit the fingers, behaviour read
developmentally before morally, the peer group used as well as policed, and unevenness
named as normal. The whole unit rests on the shift from "what is wrong with this
pupil?" to "where is this pupil, and what is the next step from there?"

## Try this at your practicum school

Take one lesson you will teach or observe this week and write down its single most
important new concept. Then answer, in three lines: what will pupils **handle or see**
of it, what will they see **pictures** of, and where does the **symbol** appear? If the
symbol appears first, discuss with your cooperating teacher whether the order could be
turned around.

## Summative task

Write **250-300 words** analysing one lesson choice.

Choose a lesson you have taught, observed or received as a pupil, in which something
was taught at the wrong level for the class. Describe the choice briefly, then analyse
it: what the pupils' stage of development (in any domain from this unit) could and could
not yet do, what the lesson demanded instead, and what the mismatch produced. Close with
the redesigned version - the same content, matched to the stage - and name the principle
that justifies your redesign.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong answer shows |
|---|---|
| Developmental analysis | The mismatch is located in a named domain and stage, not in a vague "too hard". |
| Matched redesign | The redesign is the same content taught in a different order or form, and the principle behind it is named. *(Analyze)* |
| Evidence discipline | The description of what the mismatch produced is drawn from the lesson described, not invented. |
| Plain, accurate writing | Terms used correctly; no padding; within the word count. |
