# Topic 2.2 - Physical development

## A real classroom situation

The Government Girls Primary School in a katchi abadi of Nawabshah and the private school
four streets away follow the same Sindh textbook scheme. A visitor who measured the
children would find the Class 4 pupils of the government school on average a little
smaller and lighter than their counterparts up the road. The staff room of the government
school knows why, and the staff room of the private school knows why too, and the two
explanations - food, and "breeding" - are not equally supported.

What neither staff room does with the fact is more interesting. Nobody has changed the
height of the blackboard, the weight of the buckets the pupils carry from the water
cooler, or the length of the afternoon. The fact is treated as a fact about the pupils
rather than as a fact about the room they are taught in.

## Explanation

### Physical development: height, weight and motor skills through childhood

Physical development runs through the school years in a recognisable pattern (Seifert &
Sutton, 2009). Two steep phases of growth - early childhood and adolescence - bracket a
long, steady plateau through the primary years. A well-nourished child roughly doubles in
height between age two and adulthood, but almost none of that arrives evenly.

Three cautions belong with the averages, and each one is a teaching matter.

**Averages hide a wide spread.** At age six, the average child in any population is about
115 centimetres tall, but individual healthy six-year-olds range from about 109 to 125
centimetres. In a class of sixty, the tallest and smallest pupils can differ by more than
the average child grows in two years. A teacher who seats by height, or who reads
smallness as younger-than-average, is reading a spread as if it were a rule.

**Weight diverges more than height.** By late adolescence the heaviest youngsters weigh
close to twice the lightest, while the tallest are only about ten per cent taller than
the shortest. Weight is also where nutrition bites hardest: chronic undernourishment
shows in weight long before it shows in height, and a class that is uniformly light for
age is telling the school something about food, not about heredity.

**Motor skills mature in order: gross before fine.** Running, jumping and catching - the
gross motor skills - are largely in place by the early primary years, though they keep
refining. The fine motor skills - holding a pencil with a relaxed grip, threading, using
scissors along a line, writing small letters neatly - mature later and more slowly. A
Class 1 pupil whose letters are enormous and shaky is usually not careless; the control
has not arrived yet. Demanding neat small handwriting from a six-year-old is demanding a
maturation that cannot be hurried.

The classroom consequences are ordinary and cheap. A blackboard a smaller pupil cannot
reach, a water carrier too heavy for half the class, a writing task whose lines are
closer together than the pupils' fingers can manage: these are room problems, not pupil
problems, and Topic 2.4 returns to them as educational implications.

> **The misconception to clear now.** "He is small for his age" is usually said as a
> verdict. In a classroom it is a measurement with almost no teaching consequences except
> furniture, unless it is extreme. Small-for-age predicts nothing reliable about
> intelligence, effort or maturity. The katchi abadi school's Class 4 is not a class of
> lesser ability; it is a class that is, on average, a little nearer the start of its
> growth. The difference between the two staff rooms' explanations is that one points at
> a cause the school cannot fix (food can be influenced, "breeding" cannot) and one
> points at a cause that quietly writes the pupils off.

### Puberty and adolescence: the second wave of physical change

Adolescence re-runs the steep phase of growth, and this time it brings sexual maturation
with it. <Glossary term="Adolescence" /> is the period of that second wave; puberty is
the set of changes - internal and visible - that produces sexual maturity (Seifert &
Sutton, 2009). Girls typically begin roughly two years earlier than boys, which produces
the well-known and sometimes awkward period around ages ten to fourteen when the average
girl in a mixed class is taller than the average boy.

For a teacher, three features of puberty matter more than its biology.

**It is uneven, pupil by pupil and day by day.** One Class 8 pupil is shaving; another
has not started. The same pupil may act like a young adult on Monday and a child on
Tuesday. The research advice is to respond flexibly and supportively rather than to
expect consistency (Seifert & Sutton, 2009).

**It is visible, and everyone knows it.** Early developers and late developers both get
commented on, by peers and sometimes by teachers. A teacher who joins in, even mildly,
teaches the class that bodies are fair game. A teacher who refuses to, and who redirects
attention to work, quietly protects the pupils most exposed.

**It interacts with school organisation.** Timetables, games periods, seating, and the
confidence to answer aloud in a class of sixty - all of these meet puberty differently
than they met the primary years. A girls' middle school that notices attention sag after
lunch in Class 7 and moves the hardest subject to the morning is applying developmental
knowledge; most schools apply the opposite by accident.

## Activity: Read the room

**In small groups, about 25 minutes.**

Your practicum school has asked for advice on three ordinary decisions. For each, decide
what you would recommend and justify it from this topic.

1. The Class 1 teacher wants all handwriting practice done on single-line pages "so they
   get used to it early". The pupils' letters are currently large and shaky.
2. The Class 8 timetable puts mathematics in the last period before home time. The
   mathematics teacher complains that the class "cannot concentrate".
3. The school fair's sack race is open to Classes 1 to 5 together, "for fun". Last year
   the Class 1 pupils lost every heat and two cried.

Nominate one member to report your group's recommendation for one decision, with the
reasoning, to the whole class.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the two steep phases of physical growth and the plateau between them, with
   approximate ages. *(Remember)*
2. Explain, with a classroom example, why gross-motor readiness arrives before fine-motor
   readiness matters for handwriting. *(Understand)*
3. A colleague keeps a Class 2 pupil in at break because his handwriting is "lazy".
   Using this topic, give the strongest two-sentence counter-argument. *(Apply)*
4. Give two classroom decisions (not pupil labels) that a school could change in response
   to puberty, and say what each responds to. *(Apply)*
5. Why does this topic treat "small for his age" as almost meaningless for teaching,
   while still treating the class-average difference between two schools as meaningful?
   *(Understand)*

## Summary

Physical development runs in two steep phases - early childhood and adolescence - around
a long steady plateau, with gross motor skills maturing before fine ones. Averages hide a
wide individual spread, weight diverges more than height, and puberty arrives unevenly
and visibly. For a teacher the consequences are room-scale and cheap: furniture, page
spacing, timetables, and a firm refusal to let bodies become commentary. What physical
development never supplies is a verdict on a pupil's ability.

## Try this at your practicum school

Walk one classroom with a measuring eye: blackboard height, desk-to-chair fit, the weight
of anything pupils are asked to carry, and the spacing of the lines on the writing books
of the youngest class. Note one thing in the room that assumes a body the pupils do not
yet have, and one that will stop fitting within a year.

from this topic's opening situation.

topic, recommend **two changes to the school or its routines** that respond to the
pupils' physical development, and for each say what evidence from the topic justifies it
and what it will cost. Close with one thing the school should **stop** doing because it
misreads physical development.
