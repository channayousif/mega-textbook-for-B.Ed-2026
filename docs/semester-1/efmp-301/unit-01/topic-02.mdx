# Topic 1.2 - How psychology and education meet

## A real classroom situation

At an in-service session in Sukkur, a trainer tells forty teachers about a well-known finding:
pupils remember more when they are tested on material than when they re-read it. Testing is not
just measurement, she says. Testing is itself a way of learning.

Three teachers leave and do three different things.

Mr Iqbal ignores it. His pupils are already tested constantly and are frightened of it; another
test is the last thing his class needs.

Mrs Naz adopts it wholesale and starts every lesson with a graded quiz. Within a month the weakest
pupils have stopped attempting the quizzes at all, and she concludes the finding was nonsense.

Mr Junaid changes one thing. At the end of each lesson he asks three questions, ungraded, with no
names recorded, and pupils answer in their notebooks. After a fortnight he checks whether the
class does better on the fortnightly test than the previous fortnight's class did.

All three heard the same true finding. Only one of them is doing educational psychology.

## Explanation

### Why a teacher needs educational psychology

A teacher makes hundreds of decisions a day and has time to think about almost none of them. Do
I explain that again or move on? Do I call on the girl at the back who never volunteers? Is this
class quiet because they understand or because they have given up?

Every one of those is answered by some theory of how learning works. The only question is whether
the theory was chosen or inherited.

This is the honest case for the subject, and it is worth stating plainly because the dishonest
case is so common. Educational psychology will not give you a rule that decides those questions
for you. What it gives you is, first, a set of ideas that have survived testing, so your default
guesses are better than chance; second, a vocabulary precise enough to describe what you are
seeing, so you can discuss it with a colleague and be talking about the same thing; and third,
the habit of asking what would tell you that you were wrong.

Pakistan's National Professional Standards for Teachers make this explicit rather than optional:
one of the ten standards is **Human Growth and Development**, which requires a teacher to
understand how learners develop and to use that understanding in practice (Khizar et al., 2019).
The knowledge is part of the job description, not an academic extra.

### What psychology contributes to education

Psychology brings three things across the border into education.

**Theories that have been tested.** How attention is limited, how something moves into long-term
memory, how motivation shifts when a task is graded, how children's thinking changes with age.
These are not certainties, but they are far better than staff-room proverbs, and they have been
checked against evidence rather than recalled from experience (Seifert & Sutton, 2009).

**Methods for finding things out.** The observation, experiment and case study from Topic 1.1.
These transfer directly: Mr Junaid is running a small experiment, whether or not he would call it
that.

**A language for describing learners.** "Weak" is not a description; it is a verdict. Working
memory, prior knowledge, transfer, motivation, readiness - these let two teachers point at the
same thing. Precision here is not decoration. Mrs Naz's failure was that "testing helps memory"
and "graded tests help frightened pupils' memory" sounded like the same claim to her.

### What education contributes back to psychology

The traffic runs both ways, and courses often forget to say so.

Classrooms give psychology conditions no laboratory can produce: thirty or sixty learners at
once, over months, with real consequences attached. A finding that holds for twenty volunteers
recalling word lists for an hour may simply not survive a Class 6 room in July, and the only way
to discover that is to go there.

Classrooms also pose problems psychology would not have thought to ask. Why does knowledge
learnt for a test vanish by the next term? Why do some pupils work hard for one teacher and not
another? These questions came from teachers, not from laboratories.

And classrooms are where theories go to be corrected. Mrs Naz's month is data. It does not show
the finding was nonsense; it shows the finding has a condition attached that the trainer did not
state, and that condition matters enormously for frightened pupils.

### Applied, not pure: how educational psychology differs from general psychology

Some sciences aim only to understand. Educational psychology aims to understand **in order to
improve something**, and that changes how it works.

It is judged partly by usefulness. A true finding that no teacher could act on is of limited
interest to this field, however interesting it is to psychology.

It cannot ignore context. Whether a finding holds in a Sindh government school with sixty pupils
and no electricity is not a footnote; it is the question.

And it carries an ethical weight that pure research does not. You are experimenting on other
people's children, in the time they have, and they cannot opt out. This is exactly why Mr
Junaid's version is better practice than Mrs Naz's: ungraded and unnamed, his change could not
harm a pupil's record while he found out whether it worked.

> **The misconception to clear now.** Trainees often assume the flow is one-way - that
> researchers discover and teachers apply, and a good teacher is one who implements faithfully.
> Mrs Naz implemented faithfully. The relationship is a loop: a finding arrives, a teacher tries
> it under their own conditions, and what happens feeds back. A teacher who never asks whether
> the finding held in their own room is not being scientific, only obedient.

## Activity: Three teachers, one finding

**In groups of three, about 25 minutes.**

Take one teacher each - Mr Iqbal, Mrs Naz, Mr Junaid - and argue their case as sympathetically as
you can. Each of you has two minutes.

Then, as a group, agree on answers to these:

1. What exactly did the trainer's finding claim, and what did it leave unstated?
2. Mr Iqbal did nothing. Name the circumstances in which that is the **right** professional
   decision, and the circumstances in which it is just avoidance.
3. Mrs Naz concluded the finding was nonsense. What would she have had to change about her
   month for that conclusion to be fair?

Write your group's answer to question 2 in one sentence. Be ready to defend it.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the three things psychology contributes to education, and give a one-line example of
   each. *(Remember)*
2. Explain, in two sentences, what a classroom can give psychology that a laboratory cannot.
   *(Understand)*
3. "Educational psychology is an applied field." Explain what follows from that for how its
   findings should be judged. *(Understand)*
4. Mrs Naz's quizzes were graded and named. Using the loop in this topic, explain what went wrong
   and what one change would have most improved her attempt. *(Apply)*
5. A trainer tells you that pupils learn better in groups. Write the two questions you would ask
   before changing anything about your own teaching. *(Apply)*

## Summary

A teacher needs educational psychology because teaching is decision-making under time pressure,
and every decision rests on some theory of learning - chosen or inherited. Psychology contributes
tested theories, methods for finding things out, and a precise language for describing learners.
Education contributes back the conditions, the problems and the corrections that laboratories
cannot supply. Because the field is applied, its findings are judged by usefulness and by whether
they survive real conditions, and acting on them carries an ethical weight: the pupils cannot opt
out. The relationship is a loop, not a delivery.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can state the honest case for why a teacher needs this subject, without overclaiming.
- [ ] I can name what psychology gives education and what education gives back.
- [ ] I can explain what "applied" changes about how a finding should be judged.
- [ ] I can take a research claim and say what I would check before acting on it.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your co-operating teacher this question: "Is there something you were told in training that
did not work with your pupils?"

Listen for what they changed and what they abandoned. Then ask yourself whether they had any way
of telling the difference between a finding that was wrong and a finding whose conditions did not
hold in their room. Write down what you would have suggested they check.

## Summative task

Write **300-350 words** taking one claim you have heard about teaching - from a trainer, a
relative, a teacher, or a social-media video.

State the claim precisely, including the conditions it needs in order to be true. Then say how
you would find out whether it holds for one class you have actually seen, and what result would
make you abandon it. End by saying which of the three Sukkur teachers your approach resembles,
and why that is the right choice for this particular claim.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong answer shows |
|---|---|
| Precision of the claim | The claim is restated with its unstated conditions made explicit, not repeated as a slogan. |
| Analysis of the loop | Distinguishes "the claim is false" from "the conditions do not hold here", and shows what evidence would separate them. *(Analyze)* |
| Ethical judgement | The proposed trial could not damage a pupil's record or standing while it ran. |
| Plain, accurate writing | Terms used correctly; the argument is followable; within the word count. |
