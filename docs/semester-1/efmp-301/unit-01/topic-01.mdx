# Topic 1.1 - What educational psychology is

## A real classroom situation

Miss Shazia teaches Class 6 in a government girls' school in Hyderabad. Two pupils sit at the
same desk. Both are taught the same lesson on fractions, by the same teacher, on the same
morning. Rukhsana follows it easily. Ayesha copies every step correctly into her notebook and
still cannot do a single sum on her own that evening.

Staff-room explanations come quickly. "Ayesha is weak." "Ayesha is lazy." "Some girls just
cannot do maths." Each of these is a theory about why learning failed, and each one leads
somewhere different: a weak pupil gets easier work, a lazy pupil gets a scolding, and a pupil
who "cannot do maths" gets quietly given up on.

Miss Shazia notices something the explanations miss. Ayesha can halve a roti at home without
hesitation. She can split a plate of food between three sisters and argue about fairness. What
she cannot do is connect any of that to the symbols on the blackboard.

That observation is worth more than all three staff-room theories, because it can be checked.

## Explanation

### What psychology studies, and what makes educational psychology a distinct field

Psychology is the study of mind and behaviour: why people do what they do, and what is going on
inside them while they do it. It is a wide field. A psychologist may study memory, fear,
friendship, language, or how an infant learns to walk.

<Glossary term="Educational Psychology" /> is the part of that field that studies mind and
behaviour **in educational settings**. Its question is narrower and more practical: how do
people learn, what helps and hinders that learning, and what follows for the person trying to
teach them?

Two boundaries matter, and new students blur both.

It is **not simply psychology applied to schools**. A general psychologist studying memory wants
to know how memory works. An educational psychologist wants to know what a teacher should do
differently on Tuesday because of it. The question is asked from inside the classroom.

It is **not teaching experience written down**. Experience is where the questions come from, and
an experienced teacher's hunches are usually worth taking seriously. But "in my thirty years I
have found" is not evidence that the thing works, or that it works for pupils unlike the ones
that teacher taught. Educational psychology is the discipline that tests the hunches.

> **The misconception to clear now.** Many trainees arrive believing educational psychology is
> "common sense with difficult words attached" - that a sensible person could reach the same
> conclusions without studying it. The trouble is that common sense supplies too many answers,
> and they contradict each other. "Birds of a feather flock together" and "opposites attract"
> are both common sense. "Practice makes perfect" and "you either have a gift for it or you do
> not" are both common sense. Common sense cannot tell you which is true for Ayesha, because
> whichever way the evidence falls, some proverb will have predicted it. The value of the
> discipline is not that its answers are surprising. It is that they can be wrong, and so can be
> checked.

### The scope: the learner, the learning process, and the learning situation

The field is usually described as covering three things at once, and almost every question a
teacher asks sits in one of them (Seifert & Sutton, 2009).

**The learner.** Who is this pupil? What do they already know, what are they ready for, what
moves them, what do they bring from home? Ayesha's fraction sense at the dinner table belongs
here.

**The learning process.** What actually happens when someone comes to understand something?
How does attention work, how is something held in memory, why does knowledge learnt in one place
so often fail to appear in another? Ayesha's inability to carry her roti-halving to the
blackboard belongs here, and it has a name: the problem of **transfer**.

**The learning situation.** What surrounds the learning? The way the lesson is sequenced, the
size and noise of the room, whether it is safe to be wrong out loud, what the test rewards.
Sixty girls in a hot room with one blackboard is not a neutral background; it is part of the
explanation.

The three are not separate compartments, and nothing is gained by arguing about which box a
question goes in. The reason to hold all three is that teachers reach for one and forget the
other two. "Ayesha is weak" is a learner-only explanation. It may be wrong simply because the
answer lay in the process, or in the room.

### Educational psychology as a scientific study

The course guide calls educational psychology "the scientific study of human learning,
development, and behavior in educational settings". The word doing the work in that sentence is
**scientific**, and it does not mean laboratory coats. It means claims are stated so that
evidence could count against them, and then evidence is gathered on purpose rather than
remembered selectively.

Three ways of gathering it come up constantly in this course.

**Observation.** Watching what actually happens, systematically, rather than what you assume
happens. Counting how many pupils Miss Shazia calls on, and who they are, will surprise most
teachers who try it.

**Experiment.** Changing one thing on purpose and comparing. Teach the same content to two
similar sections, one with worked examples first and one without, and see which does better on
the same test a week later.

**Case study.** Following one learner or one class closely and in depth. This will not tell you
what is true of all pupils, but it can show you a possibility nobody had considered - which is
often where the good questions come from.

None of the three is trustworthy alone. An observation may be of an unusual day. An experiment
in one school may not hold in another. A case study is a single case. What makes the field
scientific is not any one method but the habit of asking *how would I know if I were wrong?*

Notice what this gives Miss Shazia that the staff room did not. "Ayesha is weak" cannot be
wrong; it explains any result. "Ayesha has the underlying idea but has not connected it to the
symbols" can be wrong, and it tells her exactly what to try next: start from the roti, and build
the notation onto it.

## Activity: Sort the staff-room questions

**In pairs, about 20 minutes.**

Below are eight questions a teacher might genuinely ask. Decide for each whether it is mainly
about **the learner**, **the learning process**, or **the learning situation**. Expect to
disagree; the argument is the point.

1. Why does this class go silent whenever I ask a question?
2. Why can Ayesha halve a roti but not a fraction?
3. Why do my pupils remember the poem but not the grammar rule?
4. Why does Rukhsana finish early and then disturb others?
5. Why did the whole section do worse after we moved to the room by the road?
6. Why do they answer well in Sindhi and badly in English?
7. Why does nobody ask me to repeat anything, even when they have not understood?
8. Why do they do better on the test than on the homework?

Then pick the **one** you would most want a real answer to, and write a single sentence saying
what you would observe, count or try in order to get one.

## Check your understanding

1. In your own words, define educational psychology without using the words "study" or
   "learning". *(Understand)*
2. Give one question a general psychologist might ask about memory, and one question an
   educational psychologist would ask about the same topic. *(Understand)*
3. Classify each of these as learner, process or situation, and say why: a pupil who has not
   eaten breakfast; a lesson that introduces four new terms in five minutes; a pupil who is
   fluent in Sindhi and hesitant in Urdu. *(Apply)*
4. A colleague says "I do not need theory, I have taught for twenty years." Give the strongest
   fair reply you can, in two sentences. *(Apply)*
5. Miss Shazia wants to know whether starting from everyday examples helps her class with
   fractions. Describe how she could find out using **one** of observation, experiment or case
   study, and name one limit of the method you chose. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Educational psychology studies mind and behaviour in educational settings, asking its questions
from the teacher's side of the desk. Its scope covers the learner, the learning process and the
learning situation, and a teacher who reaches for only one of the three will misread most
classrooms. It is called a scientific study because its claims are stated so that evidence could
count against them, and gathered through observation, experiment and case study rather than
recalled selectively. Its value is not that it replaces a teacher's judgement, but that it gives
that judgement something to check itself against.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can define educational psychology and say what makes it different from psychology in
      general.
- [ ] I can name the three parts of its scope and give a classroom example of each.
- [ ] I can explain why "it is just common sense" is a weak objection to the field.
- [ ] I can name three ways evidence is gathered and state one limit of each.

## Try this at your practicum school

Sit at the back of one lesson with a blank page. Do not judge the teaching. Just count one
thing: how many pupils are asked a question by name, and where in the room they sit. Mark the
seating plan.

Afterwards, ask yourself which part of the scope your count belongs to, and what a second
lesson's count would need to look like before you would trust the pattern.

## Summative task

Write **250-300 words** on the Ayesha case at the top of this topic.

Take the three staff-room explanations ("weak", "lazy", "cannot do maths") and the explanation
Miss Shazia reached, and compare them. Your answer must say, for each, what evidence would count
**against** it, and must conclude with which explanation you would act on first and why.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong answer shows |
|---|---|
| Grasp of the field | Uses the scope correctly; does not treat every explanation as a learner explanation. |
| Analysis of the explanations | Separates the explanations by what evidence could disconfirm each, not by which sounds kindest. *(Analyze)* |
| Classroom judgement | The chosen next step follows from the analysis and could actually be done by one teacher with sixty pupils. |
| Plain, accurate writing | Terms used correctly; no padding; within the word count. |
