# Topic 7.1 - Learning styles and abilities

## A real classroom situation

A Sindh school spends a staff meeting sorting pupils into visual, auditory and
kinaesthetic learners. Charts go up. Teachers begin planning three versions of each
lesson. Two months later, nothing has changed except the workload, and the meeting's
energy has moved to the next initiative.

Meanwhile, in the same school, a Class 6 contains a future tailor who can measure a
window by eye, a debater who can argue any position, a farmer's son who can name a
crop from its shoot, and a pupil who is quick at everything except the two things the
examinations measure. No chart needed sorting them. The differences were already in
the room, waiting to be taught to.

## Explanation

### Learning styles: the claim and the evidence

The claim, as the staff room holds it: every pupil has a fixed preferred style -
visual, auditory, kinaesthetic - and learns best when teaching matches it.

The evidence, stated fairly, is a verdict against the second half. Pupils do report
preferences, and the preferences are real as preferences. But "a thoughtful review of
the data provides no support for style-based instruction" (Rohrer & Pashler, 2012) -
the studies that could support it, with learners randomly assigned to matched and
unmatched instruction, are the ones the advocacy rarely cites. The self-report measures what a pupil *likes*,
not what their memory and understanding run on (Unit 4's systems, which have the same
shape in everyone).

What survives the verdict is worth keeping, because it is free. **Pupils differ** -
in prior knowledge, in pace, in interest, in what Unit 5 called the profile. And
**multimodal teaching reaches more pupils**: an idea said, shown and done has three
routes in, where a lecture has one. Neither of these needs a chart, and both were
true before the styles theory was invented.

The teacher's discipline is to separate the residue from the packaging. When a
colleague says "he is a kinaesthetic learner, so he cannot learn from the board",
two claims are hiding in one sentence: the pupil is struggling (real, checkable) and
the reason is his style (a verdict the evidence does not support). The first deserves
a response; the second deserves a question - *what is the task demanding, and what
does he bring to it?*

> **The misconception to clear now.** "The styles theory is harmless - it gets
> teachers to vary their lessons." The variation is the good part, and it was never
> the theory's property. The harm is the verdict: a pupil labelled a "kinaesthetic
> learner" who then never gets the board work, the reading, the listening - the very
> practices that build the abilities he is weakest in. A wrong explanation of a real
> difference always ends by narrowing the pupil.

### Abilities: what differs, and what differences mean for teaching

If styles are the wrong account of difference, what is the right one? <Glossary term="Individual Differences" /> in ability are best read as a **profile**, not a point.

A profile is a shape across domains: language, number, spatial work, social
understanding, practical problem-solving, the body. The Class 6 described above has
four different shapes, and so does every class in the province. Two consequences
follow.

**A single rank is a poor summary of a pupil.** The examination samples two or three
domains of the profile and reports them as one number. The tailor's eye, the
debater's argument, the farmer's crop knowledge are all outside the sample - not
because they are worthless, but because the test was not built to see them (Unit 5's
measurement limits, now with a classroom address).

**Teaching to the profile is not teaching to a style.** A style is a claimed input
channel; a profile is a present level in a domain. The first says "teach him through
his hands"; the second says "his number work is two years behind his language, so
start the number where he is". The first needs a chart and a verdict; the second
needs a look at the work and a starting point.

The practical reading is Unit 2's stage idea applied to domains: where is this pupil,
in this domain, and what is the next step from there? The answer differs per pupil
per domain - which is why teaching is a profession and not a delivery system.

## Activity: The profile of a class you know

**In small groups, about 20 minutes.**

1. Each member names one real class and writes, privately, the four most able pupils
   in it - "able" defined however the member first defines it.
2. Compare the lists. Where the same class is named, do the lists agree?
3. Now re-do the task with one rule: a pupil must be named for a *domain* - "the
   strongest number work", "the strongest hands", "the strongest way with the
   others". Compare again.
4. As a group, finish this sentence in no more than fifteen words: "The difference
   between the two lists is..."

## Check your understanding

1. State the learning-styles claim and the evidence verdict, fairly, in two
   sentences. *(Understand)*
2. Name the two things that survive the verdict, and say why neither needs a chart.
   *(Understand)*
3. A colleague says "he is kinaesthetic, so no board work". Separate the real claim
   from the verdict, and give the question you would ask instead. *(Apply)*
4. Explain the difference between a style and a profile, with one example of each.
   *(Understand)*
5. The examination reports one number per pupil. Using the profile idea, say what
   that number is and is not. *(Apply)*

## Summary

The learning-styles claim - fixed styles, matched teaching, better learning - fails
at its second half: matching shows little or no gain, and the self-report measures
preference, not processing. What survives is free and was always true: pupils differ,
and multimodal teaching reaches more of them. The right account of difference is the
profile - a present level per domain, sampled poorly by any single rank. Teaching to
the profile is Unit 2's stage reading applied per domain: where is this pupil, in
this domain, and what is the next step from there.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can state the styles claim and the evidence verdict fairly.
- [ ] I can name the residue the verdict leaves and use it without the packaging.
- [ ] I can distinguish a style claim from a profile reading with an example.
- [ ] I can say what a single examination rank does and does not summarise.

## Try this at your practicum school

Take one class and draw a rough profile for five pupils: high, medium or low in
language, number and practical work. You are not diagnosing; you are looking. Note
one pupil whose profile is lopsided, and one thing the lesson could offer that
pupil's weaker domain at its own level.

## Summative task

Write **250-300 words** on the staff meeting's charts.

The school spent a meeting sorting pupils into styles and two months of planning on
three-lesson versions. Using this topic, analyse what the initiative got right (the
impulse), what it got wrong (the theory), and what it cost (the workload, the
verdicts). Then write the memo you would have given instead: what the school should
do about real differences, in three sentences or fewer.
