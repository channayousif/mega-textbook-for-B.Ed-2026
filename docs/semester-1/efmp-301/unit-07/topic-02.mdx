# Topic 7.2 - Slow learners, gifted learners and inclusive education

## A real classroom situation

A Sindh school kept a physically disabled pupil in Class 5 with one change: the bench
nearest the door was moved so his wheelchair could reach it. He stayed. His marks
were ordinary. His classmates learned to reach the shelf for him, and he learned
which classmate to ask.

Two streets away, a school with the same pupil and the same resources sent him home
in October - "the school is not equipped" - and the staff room agreed this was
honesty. The difference between the two schools was one bench and one decision about
what a classroom is for.

## Explanation

### Slow learners: recognizing and supporting

A <Glossary term="Slow Learner" /> is a pupil who needs more time and more passes
than the class average to reach the same place. The label describes **pace, not a
ceiling** - and the two must never be confused, because the first calls for patience
and the second writes a pupil off.

Recognition is straightforward: work arrives complete but late; understanding comes
after the third pass, not the first; the pupil can do what was taught last month
better than what was taught yesterday. What recognition must not do is jump to a
verdict: slow pace is not low intelligence, not disability, and not laziness,
whatever the staff room's shorthand says.

What helps is equally concrete. **Shorter steps with the same destination**: the same
concept cut into more passes, not a different concept. **Time**: the slow learner's
enemy is the timetable, not the material. **Prior-knowledge checks**: slow pace often
hides a missing earlier step (Unit 3's schema gap), and supplying the step changes
everything. **A visible record of progress**: the slow learner needs to see that
October's work is better than September's, because the class comparison will never
tell them.

What does not help: more of the same worksheet faster, public comparison, and the
withdrawal of the interesting work "until the basics are done" - which removes the
reason for the basics.

### Gifted learners: recognizing and supporting

Unit 5 established the ground: giftedness is an unusual level of skill or knowledge,
an educational need rather than a prize, appearing in every economic and cultural
group, and languishing - into boredom and behaviour problems - without accommodation
(Seifert & Sutton, 2009). This topic adds the classroom practice.

Recognition looks past the marks: unusually quick and independent learning; advanced
language; strong motivation on challenge; high self-imposed standards - and, just as
often, the early finisher disrupting the room, the pupil who argues the textbook into
a corner, the one whose questions outrun the syllabus.

Support is enrichment rather than acceleration: real problems rather than more
worksheets; some choice of what is studied; expectations kept honestly high; and -
the part most often missed - the social side made deliberate, because the gifted
pupil who is also sociable and sporty is the rule, not the exception, and needs the
group life every pupil needs.

### Inclusive education: what it means and why it matters

<Glossary term="Inclusive Education" /> means the pupil with a disability
participates in and contributes to the life of the class as much as possible:
assemblies and field days with the class, the group game including the disabled
pupil, the assignment group with the pupil in it (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). The
changes "are real, but can be positive for everyone": classmates "learn that school
is partly about providing opportunities for everyone, and not just about evaluating
or comparing individuals' skills", and the disabled pupil learns from classmates
"socially and academically" - the belonging skills of greeting and asking, which
are, as Seifert and Sutton note, "beneficial for everyone to learn, disabled or
not".

Two cautions keep the principle honest. **Disabilities are ambiguous**: categories
overlook the person, and "the behavior and qualities of a particular student with a
disability can be hard to categorize" (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). The pupil is not the
label; the barrier is specific - a step, a font size, a hearing distance - and the
school's job is to remove the barrier, not to manage the category. **Inclusion is a
direction, not a destination**: the Sindh school with one bench did not achieve full
inclusion; it took the first step instead of refusing it, and the next step was
visible from there.

> **The misconception to clear now.** "Inclusion is charity for the disabled pupil."
> Read the two schools again. The pupil in the first school gained a class; the class
> gained a member who taught them what school is for. Inclusion is not what the
> school gives up for one pupil - it is what every pupil learns because that one
> pupil is there. The charity framing is the second school's story about itself.

## Activity: The first step

**In small groups, about 20 minutes.**

A school has: a Class 4 pupil who reads three passes behind; a Class 6 pupil who
finishes everything early; and a Class 5 pupil who uses a wheelchair in a school with
two steps at every entrance.

1. For each pupil, name the recognition signs, what the staff room would probably
   say, and what this topic says instead.
2. For the school: list the first steps in order of cost, cheapest first.
3. As a group, decide the **one step you would take this month** if you were the head
   teacher, and defend it against the second choice.

## Check your understanding

1. Define the slow learner in one sentence, and say what the label must never be
   confused with. *(Understand)*
2. Name two things that help the slow learner and two that do not. *(Understand)*
3. Give three recognition signs of giftedness that look past the marks. *(Remember)*
4. State the inclusion principle in one sentence, with the two cautions that keep it
   honest. *(Understand)*
5. The second school called its exclusion "honesty". Using this topic, give the
   counter-argument in three sentences. *(Apply)*

## Summary

The slow learner needs pace, not a ceiling: shorter steps to the same destination,
time, prior-knowledge checks and visible progress - never more-faster worksheets or
public comparison. The gifted learner needs real challenge, choice and an honest
demand, with the group life every pupil needs. The pupil with a disability needs a
specific barrier removed and deliberate inclusion in every activity - and the class
needs that pupil, because inclusion teaches what school is for. Disabilities are
ambiguous and the pupil is never the label; inclusion is a direction, and the first
step is always visible from where a school stands.

- [ ] I can name the first inclusion step for a real school, cheapest first.

## Try this at your practicum school

Walk the school as if you used a wheelchair: which rooms can you reach, which
toilets, which shelf, which seat in each class? Write the three cheapest changes
that would open the most of the school. Keep the list; it is the beginning of an
inclusion plan, and it costs nothing but the walk.

## Summative task

Write **250-300 words** on the two schools.

The first school moved a bench; the second sent the pupil home. Analyse the
difference: what each school assumed a classroom is for, what each gained and lost,
and what the classmates learned in each. Then write the second school's counter-case
at its strongest - the resources argument - and answer it from this topic.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong answer shows |
|---|---|
| The assumptions named | Each school's view of what a classroom is for is stated, not implied. |
| The counter-case is real | The resources argument is made at its strongest before being answered. *(Analyze)* |
| The answer is from the theory | Inclusion, barriers and the everyone-learns argument carry the reply. |
| Plain, accurate writing | Terms used correctly; no padding; within the word count. |
