# Topic 1.3 - Kinds of computers

## A real classroom situation

Mr. Waqar runs the computer lab at a higher secondary school in Sukkur. One morning a pupil
points around the lab and asks: "Sir, the office has a big box under the desk, the head sir
carries a folding screen, you keep a small black box in the cupboard with lights on it, and
my cousin says her bank has computers the size of cupboards. Which one is the real
computer?" Mr. Waqar smiles, because the question has a real answer: they all are, and the
differences between them are exactly how computer scientists sort them into kinds. Being
able to sort them, he knows, is the day's lesson.

## Explanation

### Classification of computers by functionality: analog, digital, hybrid

One way to sort computers is by the kind of signals they work with.

A <Glossary term="Digital computer">digital computer</Glossary> works with discrete values,
in practice the 0s and 1s of the binary system. Every number, letter, photo and sound is
stored as patterns of these two states. The laptop, the phone and the lab desktop are all
digital computers, and nearly every computer you will meet is digital.

An <Glossary term="Analog computer">analog computer</Glossary> works with continuous
physical quantities, such as voltage or the rotation of a shaft, that change smoothly the
way temperature or speed do. A classic example is the slide rule, and analog computers were
once used to aim naval guns and simulate flight in the mid-1900s. An analog machine does
not count; it measures. Where a digital computer answers "exactly 7", an analog computer
answers "about there, on this dial".

A <Glossary term="Hybrid computer">hybrid computer</Glossary> combines both: it measures an
analog quantity, converts it into digital form, and processes it digitally. Hospital patient
monitors do this: they continuously measure a heartbeat as an analog signal, then convert it
to numbers for display and alarms. Modern petrol pumps do the same with fuel flow.

### Classification of computers by size and power

The second way to sort computers is by size and processing power. Introductory texts such as
Norton's *Introduction to Computers* and Shelly and Vermaat's *Discovering Computers*
(cited at bibliographic level as the course guide lists them) describe the same ladder,
from the most powerful to the smallest.

<Figure id="fig-U1-6" src="/img/figures/gict-300/unit-01/fig-U1-6.svg" alt="A five-step ladder from supercomputer down to embedded computer, each step naming the class and one line about where it is used." />

A **supercomputer** is the fastest class of machine, used for weather forecasting, climate
research and scientific simulation. Pakistan's research and meteorology communities use
supercomputing time for weather and earthquake modelling.

A **mainframe** is a large machine built to process enormous, steady streams of records for
banks, airlines, telecoms and tax departments, serving thousands of users at once. When a
bank in Karachi posts a million transactions overnight, a mainframe or mainframe-class
server is at work.

A **minicomputer** (also called a mid-range server) serves a medium organisation or one
department: a university's examination records, a hospital's stores, a factory's line. The
"classic" minicomputer is mostly history, but the mid-range server role continues.

A **microcomputer** is a computer whose entire processor fits on a single chip, the
microprocessor. The desktop, the laptop, the tablet and the smartphone are all
microcomputers. This is the class a teacher works with daily, and the phone is now the most
common microcomputer in Pakistan.

An <Glossary term="Embedded computer">embedded computer</Glossary> is a small computer fixed
inside another device to run that one device: the controller in a washing machine, a car's
engine management unit, an ATM's inner computer, a biometric attendance machine. You do not
use it as a computer; it works for you inside something else.

A caution about the ladder: it is about size and power of the machine, not importance. The
embedded controller in a hospital's infusion pump matters far more to a patient than a
supercomputer forecasting next month's weather. And the classes keep sliding: today's phone
outpowers yesterday's mainframe. Classify by what the machine is built to do, not by how
old it looks.

## Activity: Device detective

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

1. Your teacher will describe six devices, one at a time: an ATM, a smartphone, a national
   weather-forecasting system, a school office desktop, a car's engine controller, and a
   hospital patient monitor.
2. For each device, agree with your partner on two labels: its functionality class (analog,
   digital, or hybrid) and its size class (supercomputer, mainframe, minicomputer,
   microcomputer, or embedded).
3. Write your answer as "device: functionality class + size class, because ...", where the
   "because" names one feature of the device that proves the label.
4. Compare with another pair. Any device you labelled differently, discuss until you can
   agree on which feature settles it.

## Check your understanding

1. State the difference between an analog computer and a digital computer in one sentence,
   with one example of each. *(Remember)*
2. Why is a hospital patient monitor classified as a hybrid computer? Answer in two
   sentences. *(Understand)*
3. A device is fixed inside a car and controls fuel injection. It never shows a screen and
   the driver never operates it directly. Classify it on both axes and justify your answer.
   *(Apply)*
4. Explain why a smartphone is a microcomputer even though it fits in a pocket. *(Understand)*

## Summary

Computers are sorted on two axes. By functionality: analog computers measure continuous
quantities, digital computers count discrete 0s and 1s, and hybrid computers measure then
count. By size and power: supercomputers, mainframes, minicomputers, microcomputers and
embedded computers, from national-scale simulation down to the controller hidden inside a
washing machine. Almost every computer you will use as a teacher is a digital
microcomputer, but knowing the whole ladder lets you place any device you meet.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can define analog, digital and hybrid computers with one example of each.
- [ ] I can name the five size classes of computers in order of power.
- [ ] I can classify a described device on both axes and justify the label with a feature
  of the device.

## Try this at your practicum school

Walk through your practicum school and list every computer you can find, including hidden
ones (the biometric attendance machine, the photocopier, the UPS controller). For each, note
its size class and what it is used for. Aim for at least five devices.

## Summative task

A school receives a donation of five devices: a desktop computer, a tablet, a biometric
attendance machine, an old bank-server rack, and a weather receiver built to process
continuous analog signals into digital forecasts. Prepare a classification table with one
row per device and three columns: functionality class, size class, and one sentence of
justification naming the feature that settles each label.

**Marking guide (10 marks):**

- Functionality class correct for all five devices: 3 marks.
- Size class correct for all five devices: 3 marks.
- Justifications name a defining feature of the class rather than restating the label
  (Analyse): 3 marks.
- Table is clearly organised: 1 mark.
