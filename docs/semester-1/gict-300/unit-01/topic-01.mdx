# Topic 1.1 - Computer literacy and why it matters

## A real classroom situation

Miss Saima is a trainee teacher on practicum at a government girls' primary school in
Hyderabad. On her first day, the head teacher hands her the attendance register and asks her
to enter the week's figures into the school's computer and email the file to the district
office by noon. Saima has used a mobile phone every day for years. She can chat, watch
videos and scroll social media without thinking. But a spreadsheet, a file attachment and an
email to an official address are new to her, and her hands freeze at the keyboard. Being
comfortable with a phone, she discovers, is not the same as being computer literate.

## Explanation

### Concept and scope of computer literacy

<Glossary term="Computer literacy">Computer literacy</Glossary> is the knowledge and skill
needed to use computers and related technology effectively. It covers more than knowing
where the keys are. A computer-literate person can operate a computer or phone, save and
find files, create documents and presentations, search for information and judge whether it
is trustworthy, communicate using email and messaging tools, and protect themselves from
common online risks.

Many people think computer literacy means knowing how to type. That is a real
misunderstanding. Typing is one small motor skill. The European Commission's digital
competence framework, DigComp, describes digital competence in five areas: information and
data literacy, communication and collaboration, digital content creation, safety, and
problem solving (Vuorikari, Kluzer, and Punie, 2022). A person who can type but cannot tell
a reliable website from a fake one, or cannot attach a file to an email, is not computer
literate in this fuller sense. Bourgeois and colleagues (2019) make the same point from the
systems side: using technology well means understanding the people, processes and
technology together, not just pressing keys.

For a teacher, the scope is practical. Consider the tasks in the figure below. Each one
needs a different mix of skills, and a teacher meets all of them in a normal term.

### Importance of ICT in education, work and society

<Glossary term="Information and Communication Technology">Information and Communication
Technology (ICT)</Glossary> is the family of technologies that handle information and
communication together: computers, phones, networks, the internet, and the software that
runs on them. Notice what this definition includes. A phone call over the internet, a
school's Wi-Fi router and a spreadsheet are all ICT. Many people think ICT means computers
only, but the term deliberately covers networks and communication devices too.

**In education**, ICT changes what teachers can do. UNESCO's ICT Competency Framework for
Teachers (2018) describes three levels of teacher development with technology: acquiring
knowledge (learning to use the tools), deepening knowledge (using them to improve subject
teaching), and creating knowledge (helping pupils produce their own digital work). A teacher
who is computer literate can pull a science experiment video into a lesson in a village
school, keep clean digital records, and eventually guide pupils in their own projects. The
Higher Education Commission of Pakistan has also made ICT and digital literacy a stated
priority for universities and teacher education (HEC, n.d.).

**In work**, ICT has become the default way organisations run. Salaries, records, orders and
reports all move through computers. In Pakistan, most everyday services now have a digital
side: banks send SMS alerts, government offices accept online forms, and mobile wallets such
as Easypaisa and JazzCash move money without a bank branch. A person who cannot use these
systems depends on someone else for ordinary tasks.

**In society**, ICT shapes how people connect and get information. Pakistan's telecom
regulator, the PTA, reported more than 190 million cellular subscriptions and roughly 140
million broadband connections in 2025 (Pakistan Telecommunication Authority, 2025). Most of
that access is through mobile phones, not desktop computers. That single fact shapes what
computer literacy means in this country: the phone in a family's pocket is often their main
computer, their bank and their library at once.

## Activity: ICT skills audit

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

1. Each partner takes the five task rows from the second figure (attendance in a
   spreadsheet, emailing a file, finding a lesson video, preparing a notice, backing up
   records) and privately rates themselves for each task: "can do alone", "can do with
   help", or "cannot do yet".
2. Compare your ratings. Where the two of you differ, the stronger partner explains exactly
   how they would do the task, step by step.
3. Together, agree on two skills each of you will build during this course, and write them
   at the back of your notebook. You will return to this list in Unit 6.

## Check your understanding

   Using the concept of computer literacy, explain in two sentences what skill is missing
   and why it matters for the office. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Computer literacy is the full set of skills for using technology effectively: operating
devices, handling files, creating documents, finding and judging information, and
communicating safely. It is not typing. ICT covers computers, phones, networks and the
internet together, and it now runs through education, work and daily life in Pakistan, where
most people reach the internet through a phone.

## Summative task

Write a short paragraph (150 to 200 words) arguing why computer literacy matters for a
teacher in a Sindh government school today. Support your argument with at least two specific
school tasks and one fact about technology access in Pakistan.

**Marking guide (8 marks):**

- Definition used correctly, not just "typing": 2 marks.
- Two or more specific school tasks named and connected to skills: 3 marks.
- One relevant Pakistan fact or example used as evidence (Analyse): 2 marks.
- Clear, complete sentences within the word limit: 1 mark.
