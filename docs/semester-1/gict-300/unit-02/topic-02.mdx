# Topic 2.2 - Memory inside and outside the computer

## A real classroom situation

Miss Kiran is typing the class results into a spreadsheet when the power fails, as it often
does in Larkana in summer. The lights return after a minute. She switches the computer back
on, opens the spreadsheet, and finds only the version she saved yesterday. An hour of typing
is gone. Her cooperating teacher is not surprised: "The computer only remembers what you
save," she says. "While you work, it holds everything in a kind of whiteboard that wipes
itself clean when the power goes." That whiteboard is primary memory, and understanding it
and its stubborn cousin, storage, is what this topic is about.

## Explanation

### Primary memory: RAM and ROM

<Glossary term="RAM">Primary memory</Glossary> is the memory the computer uses while it
works. It has two main forms. <Glossary term="Random Access Memory">RAM (Random Access
Memory)</Glossary> is the working memory into which programs and data load while you use
them. Bourgeois and colleagues (2019) describe its two key traits: it can transfer data much
faster than the hard disk, and it is volatile, meaning its contents are lost when the power
goes off. That is the whiteboard from the classroom story. More RAM lets a computer keep
more programs and bigger files open comfortably; when people say a computer is "slow with
many tabs open", RAM is usually what they mean.

<Glossary term="Read Only Memory">ROM (Read Only Memory)</Glossary> is the other form of
primary memory. It holds the small, permanent instructions the computer needs to start
itself. ROM is non-volatile: its contents survive power off, and normally they are not meant
to be rewritten in everyday use. You do not choose ROM or manage it; you simply rely on it
every time the machine boots.

### Secondary storage and the storage hierarchy

<Glossary term="Secondary storage">Secondary storage</Glossary> is memory that keeps data
whether or not the power is on: the hard disk, the solid state drive, the flash drive, the
DVD, the memory card. A hard disk is non-volatile storage (Bourgeois et al., 2019); an SSD
performs the same job with no moving parts, which makes it faster, lighter and more
reliable, at a higher price for the same capacity. This is where your saved spreadsheet
lives, and why yesterday's copy survived the power cut while today's typing did not.

The two kinds of memory are not rivals; they are a team, and they form a ladder called the
storage hierarchy. At the top sit the processor's own tiny, extremely fast registers. Just
below is RAM: fast, moderate in size, and forgetful. Below that is the SSD or hard disk:
large, affordable, and permanent. At the bottom sits everything you keep far away but reach
through a network: the school server down the corridor, the cloud drive on the internet.
Each step down the ladder buys you more space for less money, at the cost of speed. That
single trade explains the shape of every computer you will meet.

<Figure id="fig-U2-4" src="/img/figures/gict-300/unit-02/fig-U2-4.svg" alt="Table comparing RAM, ROM and secondary storage across what they hold, whether they survive power off, speed, expandability and typical size." />

The everyday proof of the hierarchy is the save habit itself. While Kiran types, her work
lives in RAM. The moment she presses save, a copy is written to the disk. Until that press,
the disk does not have it. A teacher who understands this teaches pupils to save early and
often, and to treat an unsaved document as a loan, not a possession.

A second practical lesson follows for school purchases. When a computer feels slow, the
fix depends on which rung is exhausted: too little RAM shows as slowness when many programs
are open; a full or old disk shows as slowness in everything, including start-up. Knowing
which memory is which turns "buy a new computer" into a precise question: which rung of the
ladder is the bottleneck?

## Activity: The power-cut drill

Work in pairs. About 15 minutes.

1. Partner A describes a task being done on a computer (for example: editing the school
   magazine cover in a photo tool, with the file last saved 40 minutes ago). Partner B
   announces "power cut!". 
2. Together, state exactly what is lost, what survives, and where the surviving copy lives
   (RAM, ROM, disk, or cloud). Use the hierarchy words.
3. Swap roles with a new task. Cover at least four tasks between you, including one where
   the file was saved to a cloud drive and one where the computer was only starting up.
4. End by writing one sentence: the rule a pupil should be taught about saving.

## Check your understanding

1. Define RAM and state its two key properties in one sentence. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why a document that was never saved disappears after a power cut, using the
   words RAM and secondary storage. *(Understand)*
3. A school computer takes ten minutes to start and is slow even with one program open,
   while another starts quickly but freezes when five programs run. Say which rung of the
   storage hierarchy is the likely bottleneck in each machine, and why. *(Apply)*
4. Name one thing ROM does that RAM cannot. *(Remember)*

## Summary

Primary memory is the computer's working memory: RAM, which is fast, volatile and holds
whatever is in use; ROM, which is small, permanent and starts the machine. Secondary storage
(hard disk, SSD, flash drive, DVD) keeps files across power cuts. Together they form a
hierarchy trading speed for size at every step, and the save habit exists precisely because
RAM forgets and disks remember.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find out how much RAM and how much storage the lab or office computers have (ask the
in-charge, or look in the system information screen). Note the numbers, and ask one
question: which rung do they run out of first in daily use? Bring the answer back.

## Summative task

A school wants to keep end-of-term results for ten years and also needs its office computer
to handle the accounts software comfortably. In one page, recommend what the school should
spend on (more RAM, an SSD upgrade, or an external drive plus a cloud account), naming for
each purchase which rung of the hierarchy it serves and what problem it solves.
