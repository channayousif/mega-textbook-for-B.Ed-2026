# Topic 6.2 - Browsing safely

## A real classroom situation

During fee week, a clerk in a Larkana school receives an SMS: "Your school fee account
needs verification. Update now: fee-portal-pk.com/verify". The real portal is at a
government address. The fake address contains the words "fee" and "portal" and "pk" - all
the right words, in the wrong order, in the wrong place. The clerk, who has learned to
read addresses, notices that the domain (the part just before the first single slash) is
not the government's, closes the message, and opens the real portal by typing its address
himself. Nothing happened. That is what safe browsing looks like from the outside:
nothing happens, because the trap was seen before it was stepped on.

## Explanation

### Safe browsing practices

Safe browsing is Unit 4's habits applied at the moment of the tap, plus one new skill:
reading an address. A web address has parts, and the part that identifies the site is the
<Glossary term="Domain name">domain</Glossary> - the words just before the first single
slash. In "fee-portal-pk.com/verify", the domain is "fee-portal-pk.com": everything
before it (such as "gov.pk.") and after the slash is decoration an attacker can choose
freely. The clerk's check was one question: is the domain the organisation's real one?

Around that skill sit the checks that catch most traps. **Read the domain before the
slash**, not the words in the link text. **Look for the padlock** - it means the
connection to the site is encrypted, so nobody in between can read it; it does NOT mean
the site is honest, because fake sites can have padlocks too. **Judge the content**: does
the page ask for a password, a code, or money? Real institutions rarely demand all three
in a hurry. **Prefer the known address**: for anything involving accounts or payments,
type the address yourself or use your own bookmark rather than a link from a message -
which is exactly the phishing defence from Unit 4. And **keep the browser updated**,
because its warnings (the red screens that announce dangerous sites) are maintained by
the update.

<Figure id="fig-U6-4" src="/img/figures/gict-300/unit-06/fig-U6-4.svg" alt="Table of five safe-browsing checks with what each tells you and what it does not tell you." />

One more habit completes the set: scepticism toward perfection. A dangerous page often
looks better than the real one - clean design, urgent official tone, all the right logos.
Design is free; the domain is not. When the two disagree, believe the domain.

And for pupils, the teaching version of all this is one sentence, repeated: never type a
password into a page you did not reach yourself. The clerk's story, the head teacher's
story from Unit 4, and every fake-portal case in between reduce to that sentence.

## Activity: Address reading drill

Work in pairs. About 15 minutes.

1. Your teacher shows eight addresses (some real, some constructed), such as
   "hec.gov.pk/scholarships", "hec-scholarships-pk.com/apply", "pk.gov.verify-fee.net",
   and five others.
2. For each, write the domain (the part before the first single slash) and your verdict:
   which organisation owns it, and is the page likely to be what it claims?
3. Mark the three addresses a phishing message would most love, and write what makes each
   tempting.
4. Swap sheets. Argue any disagreement by pointing at the domain, not the words.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the part of a web address that identifies the site, and how to find it. *(Remember)*
2. Explain what the padlock does and does not tell you. *(Understand)*
3. The address "rewards.easypaisa-verify-pk.com/claim" arrives by SMS. Analyse it: who
   owns the domain, and what should you do? *(Apply)*
4. State the one sentence you would teach pupils about passwords and pages. *(Remember)*

## Summary

Safe browsing is one skill plus four habits: read the domain (the words before the first
single slash - the only part an attacker cannot fake cheaply), look for the padlock
(encrypted connection, not honesty), judge the content's demands, and reach
account-and-money pages by a known address you type or bookmark yourself. Design is free
and dangerous pages look better than real ones; the domain is the fact. For pupils, one
sentence carries it: never type a password into a page you did not reach yourself.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can read a domain out of any address and name its owner.
- [ ] I can explain what the padlock does and does not mean.
- [ ] I can analyse a suspicious address and state the safe action.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask the office for the addresses of the three portals the school uses most (fee, results,
attendance). Write them down exactly, and check how staff reach them today: typed,
bookmarked, or via links in messages. Note one improvement.

## Summative task

Design the front of a "read the address" card for the staff room: how to find the domain
(with one worked example), the four checks in order, and the two sentences that defeat
most fake-portal messages. Use one real-sounding constructed example and mark its parts.

**Marking guide (10 marks):**

- Finding the domain explained correctly with a worked example: 3 marks.
- The four checks in order, each with its limitation stated (Analyse): 4 marks.
- The two sentences are the right two: 2 marks.
- Fits a card: 1 mark.
