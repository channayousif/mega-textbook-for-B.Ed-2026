# Topic 6.3 - Artificial Intelligence and the Internet of Things

## A real classroom situation

Two scenes from one week at a Sukkur school. In the first, a trainee teacher spends an
evening with an AI chatbot, asking it to draft a Class 6 quiz on photosynthesis; in ten
minutes she has twenty questions - and, on checking, finds two facts quietly wrong. In
the second, the head teacher's office gets a smart attendance device that logs each
teacher's entry with a fingerprint and posts the day's record to the office system
automatically. Nobody typed anything. One technology learned her subject; the other
sensed her school. Both arrived without instructions about trust, privacy or error - and
that is what this topic supplies.

## Explanation

### Artificial Intelligence

<Glossary term="Artificial Intelligence">Artificial intelligence (AI)</Glossary> is the
field of building systems that perform tasks which normally need human intelligence -
recognising speech and images, translating languages, answering questions, drafting text.
Modern AI mostly works by learning patterns from very large amounts of example data: show
a system enough labelled photographs and it learns to label new ones; feed it enough text
and it learns to produce fluent sentences. It does not understand as a person does; it
predicts. That single fact explains both its power (fluency, speed, tirelessness) and its
known failure (it can be confidently, fluently wrong - the two wrong facts in the
trainee's quiz).

For a teacher, AI's present uses are already practical: drafting quizzes, worksheets and
letters to review and correct; translation support for bilingual material; speech-to-text
for accessibility; and adaptive learning apps that adjust difficulty to the learner.
UNESCO's guidance for policy-makers frames the education questions precisely: AI in
education should enhance human capacity rather than replace teachers, should protect
learners' data and agency, and should be governed by human accountability for its
outcomes (UNESCO, 2021). The trainee's rule of thumb follows: AI drafts, the teacher
decides. Nothing goes to a pupil unreviewed.

### Internet of Things

The <Glossary term="Internet of Things">Internet of Things (IoT)</Glossary> is the family
of ordinary objects given sensors, small processors and a network connection, so they can
measure and report without a human typing anything: the biometric attendance device, a
smart electricity meter, a water-level sensor in a tank, a vehicle tracker. Bourgeois and
colleagues (2019) define it as devices "embedded into a variety of objects" and connected
online, and offer the memorable test: think of IoT as devices you would not normally
consider being connected to the internet. The ITU's standard definition makes the same
point in formal terms, calling it the network of connected things with sensing capability
(ITU-T, 2012).

<Figure id="fig-U6-6" src="/img/figures/gict-300/unit-06/fig-U6-6.svg" alt="Table of AI and IoT uses in education with one school use and the caution for each." />

In a Pakistani school, IoT's honest use-cases are the unglamorous ones: attendance,
utilities and security, where automatic records beat manual registers. The cautions are
Unit 4's, sharpened. A device that senses automatically also collects automatically, and
biometric data is sensitive personal data under any standard - the four questions (why,
where, who, how long) apply to a fingerprint reader exactly as they applied to the app
vendor. And IoT devices are famous for forgotten passwords: the device that ships with a
default password and keeps it is an open door on the school's network.

Both technologies share the deeper pattern of this unit: each moves a task from people
to systems, and each therefore moves responsibility too. The teacher who lets AI draft
still owns the facts; the school that installs sensors still owns the data. Emergence
never transfers accountability.

## Activity: The briefing notebook

Work in groups of three. About 20 minutes.

1. Each group takes one technology from this topic: AI drafting, AI translation, IoT
   attendance, or IoT utilities.
2. For your technology, write the briefing skeleton a head teacher would need: what it is
   (one sentence), one concrete school use, one caution with its Unit 4 or Unit 5
   connection, and one question to ask a vendor.
3. Test the caution: write the one sentence you would tell a colleague who is about to
   adopt the technology without thinking.
4. Groups present skeletons in two minutes each; the class assembles the beginnings of
   the emerging-technology briefing for the unit's practicum task.

## Check your understanding

1. Define artificial intelligence and the Internet of Things in one sentence each. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why AI can be "confidently, fluently wrong", using how it works. *(Understand)*
3. A school installs a biometric attendance system. Name the data it collects and the
   four questions the school must answer before and during its use. *(Apply)*
4. State UNESCO's framing rule for AI in education in your own words. *(Understand)*

## Summary

Artificial intelligence learns patterns from vast examples and predicts - drafting,
translating, recognising - with fluency that can hide error, so AI drafts and the teacher
decides, under UNESCO's rule of enhancing rather than replacing human capacity. The
Internet of Things embeds sensing and connection in ordinary objects - attendance,
meters, tanks - automating records that once needed typing, at the price of automatic
collection of (often sensitive) data and forgotten default passwords. Both move tasks to
systems; neither moves responsibility.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find one automatic or smart device in the school (attendance machine, security camera,
UPS, meter). Write its briefing skeleton: what it senses, where the data goes, who can
see it, and the one question you would ask about it.

## Summative task

Write a one-page emerging-technology briefing for your head teacher on EITHER an AI
drafting tool OR a biometric attendance system: what it is, what it would do for the
school, the cautions (facts, data, accountability), and your recommendation with
conditions. Ground every caution in a rule from Units 4 or 5.

**Marking guide (10 marks):**

- The technology explained accurately in plain language: 2 marks.
- The school use is concrete and realistic for the school's resources: 2 marks.
- The cautions are tied to named rules (fluent errors, sensitive data, four questions)
  (Analyse): 4 marks.
- The recommendation carries conditions, not just yes or no: 2 marks.
