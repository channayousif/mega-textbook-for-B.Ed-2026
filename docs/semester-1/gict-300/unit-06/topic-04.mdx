# Topic 6.4 - Blockchain, Virtual Reality, and Augmented Reality

## A real classroom situation

A district office announces that teachers' professional-development records will now be
kept on "the blockchain", and a vendor offers a school a "VR lab" - four headsets and a
demo reel. The head teacher asks the B.Ed trainee on practicum what these things are, and
the trainee realises she has only slogans: unbreakable, immersive, the future. She cannot
say what a blockchain is, whether records need one, or what the difference is between
virtual and augmented reality - and a purchase decision worth a year's budget is about to
rest on slogans. This topic replaces the slogans with one-paragraph understandings, which
is what a teacher actually needs to advise a school.

## Explanation

### Blockchain

A <Glossary term="Blockchain">blockchain</Glossary> is a record-keeping design in which
entries are grouped into blocks, and each block carries a mathematical seal derived from
the previous block's contents - a chain. The consequence is the property that matters:
altering an old entry changes its seal, which breaks the link to every later block, and
everyone keeping a copy of the chain can see the break. NIST's technical overview
describes it as a tamper-evident, distributed ledger: many copies, no single silent
editor (Yaga et al., 2018). Its famous first use, cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin, is
one application of the design, not the design itself - "blockchain" and "bitcoin" are not
the same word.

For a school, the honest question is not "is blockchain good?" but "does this record need
one?" A blockchain earns its cost when many parties who do not fully trust each other must
share one record they all need to resist silent change - supply chains, land registries,
credential verification across institutions. A school's internal attendance register,
kept by one office that the school itself trusts, usually does not need one; the district
records that must survive any single office's failure might. The trainee's answer to the
head teacher begins: "it depends on whose trust problem the record has."

### Virtual and Augmented Reality

<Glossary term="Virtual reality">Virtual reality (VR)</Glossary> replaces the world: a
headset covers the eyes and ears, and the wearer stands inside a computer-generated
environment - a virtual science lab, a historical city, the inside of a cell.
<Glossary term="Augmented reality">Augmented reality (AR)</Glossary> adds to the world:
the learner looks through a phone or special glasses at the real classroom, and digital
objects appear within it - a beating heart floating above the desk, labels pinned to real
equipment. VR immerses; AR annotates.

<Figure id="fig-U6-8" src="/img/figures/gict-300/unit-06/fig-U6-8.svg" alt="Table comparing virtual reality and augmented reality across what each does, what the learner needs, one classroom use, and the main constraint." />

Reviews of AR and VR in education find real engagement gains - immersion holds attention,
and simulation lets pupils visit what a school cannot physically provide (Al-Ansi et al.,
2023) - alongside consistent constraints: devices are costly, content must match the
curriculum, and evidence of learning gain beyond engagement is still maturing. For a
Pakistani school, the realistic entry is AR on existing phones (a class set of phones is
cheaper than a class set of headsets, and phones are already in most teachers' pockets),
used for short, curriculum-anchored moments: label the skeleton, overlay the water cycle
on the real sky. The four-headset "VR lab" of the opening story, with a demo reel and no
curriculum plan, is the purchase to refuse - not because VR is bad, but because a
purchase without a lesson plan is.

Both technologies return to the unit's closing rule: the question is never "is it the
future?" but "what does it do for this lesson, at this cost, with these cautions?" The
trainee's briefing ends where the course does - with a teacher who can ask that question
in one sentence and answer it in one page.

## Activity: Slogan translation

Work in pairs. About 15 minutes.

1. Your teacher gives each pair five vendor slogans: "unbreakable blockchain records",
   "immersive VR learning", "AR brings lessons to life", "the school of the future",
   "tamper-proof certificates".
2. Translate each slogan into a claim that can be checked: what exactly would the product
   do, and what would we need to verify before buying?
3. For the blockchain-records claim, write the trust question: whose trust problem does a
   school record have, and does this solve it?
4. For the VR claim, write the two questions: what lesson, and what does it replace or
   add that a phone or a poster cannot?

## Check your understanding

1. Define blockchain in one sentence and name its key property. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why "blockchain" and "bitcoin" are not the same word. *(Understand)*
3. A school's internal attendance register is proposed for blockchain. Analyse whether it
   needs one, using the trust question. *(Apply)*
4. State the difference between VR and AR with one classroom example of each. *(Understand)*

## Summary

Blockchain is a chained, sealed, distributed ledger: old entries cannot be silently
changed without breaking every later seal - valuable when distrusting parties must share
a resistant record, unnecessary when a single trusted office keeps its own. Virtual
reality replaces the world with a generated one and needs headsets; augmented reality
adds digital objects to the real one and runs on phones. Both show engagement gains and
both demand the closing question: what does this do for this lesson, at this cost, with
these cautions?

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask what technology purchases the school has made or been offered in the last two years,
and what each actually gets used for now. Write two sentences: the pattern you notice,
and the one question you would add to the school's purchase process.

## Summative task

The head teacher has a fixed budget and three offers: a four-headset VR lab with demo
content, an AR science pack running on phones the school already owns, and a blockchain
certificate service for pupil records. In one page, assess each offer (what it is, what
it would do, its cautions) and recommend one with conditions, using this topic's ideas
and at least one rule from Units 4 or 5.

**Marking guide (10 marks):**

- Each offer explained accurately in plain language: 3 marks.
- Each assessment uses the right tool (trust question, lesson-fit question) rather than
  enthusiasm (Analyse): 4 marks.
- The recommendation carries conditions and a budget rationale: 2 marks.
- Fits one page: 1 mark.
