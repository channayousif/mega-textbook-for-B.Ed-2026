# Topic 4.4 - Privacy and data protection

## A real classroom situation

A vendor visits a school in Hyderabad offering a "free" pupil-learning app. To set it up,
he asks for the full names, ages, photographs, parent phone numbers and home addresses
of every pupil in Class 6 - about forty children. The head teacher, who has read nothing
about data protection, hands over the register. Nobody signs anything. Nobody asks where
the data will live, who else will see it, or what happens to it when the vendor's
company closes. The children, whose data it is, are never asked at all. This topic gives
you the questions the head teacher should have asked - and the habit of asking them
before the register leaves the room.

## Explanation

### Privacy and data protection

<Glossary term="Personal data">Personal data</Glossary> is any information that
identifies a living person: name, photograph, phone number, address, marks, health
notes, biometric fingerprints. Some of it is <Glossary term="Sensitive personal data">sensitive</Glossary>
- data whose leakage harms more than embarrasses: health records, religion, biometrics,
financial details. A school is one of the richest personal-data holders in any
community: it holds children's identities, addresses, photographs, results, and often
family circumstances, for years.

The core idea of <Glossary term="Data protection">data protection</Glossary> is that
personal data belongs to the person it describes, not to whoever happens to hold it. A
school is a caretaker, not an owner. From that one idea come the questions every
collection must survive. **Why** is this data being collected, and is the purpose
legitimate and specific - or is it "we might need it someday"? **Where** will it be
stored, on whose machines, and under whose protection? **Who** can see it, inside the
school and outside it? **How long** will it be kept, and what happens to it after? And
for children especially: their <Glossary term="Consent">consent</Glossary> cannot be
assumed, because a child cannot weigh consequences the way an adult can - the duty on
the school is therefore heavier, not lighter.

<Figure id="fig-U4-8" src="/img/figures/gict-300/unit-04/fig-U4-8.svg" alt="Table of five kinds of school data with who may legitimately ask for each and the handling duty each carries." />

Pakistan's legal backdrop is still developing: the Prevention of Electronic Crimes Act
2016 criminalises unauthorised access to and copying of data, and the National Cyber
Security Policy 2021 commits the country to protecting citizens' information systems
(Ministry of IT and Telecommunication, 2021); a dedicated personal-data-protection law
remains under discussion. That gap makes habits matter more, not less: a school that
waits for a statute before respecting its pupils' data has already failed them.

The practical habits for a teacher are small and real. Collect the minimum: if the app
needs given names to greet a child, it does not need home addresses. Prefer paper kept
in a locked cupboard to spreadsheets forwarded on chat. Photograph pupils only with
permission and never publish their images with their names. Do not use personal accounts
and phones to hold class data longer than the task needs. And when a vendor asks - ask
the four questions, in writing, before the register moves.

## Activity: The vendor visit role-play

Work in groups of four. About 20 minutes.

1. Roles: the vendor, the head teacher, a class teacher, and a parent who happens to be
   present. The vendor offers a free learning app and asks for the Class 6 register
   (names, ages, photographs, parent numbers, addresses).
2. Play the scene once as written - with the head teacher's likely instinct to agree.
3. Then replay it with the class teacher armed with the four questions (why, where, who,
   how long) and the child-consent concern. Let the vendor improvise answers.
4. As a group, write the school's rule in two sentences: what must be asked before any
   pupil data leaves the school, and what answer must be refused.

## Check your understanding

1. Define personal data and give one example of data that is sensitive. *(Remember)*
2. Name the four questions any collection of personal data must survive. *(Remember)*
3. The app vendor asks for home addresses but the app works without them. Using the
   collect-the-minimum principle, state what you would give and justify it. *(Apply)*
4. Explain why a child's consent to data collection cannot be treated like an adult's.
   *(Understand)*

## Summary

Personal data belongs to the person it describes; schools are caretakers of a
community's richest data, most of it about children who cannot consent for themselves.
Every collection must survive four questions - why, where, who, how long - and the
collect-the-minimum habit answers most dilemmas before they arise. Pakistan's legal
protections are developing, which makes the school's own discipline the real protection.

## Try this at your practicum school

List the kinds of personal data you saw being handled in one school day (registers,
notes, photos, messages). For each, note where it was kept and who could see it. Bring
the list back - it becomes Unit 5's ethics discussion material.

## Summative task

Write a one-page data-protection plan for your practicum school: the five kinds of data
it holds, where each currently lives (your observation), the riskiest current practice,
and three rules you would introduce first. Connect one rule to a threat from Topic 4.1
and one to the vendor scenario.
