# Topic 4.1 - Cyber threats and how to stay safe

## A real classroom situation

During exam season, the head teacher of a Karachi primary school receives a message that
looks exactly like the education department's: the logo, the official tone, a link to "view
your school's revised exam schedule". She taps the link, types the portal password, and the
page shows an error. Nothing else happens - visibly. Two days later the school's portal
account is locked and a message arrives asking for money to unlock it. She had been
phished: the page she typed her password into belonged to nobody in the department. The
teachers who later examined the message found three small tells - the sender's address was
wrong by one letter, the greeting was generic, and the link's address, read carefully, was
not the department's at all. This topic teaches you to see those tells before you tap.

## Explanation

### Common cyber threats: malware, phishing, social engineering

The threats that reach ordinary users come in three families. <Glossary term="Malware">Malware</Glossary>
is harmful software that gets onto a device to do damage or steal: a <Glossary term="Virus">virus</Glossary>
spreads by attaching itself to files and programs; <Glossary term="Ransomware">ransomware</Glossary>
locks your files and demands money to unlock them, exactly as in the head teacher's story;
<Glossary term="Spyware">spyware</Glossary> sits quietly and reports what you type, including
passwords. Malware usually arrives as an attachment or a download from a site you did not
mean to visit - the "I Love You" virus of May 2000 spread through an email attachment and
hit roughly 50,000 computers (Bourgeois et al., 2019).

<Glossary term="Phishing">Phishing</Glossary> is a message - email, SMS, or chat - that
looks like it is from a trusted source but leads you to a fake page or a harmful link. The
message is the bait; your password or your tap is the catch. Bourgeois and colleagues (2019)
describe it plainly: phishing "occurs when a user receives an e-mail that looks as if it is
from a trusted source", drawing them to a site that captures their credentials.

<Glossary term="Social engineering">Social engineering</Glossary> is the family that needs
no software at all: the attacker simply talks or tricks. Bourgeois and colleagues (2019)
give the classic case, <Glossary term="Pretexting">pretexting</Glossary>: an attacker "calls
a helpdesk or security administrator and pretends to be a particular authorized user" to
get a password reset. A caller claiming to be "from the bank's head office, verifying your
account" is the same move on a phone. The defence is not technical; it is a habit: verify
who is asking before you answer.

### Online security practices and defences

The good news is that a small set of habits stops most attacks, because most attacks are
not targeted at you personally - they are cast wide, like nets, and catch only the
careless. The <Glossary term="CIA triad">CIA triad</Glossary> names what you are
protecting: confidentiality, keeping information accessible only to those allowed
(Bourgeois et al., 2019); integrity, "the assurance that the information being accessed
has not been altered and truly represents what it intended"; and availability, information
being reachable when authorised people need it.

The habits follow from the triad. **Update** your devices and apps: most updates close
holes attackers already know about. **Verify before you tap**: read the sender's full
address, be suspicious of urgency ("your account will be closed today!"), and reach
officials through a channel you already trust rather than a link in a message. **Install
only from official stores**, and be equally careful of unknown USB drives. **Back up**
what matters, and keep one backup away from the device itself - Bourgeois and colleagues
(2019) note that disasters often destroy backups stored in the same place as the
originals. **Use antivirus**, but do not lean on it alone: it recognises known malware,
not new tricks or old-fashioned talking. Pakistan's National Cyber Security Policy names
this awareness layer explicitly, treating user awareness as part of the country's
defence (Ministry of IT and Telecommunication, 2021), and the National Cybersecurity
Alliance's guidance for everyday users is built on exactly these habits (National
Cybersecurity Alliance, n.d.).

A caution against two common misconceptions. "My data is not worth stealing" is false: a
school marks sheet, a parent-contact list, or simply your account itself (useful for
sending more phishing in your name) is worth an attacker's while. And "antivirus software
alone keeps me safe" is the more dangerous half-truth: the head teacher's portal was
phished, and no antivirus stops a person typing a real password into a real-looking fake
page. Nor is everyday confidence evidence of knowledge: a survey of 517 university
students found weak cybersecurity awareness across malware, passwords and social media,
despite lives lived online (Erendor and Yildirim, 2022). People, not only software, are
the defence.

## Activity: Spot the phish

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

1. Your teacher shows four messages (printed or projected), each supposedly from a school,
   a bank, a courier company, or the education department. One is genuine; three are
   phishing.
2. For each message, list every tell you can find: the sender's address, the greeting, the
   urgency, the link's real destination (described by the teacher), the request it makes.
3. Mark each message genuine or phishing, and write the single strongest piece of evidence
   for your verdict.
4. For one phishing message, write the reply you would actually send - or better, the
   action you would take instead of replying.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the three families of cyber threats from this topic, with one example of each.
   *(Remember)*
2. Explain the difference between phishing and social engineering in two sentences. *(Understand)*
3. A message says: "Your account will be closed in 2 hours, click here to verify." List
   three tells that suggest phishing and the action you would take instead. *(Apply)*
4. Name the three parts of the CIA triad and say what each protects. *(Remember)*

## Summary

The threats that reach ordinary users are malware (virus, ransomware, spyware), phishing,
and social engineering - and most are cast wide, catching the careless rather than the
chosen. The defences are habits, not gadgets: update, verify before you tap, install from
official sources, back up away from the device, and treat unexpected human asks with the
same suspicion as unexpected attachments. The CIA triad - confidentiality, integrity,
availability - names what all of it protects.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask the office how they receive official notices from the department (email, WhatsApp,
portal). Write three sentences: which channel a phishing message could imitate, what the
office currently checks before trusting a message, and one habit you would recommend.

## Summative task

The head teacher asks you to prepare a one-page "safe messages" guide for the school's
staff room: the three threat families in plain words, five tells of a phishing message,
and the school's rule for what to do when a message asks for a password or money. Use one
real-sounding example (invented by you) and mark its tells.

**Marking guide (10 marks):**

- The three threat families explained accurately and plainly: 3 marks.
- Five tells chosen well, each with a one-line explanation: 3 marks.
- The rule is actionable and the example's tells are marked correctly (Analyse): 3 marks.
- Fits one page, plain language for staff: 1 mark.
