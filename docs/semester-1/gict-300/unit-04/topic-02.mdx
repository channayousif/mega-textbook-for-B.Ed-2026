# Topic 4.2 - Your digital footprint

## A real classroom situation

A B.Ed trainee applies for a teaching post and is shortlisted. Before the interview, a
panel member searches her name. The results: her poetry blog, a fundraising appeal she
signed, and - from six years ago - a public comment in which she mocks her own college
teachers in crude language. Nothing she wrote was illegal. All of it is her <Glossary term="Digital footprint">digital
footprint</Glossary>: the trail of data a person leaves behind online, and which other
people can find. The panel did not ask about the comment. They simply formed an
impression. This topic is about making that impression a true one.

## Explanation

### Digital footprints

A digital footprint is everything about you that exists online because of what you do
there. It grows in two ways. The **active** footprint is what you deliberately put out:
your posts, photos, comments, likes, profile details, the reviews you write. The
**passive** footprint is what is collected about you as you simply use the internet: the
sites you visit, the searches you make, your approximate location, the device and apps
you use, what you pause to watch. You chose none of the passive part, and you see almost
none of it - but it exists, and it is often sold, merged, and profiled.

Why does this matter for a teacher more than for most people? Three reasons. First,
teachers are searched: by employers, by parents, by pupils. A pupil who finds a teacher's
old post does not read it charitably. Second, teachers model behaviour: a class that
watches its teacher share a forwarded rumour without checking learns exactly that habit.
Third, in Pakistan's context, where the same phone carries your professional life, your
family groups and your banking, a footprint is not a separate identity - it is your
identity, searchable.

<Figure id="fig-U4-4" src="/img/figures/gict-300/unit-04/fig-U4-4.svg" alt="Table of six footprint traces sorted into active and passive, each with who can see it." />

Managing the footprint is not hiding; it is choosing. The working audit has three
questions. **What is already there?** Search your own name in more than one search
engine, look at the images tab, and check what your public profile shows to a stranger.
**What keeps growing?** Your settings: review who can see future posts, turn off
location sharing for apps that do not need it, and think before each post - the internet
forgets nothing reliably. **What do you put out deliberately?** This is the part you
control completely, and for a trainee teacher it is worth curating: the poetry blog
helped the candidate above far more than the old comment hurt her, and both were equally
hers.

One more habit belongs here because it is about other people's footprints, not yours: do
not post photographs of pupils or share their details without the school's permission.
Children cannot consent to a footprint, and in Unit 6's terms, they will carry it longer
than you will carry yours.

## Activity: The three-question audit

Work in pairs. About 15 minutes.

1. Each partner writes (privately, on paper, not to share) the answers to the three audit
   questions for themselves: what is already there, what keeps growing, what I put out
   deliberately.
2. Together, without reading each other's notes, discuss: which of the six traces in the
   second figure would a future employer most likely see? Rank the top three.
3. Agree on one setting each of you will change tonight (privacy, location, or
   notifications) and write it down.
4. Swap lists of what counts as a "good deliberate trace" for a teacher - what would you
   want a panel to find?

## Check your understanding

1. Define a digital footprint in one sentence. *(Remember)*
2. Explain the difference between the active and passive footprint, with one example of
   each. *(Understand)*
3. A friend argues: "I have nothing to hide, so I do not care about my footprint." Give
   two reasons why a teacher specifically should care anyway. *(Apply)*
4. Name two traces you can reduce tonight and the setting that controls each. *(Apply)*

## Summary

A digital footprint is the trail you leave online: the active part you publish (posts,
comments, profiles) and the passive part collected about you (searches, location, app
activity). Teachers are searched more than most people, model online behaviour for
pupils, and live professional and personal lives on one phone - so the footprint is worth
auditing on three questions: what is already there, what keeps growing, what I put out
deliberately. And pupils' data is never yours to publish.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can define a digital footprint and distinguish active from passive traces.
- [ ] I can run the three-question audit on myself.
- [ ] I can explain why a teacher's footprint matters more than most people's.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask two or three colleagues (or your cooperating teacher) whether anyone has ever
searched them online, and what they found. Note the answers anonymously. Bring back one
sentence: does the school have any habit or rule about posting pupil photographs?

## Summative task

Write a one-page "footprint audit" for yourself as a future teacher: what a search of
your name currently shows (or would show), which traces help and which harm your
professional image, and three concrete actions you will take - one to clean, one to
curate, one to prevent.

**Marking guide (10 marks):**

- The audit describes actual trace types, not vague worries: 3 marks.
- Help and harm judged with reasons tied to the teaching profession (Analyse): 4 marks.
- Three actions are concrete and each names the setting or habit involved: 2 marks.
- Clear, honest, one page: 1 mark.
