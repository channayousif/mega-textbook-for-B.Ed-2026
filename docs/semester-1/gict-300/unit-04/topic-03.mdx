# Topic 4.3 - Passwords, authentication, and the cloud

## A real classroom situation

The school office computer's marks portal is protected by the password "school123". It
meets the portal's rule - eight characters, one word, easy for everyone on the staff to
remember and share. That is exactly the problem: a password everyone can share is a
password everyone can use, including someone who should not. When the portal is
compromised, the technician's first instruction is not a new firewall; it is a new way of
thinking about secrets - and a second lock on the door that no shared word can be.

## Explanation

### Password management and authentication

A password works by being a secret between you and a system. Three habits keep it one.
**Length and variety beat cleverness**: Bourgeois and colleagues (2019) report that the
most common real-world passwords were literally "password", "123456" and "12345678" -
the first things an attacker tries - and recommend at least twelve characters for a
personal password, mixing letters of both cases, digits and symbols, with no dictionary
words. **Never reuse a password across accounts**: one phished site should not unlock
your email, your bank and the school portal. **Never share it**: an attacker who cannot
guess a password will often simply ask for it (that is pretexting from Topic 4.1).

Because human memory is the weakest link, the modern answer is a
<Glossary term="Password manager">password manager</Glossary>: an application that
generates and stores a different strong password for every account, locked behind one
master password you do remember. One good secret replaces forty weak ones.

<Glossary term="Authentication">Authentication</Glossary> is the system's act of
checking that you are who you claim to be, and it can rest on three kinds of evidence:
something you know (a password), something you have (a phone or a security key), and
something you are (a fingerprint or face). <Glossary term="Multi-factor authentication">Multi-factor
authentication (MFA)</Glossary> combines two different kinds - a password plus a code
sent to your phone, as the figure shows. Its power is simple: an attacker who phishes
your password still cannot log in, because the second factor is in your pocket. Every
account that matters - email first of all, because email resets everything else -
should have MFA turned on.

### Cloud computing basics

"The cloud" is not a place in the sky; it is other people's computers, rented by the
minute. The standard definition, from NIST, runs: cloud computing is "a model for
enabling ubiquitous, convenient, on-demand network access to a shared pool of
configurable computing resources... that can be rapidly provisioned and released with
minimal management effort or service provider interaction" (Mell and Grance, 2011). The
definition names five essential characteristics: on-demand self-service, broad network
access, resource pooling, rapid elasticity, and measured service - you get what you need
when you need it, over the network, from a shared pool, scaling up and down, paying for
what you use.

For a school, the practical translation is threefold. **Storage**: files kept in the
cloud (a drive, a backup service) live on the provider's machines, copied across data
centres - which is why a stolen laptop no longer means lost records. **Services**:
email, documents, and learning platforms that run in the browser are cloud services,
nothing installed on the lab machines. **Someone else's responsibility, someone else's
rules**: the provider runs the servers, but you have handed them your data, so the
provider's reputation, terms and location matter. The cloud is a trade: convenience and
durability in exchange for trust. Topic 4.4 takes up what that trust should require.

## Activity: Password workshop

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

1. Your teacher writes five passwords on the board (from the second figure's list, plus
   two new ones). For each, agree a verdict - weak, strong, or dangerous habit - and one
   clause of reasoning.
2. Together, invent a strong password you could actually remember, using a method (not a
   word): for example, the first letters of a sentence only you know, with a number and
   symbol added. Write the method, not the password itself.
3. List which of your own accounts currently has MFA turned on, and mark the two that
   most need it.
4. Discuss: what would you tell the school office that currently shares one password
   among five people? Propose a workable alternative in two sentences.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the three kinds of authentication evidence, with one example of each. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why "school123" is a weak password even though it has eight characters. *(Understand)*
3. Your email account is phished but has MFA enabled. Explain what happens when the
   attacker tries to log in. *(Apply)*
4. State the NIST definition of cloud computing in your own words, naming two of its
   five characteristics. *(Understand)*

## Summary

A password is a secret, and the habits that keep it one are length and variety, no
reuse, and no sharing - with a password manager doing the remembering. Authentication
can rest on something you know, have, or are, and multi-factor authentication combines
two kinds, so a phished password alone opens nothing. The cloud is other people's
computers: on-demand, networked, pooled, elastic, metered - convenient and durable, at
the price of trusting the provider with your data.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask the office (politely) whether the school's portal or email uses any second factor
today, and how many people share the main password. Write two sentences: the current
practice, and the single change you would recommend first.

## Summative task

The school office shares one password for the marks portal among five staff, and the
head teacher keeps the only backup drive in the same cupboard as the computer. In one
page, recommend a security upgrade: what to change about the passwords and why, whether
and how to add a second factor, and what to do about the backup - connecting each
recommendation to a threat from Topic 4.1.

**Marking guide (10 marks):**

- The password problem diagnosed with its real risks (sharing, weakness, reuse): 3 marks.
- The MFA recommendation explains the mechanism, not just the name (Analyse): 3 marks.
- The backup recommendation addresses the same-location failure: 2 marks.
- Each recommendation is tied to a named threat: 1 mark.
- Clear, one page: 1 mark.
