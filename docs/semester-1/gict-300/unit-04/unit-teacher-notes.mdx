# Unit 4 teacher notes

## Sequencing note

The topics run from outside in: the threats that arrive (4.1), the trail you leave
(4.2), the locks you control (4.3), and the duty you carry for others' data (4.4). Topic
4.1 is the longest and the anchor: its threat vocabulary is used by every later topic.
If time is short, protect 4.1 and 4.4 - the incoming threat and the children's data -
above all.

## Common misconceptions to probe

1. **"My data is not worth stealing."** Probe with the school marks sheet and the
   parent-contact list: who is harmed when they leak? The account itself is also
   valuable as a phishing launcher in your name.
2. **"Antivirus software alone keeps me safe."** The head teacher's story in 4.1 is the
   cure: she was phished, and no scanner watches a human typing a real password into a
   real-looking page.
3. **"The cloud is a place in the sky."** Ask where a file physically is when saved to
   the cloud; the answer (a data centre, on machines you rent by the minute) reframes
   every later privacy question.
4. **"A strong password is enough."** Probe with the phished-MFA scenario: what does
   the attacker still lack?
5. **"Privacy rules are for companies, not schools."** The vendor scenario in 4.4: who
   protects the children when the law is still developing?

## Managing the activities

**Topic 4.1 - Spot the phish (20 minutes, pairs).** Build the four messages yourself
beforehand, with real-looking details and deliberate tells. The trap is verdicts without
evidence; require the single strongest tell for each. The "action instead of replying"
step is the real assessment.

**Topic 4.2 - The three-question audit (15 minutes, pairs).** The notes are private; do
not collect them. The ranking discussion (which traces would an employer see) is where
the learning lands. End with the one-setting change, and follow it up next session.

**Topic 4.3 - Password workshop (20 minutes, pairs).** The trap is writing an actual
password down anywhere shared; insist on methods, not secrets. The office scenario
(shared password among five people) is deliberately close to home - let the discomfort
do the teaching.

**Topic 4.4 - The vendor visit role-play (20 minutes, groups of four).** Play the first
pass quickly so the second pass shows the difference the four questions make. The
vendor should improvise plausible-but-vague answers ("our servers are very secure");
teach trainees to notice vagueness as an answer.

## Working with the guide's strategies

**Case study analysis** is this unit's natural form: the head teacher's phishing story,
the shortlisted candidate's search results, and the vendor visit are ready-made cases -
open each topic with its case before any explanation. **Demonstrations** work well in
4.3: show a real MFA prompt (or a screenshot), and show a password-strength checker on
an invented password. **Interactive discussion** fits the misconception probes.
**Question-answer sessions** suit the end of 4.1 using the phishing tells as rapid
rounds. **Lectures** are needed only for the CIA triad and the NIST definition - keep
them to the figures.

## Practical work and practicum links

- **Hands-on computer exercises:** turning on MFA on a real account, checking privacy
  and location settings, and searching one's own name in two search engines.
- **Individual assignments:** the footprint audit (4.2) and the data-protection plan
  (4.4).
- **Group assignments:** the phishing spot and the vendor role-play.
- **Internet research tasks:** optional - find whether a favourite app has had a data
  leak reported; require a source.
- **Presentations:** the safe-messages guide (4.1) presents well in a staff-meeting
  format.

The practicum tasks (the office's notice channels, the shared-password question, the
one-day data inventory) feed Unit 5's ethics discussions and Unit 6's emerging-technology
briefing, where the vendor scenario returns with a new technology attached.
