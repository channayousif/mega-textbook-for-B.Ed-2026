# Topic 3.3 - Managing input and output

## A real classroom situation

The teacher plugs a new printer into the lab's computer. Nothing happens for a few
seconds; then a small message appears: "installing device software". A minute later the
printer works, and - the remarkable part - the word processor, the browser and the
results spreadsheet can all print to it, though none of them was written with this
printer in mind. What happened in that minute is the quiet centre of this topic: the
operating system installed a <Glossary term="Device driver">device driver</Glossary>,
the translator that lets any application talk to any device, and took over the job of
managing the traffic between them.

## Explanation

### Managing input and output

Every device - keyboard, screen, disk, printer, USB drive - speaks its own low-level
language of signals and registers. No application wants to know those languages, and no
device maker wants to write a different version of every application. The operating
system stands in the middle and makes both sides simple.

The chain works like this. An **application** asks for something ordinary: "print this
page", "read this file", "show this frame". It addresses the request to the operating
system, never to the device directly. The **operating system** accepts the request,
checks that it is allowed, and places it in a queue. The **driver**, a small piece of
system software written for one specific device, translates the request into that
device's own commands. The **device** does the work and reports back, and the operating
system passes the result to the application. Your click on "Print" and the whir of the
printer are the two visible ends of that chain; the middle is the OS doing what it does
all day.

Two more ideas complete the picture. A <Glossary term="Buffer">buffer</Glossary> is a
small holding area in memory where the OS parks data in transit - the first part of a
print job sits in a buffer so the application can move on while the slow printer catches
up. And <Glossary term="Spooling">spooling</Glossary> is the same idea at larger scale:
print jobs queue up one after another, which is why three teachers can click Print at
once and the pages still come out in order, one job at a time.

<Figure id="fig-U3-6" src="/img/figures/gict-300/unit-03/fig-U3-6.svg" alt="Table of five everyday input/output requests, what the operating system does for each, and what the user notices." />

The payoff of this arrangement is enormous and usually invisible: applications stay
simple because they speak one language (the OS), and devices stay interchangeable
because each plugs in through its own driver. When a device "does not work", the chain
gives you a diagnostic ladder: is it the device (does it have power?), the driver (does
the OS recognise it?), or the application (does the request even reach the OS?)? Naming
the rung turns a shrug into a plan.

A note on the blocked state from Topic 3.2: now you can see why a process that starts an
input/output operation goes blocked. The disk will take its time whatever the process
does, so the OS parks the process and gives the processor to someone else until the
device reports completion. Waiting is not wasted; it is scheduled.

## Activity: The diagnostic ladder

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

1. Your teacher describes five faults: a printer that shows no lights; a printer with
   lights that no program can print to; a USB drive that works on one computer but not
   another; a keyboard that types nothing; a file that opens on the office machine but
   not on the lab machine.
2. For each fault, agree where the chain is most likely broken: device, driver, or
   application - and write one sentence naming the evidence that points there.
3. For one fault of your choice, write the two questions you would ask the user first.
4. Compare ladders with another pair. Where you disagree, test both against the chain in
   the first figure.

## Check your understanding

1. Define a device driver in one sentence. *(Remember)*
2. Trace the journey of a print request from the word processor to the page, naming all
   four links in the chain. *(Understand)*
3. A USB drive works on one computer but not another. Using the chain, give the most
   likely broken link and your reason. *(Apply)*
4. Explain what a buffer does and why printing does not freeze the word processor.
   *(Understand)*

## Summary

Input/output management is the operating system's translation service: applications make
ordinary requests, the OS queues and polices them, drivers translate them into each
device's own language, and buffers and spooling smooth the speed differences so
everything appears to happen at once. The chain doubles as a diagnostic ladder - device,
driver, or application - and it explains why a process doing input/output goes blocked
rather than busy.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find one device that is currently not working in the school (or ask about the last one
that failed). Write three sentences: the symptom, which link of the chain the fault
belonged to, and what fixed it.

## Summative task

A teacher complains: "I clicked Print, the printer has power and paper, but nothing comes
out, and only my computer has this problem; others print fine." In one page, analyse the
fault using the input/output chain: name the link you would check first and why, what
the next two checks would be, and what the likely fix is at each step.

**Marking guide (10 marks):**

- The chain named and applied to the symptom (Analyse): 4 marks.
- Checks ordered sensibly with a reason for each: 3 marks.
- Likely fixes matched to the right link: 2 marks.
- Clear writing: 1 mark.
