# Topic 5.2 - Online etiquette (netiquette)

## A real classroom situation

A head teacher in Nawabshah receives a message at 11 p.m. from a parent, written in
capitals: "WHY HAS MY DAUGHTER'S MARK NOT BEEN UPDATED???" He replies with two lines,
also short, also in capitals. By morning the exchange has been forwarded to the school's
trustees as "evidence" of rudeness on both sides. Two people who would have been
perfectly courteous face to face managed, in text, to insult each other - without either
writing a single insult. The cause is not bad character; it is bad netiquette: written
communication strips away tone, face and pause, and what you fail to add deliberately,
the reader supplies angrily.

## Explanation

### Netiquette rules

<Glossary term="Netiquette">Netiquette</Glossary> - network etiquette - is the set of
conventions for behaving well in written digital communication: email, chat, forums,
comment threads. Its entire reason for existing is that writing is a poor carrier of
tone. Speech carries your face, your pause, your softening half-smile; text carries only
the words, and the reader fills in the rest from their own mood. The 11 p.m. parent read
indifference; the head teacher read aggression; both were reading themselves.

The rules fall into three groups. **Before you send**: identify yourself (a greeting and
your name in email; a clear profile name in chat); state the matter plainly; check the
recipient line - the wrong group is the classic disaster; and do not send heat. If a
message makes your jaw tighten, write the reply, then leave it for an hour, then decide.
**While you write**: mind the caps (a whole message in capitals reads as shouting); mind
the punctuation ("???" reads as a slap); keep the message about the matter, not the
person; and match the channel - a two-line "ok" to a colleague's careful paragraph reads
as dismissal. **After you send**: accept that it is permanent and forwardable. The
screenshot does not carry your apology.

<Figure id="fig-U5-4" src="/img/figures/gict-300/unit-05/fig-U5-4.svg" alt="A triangle diagram with three labelled corners - the reader, the purpose and the context - and a caption to read your message through all three corners before sending." />

A working tool for the moment of sending is the tone triangle in the figure: read your
draft once through each corner. Through the **reader**: who receives this, and in what
mood, and what will they fill in where my tone is missing? Through the **purpose**: what
do I want to happen after they read it - and does this draft make that more or less
likely? Through the **context**: is this the right channel, the right hour, the right
point in the exchange? A draft that survives all three corners is usually fit to send.

A caution against the misconception that netiquette is "just politeness". Politeness
smooths; netiquette also protects. The rule about not forwarding a pupil's or colleague's
message outside its intended circle is Unit 4's data-protection duty in miniature. The
rule about checking before forwarding a claim is Unit 6's misinformation defence. The
rule about school hours is a boundary that protects the teacher as much as the parent.
Etiquette, done seriously, is applied ethics.

## Activity: The message clinic

Work in pairs. About 15 minutes.

1. Your teacher gives each pair three messages: the 11 p.m. capitals message from the
   opening story, a curt two-line reply to a parent's careful paragraph, and a class-chat
   message mocking a pupil's wrong answer.
2. For each message, diagnose the failures using the netiquette rules: what exactly went
   wrong, rule by rule?
3. Rewrite each message so it would achieve the same legitimate purpose (the parent
   wanted the mark updated; the teacher was busy; the pupil was wrong) without the
   damage.
4. Swap rewrites with another pair and check them against the tone triangle: reader,
   purpose, context.

## Check your understanding

1. Define netiquette in one sentence and state why writing needs it more than speech
   does. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why a message in capitals reads as shouting, even though capitals carry no
   sound. *(Understand)*
3. A colleague sends you an angry message about a shared duty. Using the before-you-send
   rules, describe your next three actions. *(Apply)*
4. Name the three corners of the tone triangle and one question each corner asks. *(Remember)*

## Summary

Netiquette is the etiquette of written digital communication, needed because text
carries no tone and readers supply what is missing from their own mood. Its rules group
into before you send (identify, state, check the recipient, do not send heat), while you
write (caps, punctuation, matter-not-person, match the channel) and after you send
(permanent and forwardable, screenshot included). The tone triangle - reader, purpose,
context - is the check at the moment of sending.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can name the netiquette rules for email, chat and forums.
- [ ] I can diagnose what went wrong in a careless message, rule by rule.
- [ ] I can rewrite a careless message to achieve its purpose without the damage.

## Try this at your practicum school

Look at one real message thread from the school's official communication (with
permission), or recall one you have seen. Write three sentences: the tone failures it
contained, the rule each failure broke, and how the exchange could have ended
differently.

## Summative task

Prepare a "netiquette card" for the staff room: one side for email to parents, one side
for class groups, each side with five rules in imperative form and one worked example
(before and after) of a message rewritten by the rules.

**Marking guide (10 marks):**

- Ten rules, each imperative, workable and distinct: 4 marks.
- The worked example's "before" has real failures and the "after" repairs them by the
  named rules (Analyse): 4 marks.
- Fits a card, plain language: 2 marks.
