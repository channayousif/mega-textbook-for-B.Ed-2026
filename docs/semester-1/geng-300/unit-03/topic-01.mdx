# Topic 3.1 - Principles and structure of effective communication

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U3-1" src="/img/figures/geng-300/unit-03/fig-U3-1.svg" alt="Concept map showing six principles of effective communication at the centre: clarity, coherence, conciseness, correctness, courtesy and consideration, with branches showing what each principle demands of the writer or speaker." width={780} height={470} />

Mr Tariq teaches Class 10 in a government school in Hyderabad, Sindh. He writes two versions of
the same message to parents about a school event. The first version runs to half a page, uses
long formal words, and buries the date and time in the third paragraph. The second version is
three short paragraphs: the purpose, the key details (date, time, venue), and what parents need to
do. Parents respond faster to the second version. This topic is about why the second version works
better, and how you can produce it reliably.

## Explanation

Effective communication is not about using impressive words. It is about getting your message across
with the least effort for your reader or listener. This topic covers six principles that make
communication effective and then shows how to apply them when you structure a document.

### The principles of effective communication

Communication scholars describe effective communication through a set of principles often called
the six Cs (Verma & Raman). They apply to both spoken and written English.

**Clarity** means your message is easy to understand. A clear message uses simple, direct language
and avoids ambiguity. "The meeting is at 3 p.m. on Friday" is clear. "The meeting is at some point
towards the end of the week" is not.

**Coherence** means your ideas are logically connected. Each sentence follows from the previous one,
and the reader can see the relationship between your points. Signalling words such as "first",
"however" and "therefore" help the reader follow your thinking.

**Conciseness** means saying what you need to say without unnecessary words. Conciseness is not
about writing as little as possible; it is about making every word earn its place. "Due to the fact
that" can usually be replaced with "because".

**Correctness** means your grammar, spelling, punctuation and facts are accurate. Errors in
correctness distract the reader and reduce your credibility. In a formal email, a subject-verb
agreement error or a wrong date can undermine the whole message.

**Courtesy** means respecting your reader or listener. Courteous communication is polite, considers
the other person's perspective, and avoids language that sounds demanding or dismissive. In Pakistani
professional writing, courtesy often matters as much as clarity.

**Consideration** means tailoring your message to your audience. A message to a colleague can be
shorter and less formal than a message to a headteacher. Consideration asks: what does this person
already know, and what do they need from me?

### Structuring documents

<Figure id="fig-U3-5" src="/img/figures/geng-300/unit-03/fig-U3-5.svg" alt="Table comparing document-structure templates for business emails, short reports and formal letters, each with the recommended sections and formatting notes." width={780} height={470} />

Even a clear, concise message needs a structure the reader can follow. The most widely used
structure has three parts: introduction, body and conclusion (Verma & Raman). The figure
above maps this structure for three common document types.

**Introduction.** State your purpose early. In an email, this is usually the first sentence: "I am
writing to request ..." or "This email confirms ...". In a report, the introduction states what the
report covers and why. Do not make the reader guess why you are writing.

**Body.** Present your main points, one at a time. In a short email, the body may be a single
paragraph with the key details. In a report, each section of the body covers one aspect of the
topic. Use headings in longer documents so the reader can find what they need.

**Conclusion.** Tell the reader what happens next. This may be a deadline ("Please reply by
Friday"), a call to action ("I would welcome a meeting to discuss this further"), or a brief summary
of the main point. End politely.

The figure above compares the structure of a business email, a short report and a formal letter.
Notice that all three follow the same introduction-body-conclusion pattern; the differences are in
tone, length and formatting.

**Common misconception.** "More words means better communication." It does not. A long, wordy
message forces the reader to work harder to find the point. Effective communication respects the
reader's time. Conciseness and clarity matter more than length.

## Activity: Rewrite for the six Cs

Work in pairs. Take the following short message from a teacher to a headteacher:

> I am writing this email to you because I wanted to bring to your kind attention that there is a
> problem with the water supply in the school and it has been like this for some time now and I think
> something should be done about it as soon as possible because the children are facing difficulty.

Rewrite the message so it is clear, concise, coherent, correct, courteous and considerate. Use the
three-part structure (introduction, body, conclusion). Allow 20 minutes. Then compare your version
with another pair and explain which of the six Cs you improved most.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the six principles of effective communication (the six Cs) and state what each one demands
   of the writer or speaker. *(Remember)*
2. A pupil writes, "I wanted to inform you that the thing is that the event has been postponed."
   Identify which of the six Cs is weakest in this sentence and rewrite it. *(Apply)*
3. Explain the difference between courtesy and consideration, and give one example of each.
   *(Understand)*
4. A friend says, "A good email should be as long as possible so nothing is missed." Using this
   topic's account, explain what that claim overlooks. *(Analyze)*
5. A report has no headings and no clear introduction. Identify the structural problem and explain
   how the three-part structure would improve it. *(Analyze)*

## Summary

The six principles of effective communication are clarity, coherence, conciseness, correctness,
courtesy and consideration. They apply to both spoken and written English. Every document, from a
short email to a formal report, benefits from a three-part structure: introduction (state the
purpose), body (present the points) and conclusion (say what happens next). Effective
communication is not about using more words; it is about making every word earn its place.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can name the six principles of effective communication and explain what each one demands.
- [ ] I can apply the six Cs to evaluate a described message and suggest improvements.
- [ ] I can structure an email, report or letter with a clear introduction, body and conclusion.
- [ ] I can explain why "more words" does not mean "better communication".

## Try this at your practicum school

Collect one piece of written communication from the school (a notice, a letter to parents, or a
report). Analyse it against the six Cs: which principles does it follow well, and which need
improvement? Using the three-part structure, rewrite the weakest section. Write half a page on
what you changed and why.

Do not name the school or any person in your write-up.

## Summative task

Write 350 to 400 words on the following.

> A colleague sends the following email to a headteacher: "I am writing to you because I wanted to
> let you know that there are some issues with the classroom furniture in my room and I think it
> would be good if something could be done about it because the pupils are not comfortable and it is
> affecting their learning." Using this topic's account of the six Cs and the three-part structure,
> rewrite the email so it is clear, concise, correct and courteous. Then explain three specific
> changes you made and which principle each change serves.
