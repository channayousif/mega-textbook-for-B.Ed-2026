# Topic 2.2 - Strategies for reading and listening

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U2-2" src="/img/figures/geng-300/unit-02/fig-U2-2.svg" alt="Flowchart showing the SQ4R reading strategy step by step: survey, question, read, recite, review, reflect." width={780} height={470} />

Miss Fatima teaches Class 8 English in a government school in Peshawar. She gives her pupils a
three-page textbook chapter and tells them to read it. Some pupils read every word from start to
finish and remember little. Others look at the headings first and do better. This topic is about
why some reading strategies work better than others.

## Explanation

Good readers do not read every text the same way. They adjust their strategy to their purpose.
This topic covers the main reading strategies and the listening strategies that complement them.

### Reading strategies

<Figure id="fig-U2-5" src="/img/figures/geng-300/unit-02/fig-U2-5.svg" alt="Table comparing reading strategies matched to purpose: skimming, scanning, SQ4R and critical reading." width={780} height={470} />

The figure above maps the main reading strategies and when to use each one.

**Skimming** is reading quickly to get the main idea of a text. You read the title, the
headings, the first and last paragraphs, and any bold or italic text. Skimming answers the
question: "What is this text about, and do I need to read it in more detail?" It is the right
strategy when you are deciding whether a text is relevant.

**Scanning** is reading quickly to find specific information. You look for a name, a date, a
number or a keyword. Scanning answers the question: "Does this text contain the information I
need?" It is the right strategy when you know what you are looking for.

**Predicting, questioning, and summarising.** Beyond skimming and scanning, proficient readers
actively interact with a text through three linked strategies before, during, and after reading:
- **Making predictions:** Before reading, effective readers inspect the title, section headings,
  and illustrations to anticipate the main argument or narrative arc. During reading, they generate
  predictions about what evidence or counter-argument will appear next, continually confirming or
  revising their predictions as they encounter new evidence. For example, when encountering the
  subheading "Barriers to active listening", a trainee teacher predicts that physical noise or
  unfamiliar vocabulary will be discussed; reading the section tests and refines that prediction.
- **Questioning:** Strategic readers turn headings into questions before reading and sustain inquiry
  throughout the passage. Asking questions such as "What is the writer's main claim?", "What reason
  supports this point?", and "How does this apply to my classroom?" keeps attention focused and
  monitors comprehension.
- **Summarising:** At regular checkpoints, such as the end of a section or chapter, readers pause
  to restate key ideas in their own words, extracting the essential meaning and setting aside minor
  details.

**Practise making predictions.** Before reading any new text with your learners, model this three-step
predictive routine: (1) preview the title and first sentence, (2) write down two predictions about what
the text will claim, and (3) read the opening two paragraphs to check whether your predictions were
accurate, partially accurate, or need revision.

**SQ4R** is a structured strategy for reading a text in depth that brings these moves together
(illustrated in the flowchart in the classroom situation above):
**S**urvey (skim the text to get an overview and make initial predictions), **Q**uestion (turn each
heading into a question), **R**ead (read actively to answer the question and verify predictions),
**R**ecite (summarise the answer in your own words), **R**eview (go back and check your understanding),
**R**eflect (connect the content to what you already know). SQ4R is the right strategy when you need
to understand and remember a text.

**Critical reading** is reading to evaluate a text, not just understand it. You ask: What is the
writer's purpose? What assumptions are being made? What evidence is provided? What is left out?
Critical reading is the right strategy when you need to assess the quality or reliability of a text.

### Active listening

<Glossary term="Active listening">Active listening</Glossary> is listening with the intention to
understand, not just to hear. It is the aural equivalent of critical reading. The guide lists
several barriers to active listening and strategies for overcoming them.

**Overcoming listening barriers.** Common barriers include distractions (noise, movement,
daydreaming), bias (stopping listening when you disagree), and language difficulty (losing the
thread when the speaker uses unfamiliar vocabulary). Strategies include: sitting where you can hear
clearly, focusing on the speaker rather than your own thoughts, and noting key words rather than
trying to understand every word.

**Focused listening** is listening for a specific purpose. If you are listening for the main idea,
you focus on the introduction and conclusion. If you are listening for detail, you focus on
specific facts and figures. If you are listening for tone, you focus on the speaker's voice and
word choice.

**Note-taking** is recording what you hear in a way that helps you remember and review. Common
note-taking formats include linear notes (headings and bullet points), mind maps (branches radiating
from a central idea), and Cornell notes (a divided page with cues, notes and a summary). Different
formats suit different purposes.

**Common misconception.** "Listening is a passive skill." It is not. Active listening requires
focus, strategy and engagement, just like critical reading.

## Activity: Apply SQ4R to a text

Work alone. Take a three-page English text (a textbook chapter, a newspaper feature, or an
academic article). Apply the six steps of SQ4R: survey, question, read, recite, review, reflect.
Allow 30 minutes. Then compare your notes with a partner and discuss which steps were most useful.

3. Explain the difference between skimming and scanning, and give one example of each.

   why this approach is unreliable and propose three specific strategies. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Reading strategies (skimming, scanning, predicting, SQ4R, critical reading) are tools that good
readers choose according to their purpose. Active listening requires focus, strategy and engagement.
Note-taking formats (linear, mind map, Cornell) help you record and review what you hear.
