# Topic 1.4 - Putting it together: editing for accuracy

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U1-7" src="/img/figures/geng-300/unit-01/fig-U1-7.svg" alt="Table listing common error types and how to correct them: subject-verb agreement, tense shift, fragment, run-on, misplaced modifier, wrong word choice, with a correction strategy for each." width={780} height={470} />

Miss Rabia gives her Class 9 pupils a writing task: write a paragraph about a memorable day. One
pupil hands in a paragraph that is full of good ideas but riddled with errors - agreement problems,
tense shifts, a fragment, a wrong word. Miss Rabia could simply mark every error in red. Instead,
she teaches the pupil to edit. This topic is about that editing process: the skill that turns a
rough draft into a polished piece of writing.

## Explanation

The first three topics of this unit gave you the tools: vocabulary (1.1), grammar (1.2) and
sentence structure (1.3). This topic puts those tools to work in the process that every good
writer uses: editing.

### Sound production and pronunciation

<Figure id="fig-U1-8" src="/img/figures/geng-300/unit-01/fig-U1-8.svg" alt="Flowchart of the editing process: read the text, identify errors, correct errors, re-read aloud to check, then finalise - with arrows showing the cycle repeats until the text is clean." width={780} height={470} />

Clear pronunciation supports clear writing. A pupil who can hear the difference between "ship" and
"sheep", "live" and "leave", "think" and "sink" is a pupil who can spell those words correctly and
use them accurately in writing.

The areas of pronunciation that most affect writing accuracy in Pakistani English are:
- **Consonant clusters**: "asked" (not "ast"), "texts" (not "texs"). Pupils who drop final
  consonants in speech often drop them in writing too.
- **Vowel length**: "ship" /ɪ/ vs. "sheep" /iː/, "full" /ʊ/ vs. "fool" /uː/. Confusing these vowels
  leads to spelling errors.
- **Stress patterns**: "REcord" (noun) vs. "reCORD" (verb), "PREsent" (noun) vs. "preSENT" (verb).
  Stress changes meaning, and a writer who hears stress correctly chooses the correct word.

The guide treats pronunciation as a support for writing, not as a separate skill. A teacher who
integrates brief pronunciation checks into writing lessons helps pupils write more accurately.

### The editing process

The figure above maps the editing process. It is a cycle, not a linear sequence:

1. **Read** the text once for meaning. Does it say what the writer intended?
2. **Identify** errors at three levels: word level (vocabulary, spelling), grammar level
   (agreement, tense, fragments, run-ons, modifiers, articles) and structure level (sentence
   variety, paragraph organisation).
3. **Correct** each error. The figure above maps common error types and their corrections.
4. **Re-read** the corrected text aloud. Reading aloud catches errors that reading silently
   misses, because the ear hears what the eye skips.
5. **Repeat** until the text is clean.

**Common misconception.** "Editing means fixing spelling." It does not. Editing is the complete
process of improving a text at every level - from individual words to the overall structure.
Spelling is only one small part.

### Integrating the foundations

The figure above maps the error types. A skilled editor checks for all three levels:

- **Word level**: Is each word the right word? Is it spelled correctly? Is it the right form
  (noun, verb, adjective)?
- **Grammar level**: Does each sentence have a subject and a verb? Is the tense consistent? Is
  the agreement correct? Are modifiers in the right place?
- **Structure level**: Does the paragraph have a clear order? Is there variety in sentence
  structure? Does one sentence flow naturally into the next?

The figure above maps the error types and their corrections. The key is to check systematically,
one level at a time, rather than trying to catch everything in a single read.

## Activity: Edit a peer's paragraph

Work in pairs. Swap the paragraph you wrote for the Topic 1.3 summative task (or write a new
paragraph of about eight sentences on a topic of your choice). Now edit your partner's paragraph
using the five-step process: read, identify, correct, re-read aloud, repeat. For each change you
make, note the level (word, grammar or structure) and the reason. Allow 25 minutes. Then return
the edited paragraph to your partner and discuss the changes.

## Check your understanding

1. State the three levels a skilled editor checks, and give one example of an error at each level.
   *(Remember)*
2. A pupil writes, "The students was very tired because they have been studying all day." Identify
   the errors at each level and correct them. *(Analyze)*
3. Explain why reading a text aloud is a useful editing strategy. *(Understand)*
4. A friend says, "I can edit my own work as soon as I finish writing it." Using this topic's
   account of the editing process, evaluate that claim. *(Evaluate)*
5. Take the following paragraph and edit it for accuracy (there are at least five errors):
   "Me and my friend goes to the market yesterday. We buys some fruits. The shopkeeper was very
   friendly he gives us a discount. We was happy." *(Apply)*

## Summary

Editing is the process that turns a rough draft into a polished text. It works at three levels -
word, grammar and structure - and it follows a cycle: read, identify, correct, re-read, repeat.
Pronunciation supports writing accuracy because a pupil who hears English correctly writes English
correctly. The foundations of vocabulary, grammar and sentence structure come together in the
editing process.

## Try this at your practicum school

Over two lessons, ask a pupil to write a short paragraph (about six sentences) on a topic of their
choice. Then guide them through the five-step editing process, one level at a time. Write half a
page on which level had the most errors and what it tells you about what to teach next.

Do not name the school or the teacher in your write-up.
