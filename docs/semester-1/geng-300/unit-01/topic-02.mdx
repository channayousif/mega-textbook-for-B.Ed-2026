# Topic 1.2 - Grammar that communicates

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U1-3" src="/img/figures/geng-300/unit-01/fig-U1-3.svg" alt="Diagram showing the subject-agreement pattern in a simple sentence: a subject box linked by an arrow labelled 'must agree' to a verb box, with examples 'The boy runs' and 'The boys run'." width={780} height={470} />

Miss Hina marks her Class 7 pupils' essays. She sees the same errors again and again: "The boys
was playing", "She have finished", "Running down the street." None of these sentences communicates
clearly because the grammar is broken. This topic is about the grammar that makes meaning clear -
and about how to fix the errors that get in the way.

## Explanation

The guide treats grammar not as a set of rules to memorise but as a system for making meaning
clear. A teacher who understands why an error confuses the reader can teach grammar as a tool for
communication, not as a list of dos and don'ts.

### Subject-verb agreement

<Glossary term="Subject-verb agreement">Subject-verb agreement</Glossary> means that the verb
must match its subject in number: singular subjects take singular verbs, plural subjects take
plural verbs. "The boy runs" (singular) but "The boys run" (plural). The figure above maps this
pattern.

The most common agreement errors in Pakistani English classrooms are:
- **Collective nouns treated as plural**: "The team are playing" (should be "is playing" when the
  team acts as one unit).
- **Intervening phrases**: "The box of chocolates are on the table" (should be "is" - the subject
  is "box", not "chocolates").
- **Indefinite pronouns**: "Everyone have their book" (should be "has" - "everyone" is singular).

### Verb tenses

Verb tense tells the reader **when** an action happens. The three basic time frames - past, present,
future - each have simple, continuous, perfect and perfect-continuous forms, giving twelve tenses
in total. For most classroom writing, four matter most:

<Figure id="fig-U1-4" src="/img/figures/geng-300/unit-01/fig-U1-4.svg" alt="Table of the six verb tenses with an example of each: simple present, simple past, present perfect, and simple future." width={780} height={470} />

The figure above maps the tense system. The key rule is **consistency**: do not shift tense within
a paragraph unless the time frame genuinely changes.

**Common misconception.** "Grammar is a set of rules to memorise." It is not. Grammar is a system
for making meaning clear. A rule learned with an example and a reason is a rule that sticks; a rule
learned as a bare statement is a rule that is forgotten as soon as the test is over.

### Fragments, run-ons, modifiers, articles and word classes

- A **fragment** is an incomplete sentence - it is missing a subject, a verb, or both. "Running
  down the street" has no subject and no main verb. Add "He was" and it becomes a sentence.
- A **Run-on** is two complete sentences joined without proper punctuation or a conjunction. "I
  came home I was tired" needs a full stop or a conjunction: "I came home. I was tired" or "I came
  home because I was tired."
- A **modifier** is a word or phrase that describes another word. A **misplaced modifier** creates
  confusion: "She almost drove the car for six hours" (she almost drove, but did not) vs. "She
  drove the car for almost six hours" (she drove for nearly six hours). Position matters.
- **Articles** ("a", "an", "the") signal whether a noun is specific or general. "A book" means any
  book; "the book" means a specific book the reader already knows about. Errors with articles are
  among the most common in Pakistani English because Urdu does not have an exact equivalent.
- **Word classes** (parts of speech) are the categories words fall into: noun, verb, adjective,
  adverb, preposition, conjunction, pronoun, article. Knowing the word class tells you what job a
  word does in a sentence and whether it is in the right place.

## Activity: Error correction workshop

Work in groups of three. Each group receives a short paragraph (about six sentences) containing
exactly five grammatical errors: one agreement error, one tense shift, one fragment, one run-on,
and one misplaced modifier. Identify each error, correct it, and explain why the original was
confusing. Allow 20 minutes. Then swap paragraphs with another group and check each other's work.

## Check your understanding

1. State the rule for subject-verb agreement and give one example of a common error. *(Remember)*
2. A pupil writes, "The news are very bad today." Explain the error and correct it.
   *(Understand)*
3. Identify the tense used in each of the following sentences and state whether the tense is used
   consistently: "I woke up early. I eat breakfast. I went to school." *(Analyze)*
4. A friend says, "Grammar rules are the same in every language." Using this topic's account of
   articles, evaluate that claim. *(Evaluate)*
5. Rewrite the following to fix the fragment and run-on: "Because it was raining. We stayed home
   we watched a film." *(Apply)*

## Summary

Communicative grammar makes meaning clear. Subject-verb agreement, consistent verb tenses,
complete sentences (no fragments or run-ons), correctly placed modifiers, accurate articles and
an understanding of word classes are the tools a writer uses to be understood. Grammar learned as
a system for making meaning is grammar that lasts.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can identify and correct subject-verb agreement errors in described sentences.
- [ ] I can name the four most common verb tenses and use them consistently in a paragraph.
- [ ] I can distinguish a fragment from a run-on and fix both.
- [ ] I can explain why a misplaced modifier confuses the reader and correct it.
- [ ] I can use articles accurately in described sentences.

## Try this at your practicum school

Over two lessons, collect five examples of grammatical errors from pupils' written work (with
permission). For each error, identify the type, write the correct version, and note whether the
error affected the reader's understanding. Write half a page on which error type was most common
and what it tells you about what to teach next.

Do not name the school or the teacher in your write-up.
