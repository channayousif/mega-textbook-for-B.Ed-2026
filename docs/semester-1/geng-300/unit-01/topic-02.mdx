# Topic 1.2 - Building words and sentences

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U1-2" src="/img/figures/geng-300/unit-01/fig-U1-2.svg" alt="Diagram showing the word-formation processes: affixation, compounding, clipping and back formation, each illustrated with examples." width={780} height={470} />

Mr Tariq teaches Class 7 English in a government school in Multan. He writes "unhappiness" on
the board and asks his pupils what it means. One pupil says it means "not happy". Mr Tariq asks
how they knew. The pupil says "un means not, and ness makes it a noun". This topic is about the
processes that build words and the structures that build sentences.

## Explanation

English has over a million words, but most of them are built from a much smaller set of parts.
Understanding how words are formed helps you decode unfamiliar vocabulary. Understanding how
sentences are built helps you write with clarity and variety.

### Word-formation processes

<Figure id="fig-U1-5" src="/img/figures/geng-300/unit-01/fig-U1-5.svg" alt="Table comparing the four sentence structures with examples: simple, compound, complex and compound-complex." width={780} height={470} />

The figure above maps the four main processes this topic describes.

**Affixation** is the addition of a prefix (at the beginning) or a suffix (at the end) of a
word. "Un-happiness" adds the prefix "un-" and the suffix "-ness" to the root "happy". English
has over a hundred common prefixes and suffixes. Learning the most common ones (un-, re-, pre-,
dis-, -ment, -tion, -ly, -ful, -less) gives you access to thousands of words.

**Compounding** is joining two or more words to create a new one. "Blackboard", "textbook",
"notebook" and "keyboard" are all compounds. Some compounds are written as one word, some as
two, and some with a hyphen. The meaning of a compound is usually (but not always) related to
the meanings of its parts.

**Clipping** is the shortening of a word. "Exam" (examination), "lab" (laboratory), "phone"
(telephone) and "maths" (mathematics) are all clippings. They are common in informal English and
are often the form that Pakistani learners encounter first.

**Back formation** is the creation of a new word by removing what looks like an affix but is
not. "Edit" was formed from "editor" (not the other way around), and "televise" from
"television". Back formations are a sign of a living language that is still changing.

### Sentence structures

A sentence is a group of words that expresses a complete thought. English has four main
sentence structures, and the figure above maps them with examples.

A <Glossary term="Simple sentence">simple sentence</Glossary> has one independent clause: a
subject and a verb that express a complete thought. "The pupils read their books." A simple
sentence can be long ("The pupils in Class 7 read their English books quietly during the free
period") but it still has only one clause.

A <Glossary term="Compound sentence">compound sentence</Glossary> has two or more independent
clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (and, but, or, so, yet, for, nor). "The pupils read
their books and the teacher wrote on the board." Each clause could stand alone as a sentence.

A <Glossary term="Complex sentence">complex sentence</Glossary> has one independent clause and
one or more dependent clauses (clauses that cannot stand alone). "When the bell rang, the pupils
packed their bags." "When the bell rang" is a dependent clause; it needs the independent clause
to complete its meaning.

A <Glossary term="Compound-complex sentence">compound-complex sentence</Glossary> has at least
two independent clauses and at least one dependent clause. "When the bell rang, the pupils
packed their bags and the teacher left the room." This is the most common structure in academic
writing.

**Common misconception.** "Longer sentences are better sentences." They are not. The best
sentence is the one that communicates the clearest meaning. A mix of simple, compound and
complex sentences produces the most readable writing.

## Activity: Analyse word formation and sentence structure

Work in groups of three. Take a short English newspaper article (or a textbook paragraph).
First, identify five words formed by affixation, compounding, clipping or back formation, and
explain the process. Second, identify five sentences and classify each as simple, compound,
complex or compound-complex. Allow 25 minutes. Then present and compare with another group.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the four word-formation processes and give one example of each. *(Remember)*
2. A pupil writes, "The teacher gave us a homework about the exam." Identify the clipping in this
   sentence and explain its full form. *(Understand)*
3. Classify the following sentence as simple, compound, complex or compound-complex: "After the
   rain stopped, the children went outside and played in the mud." *(Apply)*
4. A friend says, "If I would have known, I would have come earlier." Identify the error in the
   conditional structure and explain the rule. *(Analyze)*
5. Explain why "edit" is an example of back formation, not affixation. *(Understand)*

## Summary

Word-formation processes (affixation, compounding, clipping, back formation) explain how English
builds new words from existing parts. Sentence structures (simple, compound, complex, compound-
complex) explain how clauses combine to create meaning. Understanding both helps you decode
unfamiliar vocabulary and write with clarity and variety.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can identify the four word-formation processes and give an example of each.
- [ ] I can distinguish simple, compound, complex and compound-complex sentences.
- [ ] I can explain how affixation and compounding create new words.
- [ ] I can classify sentences by their structure.
- [ ] I can identify back formations and explain why they are not affixation.

## Try this at your practicum school

Collect a page of English textbook material used by the class you are observing. Identify ten
words formed by affixation, compounding, clipping or back formation, and ten sentences of
different structures. Write half a page on which structures are most common in the textbook and
what that tells you about the level of the pupils.

## Summative task

Write 300 to 350 words on the following.

> A pupil writes: "The teacher was teaching us about the prefixes and suffixes. She told us that
> we can make new words by adding letters before or after the root word. I think it is very
> helpful for learning vocabulary." Using this topic's account of word-formation processes,
> rewrite this paragraph so that it includes examples of all four processes (affixation,
> compounding, clipping, back formation) and uses at least one example of each sentence structure
> (simple, compound, complex, compound-complex).
