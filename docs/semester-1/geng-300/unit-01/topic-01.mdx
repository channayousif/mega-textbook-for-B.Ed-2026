# Topic 1.1 - Words: vocabulary and how to build it

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U1-1" src="/img/figures/geng-300/unit-01/fig-U1-1.svg" alt="Concept map showing word-formation processes: affixation, compounding, clipping and back formation, each with examples branching from a central 'word formation' node." width={780} height={470} />

Mr Adeel teaches English to Class 6 in a government school in Lahore. He asks his pupils to write
a paragraph about their weekend. One pupil writes, "I went to the park and I was very happy."
Another writes, "I visited the crowded park and felt delighted." Both are correct, but the second
painting is more vivid. This topic is about how words like "delighted" and "crowded" work, and
about how you can deliberately expand the words you have to draw from.

## Explanation

The guide treats vocabulary not as a list to be memorised but as a system to be understood. A
teacher who understands how words are formed can help pupils build their own vocabulary rather
than simply assigning lists.

### Vocabulary building: contextual usage, synonyms, antonyms and idiomatic expressions

<Glossary term="Vocabulary building">Vocabulary building</Glossary> is the deliberate process of
expanding the words you can use accurately. The most effective approach is **contextual usage**:
learning a word by seeing it in a sentence, understanding its nuance, and using it yourself in a
new sentence, rather than memorising a dictionary definition.

**Synonyms** are words with similar meanings. "Happy", "joyful", "delighted" and "pleased" are
synonyms, but they are not interchangeable: "delighted" is stronger than "pleased", and "joyful"
suggests a deeper, more lasting emotion. A good writer chooses the synonym that fits the exact
shade of meaning intended.

**Antonyms** are words with opposite meanings. "Hot" / "cold", "generous" / "stingy",
"ancient" / "modern". Knowing antonyms helps you understand a word more precisely and gives you
tools for contrast in your writing.

**Idiomatic expressions** are phrases whose meaning cannot be worked out from the individual
Words. "Break the ice", "hit the books", "under the weather" - none of these means what the
words literally say. Idioms are one of the hardest parts of English for learners because they
must be learned as whole units. The figure above maps the word-formation processes this topic
describes.

**Common misconception.** "Vocabulary means memorising long word lists." It does not. A word
learned in isolation is a word quickly forgotten. A word learned in context, used in a sentence,
and connected to its synonyms and antonyms is a word that stays.

### How English words are formed

<Figure id="fig-U1-2" src="/img/figures/geng-300/unit-01/fig-U1-2.svg" alt="Table of common prefixes and suffixes with their meanings and examples: un-, re-, pre-, -tion, -ness, -able, -ly." width={780} height={470} />

English words are built from smaller parts. Understanding these parts helps you decode unfamiliar
words and build new ones deliberately.

- **Affixation** - adding a **prefix** (at the beginning) or a **suffix** (at the end) to a root
  word. "Un-happiness" = un- (not) + happy + -ness (state of). The figure above maps common
  prefixes and suffixes.
- **Compounding** - joining two complete words to make a new one. "Sunflower", "notebook",
  "blackboard". Each part contributes to the meaning.
- **Clipping** - shortening a longer word. "Ad" (advertisement), "exam" (examination), "flu"
  (influenza). Common in informal English.
- **Back formation** - creating a new word by removing what looks like an affix. "Edit" came from
  "editor" (not the other way around), "televise" from "television". This is how English grows
  new words over time.

The figure above maps these processes. The point is that English vocabulary is not a random
collection; it is a system, and understanding the system makes you a more confident user of the
language.

## Activity: Build a word family

Work in pairs. Take the root word "comfort". Now build as many related words as you can using
affixation, compounding and back formation. For each word, write a sentence that shows its meaning.
Allow 15 minutes. Then present your word family to another pair and identify one word that
another pair found that you missed.

## Check your understanding

1. State the difference between learning a word through contextual usage and learning it from a
   word list, and give one advantage of each approach. *(Remember)*
2. A pupil writes, "The teacher was very angry and she shouted." Using the idea of synonyms and
   their shades of meaning, propose two alternative versions that convey different degrees of
   anger. *(Apply)*
3. Explain why idiomatic expressions are particularly difficult for learners of English as a
   second language. *(Understand)*
4. A friend says, "I need to learn 50 new words a week to improve my English." Using this topic's
   account, evaluate that claim. *(Evaluate)*
5. Identify the word-formation process in each of the following: "sunflower", "edit", "unhappy",
   "flu". *(Remember)*

## Summary

Vocabulary building is a deliberate process grounded in context. Synonyms, antonyms and idiomatic
expressions add precision and range to your English. Word formation - affixation, compounding,
clipping and back formation - is the system that connects words to each other and lets you decode
unfamiliar vocabulary.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can explain why contextual usage is more effective than memorising word lists.
- [ ] I can distinguish synonyms by their shade of meaning and choose the right one for a described
      context.
- [ ] I can identify and explain at least five idiomatic expressions.
- [ ] I can recognise affixation, compounding, clipping and back formation in described words.
- [ ] I can build a word family from a given root word.

## Try this at your practicum school

Over one week, keep a vocabulary notebook. Each day, write down three new words you encounter in
reading or conversation. For each word, write: the sentence where you found it, one synonym, one
antonym, and your own sentence using the word. At the end of the week, write half a page on which
word you are most likely to remember and why.

Do not name the school or the teacher in your write-up.

## Summative task

Write 300 to 350 words on the following.

> A teacher asks their class to learn 20 new words over the weekend for a test on Monday. Using
> this topic's account of vocabulary building, argue whether this is an effective approach, and
> propose one specific alternative that would help the pupils remember the words longer.
