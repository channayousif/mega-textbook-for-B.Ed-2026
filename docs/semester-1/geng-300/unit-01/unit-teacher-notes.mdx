# Unit 1 teacher notes

## Sequencing note

The four topics follow the logic of a single piece of writing. Topic 1.1 asks what words to use;
Topic 1.2 asks how to arrange them grammatically; Topic 1.3 asks how to build larger structures;
Topic 1.4 asks how to check and improve what has been written.

A useful session order is to teach the topics in numerical order across the five weeks the guide
allows (Weeks 1 to 5), using the first session of the unit to diagnose trainees' existing
vocabulary and grammar levels and the last to rehearse the integrative assessment. The unit is
designed so that a tutor who needs to compress it can merge the activity time of Topics 1.1 and
1.2 into a single workshop without losing the conceptual spine.

## Common misconceptions to probe

These are the misconceptions the unit names explicitly. Surface them early and return to them;
trainees often assent to the correct version in a discussion and then revert when they write.

1. **"Grammar is a set of rules to memorise."** This is the most persistent one. Probe it by
   asking: "If you know the rule but still make the error in your writing, do you truly know it?"
   Trainees who equate knowing a rule with knowing grammar will find Topic 1.2's communicative
   approach counter-intuitive at first.

2. **"Vocabulary means memorising long word lists."** Probe it by asking: "If you memorise a word
   today and forget it next week, did you learn it?" Trainees who have been taught through
   list-memorisation will need time to trust the contextual approach.

3. **"A long sentence is a good sentence."** This one belongs to Topic 1.3. Probe it by asking:
   "Which is better: one clear simple sentence or one confusing compound-complex one?" The answer
   reveals that length is not the goal.

4. **"Pronunciation does not matter in written English."** Probe it by asking: "If you cannot hear
   the difference between 'ship' and 'sheep', can you spell the difference?" Trainees who separate
   speaking from writing are surprised by the connection.

## Managing the activities

**Topic 1.1 - Build a word family (15 minutes, pairs).** The key teaching move is the sharing:
ask each pair to present one word they built that another pair did not. This makes the learning
visible and shows trainees that word formation is generative, not fixed.

**Topic 1.2 - Error correction workshop (20 minutes, groups of three).** The trap is that trainees
correct only the most obvious errors and miss the subtler ones. Circulate and ask: "Are you sure
that is the only error in that sentence?" Push them to check all three levels.

**Topic 1.3 - Sentence transformation (15 minutes, pairs).** The key teaching move is the
comparison: ask two pairs to read their versions aloud and discuss which flows best and why.
This makes the point that there is no single correct answer, only more or less effective ones.

**Topic 1.4 - Edit a peer's paragraph (25 minutes, pairs).** This is the most important activity
in the unit. The trap is that trainees correct surface errors (spelling, punctuation) and miss
deeper ones (tense, agreement, structure). Remind them of the three levels and the five-step
process. Circulate and ask: "Have you checked for agreement? Have you checked for variety?"

## Practical work and practicum links

Each topic carries a "Try this at your practicum school" task. The unit works best when the tutor
sets these up explicitly before trainees leave for practicum and debriefs them when trainees
return.

- **Topic 1.1 practicum task** (a vocabulary notebook over one week) produces a collection of
  contextualised words. Use it to open Topic 1.2: ask trainees which words they are most likely to
  remember and why, and use their examples to motivate the grammar topics.

- **Topic 1.2 practicum task** (collecting five errors from pupils' work) produces a small data
  set. Use it to make the point that certain errors recur, and that teaching grammar as a
  meaning-making system is more effective than re-teaching rules.

- **Topic 1.3 practicum task** (analysing a paragraph for sentence variety) produces a concrete
  example. Use it to make the point that variety is a choice, not an accident.

- **Topic 1.4 practicum task** (guiding a pupil through the editing process) produces the
  evidence trainees need for ERQ 5. If the practicum school has no suitable pupil, ask trainees
  to edit a partner's paragraph using the five-step process and reflect on what they found.

## Assessment note

The 10/10/5 bank is designed to be used in two passes: the MCQs and RRQs as formative checks at
the end of Topics 1.2 and 1.4, and the ERQs as a summative task at the end of the unit. ERQ 5 (the
integrative item) is the one that most reliably separates trainees who have understood the unit
from those who have memorised its definitions. If you use only one ERQ, use that one.
