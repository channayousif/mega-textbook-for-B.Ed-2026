# Topic 1.3 - Building words, building sentences

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U1-5" src="/img/figures/geng-300/unit-01/fig-U1-5.svg" alt="Flowchart showing how to combine simple sentences into compound and complex ones: two simple sentences joined by a coordinating conjunction (and, but, or) become a compound sentence; a simple sentence with a subordinating conjunction (because, although, when) becomes a complex sentence." width={780} height={470} />

Mr Tariq asks his Class 8 pupils to describe a festival. One pupil writes only simple sentences:
"I went to the park. I saw my friends. We played cricket. We came home." The facts are clear, but
the writing is flat. Another pupil writes: "When I went to the park, I saw my friends, and we played
cricket until it was time to come home." Same facts, but the second version flows. This topic is
about why - and about how word formation and sentence structure work together to make writing
alive.

## Explanation

Topics 1.1 and 1.2 looked at words and grammar. This topic puts them together: how words are formed
and how sentences are structured. A writer who controls both can produce writing that is accurate
and varied.

### Word formation: affixation, compounding, clipping, back formation

Topic 1.1 introduced the four word-formation processes. This topic applies them.

<Figure id="fig-U1-6" src="/img/figures/geng-300/unit-01/fig-U1-6.svg" alt="Diagram showing a tree diagram of a complex sentence: 'When the rain stopped, we went outside because we wanted to play' broken into subordinate clause, main clause and reason clause." width={780} height={470} />

- **Affixation** is the most productive process in English. The prefix "un-" reverses meaning
  (happy → unhappy); the suffix "-tion" turns a verb into a noun (educate → education); the suffix
  "-able" means "can be done" (read → readable). A pupil who knows 20 common prefixes and 20
  common suffixes can decode or build thousands of words.
- **Compounding** creates new meanings from old words. "Sun" + "flower" = sunflower (a flower that
  faces the sun); "note" + "book" = notebook (a book for notes). The meaning of the compound is
  often more than the sum of its parts.
- **Clipping** and **back formation** are less common in formal writing but everywhere in everyday
  English. Knowing them helps pupils understand informal texts and choose the right register.

### Sentence structure: simple, compound, complex and compound-complex

The four sentence structures are the building blocks of all English writing.

| Structure | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | One independent clause | "The teacher explained the lesson." |
| Compound | Two independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (and, but, or, so) | "The teacher explained the lesson, and the pupils took notes." |
| Complex | One independent clause + one or more dependent clauses (introduced by because, although, when, if, etc.) | "When the teacher explained the lesson, the pupils took notes." |
| Compound-complex | Two independent clauses + one or more dependent clauses | "When the teacher explained the lesson, the pupils took notes, and they asked questions." |

The figure above maps these structures. The key insight is that **variety matters**. A paragraph of
only simple sentences feels choppy; a paragraph of only complex sentences feels heavy. Good writing
mixes the four structures to match the pace and emphasis the writer wants.

**Common misconception.** "A long sentence is a good sentence." It is not. A good sentence is one
that says clearly what the writer means. Sometimes that is a simple sentence; sometimes it is a
compound-complex one. The skill is in choosing the right structure for the job.

## Activity: Sentence transformation

Work in pairs. Take the following four simple sentences and combine them into one compound-complex
sentence that flows naturally. You may add or remove words, but keep all four facts.

> The school bell rang. The pupils packed their bags. They walked to the gate. Their parents were
> waiting.

Allow 15 minutes. Then share with another pair and compare your versions. Discuss: which version
flows best, and why?

## Check your understanding

1. Name the four word-formation processes and give one example of each. *(Remember)*
2. State the difference between a compound sentence and a complex sentence, and give one example of
   each. *(Remember)*
3. A pupil writes an entire essay using only simple sentences. Using this topic's account, explain
   what the essay loses and how you would advise the pupil. *(Analyze)*
4. Identify the sentence structure (simple, compound, complex, compound-complex) in each of the
   following: "Although it was hot, we went outside and we played until sunset." *(Analyze)*
5. Build a new word using affixation from each of the following roots: "create", "happy", "write".
   *(Apply)*

## Summary

Word formation and sentence structure are the building blocks of English writing. Affixation,
compounding, clipping and back formation are how words grow; simple, compound, complex and
compound-complex sentences are how those words are arranged. A writer who controls both can produce
writing that is accurate, varied and clear.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can identify and produce examples of all four word-formation processes.
- [ ] I can distinguish the four sentence structures and explain when each is effective.
- [ ] I can combine simple sentences into compound and complex ones.
- [ ] I can analyse a described paragraph for variety of sentence structure.
- [ ] I can edit a described paragraph to improve its variety of sentence structure.

## Try this at your practicum school

Over two lessons, collect one paragraph of pupils' written work. Analyse it for sentence structure:
how many simple, compound, complex and compound-complex sentences does it contain? Write half a
page on whether the variety is effective and what you would suggest to improve it.

Do not name the school or the teacher in your write-up.

## Summative task

Write 350 to 400 words on the following.

> A teacher says, "I tell my pupils to write longer sentences, but their writing just becomes
> confusing." Using this topic's account of sentence structure, explain why "longer" is not the
> right goal, and propose one specific alternative that would help pupils improve their writing.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong response does |
|---|---|
| Grasp of the four sentence structures (Understand) | Names and distinguishes the four types with examples |
| Use of variety as a concept (Apply) | Uses the concept of variety to support the argument |
| **Argument for the alternative (Analyze)** | **Argues for one alternative using a specific feature of sentence structure, not a generic writing tip** |
| Register and clarity | Writes for a colleague, defines any technical term used, stays within the word count |
