# Topic 6.1 - Financial literacy: profit, loss, interest and budgeting

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U6-1" src="/img/figures/gqur-300/unit-06/fig-U6-1.svg" alt="Flowchart of building a monthly budget: income, fixed costs, savings, variable costs, review, looping to next month's plan." />

The Class 8 stall at the school fair sells 120 plates of biryani at 60 rupees each. The
ingredients cost 4,800 rupees, the stall fee was 500, and a borrowed canopy cost 300 to
hire. The stall's takings were 7,200 rupees, and the pupils are thrilled - "we made
7,200!" Their teacher, who has this topic, writes four lines on the board: takings
7,200; costs 4,800 + 500 + 300 = 5,600; <Glossary term="Profit">profit</Glossary> =
7,200 - 5,600 = 1,600 rupees; and profit as a share of costs, 1,600 / 5,600 = about
28.6 percent. Same event, two different numbers - and only the second pair is a
financial statement. Financial literacy is not a new branch of mathematics; it is the
arithmetic of Units 2 and 3 pointed at money, where the difference between takings and
profit is the difference between a feeling and a fact.

## Explanation

### Financial literacy: profit and loss

<Glossary term="Loss">Loss</Glossary> is profit's negative twin: costs above takings.
Both are always computed against costs when a percentage is wanted:

- profit percent = (profit / total costs) x 100
- loss percent = (loss / total costs) x 100

**Worked example.** If a rainstorm halves the fair's customers, takings fall to 3,600
rupees against the same 5,600 of costs: loss = 2,000 rupees, and loss percent =
2,000 / 5,600 = about 35.7 percent. The percentage is what lets one stall compare
itself with another of a different size - a 2,000-rupee loss on a small stall is a
disaster, on a large one a shrug. Steen (2001) built the case for quantitative
literacy on ground like this: a person who cannot check the arithmetic of their own
livelihood must simply take the quoted number on trust.

### Financial literacy: interest

<Figure id="fig-U6-2" src="/img/figures/gqur-300/unit-06/fig-U6-2.svg" alt="Table of four financial terms with what each measures and a worked rupee example." />

<Glossary term="Interest">Interest</Glossary> is the price of money over time: the
saver's reward or the borrower's cost. <Glossary term="Simple interest">Simple
interest</Glossary> pays a fixed percentage of the original amount each year:

simple interest = principal x rate x time

**Worked example.** A teacher saves 10,000 rupees at 5 percent simple interest per
year. After 3 years: interest = 10,000 x 0.05 x 3 = 1,500 rupees; the account holds
11,500. After 6 years: 3,000 rupees of interest. The interest grows in a straight
line - which is exactly what "simple" means, and the pattern-and-sequence eye from
Unit 3 sees it at once.

Borrowing flips the sign. A 20,000-rupee loan at the same 5 percent simple interest
costs 1,000 rupees a year; the interest the saver earns and the interest the borrower
pays are the same formula wearing opposite signs. Shops and banks rarely advertise the
formula; they advertise the monthly instalment, and the instalment hides the rate. The
asymmetry is the point: one side has done the arithmetic, the other has not - and
Steen's (2001) case for quantitative literacy is written for citizens on exactly that
side of the bargain. A
trainee who can rebuild the rate from the instalment is a trainee an advertisement
cannot easily fool - which is Topic 6.2's bridge.

### Financial literacy: budgeting

A <Glossary term="Budget">budget</Glossary> is a plan for money before it arrives, not
a record after it leaves. The flowchart above shows its five parts. The order matters:
**savings come out before variable spending, not after**, because what is left after
spending is usually nothing.

**Worked example.** A trainee's monthly stipend is 25,000 rupees. Fixed costs (room
rent, transport pass, a loan instalment): 12,000. Savings target: 3,000. Variable
costs (food, phone, extras): the remaining 10,000. The budget is not the arithmetic
alone - it is the discipline of checking at month end: did variable spending really
stay at 10,000? A budget without the review step is a wish. The same discipline scales
beyond the stipend: a school council planning an enrolment drive budgets against
counted need, not felt need - Sindh's out-of-school rate for ages 5 to 16 stands at
46.29 percent (PBS, 2023), and a budget line aimed at that number is arithmetic in
the service of a plan.

**A common misconception to correct.** Trainees often believe "profit is whatever
money comes in". The fair stall exists for this: takings of 7,200 felt like profit
until the 5,600 of costs appeared. The second misconception follows the first: that a
budget restricts. A budget is a decision made once, calmly, instead of thirty small
decisions made under hunger and hurry - it is freedom with a plan, not a cage.

## Activity: The fair stall audit

Work in groups of four. Your tutor gives each group a mock fair-stall ledger (takings,
ingredient costs, stall fee, one hire cost, and two days of records). Each group
computes profit or loss for each day and for the fair overall, then the percentage,
then writes a two-sentence recommendation for next year's stall: keep, change the
price, or change the quantity - with the computation that supports it. About 25
minutes.

## Check your understanding

1. Compute the profit and profit percent: takings 9,000 rupees, costs 7,500 rupees.
   *(Apply)*
2. Compute the loss and loss percent: takings 4,000 rupees, costs 5,600 rupees. *(Apply)*
3. Compute the simple interest on 15,000 rupees at 4 percent for 5 years, and the
   total in the account. *(Apply)*
4. A stipend of 25,000 rupees has fixed costs of 12,000 and a savings target of 3,000.
   State the variable-costs allowance, and why savings are subtracted before variable
   costs. *(Understand)*
5. A friend says the fair stall "made 7,200 rupees". Correct the statement in two
   sentences using the cost and percentage ideas. *(Analyze)*

## Summary

Profit is takings minus total costs, and its percentage is always against costs; loss
is the same subtraction with the sign flipped. Simple interest is a fixed percent of
the original amount per year - principal x rate x time - growing in a straight line
for savers and borrowers alike. A budget plans income, fixed costs, variable costs and
savings (in that order, savings before spending), and its review step turns a wish
into a plan.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can compute profit, loss and their percentages from a ledger.
- [ ] I can compute simple interest for saving and borrowing, and rebuild a rate from
      an instalment.
- [ ] I can build a five-part monthly budget with savings before variable spending,
      and say why the review step matters.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask the clerk (or canteen manager) for one week's canteen takings and costs (anonymised
if needed). Compute the week's profit or loss and its percentage, and write one
sentence on what the number means for next week's stock order.

## Summative task

A school cooperative wants to lend 50,000 rupees to buy a photocopy machine that earns
3,000 rupees a month for the school. A bank offers the same 50,000 at 6 percent simple
interest per year. In a short written answer: (a) compute the school's yearly earnings
from the machine and the yearly interest the bank would charge, (b) state whether the
machine pays for its loan, and by how much per year, and (c) identify one cost the
comparison ignores (paper, repairs, the machine's life) and show how a rough estimate
of it could change the conclusion.

**Mini-rubric** (each criterion 0-2 marks):

- *Yearly figures*: machine 3,000 x 12 = 36,000 rupees; interest 50,000 x 0.06 x 1 =
  3,000 rupees.
- *Comparison*: the machine earns 33,000 rupees more than the loan costs.
- *Ignored cost analysed*: a real cost named with a rough estimate (for example, paper
  at even 5,000 a year still leaves the machine ahead; a machine lasting only two
  years changes the picture) and its effect on the conclusion shown. **(Analyze)**
