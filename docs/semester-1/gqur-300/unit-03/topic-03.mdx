# Topic 3.3 - Using algebra in problem solving

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U3-5" src="/img/figures/gqur-300/unit-03/fig-U3-5.svg" alt="Concept map of algebra in problem solving with a central node and four panels: translate, solve, interpret, and decide, linked back to the four-step strategy." />

Two tailors in the same bazaar post different rate cards. Tailor A: "500 rupees per suit,
plus 200 for buttons, however many suits." Tailor B: "600 rupees per suit, buttons free."
A trainee, Amjad, needs one suit with buttons and cannot decide. His friend laughs: "Just
try both - A is 700, B is 600, take B." But Amjad's teacher asks a better question: "What
if the school orders suits for the whole cricket team?" With one suit B wins, but the two
situations follow two rules: A costs 500n + 200 and B costs 600n, where n is the number of
suits. Setting the rules side by side - which is all algebra is doing here - shows that at
n = 2 they tie at 1,200 rupees, and from n = 3 onward A is cheaper, because A grows by 500
per suit while B grows by 600. One comparison of two expressions settled a whole family
of questions at once.

## Explanation

### Use of algebra in problem solving

<Figure id="fig-U3-6" src="/img/figures/gqur-300/unit-03/fig-U3-6.svg" alt="Table translating five everyday phrase patterns into algebra, each with a fare or budget example." />

Algebra earns its place in problem solving by **compressing a whole family of situations
into one rule**. The method is a loop, shown in the concept map above: translate the
situation into an expression or equation, solve with the balance method from Topic 3.2,
interpret the result back into the situation, and decide. It is the four-step strategy of
Unit 1 with algebra as the plan step's sharpest tool.

The table above collects the five translation patterns that cover most daily problems.
Study the right-hand column: each everyday phrase has exactly one algebraic shape.

**Worked example.** A tutor charges 400 rupees per session plus 250 for materials. A
family paid 2,650 rupees in total. How many sessions did they take?

1. **Translate**: cost = 250 + 400s, and the cost was 2,650, so 250 + 400s = 2,650.
2. **Solve**: subtract 250 from both sides: 400s = 2,400; divide by 400: s = 6.
3. **Interpret**: s counts sessions, so the family took 6 sessions.
4. **Decide and check**: 250 + 400 x 6 = 250 + 2,400 = 2,650. True.

**A common misconception to correct.** Trainees who fear letters treat algebra as a
subject separate from "real maths". The opposite is true: algebra is what makes daily
arithmetic reusable. The tailor's rate card, the van fare, the tutor's bill and the
stationery budget are all the same equation wearing different clothes. Once you see the
shape, every new instance is already solved. Marecek, Anthony-Smith and Mathis (2020)
build their algebra chapters on exactly this translation skill, from "the language of
algebra" to solving applied problems.

## Activity: Rate card clinic

Work in pairs. Each pair writes two rate cards from real life (van fares, photocopy
charges, canteen prices with a fixed part, mobile packages with a monthly fee plus per-
minute rate). Swap cards with another pair. For each card: write the cost expression,
compute the cost for 3, 10 and 25 units, and state the smallest number of units for which
the card beats a flat rate of 500 rupees. Defend one answer to the class. About 25
minutes.

## Check your understanding

1. Write the cost expression for a photocopy shop that charges 30 rupees to open the
   machine plus 8 rupees per copy, and compute the cost of 25 copies. *(Apply)*
2. Translate into an equation and solve: "A family paid 2,650 rupees to a tutor who
   charges 250 rupees for materials plus 400 per session. How many sessions?" *(Apply)*
3. A van costs 500 rupees plus 30 per kilometre; a rival costs 300 rupees plus 40 per
   kilometre. Write both cost expressions, and find the distance at which they cost the
   same. *(Apply)*
4. In your own words, explain why comparing two expressions answers a whole family of
   questions at once, using the tailor example. *(Understand)*
5. A trainee solves 250 + 400s = 2,650 as s = 2,650 - 250 - 400 = 2,000. Identify the
   error and state the correct first move. *(Analyze)*

## Summary

Algebra compresses a family of situations into one rule: translate the situation into an
expression or equation, solve with the balance method, interpret the result back into the
situation, and decide. Five translation patterns cover most daily problems, and comparing
two expressions settles questions the arithmetic of a single case cannot even ask.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can translate a fare, budget or rate situation into an expression or equation.
- [ ] I can solve the equation and interpret the answer back into the situation, with a
      check.
- [ ] I can compare two pricing rules by comparing their expressions.

## Try this at your practicum school

Collect one real rate card or price rule from the school or its bazaar (photocopy shop,
van, canteen). Write its cost expression, compute the cost for three realistic quantities,
and ask the shopkeeper whether your numbers match their bill. Note any difference and its
cause.

## Summative task

A school event needs printed programmes. Printer A charges 2,000 rupees setup plus 15
rupees per programme; Printer B charges 1,200 rupees setup plus 25 rupees per programme.
The school needs 150 programmes. In a short written answer: (a) write both cost
expressions, (b) compute both totals for 150 programmes and state which is cheaper, and
(c) find the number of programmes at which both printers cost the same, and recommend
which printer the school should choose if the print run might grow or shrink, with a
reason.

**Mini-rubric** (each criterion 0-2 marks):

- *Expressions*: A = 2,000 + 15n and B = 1,200 + 25n (variables named).
- *Totals and comparison*: A costs 4,250 and B costs 4,950 for 150, so A is cheaper by
  700 rupees.
- *Break-even and recommendation*: 2,000 + 15n = 1,200 + 25n gives 800 = 10n, so n = 80;
  the recommendation weighs the break-even against the chance of a smaller run (below 80,
  B is cheaper) with a reason. **(Analyze)**
