# Topic 2.3 - Powers, roots and daily-life applications

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U2-5" src="/img/figures/gqur-300/unit-02/fig-U2-5.svg" alt="Diagram of square numbers 1 to 25 drawn as growing tile grids, with sides 1 to 5 above and areas 1 to 25 below, and paired arrows naming squaring and square root." />

The school in Nawabshah is re-tiling its verandah with square tiles. The mason lays one
tile and says "one". He makes a 2 by 2 square: "four tiles". A 3 by 3 square: "nine". The
headteacher asks: "I have 64 tiles. How wide a square can I lay?" The mason answers at
once: "8 by 8." He has never written 8 squared or the square root of 64, but he owns both
ideas. This topic gives you the notation for what the mason already knows, and then a
wider tool: a flowchart for choosing the right operation when a daily problem does not
tell you which one to use.

## Explanation

### Powers and roots

A <Glossary term="Power">power</Glossary> is repeated multiplication: 8 squared (written
8^2) means 8 x 8 = 64, and 2 cubed (2^3) means 2 x 2 x 2 = 8. The number being multiplied
is the base; the small raised number, the <Glossary term="Exponent">exponent</Glossary>,
counts the multiplications. The square of a number is its second power, and it is
genuinely a square: a square floor 8 tiles on a side holds exactly 8 x 8 = 64 tiles. The
diagram above shows the square numbers growing as tile grids; the "squared" arrow climbs
from side to area, and the <Glossary term="Square root">square root</Glossary> arrow
climbs back down. The square root of 64, written sqrt(64), is 8: the side of the square
that holds 64 tiles.

**Worked example.** A store room has a square floor of 50 square metres. What is the length
of one side, to the nearest metre? The side is sqrt(50). Since 7 x 7 = 49 and 8 x 8 = 64,
sqrt(50) sits between 7 and 8, much closer to 7: about 7.1 metres. Estimation first
(Unit 1), exactness when needed: 7.07 metres if the carpenter must cut a skirting board.

**A common misconception to correct.** Trainees sometimes read 8^2 as 8 x 2 = 16. The
exponent counts multiplications of the base, not a multiplication by itself. The tile
picture is the cure: 8 squared is an 8-by-8 floor, not an 8-by-2 corridor. The same picture
prevents the twin error of thinking squaring doubles: 8 squared is 64, not 16.

### Applications of numbers and operations in daily life

<Figure id="fig-U2-6" src="/img/figures/gqur-300/unit-02/fig-U2-6.svg" alt="Flowchart for choosing the operation in a word problem: read the situation, decide between multiply, divide, or add and subtract using what the total does, then check reasonableness." />

Most daily-life arithmetic fails before any number is touched: the wrong operation is
chosen. The flowchart above is a repair. Its core question is: **what does the total do?**

- If the total **combines equal groups** (20 boxes of 12 chalks each), multiply.
- If the total **breaks into equal groups** (240 pupils into groups of 15), divide.
- If the total **grows or shrinks by a change** (a fee of 2,000 rises by 200), add or
  subtract.

**Worked example.** A school orders transport for 187 pupils; each van carries 26. How many
vans? The total (pupils) breaks into equal groups (vanloads), so divide: 187 divided by 26
is 7 with remainder 5. Look back: 7 vans carry 182 pupils, but 5 pupils remain, so the
real-world answer is 8 vans. The look-back step is not decoration; it changes the answer.

**Choosing units as part of choosing the operation.** Daily problems arrive wearing units:
rupees, metres, pupils per van, rupees per notebook. A rate (rupees per notebook) multiplies
a count (notebooks) to give a total (rupees). Keeping the units attached through the
computation - 80 rupees/notebook x 100 notebooks = 8,000 rupees - makes the operation
choice almost automatic and errors visible.

## Activity: Tile the courtyard

Work in groups of three. Your tutor gives each group a courtyard plan (a rectangle, say
12 metres by 9 metres) and a choice of square tile sizes (0.5 m, 1 m, 1.5 m). For each
tile size, compute how many tiles fill the courtyard and how many full tiles a 64-tile
budget would cover. Then decide which tile size wastes the least cutting, and defend the
choice in two sentences. About 25 minutes.

## Check your understanding

1. Compute: 6 squared, 9 squared, and the square roots of 81 and 100. *(Remember)*
2. A square classroom floor has an area of 36 square metres. What is the length of each
   wall, and how do you know? *(Apply)*
3. Between which two whole numbers does the square root of 40 lie? Justify in one
   sentence. *(Apply)*
4. For each situation, name the operation and the reason: (a) 15 boxes of 40 books each;
   (b) 300 pupils into classes of 30; (c) a 5,600-rupee bill paid with 6,000 rupees.
   *(Apply)*
5. A trainee computes 187 pupils divided by 26-pupil vans as 7 and orders 7 vans. Identify
   the error in one sentence using the look-back idea. *(Analyze)*

## Summary

Powers are repeated multiplication, and squares are genuinely areas: an 8-by-8 floor holds
64 tiles, which is why the square root of 64 is 8. Estimation brackets a root between
neighbouring squares before any exact work. When a daily problem does not name its
operation, ask what the total does: combine equal groups (multiply), break into equal
groups (divide), or change by an amount (add or subtract) - then check the answer for
reasonableness, because the real-world answer may need the remainder you were about to
discard.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can compute squares and square roots of perfect squares, and bracket other roots
      between neighbouring squares.
- [ ] I can name the correct operation for a daily-life problem by asking what the total
      does.
- [ ] I keep units attached through a computation and use them to check the operation.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find one tiled or bricked area (verandah, courtyard, walkway). Estimate its dimensions,
compute its area, and work out how many square tiles of one size would cover it. Ask the
caretaker how many tiles were actually used, and note any difference and its likely cause.

## Summative task

The school wants a square mosaic of 225 small tiles on a corridor wall, and the wall space
is a square of side 20 tiles. In a short written answer: (a) state how wide the mosaic is
(the square root of 225) and whether it fits the wall, (b) the school has only these 225
tiles in stock: compute how many more tiles a full 20-by-20 design would need, and (c)
propose one real reason a school might prefer the smaller mosaic, with a computation that
supports it.

**Mini-rubric** (each criterion 0-2 marks):

- *Root and fit*: the mosaic is 15 by 15 and fits inside 20 by 20, with reasoning shown.
- *Tile count*: a 20-by-20 design uses 400 tiles, so 175 more tiles would be needed; the
  comparison with 225 is computed correctly.
- *Supported proposal*: the reason is argued with a computation (for example, cost per tile
  or leftover tiles for repairs), not merely asserted. **(Analyze)**
