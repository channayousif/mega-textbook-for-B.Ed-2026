# Topic 2.2 - Ratios, proportions and percentages

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U2-3" src="/img/figures/gqur-300/unit-02/fig-U2-3.svg" alt="Triangle diagram linking ratio, proportion and percentage at its corners, each with a one-line example, showing they are one connected idea." />

The school function is tomorrow, and the cook must scale up her tea recipe. For 20 people
she uses 6 spoons of sugar to 4 spoons of tea leaves. The headteacher says 60 people are
coming. "Then triple everything," says the cook instantly: 18 spoons of sugar, 12 spoons of
tea. She has never studied algebra, but she has flawless proportional reasoning: she knows
that 18 to 12 is the same ratio as 6 to 4, because both simplify to 3 to 2. This topic
takes the reasoning the cook already owns, names it, and connects it to the percentage
table that will follow, so you can use it deliberately and teach it explicitly.

## Explanation

### Ratios and proportions

A <Glossary term="Ratio">ratio</Glossary> compares two quantities of the same kind: 6
spoons of sugar to 4 spoons of tea leaves, 40 girls to 20 boys in a school. A
<Glossary term="Proportion">proportion</Glossary> is a statement that two ratios are equal:
6 to 4 equals 18 to 12, because both are 3 to 2. The cross-products check makes a
proportion verifiable without trusting the eye: 6 x 12 = 72 and 4 x 18 = 72, so the
proportion holds.

**Worked example (scaling up).** The recipe serves 20 people with 6 spoons of sugar. For 60
people: the crowd is 3 times as large, so every ingredient is 3 times as large: 18 spoons.
For 50 people: the crowd is 2.5 times as large, so 15 spoons. The reasoning move is always
the same: find the multiplier between the situations and apply it to every quantity.

**Worked example (finding the multiplier).** A school has 40 girls and 20 boys. The ratio
of girls to boys is 40 to 20, which simplifies to 2 to 1: two girls for every boy. If the
next school year admits 30 more pupils in the same ratio, expect 20 girls and 10 boys.

**A common misconception to correct.** Trainees often add instead of multiplying when
scaling: "30 more people, so add one more batch of 6 spoons of sugar and 4 of tea." Adding
one original batch happens to feed exactly 20 more people, so the rule works only when the
extra crowd is exactly 20. The 50-person function needs 2.5 times the recipe - 15 spoons of
sugar and 10 of tea - while the friend's addition cooks for only 40, and ten guests go
hungry. The test is to ask what must stay the same: the amount per person, so multiply
every ingredient by the crowd multiplier, never add a fixed batch.

### Percentages

<Figure id="fig-U2-4" src="/img/figures/gqur-300/unit-02/fig-U2-4.svg" alt="Table of four percentage situations with the question each asks and a worked rupee example for each." />

A <Glossary term="Percentage">percentage</Glossary> is a ratio expressed out of 100:
"per cent" literally means "by the hundred". Percentages matter because so many daily
quantities are quoted in them: fee rises, discounts, exam marks, battery levels, tax. The
table above sorts the four situations you will meet, each with a rupee example. The fourth
row deserves special care: finding the original after a rise is the classic trap, because
the rise acts on the original, not on the final figure.

**Worked example (marks).** A pupil scores 36 out of 45 in a math test. As a percentage:
36 divided by 45 is 0.8, which is 80 percent. The reasoning: the fraction 36/45 tells what
share of the marks was earned; multiplying by 100 renames that share per hundred.

**Worked example (discount).** A bookshop offers 25 percent off a 640-rupee atlas. The
discount is 25/100 x 640 = 160 rupees, so the sale price is 640 - 160 = 480 rupees. A
faster route: paying 75 percent of the price is 0.75 x 640 = 480; both routes must agree,
and checking that they do is the look-back habit from Unit 1.

## Activity: The marksheet percentage relay

Work in groups of four. Your tutor gives each group five anonymised test results in
different totals (for example 36/45, 42/60, 55/75, 18/25, 27/30). Each member converts two
results to percentages by hand (no calculator), then the group ranks all five pupils from
best to worst percentage. The debrief question: which raw scores looked biggest but were
not best, and why the total matters. About 20 minutes.

## Check your understanding

1. Write the ratio 40 girls to 20 boys in its simplest form, and state what it means in one
   sentence. *(Understand)*
2. A recipe for 12 people uses 4 eggs. How many eggs for 30 people? Show the multiplier you
   used. *(Apply)*
3. Compute 15 percent of a 2,000-rupee fee, and state the new fee after a 15 percent rise.
   *(Apply)*
4. After a 10 percent rise, a school's monthly electricity bill is 17,600 rupees. What was
   the bill before the rise? Show your reasoning. *(Apply)*
5. A friend scales a recipe by adding: "30 more people, so add one more batch of 6 spoons
   of sugar and 4 of tea." Explain in two sentences why this leaves the function short and
   what to do instead. *(Analyze)*

## Summary

Ratio compares, proportion states that two ratios are equal, and percentage is a ratio out
of 100; they are one idea in three notations. Scaling multiplies every ingredient by the
same multiplier - it never adds. The four percentage situations (of, increase, decrease,
reverse) each need their own setup, and the reverse case is the classic trap because the
rise acts on the original.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can simplify a ratio and explain what it means in one sentence.
- [ ] I can scale a recipe or a plan by finding and applying the multiplier.
- [ ] I can handle all four percentage situations with rupee examples, including the
      reverse case.

## Try this at your practicum school

Take one class's latest test (anonymised, with permission). Convert any five pupils' raw
scores to percentages. Then ask the teacher one question: does the school report raw scores
or percentages to parents, and why? Note the answer with one example.

## Summative task

A stationery supplier offers two deals on notebooks costing 80 rupees each: Deal A gives
"20 percent off", Deal B gives "5 notebooks free with every 20 bought". The school needs
100 notebooks. In a short written answer: (a) compute the total cost under each deal, (b)
state which deal is cheaper and by how much, and (c) identify one circumstance (a different
quantity, budget timing, or storage) under which the school might still prefer the other
deal, with a reason.

**Mini-rubric** (each criterion 0-2 marks):

- *Both totals correct*: Deal A costs 6,400 rupees (80 x 100 x 0.8); Deal B costs 6,400
  rupees (pay 80, receive 100) - or the equivalent correct computation shown.
- *Comparison stated*: the cheaper deal named with the difference (here they tie, and
  noticing the tie earns the mark).
- *Circumstance analysed*: a real non-price factor is identified and argued, not merely
  named. **(Analyze)**
