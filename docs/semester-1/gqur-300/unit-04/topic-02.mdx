# Topic 4.2 - Perimeter, area, volume and basic shapes

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U4-3" src="/img/figures/gqur-300/unit-04/fig-U4-3.svg" alt="Diagram of one classroom corner shown three ways: a fence line for perimeter, a floor carpet for area, and a water tank for volume, each labelled with its dimension and unit." />

The headteacher of a primary school in Matiari has one corner of a classroom and three
questions. Question one: how much skirting board does the corner need? Question two: how
many floor tiles cover it? Question three: how much water fits in the tank that will sit
there? Same corner, three different quantities: the boundary (skirting), the cover
(tiles), the fill (water). A carpenter, a mason and a plumber would each compute
something different, and confusing them is expensive - order skirting board by square
metres and you will drown in timber. This topic gives the three quantities their names,
their formulas and their units, so the right one answers the right question.

## Explanation

### Perimeter, area, and volume

<Glossary term="Perimeter">Perimeter</Glossary> is the distance around a shape: the
fence line. <Glossary term="Area">Area</Glossary> is the amount of surface a shape
covers: the carpet. <Glossary term="Volume">Volume</Glossary> is the amount of space a
solid fills: the water in the tank. The diagram above shows all three on one classroom
corner; the units keep them apart - metres (m) for perimeter, square metres (m²) for
area, cubic metres (m³) or litres for volume.

<Figure id="fig-U4-4" src="/img/figures/gqur-300/unit-04/fig-U4-4.svg" alt="Table of six measurement formulas with the formula in symbols and one worked classroom example for each." />

The table above collects the formulas with a worked classroom example for each. Three
deserve a closer look.

**Rectangle perimeter and area.** A classroom 8 metres by 6 metres: perimeter = 2 x (8 +
6) = 28 metres of skirting; area = 8 x 6 = 48 square metres of tiles. Notice the
perimeter counts the boundary once around, while the area counts the unit squares
inside - the same two measurements serve both, but they answer different questions.
Marecek, Anthony-Smith and Mathis (2020) build their math-models and geometry chapter on
exactly this: linear, square and cubic measures introduced together so the unit tells the
story.

**Triangle area.** A triangular notice board of base 1.2 metres and height 0.8 metres:
area = (1.2 x 0.8) / 2 = 0.48 square metres. Half of the enclosing rectangle: that is
where the divide-by-two comes from, and picturing it prevents the most common triangle
error (forgetting the half).

**Box volume.** A store box 2 m by 1 m by 1.5 m: volume = 2 x 1 x 1.5 = 3 cubic metres.
Because 1 cubic metre is 1,000 litres, the box holds 3,000 litres - the conversion from
Topic 4.1 riding along.

### Basic geometric shapes and properties

A <Glossary term="Rectangle">rectangle</Glossary> has four right angles and opposite
sides equal; a <Glossary term="Square">square</Glossary> is a rectangle with all sides
equal; a <Glossary term="Triangle">triangle</Glossary> has three sides whose angles sum
to 180 degrees; a <Glossary term="Circle">circle</Glossary> is every point at one fixed
distance (the <Glossary term="Radius">radius</Glossary>) from a centre. The circle's
own numbers are special: its boundary (the circumference) is about 6.28 radii around -
the number pi (about 3.14) times the diameter - and its area is pi times the radius
squared. For school work, taking pi as 3.14 is standard.

**A common misconception to correct.** Many trainees believe "a bigger area always
means a bigger perimeter". The two can move separately: a long thin corridor of
2 metres by 20 metres has area 40 square metres and perimeter 44 metres, while a
near-square room of 6 by 7 metres has more area (42) with less perimeter (26). Fencing
buys perimeter; carpet buys area; a school that confuses them buys the wrong one.

## Activity: Fence or carpet

Work in groups of three. Your tutor gives each group four room plans on paper (a
classroom, a corridor, a store room and an odd-shaped staff room made of two
rectangles). For each plan, compute the perimeter and the area, then decide: if the
school can afford either 100 metres of skirting or 700 square metres of tiles in total,
which rooms get which first? Defend the priority order in two sentences. About 25
minutes.

## Check your understanding

1. Compute the perimeter and area of a classroom 9 metres by 5 metres. *(Apply)*
2. A triangular flower bed has base 3 metres and height 2 metres. Compute its area, and
   explain in one sentence where the divide-by-two comes from. *(Apply)*
3. A water tank is a box 1.5 m by 1 m by 1 m. Compute its volume in cubic metres and in
   litres. *(Apply)*
4. A circular flower bed has radius 4 metres. Compute its circumference and area (take
   pi as 3.14). *(Apply)*
5. A corridor of 2 m by 20 m and a room of 6 m by 7 m: state which has the larger area
   and which the larger perimeter, and say what a school buys with each quantity.
   *(Analyze)*

## Summary

Perimeter is the boundary in metres, area the cover in square metres, volume the fill
in cubic metres or litres; the units are part of the answer. The rectangle, triangle,
circle and box formulas cover most school measurement, and each formula's shape (the
half in the triangle, the pi in the circle) is a picture, not a spell. Area and
perimeter move independently: fencing buys one, carpet the other, and the school that
confuses them buys wrongly.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can compute perimeter, area and volume for rectangles, triangles, circles and
      boxes, with units attached.
- [ ] I can explain where each formula's key step comes from (the half, the pi).
- [ ] I can state, for a given school task, whether it buys perimeter, area or volume.

## Try this at your practicum school

With permission, measure one rectangular room's length and width (pace it if no tape is
available, and note that your method is an estimate). Compute its perimeter and area,
then ask the caretaker how much skirting or tiling the room actually used, and compare.

## Summative task

The school wants to fence a rectangular kitchen garden 12 metres by 8 metres, carpet a
staff room 5 m by 4 m, and install a cylindrical water tank of radius 1 metre and
height 2 metres. In a short written answer: (a) compute the fencing needed (perimeter),
the carpet needed (area), and the tank's volume in litres (pi as 3.14), (b) state the
unit of each answer, and (c) check one of the three answers with an estimation
sentence, and write the whole answer so a colleague could re-check every step.

**Mini-rubric** (each criterion 0-2 marks):

- *Fencing*: perimeter = 2 x (12 + 8) = 40 metres.
- *Carpet*: area = 5 x 4 = 20 square metres.
- *Tank*: volume = 3.14 x 1 x 1 x 2 = 6.28 cubic metres = 6,280 litres, with the
  unit stated on every answer.
- *Check and clarity*: one estimation check shown (for example, the tank is roughly a
  2 m by 2 m by 2 m box = 8,000 litres, so 6,280 is plausible) and the steps are laid
  out for re-checking. **(Analyze)**
