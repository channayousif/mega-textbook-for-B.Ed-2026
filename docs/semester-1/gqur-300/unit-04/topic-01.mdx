# Topic 4.1 - Units of measurement

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U4-1" src="/img/figures/gqur-300/unit-04/fig-U4-1.svg" alt="Table of metric unit ladders for length, mass and capacity with conversion steps and a market example under each group." />

At a fabric shop in Hyderabad's Resham Gali, a customer asks for "do gaz" of cloth. The
shopkeeper measures with his arm-length yard stick, then writes the bill in metres. The
tailor who receives the cloth cuts patterns in centimetres. One purchase, three units -
and no confusion, because every trader in the chain knows the conversions. A teacher
lives in the same world: the school's sports day is measured in metres, the science
practical in millilitres, the midday meal in kilograms. This topic fixes the metric
system that holds all of it together.

## Explanation

### Units of measurement

A <Glossary term="Unit of measurement">unit of measurement</Glossary> is a fixed amount
that other amounts are counted in. The metric system builds every unit from a base unit
and a prefix: <Glossary term="Kilogram">kilo-</Glossary> means a thousand (1 kilometre =
1,000 metres; 1 kilogram = 1,000 grams), <Glossary term="Centimetre">centi-</Glossary>
means a hundredth (1 metre = 100 centimetres), and <Glossary term="Millilitre">milli-</Glossary>
means a thousandth (1 litre = 1,000 millilitres). The table above lays out the three
ladders a teacher uses most: length, mass and capacity. The national mathematics
curriculum carries these same measurement strands across the school years (NCC, n.d.),
which is why a B.Ed trainee meets them here at teacher level.

**Converting along a ladder.** Each step multiplies or divides by a power of ten, so
conversion is a matter of moving the decimal point, never of long division:

- 2.5 metres to centimetres: multiply by 100, giving 250 centimetres.
- 3,200 millilitres to litres: divide by 1,000, giving 3.2 litres.
- 0.75 kilograms to grams: multiply by 1,000, giving 750 grams.

**Worked example.** A school function needs 15 metres of bunting, sold in rolls of 250
centimetres. How many rolls? Convert first: 15 metres = 1,500 centimetres. Divide:
1,500 / 250 = 6 rolls exactly. The look-back check: 6 rolls x 2.5 metres = 15 metres.
True. Converting before computing is the habit that prevents most unit errors; the
classic school mistake is dividing 250 by 15 without converting first (16.67, rounded up
to 17 rolls) - a number that hides the unit mix-up.

**A common misconception to correct.** Trainees often believe "bigger things need bigger
numbers". The opposite happens whenever the unit shrinks: 0.75 kilograms is the same
mass as 750 grams, a bigger number for the same thing. The number and the unit travel
together, and only the pair carries meaning. Saying "the water tank holds 500" is not a
measurement; "500 litres" is.

<Figure id="fig-U4-2" src="/img/figures/gqur-300/unit-04/fig-U4-2.svg" alt="Diagram of four basic shapes - rectangle, square, triangle and circle - each with its defining property written beneath." />

The shapes above close this topic because measurement always measures a shape: cloth is
a rectangle of material, a water tank is a cylinder, a courtyard is a rectangle, and a
roundabout is a circle. Knowing each shape's defining property tells you which
measurement (side, radius, height) a task will need.

## Activity: The conversion market

Work in pairs. Your tutor gives each pair six market slips (for example: "2.5 m of
ribbon", "750 g of sugar", "3,200 ml of milk", "0.9 kg of flour", "150 cm of tape",
"1.75 L of juice"). Each pair must convert every slip into the unit named on a second
card (centimetres, grams, litres), then price the whole basket using a rate card
(ribbon at 40 rupees per metre, sugar at 220 per kilogram, and so on). The first pair
to finish with all conversions correct wins. About 20 minutes.

## Check your understanding

1. Convert: (a) 3.4 metres to centimetres; (b) 5,600 millilitres to litres; (c) 0.9
   kilograms to grams. *(Apply)*
2. A roll of cloth is 250 centimetres long. How many rolls make 15 metres? Show the
   conversion before the division. *(Apply)*
3. A friend says "the tank holds 500". State in one sentence why this is not a
   measurement, and give the two possible meanings if the tank is a water tank. *(Understand)*
4. Name the shape and the measurement you would need to buy: (a) a round tablecloth;
   (b) a lace border for a rectangular dupatta. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Every measurement is a number-unit pair from a ladder: length (metres), mass
(kilograms), capacity (litres), with prefixes kilo-, centi- and milli- stepping by
powers of ten. Convert before computing, and never say a bare number: 0.75 kilograms
and 750 grams are the same mass, and only the unit tells you which number to expect.
Every measurement lands on a shape, and the shape's defining property names the length
you must measure.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can convert between metric units for length, mass and capacity by powers of ten.
- [ ] I can convert before computing in a shopping or school problem, and check the
      answer by converting back.
- [ ] I can name the defining property of a rectangle, square, triangle and circle, and
      say which measurement each one needs.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find three posted measurements in or near the school (a water tank's capacity, a sports
record in metres, a ration quantity in kilograms). Record each as a number-unit pair,
and convert each into the next unit up and down its ladder. Bring the note to class.

## Summative task

The school kitchen prepares rice for 120 pupils at 75 grams per pupil. Rice comes in
25-kilogram sacks at 4,200 rupees per sack. In a short written answer: (a) compute the
total rice needed in kilograms, (b) compute how many full sacks to order and the rice
left over if any, and (c) compute the cost, and check the order with one estimation
sentence.

**Mini-rubric** (each criterion 0-2 marks):

- *Total in kilograms*: 120 x 75 g = 9,000 g = 9 kg.
- *Sacks and leftovers*: 9 / 25 is under one sack, so 1 sack with 16 kg left over
  (25 - 9).
- *Cost with check*: 1 sack = 4,200 rupees; the check notes 9 kg is well under 25 kg, so
  one sack must cover it. **(Analyze)**
