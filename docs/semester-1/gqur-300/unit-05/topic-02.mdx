# Topic 5.2 - Tables, graphs and charts

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U5-3" src="/img/figures/gqur-300/unit-05/fig-U5-3.svg" alt="Diagram for choosing a display: comparing categories leads to a bar chart, parts of a whole to a pie chart, and change over time to a line graph, each with a school example." />

Two pupils are asked to show the class's travel data to the Parent Teacher
Association. One draws a pie chart of travel modes; the other draws a line graph of the
same numbers. Both are neatly drawn. Both are wrong - not in their arithmetic, but in
their choice. A pie chart answers "what share of the class walks?", and a line graph
answers "how does something change over time?" Neither answers the question the PTA
actually asks: "which mode is most common, and by how much?" - which is a bar chart's
job. The display is not decoration after the data; it is the sentence the data speaks.
Choosing it is a quantitative decision, and this topic teaches the choice.

## Explanation

### Tables, graphs, and charts

<Figure id="fig-U5-4" src="/img/figures/gqur-300/unit-05/fig-U5-4.svg" alt="Table of five display types with what each shows best and one pitfall for each." />

The table above is the topic in one view: five displays, what each shows best, and the
pitfall each carries. Three of them deserve a closer look.

**The bar chart.** A <Glossary term="Bar chart">bar chart</Glossary> compares
categories with bars whose heights are the values. The class travel data (walk 16, van
11, rickshaw 8, bicycle 4, other 1) becomes five bars, and the eye answers "which is
most common" before any number is read. The bar chart's one rule: bars start at zero.
Start the vertical axis at 10 instead, and the walk bar towers over the van bar while
the values differ by only 5 - the picture lies.

**The pie chart.** A <Glossary term="Pie chart">pie chart</Glossary> shows parts of one
whole as slices of a circle, with each slice's angle equal to its share of 360 degrees.
The travel data at 40 pupils: walk is 16/40 = 40 percent, so its slice is 40 percent of
360 = 144 degrees. A pie chart is honest only when the slices are shares of one
meaningful whole - the class, the budget, the day. Slicing unrelated quantities into a
pie is the chart's commonest abuse.

**The line graph.** A <Glossary term="Line graph">line graph</Glossary> tracks change
over time, point by point, joined so the trend is visible. Attendance by day, marks by
term, temperature by month: whenever the horizontal axis is time, the line graph is the
default. The <Glossary term="Pictograph">pictograph</Glossary> uses repeated icons
(each stands for a fixed count) and suits young pupils and public notices, provided
its key states exactly what one icon means.

**A common misconception to correct.** Many trainees believe "a graph's impression is
the data". The impression is a product of choices: truncated axes, unequal bar widths,
slices rearranged to hide a small one. Reading a display means reading its construction
too - where does the axis start, what does one icon mean, what is left out. The
PISA framework makes this exact demand: interpreting mathematics in context includes
recognising how a display frames what it shows (OECD, n.d.).

## Activity: Display the same data three ways

Work in pairs. Your tutor gives each pair the class travel data (walk 16, van 11,
rickshaw 8, bicycle 4, other 1) and blank grids. Each pair draws (a) a bar chart with
the axis starting at zero, (b) a pie chart with the slice angles computed, and (c) a
pictograph with a stated key. Then the pairs swap drawings and answer one question
about each: which single question does this display answer best? About 25 minutes.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the display that best answers each question: (a) which travel mode is most
   common; (b) what share of the class walks; (c) how attendance changed across the
   week. *(Apply)*
2. Compute the pie-slice angle for a category that is 30 percent of the whole. *(Apply)*
3. A bar chart's vertical axis starts at 10 instead of 0. State in one sentence what
   the picture now exaggerates. *(Understand)*
4. A pictograph shows 3 bus icons for Class A and 5 for Class B, with the key "one icon
   = 10 pupils". State the two class counts, and one way the display could mislead if
   the key were missing. *(Analyze)*
5. Sketch (in words) the line graph for attendance of 92, 95, 88, 96, 91 percent over
   five days, and state what the line's dips and rises show. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Each display answers one kind of question: bar charts compare categories, pie charts
show shares of one whole, line graphs track change over time, pictographs carry a
stated key into young readers' hands, and the frequency table hides nothing. The
display is a choice with rules - bars start at zero, slices are shares of one whole,
keys are stated - and reading a display means reading its construction, not just its
impression.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can choose the right display for a given question and say why.
- [ ] I can draw a bar chart from a frequency table, and compute pie-slice angles.
- [ ] I can spot a misleading construction (truncated axis, missing key) and state
      what it hides.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find one chart or table displayed in the school (a results board, a duty roster, a
canteen price list). Identify which question it answers best, and one construction
choice (axis start, ordering, key) that shapes the reader's impression. Bring a photo
or sketch with your note.

## Summative task

The school's five class strengths are 38, 42, 35, 40 and 45. The headteacher wants the
managing committee to see which class is largest and by how much. In a short written
answer: (a) name the best display for this question and draw or describe it precisely,
(b) name one display that would be a poor choice and say what it would hide or
exaggerate, and (c) compute the pie-slice angle for the largest class's share of total
pupils, showing the working.

**Mini-rubric** (each criterion 0-2 marks):

- *Best display*: the bar chart chosen with a precise description (labels, axis from
  zero).
- *Poor choice analysed*: for example, a pie chart hides "by how much" between
  adjacent classes, or a pictograph's icons blur small differences - stated with a
  reason. **(Analyze)**
- *Slice angle*: total = 200; largest class 45/200 = 22.5 percent; angle = 22.5% x 360
  = 81 degrees, with the working shown.
