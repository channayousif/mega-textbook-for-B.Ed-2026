# Topic 2.1 - What professional ethics are, and why teaching needs them

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U2-1" kind="table" src="/img/figures/efmp-302/unit-02/fig-U2-1.svg" alt="Table comparing personal morality, the law and professional ethics by what each governs, who enforces it, what it cannot reach, and a teaching example." width={780} height={470} />

Miss Rabia teaches Class 8 mathematics in a government girls' school in Karachi. On the day she
returns the monthly test, she reads the marks aloud, starting with the highest and working down,
so that the class can hear where everyone stands. She has done this for years. She believes it
motivates the girls at the bottom to work harder.

Nothing Miss Rabia has done is against any rule. No law has been broken. No line in any service
regulation forbids reading marks aloud. Ask her about her own character and you would find a
conscientious person who arrives early, prepares her lessons and has never taken a rupee she was
not owed.

And yet something has gone wrong. Sana, who scored 11 out of 40, has heard her failure announced
to thirty classmates. She will carry that into every future maths lesson. Whatever Miss Rabia
intended, what she actually did was use a pupil's weakness as a public instrument.

This topic is about the space that situation occupies: the space where you break no rule and are
not a bad person, and still act wrongly as a teacher.

## Explanation

### What "professional ethics" means, and how it differs from personal morality and from law

<Glossary term="Professional Ethics">Professional ethics</Glossary> are the standards of conduct
that belong to a particular occupation, owed by the people who practise it because of the
position that practice puts them in. They are not a private matter and not an optional extra.
They come with the job.

Three things are easy to confuse, and the difference between them is the whole of this topic.

**Personal morality** is what you believe is right, formed by your family, your faith, your
conscience and your experience. It is yours. It varies legitimately between people, and a
teacher's own morality is not what a pupil signed up for.

**The law** is what the state requires and will punish you for breaching. It is the same for
everyone and it is enforceable, but it is also a floor rather than a standard. The law sets out
what is intolerable, not what is good.

**Professional ethics** sit in between and cover what neither reaches. They are shared rather
than private, so unlike personal morality they can be stated, taught and argued about. They are
aspirational rather than minimal, so unlike the law they describe good practice rather than the
edge of the punishable.

Miss Rabia's case sits exactly in that gap. The law is silent. Her personal morality is
untroubled, because she meant well. Only professional ethics has anything to say, and what it
says is that a teacher does not trade a child's dignity for a motivational effect.

This is also the answer to a common objection, which is worth naming because you will hear it:
*ethics is just being a good person, so it cannot really be taught.* If professional ethics were
personal morality, that would be true. But being a decent person did not tell Miss Rabia what
was wrong with reading marks aloud, and no amount of additional decency would have. What tells
her is a professional understanding of what her position does to an ordinary act. That can be
taught, which is why this unit exists.

A second objection is the mirror image: *if it is not in the code, it is allowed*. Miss Rabia
breached no written rule. A code is a summary of settled judgement, not an exhaustive list of
forbidden acts, and it is always shorter than the situations a teacher meets. Topic 2.2 returns
to what a code can and cannot settle.

### Why teaching needs a shared ethic: the power asymmetry between teacher and pupil

Every profession with an ethic has one feature in common: the practitioner holds something the
client cannot hold for themselves, and the client cannot easily tell whether it is being held
well. A patient cannot assess a diagnosis. A litigant cannot audit their own lawyer. The
imbalance is the reason the ethic exists.

Teaching has this imbalance in an unusually strong form, for three reasons.

**The gap is large and permanent.** A pupil is a child and the teacher is an adult. Where a
patient may be the doctor's equal in every respect except medical knowledge, a Class 8 pupil is
the teacher's junior in knowledge, age, status and institutional power simultaneously.

**The pupil cannot leave.** A dissatisfied client changes accountants. A pupil in a government
school in a village with one school cannot change teachers, cannot appeal, and in practice
cannot even complain without cost. Consent, which does a lot of ethical work in other
professions, is largely unavailable here.

**The effects are formative and invisible for years.** A pupil who concludes at thirteen that
they are "bad at maths" may not discover the cost of that belief until they are excluded from a
course at twenty. Neither Miss Rabia nor Sana will connect the two events.

Carr (2000) makes this the ground of teaching's ethical claim: the work is not the delivery of a
service to a customer but the formation of a person who cannot yet judge what is being done to
them. That is why a teacher's ethics cannot be left to individual conscience. The person most
affected is least able to hold you to account.

### Trust as the working capital of a teacher's authority

<Figure id="fig-U2-2" kind="concept-map" src="/img/figures/efmp-302/unit-02/fig-U2-2.svg" alt="Concept map with trust at the centre and four branches showing what pupils, parents, the state and colleagues each entrust to a teacher, with an example breach on each branch." width={780} height={470} />

Authority in a classroom looks like it comes from position. It does not, or not for long. A
teacher who is not trusted can compel silence but cannot produce learning, because learning
requires a pupil to risk being wrong in front of someone.

Think of trust as working capital. It is accumulated slowly through ordinary reliable acts:
marking returned when promised, a question answered rather than deflected, a confidence kept.
It is spent invisibly. And it can be destroyed in a single act far faster than it was built,
because the pupil's inference from one betrayal is not "that was unlike her" but "I was wrong
about what she is."

Four parties extend this trust, and the figure above maps them. Pupils entrust their learning and
their safety. Parents entrust their children, often the only asset they have. The state entrusts
its curriculum and its certification, since a mark you award is a public claim. Colleagues
entrust the standing of the profession itself, which is why one teacher's misconduct damages
every teacher in the district.

Icka and Kochoska (2024) put the point about pupils sharply: a teacher functions as an ethical
model whether or not they intend to, because pupils learn conduct by watching an adult who holds
authority. A teacher who announces they value fairness and then grades a relative's child gently
has taught something about fairness. It is simply not the lesson they announced.

### Significance in practice: the everyday decisions that are ethical decisions

The word "ethics" suggests rare, dramatic dilemmas. In teaching, the opposite is true. The
ethical weight of the work sits in decisions so routine that they are not experienced as
decisions at all:

- Which pupil you call on when several hands are up, and which you stop calling on.
- Whether you mark the last ten scripts as carefully as the first ten.
- What you say about a pupil in the staff room.
- Whether you prepare a lesson properly for a class you find tiring.
- Whether a parent's gift changes anything about how you treat their child.

None of these will ever reach a disciplinary hearing. All of them are decisions about what a
pupil is owed, and all of them are noticed. A class knows, with considerable accuracy, which
pupils their teacher has given up on.

Recognising these as ethical decisions is the practical payoff of this topic. You cannot reason
carefully about a choice you have not noticed making, which is exactly where the model in Topic
2.3 begins.

## Activity: Sorting the three columns

Work in groups of three. Below are eight short descriptions of a teacher's action. For each one,
decide together whether it is primarily a matter of **law**, of **personal morality**, or of
**professional ethics**, and be ready to defend the placement. Some belong in more than one
column, which is the interesting part.

1. A teacher strikes a pupil.
2. A teacher reads the class's marks aloud in rank order.
3. A teacher does not pray.
4. A teacher tells a colleague, in the staff room, which pupils in the class are "hopeless".
5. A teacher accepts a large gift from a parent before the examinations.
6. A teacher arrives ten minutes late to every lesson.
7. A teacher posts a photograph of their class on a public social media account.
8. A teacher lends money to a struggling student's family.

Allow 20 minutes. Then compare with another group and find one placement you disagree on. Spend
your last five minutes on that disagreement rather than on the seven you agreed about.

## Check your understanding

1. In your own words, state the difference between the law and professional ethics, and give one

2. Miss Rabia broke no rule and meant well. Explain why her action is still a professional

3. Name the three features that make the teacher-pupil power imbalance stronger than the

4. A teacher says: "My private life is my own business, so my personal morality has nothing to
   do with my professional ethics." Identify what is right and what is wrong in that claim.
   *(Analyze)*
5. Give two examples from your own schooling of routine decisions by a teacher that you now
   recognise as ethical decisions. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Professional ethics are the shared standards of conduct that come with a professional position.
They are not personal morality, which is private and varies; they are not the law, which is a
floor rather than a standard. They occupy the large space between the two, and most of a
teacher's ethical life happens there.

Teaching needs such an ethic because of the size and permanence of the gap between teacher and
pupil, the pupil's inability to leave, and the delayed invisibility of the harm. Trust is the
working capital that makes teaching possible, and it is extended by pupils, parents, the state
and colleagues at once. The ethical weight of teaching sits mostly in decisions too routine to
feel like decisions.

## Try this at your practicum school

Over one full teaching day, keep a private tally of a single decision: which pupils the teacher
calls on. Record the name or seat position each time a pupil is invited to answer.

At the end of the day, look at the distribution. Who was never called on? Was the pattern
visible to the teacher? Write half a page on whether the pattern you found was an ethical
decision, and note whether anyone in the room, including you, would have described it as one at
the time.

Do not show the tally to the teacher or discuss individual pupils by name.

## Summative task

Write 350 to 400 words on the following.

> A teacher in your practicum school says: "I follow the rules and I have a clear conscience.
> What more can anyone ask of me?"

Write a reply that takes the claim seriously rather than dismissing it, explains what it leaves
out, and uses one concrete classroom example of your own to show the gap between following the
rules and acting professionally.
