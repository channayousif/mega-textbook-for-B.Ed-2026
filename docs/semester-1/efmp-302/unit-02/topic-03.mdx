# Topic 2.3 - Models for deciding: Rest, and the four-step framework

## A real classroom situation

From each stage a branch drops away labelled with that stage's failure: "did not notice", "judged wrongly", "was outweighed", "did not follow through". The dropped branches all lead to a single box reading "no ethical action". Flat vector flowchart, wordmark bottom right.; alt: Flowchart of Rest's four components - sensitivity, judgement, motivation and character - in sequence, with a failure branch dropping away from each stage to a single outcome box reading no ethical action. */}

Two teachers watch the same thing happen.

In the corridor between periods, a group of Class 7 boys surround a smaller boy, Imran, and push
his books out of his hands. It takes about four seconds. Then everyone moves on.

Mr Khalid, walking behind, does not register it as anything. Boys jostle; books get dropped. He
is thinking about his next lesson.

Miss Nadia sees it and knows exactly what it is, because it is the third time this month with
the same boy. She also knows that raising it means a conversation with the head, who dislikes
complaints, and that the ringleader's father is a member of the school management committee. She
decides to keep an eye on it.

Neither teacher acts. But they failed in completely different places, and a model that cannot
tell those two failures apart is useless for improving either one.

## Explanation

### Rest's four-component model of moral behaviour

James Rest's model, set out by Bebeau, Rest and Narvaez (1999), makes one central claim: acting
well is not a single act but four distinct things that must all succeed. Fail any one and no
ethical action occurs, no matter how well the other three worked.

**Moral sensitivity** is noticing that a situation has an ethical dimension at all, and
recognising who is affected and how. This is perception, not reasoning. Mr Khalid failed here.
No amount of ethical knowledge would have helped him, because he never saw a situation to apply
it to.

**Moral judgement** is working out which course of action is right. This is the part that
looks like ethics, and it is the part most teaching about ethics concentrates on. It draws on
principles, codes and reasoning about consequences.

**Moral motivation** is choosing to prioritise the ethical course over competing values. The
competing values are rarely wicked ones. They are usually ordinary: a quiet life, a manageable
workload, a good relationship with the head, the wish not to make trouble for a colleague. Miss
Nadia failed here. She noticed correctly and judged correctly, and then the cost outweighed the
judgement.

**Moral character** is following through: persistence, and enough courage to carry out the
action once distraction, fatigue or resistance appears. A teacher who reports the incident, is
told to drop it, and drops it has failed at this fourth stage rather than the third.

The practical value is diagnostic. When you find yourself or a colleague not acting well, the
useful question is not "why was that wrong?" but **which of the four failed?** The remedy differs
completely at each stage:

| Component | The failure | What actually helps |
|---|---|---|
| Sensitivity | Did not notice | Practice in reading situations; hearing colleagues describe what they saw |
| Judgement | Noticed, reasoned poorly | Codes, principles, worked cases, discussion |
| Motivation | Judged well, chose otherwise | Honest reckoning with what competes; institutional support |
| Character | Intended well, did not follow through | Rehearsal, allies, small commitments made in advance |

Notice that the most common remedy offered to teachers, which is more instruction in what is
right, only addresses the second. Rest's point is that a great deal of unethical behaviour comes
from people who know perfectly well what is right.

This also dismantles a misconception worth naming: *if a teacher knows the right thing, they
will do it.* Rest's model exists precisely because that is false, and the two teachers in the
vignette show the two most common ways it fails.

### The four-step ethical decision-making framework: Empathy, Context, Reflect, Action

{/* FIGURE[fig-U2-6]: A flowchart drawn as a closed loop with four stations - Empathy, Context, Reflect, Action - connected by arrows in a cycle rather than a line. Each station carries its guiding question in small text. The return arrow from Action back to Empathy is labelled "what did this teach me for next time". Flat vector flowchart, wordmark bottom right.; alt: Flowchart of the four-step ethical decision framework drawn as a loop - Empathy, Context, Reflect, Action - with each station carrying its guiding question and the return arrow labelled what did this teach me for next time. */}

Rest's model describes how ethical action happens or fails. It does not tell you what to do on
Tuesday. The four-step framework is the working procedure, and it maps onto Rest: the first two
steps build sensitivity, the third does judgement, the fourth demands motivation and character.

**Empathy.** Ask who is affected and what this looks like from where they stand. Not "what would
I feel", but what *they* feel given what they know and what they can do about it. For Imran, the
four seconds in the corridor are not four seconds; they are a standing condition of his school
day.

**Context.** Establish the facts and the constraints before deciding. What actually happened,
how often, what has been tried, what the school's rules require, what you are permitted and
obliged to do, who else knows. Miss Nadia had unusually good context, which is what made her
inaction a motivation failure rather than an ignorance one.

**Reflect.** Weigh the options against your obligations, not against your comfort. Name the
competing goods honestly. Ask the test question: if the reasoning behind this decision were
published, would it stand? Ehrich and colleagues (2011) found that teachers facing dilemmas
routinely weigh the institution's interests against the pupil's, and that naming that tension
explicitly is what separates a reasoned decision from a drift.

**Action.** Decide, do it, and then close the loop. The return arrow in the figure is the part
most often dropped. What happened? Was the pupil better off? What would you do differently?

The framework is drawn as a loop rather than a line for a reason. A decision that is never
reviewed cannot teach you anything, and most ethical skill in teaching is accumulated from cases
you handled imperfectly and thought about afterwards.

### Reflective decision making as a standing habit

<Glossary term="Reflective Decision Making">Reflective decision making</Glossary> is the habit of
treating your own decisions as things to be examined rather than defended. It is what makes the
loop above work, and it is a professional disposition rather than a procedure.

Three things distinguish it from simply thinking about your day.

**It is structured.** "I felt bad about that lesson" is not reflection. "I did not call on any
girl in the back three rows, and I think it was because they answer slowly and I was behind" is.

**It seeks disconfirmation.** A reflective teacher goes looking for the evidence that their
decision was wrong. This is uncomfortable and it is the whole value.

**It is habitual, not exceptional.** A teacher who reflects only after something goes badly will
only ever examine dramatic cases, which are rare and unrepresentative. Most of what needs
examining is routine, as Topic 2.1 argued.

A practical form: at the end of each teaching week, write three sentences on one decision you
made about a pupil and are not certain was right. Three sentences, once a week, sustained for a
term, will teach you more about your own practice than a course will.

## Activity: Locating the failure

Work in pairs. Below are four short accounts of a teacher who did not act well. For each,
identify **which of Rest's four components failed**, and state what would actually have helped
that teacher, using the remedy column in the table above.

1. A teacher marks a colleague's relative generously without ever consciously deciding to.
2. A teacher knows a pupil is being bullied, plans to raise it at the staff meeting, and does not
   because the meeting runs late and the moment passes.
3. A teacher believes that publicly comparing pupils' marks is an effective motivator and
   continues to do it.
4. A teacher realises mid-year that a pupil cannot see the board, and says nothing because the
   parents cannot afford spectacles and raising it would embarrass them.

Then swap with another pair. Where you disagree about which component failed, argue it out: the
disagreements are usually about whether the teacher genuinely did not notice, or noticed and
looked away, and that distinction is the hardest and most useful one in the model.

Allow 25 minutes.

## Check your understanding

1. Name Rest's four components in order and state what each one does. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why a teacher who knows exactly what is right may still fail to act, using two
   different components to give two different explanations. *(Understand)*
3. Apply the four-step framework to Miss Nadia's situation. What does each step require of her?
   *(Apply)*
4. Distinguish a failure of moral sensitivity from a failure of moral motivation, and say what
   evidence would let you tell them apart from outside. *(Analyze)*
5. Why is the four-step framework drawn as a loop rather than a line? *(Understand)*

## Summary

Rest's four-component model holds that ethical action requires four separate things to succeed:
noticing the ethical dimension (sensitivity), working out what is right (judgement), prioritising
it over what competes (motivation), and carrying it through (character). Failure at any one
produces the same visible result, which is why diagnosis matters: the remedy differs at every
stage, and only judgement is addressed by being told more rules.

The four-step framework, Empathy, Context, Reflect and Action, is the working procedure that maps
onto those components. Drawn as a loop, it makes reviewing the decision part of the decision.
Reflective decision making is the standing habit that keeps the loop turning: structured, seeking
disconfirmation, and applied to routine cases rather than only dramatic ones.

## Try this at your practicum school

For one week, keep a decision log. At the end of each teaching day, write three sentences about
one decision you made involving a pupil that you are not certain was right.

At the end of the week, read the five entries together and ask Rest's diagnostic question of
each: had you not acted well, which component would have been the one to fail? Most people find
the same component recurring. That recurrence is the single most useful thing this unit can tell
you about yourself.

Keep the log private and do not name pupils.

## Summative task

Write 400 to 450 words.

> Take Miss Nadia's situation from the top of this topic. Work it fully through the four-step
> framework and reach a decision.

Your answer must complete all four steps, state a specific decision rather than a sentiment, and
identify honestly what competes with the ethical course in her case. Finish by saying which of
Rest's components your proposed course of action most depends on, and why that makes it fragile.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong response does |
|---|---|
| Completeness (Apply) | Works all four steps, with Context establishing facts rather than restating the problem |
| Specificity of decision (Apply) | Reaches a concrete, actionable decision that another teacher could carry out |
| **Analysis of what competes (Analyze)** | **Names the competing goods honestly, including the teacher's legitimate self-interest, rather than treating the obstacle as simple cowardice** |
| Self-critique | Identifies which component the plan depends on and why that is its weak point |
