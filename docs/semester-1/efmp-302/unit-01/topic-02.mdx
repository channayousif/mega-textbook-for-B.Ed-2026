# Topic 1.2 - From an industrial model to inquiry-based teaching

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U1-2" kind="diagram" src="/img/figures/efmp-302/unit-01/fig-U1-2.svg" alt="Two classroom scenes side by side - on the left, pupils in rows copying silently; on the right, the same pupils working in groups while the teacher listens and takes notes." width={800} height={470} />

Two teachers in the same school both teach fractions to Class 5 on the same day.

The first follows the textbook exactly, in order, at the planned pace. The class is quiet, the
board is neat, and the period ends on time. The second starts the same way, but partway through
notices that most of the class is adding the denominators. She stops, puts three wrong answers
on the board without names, and asks the class what rule would produce each one. The lesson
runs five minutes over.

Both lessons are "covered". Only one of them responded to what the pupils were actually
thinking. This topic is about the difference between those two pictures of teaching.

## Explanation

### The industrial model of teaching

Early twentieth-century thinking about schooling borrowed heavily from factory management. On
this **industrial** view, a school is like a plant: pupils are raw material, the curriculum is
a fixed production line, and the teacher's job is to deliver it **efficiently**, keep the class
under **control**, and **comply** with set procedures. Success is measured by covering the
syllabus on time.

The industrial model is not simply "bad teaching". It brought real gains - mass schooling,
predictable coverage, clear routines - and parts of it are still useful. A teacher who wastes
no time and keeps an orderly room is doing something the industrial model valued. The problem
is what it leaves out: it treats every class as interchangeable and gives the teacher little
reason to ask *why* a particular group of pupils is or is not learning.

**Worked example.** A teacher marks the same end-of-unit test with the same key every year, in
the same order, and never asks why particular questions are missed. The work is orderly and
efficient. But nothing in the routine turns the pattern of wrong answers into a change in
teaching. That is the industrial model doing exactly what it was designed to do.

### The shift to inquiry-based professionalism

Over recent decades the field has moved toward a different picture, often called
**inquiry-based professionalism**: teaching as a practice in which the teacher studies their
own classroom, reflects on what worked and why, and adjusts based on evidence rather than habit
alone. The learner, not the syllabus, is at the centre.

Hargreaves (2000) describes teacher professionalism as passing through four "ages": a
**pre-professional** age in which teaching was seen as simple work learned by watching; an
**age of the autonomous professional** in which teachers won the right to make their own
classroom decisions but often worked alone; an **age of the collegial professional** in which
teachers plan, observe, and solve problems together; and a fourth, **post-professional** or
postmodern age in which teachers face competing pressures - greater public accountability,
faster change, more stakeholders - and need inquiry and collaboration to cope. The point is not
that the industrial model has vanished. It is that professional expectations now include
noticing, reflecting, and adjusting.

**Common misconception - "inquiry-based teaching just means group work and less structure."**
It is not about a seating plan or about relaxing rules. A tightly run, teacher-led lesson can
be fully inquiry-based if the teacher is watching how pupils think and changing the next step
in response. The defining move is *noticing and adjusting on evidence*, not the furniture.

## Activity: The same lesson, two ways

**Work individually, then discuss. About 25 minutes.**

1. Think of one lesson you have seen taught, or plan one on a topic you know well.
2. **Describe it as an industrial lesson**: fixed procedure, cover the point on time, keep the
   class quiet, mark against a fixed key.
3. **Describe the same lesson as an inquiry-based lesson**: the teacher watches how pupils are
   thinking, notices a common mistake, and changes the next step in response.
4. Name **one concrete change** that would move the industrial version toward the inquiry-based
   one. It must be something a teacher could actually do next week - not "care more".
5. In groups of four, read out your one change. Pick the change that would make the biggest
   difference for the least effort, and be ready to say why.

## Check your understanding

1. Name two things the industrial model of teaching values, and one important thing it leaves
   out. *(Remember / Understand)*
2. In two or three sentences, explain what "inquiry-based professionalism" means, using the
   phrase *noticing and adjusting*. *(Understand)*
3. A teacher runs a strict, silent, teacher-led classroom. A visitor says this cannot be
   inquiry-based teaching. Explain why the visitor might be wrong. *(Apply)*
4. Give one concrete, do-able change that would move a described lesson toward inquiry-based
   professionalism, and explain why it would help. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Ideas about teaching have shifted from an **industrial** model - efficiency, control,
compliance, covering the syllabus - toward **inquiry-based professionalism**, in which the
teacher notices how pupils are thinking and adjusts on evidence. Hargreaves' "four ages" show
this as a long change from working alone toward working collegially and inquiringly, not a
simple before-and-after. Both models still appear in real schools; the inquiry stance is a
habit of noticing and adjusting, not a particular seating arrangement.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can describe the industrial model of teaching and name what it values and what it
      leaves out.
- [ ] I can explain inquiry-based professionalism as *noticing and adjusting on evidence*.
- [ ] I can tell the difference between "inquiry-based" and "unstructured", and explain why a
      strict classroom can still be inquiry-based.
- [ ] I can take a described lesson and name one realistic change that makes it more
      inquiry-based.

## Try this at your practicum school

Watch any ten minutes of a lesson. Note **one moment that looked "industrial"** (a fixed
routine carried out regardless of pupil response) and **one moment that looked
"inquiry-based"** (the teacher changed something because of what a pupil said or did). Do not
judge the teacher - you are practising the lens, not rating a person. Bring both notes.

## Summative task

Take a lesson you know and write two short descriptions of it, about 150 words each: one
industrial, one inquiry-based. Then, in a final paragraph, name **one realistic change** that
moves the industrial version toward the inquiry-based one and explain, using the idea of
*noticing and adjusting*, why it would help learning.

**Mini-rubric (10 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong | Adequate | Weak |
|---|---|---|---|
| Two contrasting descriptions of the same lesson (4) | Both clearly recognisable; the difference is *noticing and adjusting*, not just tone | Descriptions differ but the key contrast is blurred | One description only, or the two are near-identical |
| The proposed change (4) | Specific, do-able next week, and its effect on learning is explained (**Analyze**) | Named but generic ("be more flexible") | Missing or unrelated to the descriptions |
| Plain, accurate use of terms (2) | "Industrial" and "inquiry-based" used correctly | Minor slips | Terms confused |
