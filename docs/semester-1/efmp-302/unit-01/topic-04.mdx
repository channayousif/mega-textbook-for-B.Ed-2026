# Topic 1.4 - Becoming a teacher: developing your identity

## A real classroom situation

A trainee grew up in a village in interior Sindh, in a multi-grade primary school with one
teacher and few books. He is now on practicum in a well-resourced city school. He notices he
keeps expecting pupils to sit silently and copy, the way he was taught - and that this is not
what his cooperating teacher does, or what his B.Ed course recommends.

That gap between how he was taught and how he now wants to teach is where **teacher identity**
is formed. This topic is about how that identity develops and how to work on it deliberately
from the start.

## Explanation

### The influence of sociocultural and policy context

Your **teacher identity** is your evolving sense of who you are as a teacher - your purposes,
your values, and the relationships you want with pupils. Beijaard, Meijer, and Verloop (2004),
reviewing research on teacher identity, stress that it is formed *in relation to context*, not
in isolation: the status teachers hold in a society, how they are paid and trusted, the
language and cultural background of their pupils, and the national policies and standards they
work under all shape what "being a professional teacher" feels like.

**Worked example.** A teacher in an under-resourced rural school in Sindh, working across three
grades in one room, is forming a professional identity under very different pressures from a
teacher in a well-equipped city school - even though both follow the same national standards.
The rural teacher's identity may come to centre on resourcefulness and multi-grade management;
the city teacher's on subject depth. Neither is more "professional"; the context has pulled
their identities in different directions.

### How personal beliefs and values shape teacher identity

Identity is also shaped from the inside. It is strongly influenced by your own experience as a
pupil: the teachers you admired or feared, what you came to believe about how learning happens,
and why you chose teaching. Beijaard and colleagues (2004) note that teachers' prior beliefs
act as a filter through which new ideas from training are accepted, adapted, or quietly
resisted. Two trainees can complete the same course and still teach differently, because they
bring different beliefs about what matters.

### Reflection and self-awareness in teacher-identity formation

Identity does not settle once and stay fixed. It keeps developing through **reflection** -
deliberately looking back at your teaching, noticing the gap between what you intended and what
happened, and asking what that says about your assumptions. Brookfield (2017) describes the
*critically reflective teacher* as one who examines their practice from several viewpoints -
their pupils', their colleagues', the research literature, and their own past - rather than
trusting first impressions. Suarez and McGrath (2022), writing for the OECD, similarly treat
teacher identity as something that must be **actively developed and supported**, especially
during periods of change, rather than left to form on its own.

**Common misconception - "reflective practice just means keeping a diary."** A diary is a
record. Reflection is *using* that record to find and question an assumption, and to change
what you do next. Six dated notes that each name one belief a lesson challenged are reflection;
six pages of "today we did fractions, it was fine" are not.

### "Who am I becoming as a teacher?"

A useful habit from the very start of training is to ask, and keep returning to, one question:
*who am I becoming as a teacher?* Answering it honestly - in a journal, in conversation with a
mentor, after a difficult lesson - turns experience into learning instead of letting it pass
by. It is itself a professional practice, and it connects directly to the reflective tools you
will meet again in Unit 6.

## Activity: First reflection - who am I becoming as a teacher?

**Individual writing. About 15 minutes. Keep this; you will add to it through the course.**

Write half a page answering these prompts honestly. It is not marked for "correct" answers.

1. Name one teacher from your own schooling who influenced how you think a class should be run.
   What did they do?
2. Complete the sentence: *"I believe pupils learn best when…"* - then give one reason you
   believe it.
3. Why did you choose teaching?
4. Name one thing about teaching you are not yet sure you can do well.

Date the page. You will look back at it in Unit 6 when you build your professional development
plan.

## Check your understanding

1. Define **teacher identity** in one sentence. *(Remember)*
2. Give two ways the social or policy context a teacher works in can shape their professional
   identity, with an example of each. *(Understand)*
3. Explain, in two or three sentences, why keeping a teaching diary is **not the same as**
   reflective practice. *(Understand)*
4. "Two trainees can finish the same B.Ed course and still teach differently because…" -
   complete this and link your answer to the idea of prior beliefs acting as a filter.
   *(Apply)*

## Summary

**Teacher identity** is your developing sense of who you are as a teacher. It is shaped from
the outside by sociocultural and policy context - status, trust, pupils' backgrounds, national
standards - and from the inside by your own beliefs and values, which filter what you take from
training. Identity keeps changing through **reflection**: examining your practice from several
viewpoints and questioning your assumptions, not just recording events. Returning regularly to
*"who am I becoming as a teacher?"* is a professional habit that turns experience into learning.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can define teacher identity and explain that it develops rather than being fixed.
- [ ] I can give examples of how context and personal beliefs each shape a teacher's identity.
- [ ] I can explain the difference between recording events and reflecting on them.
- [ ] I have written and dated a first reflection on who I am becoming as a teacher.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher one question: *"What is one way you teach now that is different
from how you were taught, and what changed your mind?"* Write down their answer and one thing
it makes you think about your own beliefs.

## Summative task

In about 300–400 words, analyse **one influence** on your own developing teacher identity - for
example your own schooling, your family's view of teaching, the community you will teach in, or
a national policy you have met in this course. Your analysis must:

1. name the influence and describe it concretely;
2. explain **how** it is shaping the kind of teacher you are becoming - your purposes, values,
   or the relationships you want with pupils;
3. name **one belief** it has given you that you want to keep, and **one** you want to
   question, and say why.

**Mini-rubric (10 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong | Adequate | Weak |
|---|---|---|---|
| The influence described concretely (3) | Specific, real, and clearly relevant to identity | Named but thin or generic | Vague or not really an identity influence |
| How it shapes the teacher you are becoming (4) | Traces a clear line from the influence to purposes/values/relationships (**Analyze**) | Some connection made but under-explained | Asserts influence with no reasoning |
| One belief to keep, one to question (3) | Both named with honest reasons | Both named, reasons thin | One or both missing |
