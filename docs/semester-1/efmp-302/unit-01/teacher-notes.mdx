# Teacher Notes

## Where this unit sits

This is the opening unit of EFMP-302 and, for most students, the opening unit of the B.Ed. It
covers **Weeks 1–3**. Students arrive with strong everyday opinions about teaching and almost
no technical vocabulary for it. The unit's job is to give them that vocabulary — *profession*,
*professionalism*, *professionalisation*, *dimensions of professionalism*, *teacher identity* —
and the habit of examining a case rather than reacting to it. Units 2–4 build directly on the
"dimensions" and "identity" material introduced here.

## Teaching strategies

The course description names **interactive lecture with local examples, case analysis,
reflective practice, and collaborative inquiry**. Use all four in this unit:

- **Open with a question, not a definition.** Before naming any term, ask students to think of
  one job they would call a "true profession" and one they would not, and say why. Collect a
  few, then introduce the four features and let students test their own examples. This surfaces
  the "well-paid job" misconception straight away.
- **Teach the industrial → inquiry shift as a contrast, not a lecture.** Describe one lesson
  two ways in two minutes of talk (scripted/procedural, then reflective/adjusting). Students
  grasp the distinction far faster from a concrete contrast than from a history of ideas.
  Hargreaves' "ages of professionalism" can be mentioned briefly for students who want the
  fuller picture — do not make it the centre.
- **Use think–pair–share for Activity 1** so every student commits to a judgement before the
  whole-class comparison.
- **Protect time for Activity 4 (first reflection).** It is short but important; it is the
  first entry in a habit the course returns to in Unit 6. Do not let it be squeezed out.

## Likely misconceptions and how to handle them

| Misconception | What to do |
|---|---|
| "A profession = a respectable, well-paid job." | Push for the four features; ask them to name a well-paid job that is *not* a profession and a modestly-paid one that *is*. |
| "Teaching is a profession only because the state says so." | Separate *recognition* from *basis*: the knowledge base and ethical code exist whether or not policy names them. |
| "Reflection = keeping a diary." | Show the difference in Activity 4: a record vs. using the record to question an assumption. |
| "Professionalism is dress and discipline." | Acknowledge these matter, then weight the discussion toward knowledge, ethics, and continued learning. |
| Treating "semi-profession" as an insult. | Frame it as a description of what teaching is still building (longer training, stronger bodies, more judgement), not a ranking. |

## Practical work

- **Activity 2 doubles as practical work.** Have each student describe one real or imagined
  lesson two ways — industrial framing, then inquiry-based framing — and name one concrete,
  do-able change between them. Collect a sample aloud at the end so the class hears that
  "inquiry-based" is a habit of *noticing and adjusting*, not an abstract label.
- **Mini-observation (optional, if a practicum school is available).** Ask students to watch
  ten minutes of any lesson and note one moment that looked "industrial" and one that looked
  "inquiry-based", with no judgement of the teacher. Discuss patterns, not individuals.

## Assessment guidance

- The **formative set** (8 items) is diagnostic — mark lightly, look for which of the five
  key ideas are shaky, and reteach those before the summative.
- The **summative task** reaches **Analyze**. Give students the one-page case at least a few
  days early. Mark against the rubric; the most common shortfall is asserting an
  industrial/inquiry judgement without quoting specific details — flag that explicitly when
  you set the task.
- Weighting follows the course default: **60% summative / 40% formative**.
