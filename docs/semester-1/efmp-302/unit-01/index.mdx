# Understanding Teaching

Before you can grow as a teacher, it helps to be clear about what teaching actually asks of
you. This unit looks at three things: what makes teaching a
<Glossary term="Profession">profession</Glossary>, how ideas about that profession have
changed over time, and how your own sense of who you are as a teacher begins to take shape.

## What a profession is

Not every job is a profession. In everyday speech people sometimes call any respectable,
salaried job "a profession", but the word has a narrower meaning. A **profession** is an
occupation that is built on a body of specialised knowledge, entered only after a long period
of training, governed by a shared code of conduct, and held accountable to a wider community
of practitioners — not only to an employer.

A **professional** is a member of such an occupation who is expected to use judgement, keep
learning, and act in the interest of the people they serve even when no one is watching.

> Example: A doctor at a Rural Health Centre in Sindh cannot simply follow a fixed script.
> They studied for years, they belong to a medical council that can remove their licence, and
> they are trusted to decide what a particular patient needs. A teacher in a nearby government
> primary school is in the same position: trained, licensed, and trusted to make classroom
> decisions.

## Features that distinguish a profession from an occupation

Four features are usually used to tell a profession apart from an ordinary occupation:

1. **Specialised knowledge** — a body of knowledge specific to the work that outsiders do not
   have.
2. **Extended training and a qualification** — you cannot enter by simply being hired; you
   must complete a recognised course of study.
3. **A code of conduct** — shared standards of ethical behaviour that members agree to follow.
4. **Accountability to a professional community** — a body of peers, an association, or a
   regulator that can judge your practice, not just your employer.

Teaching has all four: teachers study the subject matter and how people learn, they train for
years and are certified, they follow codes of ethical conduct, and they answer to professional
standards bodies as well as to their head teacher.

> Example: Compare a teacher, a shopkeeper, and a doctor. The shopkeeper needs no
> qualification, follows no shared professional code, and answers only to the owner — a clear
> occupation. The teacher and the doctor meet all four features. Some jobs sit in between and
> are worth arguing about, which is a useful class discussion.

## Professionalism *in* teaching versus professionalisation *of* teaching

Two similar-sounding ideas are easy to confuse.

- **Professionalism in teaching** is about how one teacher conducts themselves day to day —
  their skill, their ethics, their judgement, their willingness to keep improving.
- **Professionalisation of teaching** is a slower, collective process: the way teaching as a
  *field* has built its own training routes, licensing, standards, and associations, in the
  way that medicine and law did earlier.

The two are linked but separate. A teacher can show strong personal professionalism even in a
system where professionalisation is still developing. Pakistan's move toward the National
Professional Standards for Teachers (covered in Unit 4) is part of that larger
professionalisation process.

## Comparative perspective: teaching and other professions

Set against medicine, law, or engineering, teaching is sometimes called a "semi-profession":
its training was historically shorter, its practitioners have had less control over entry to
the field, and much of its work is done in public institutions under close administrative
supervision. Demirkasımoğlu (2010), reviewing how different writers define teacher
professionalism, shows there is no single agreed definition — some stress autonomy and
expertise, others stress accountability and service.

This comparison is not meant to rank teaching below other professions. It is useful because it
points to the parts of professional status that teaching is still building: a longer and more
rigorous training route, stronger professional bodies, and more room for teachers to exercise
informed judgement.

## Historical "industrial" metaphors of teaching

Early twentieth-century thinking about schooling borrowed heavily from factory management. On
this **industrial** view, a school is like a plant, students are raw material, the curriculum
is a fixed production line, and the teacher's job is to deliver it **efficiently**, keep the
class under **control**, and **comply** with set procedures. Success is measured by covering
the syllabus on time.

> Example: A teacher who marks the same test with the same key every year, in the same order,
> and never asks why particular questions are missed, is working close to the industrial
> model. The work is orderly, but it does not change in response to evidence about learning.

## The shift toward reflective, inquiry-based, learning-centred professionalism

Over the last few decades the field has moved toward a different picture, often called
**inquiry-based professionalism**: teaching as a practice in which the teacher studies their
own classroom, reflects on what worked and why, and adjusts their approach based on evidence
rather than habit alone. The learner, not the syllabus, is at the centre.

Hargreaves (2000) describes teacher professionalism as passing through several "ages" —
moving from an early autonomous craft, through a collegial stage of working and planning
together, toward a stage where teachers routinely inquire into their own practice. The point
is not that the industrial model has vanished, but that professional expectations now include
reflection and adjustment.

> Example: A teacher notices that many students confuse *area* and *perimeter* on every test.
> Instead of re-teaching the same way, she changes how she introduces the two ideas next term
> and checks whether the confusion drops. That noticing-and-adjusting habit is inquiry-based
> professionalism.

## Specialised knowledge and training

The first dimension of teacher professionalism is a distinct **knowledge base**: knowledge of
the subject, knowledge of how students of a given age learn and develop, and *pedagogical
content knowledge* — knowing the best ways to represent a particular topic so that particular
learners understand it. This knowledge is built through a recognised programme of study, not
picked up casually on the job.

> Example: Knowing Urdu grammar is subject knowledge. Knowing that Class 3 pupils in a
> multi-grade room grasp tenses faster through short spoken drills than through written rules
> is pedagogical content knowledge — and it is the kind of knowledge a B.Ed programme is meant
> to build.

## Professional code of conduct and ethical behaviour

The second dimension is a shared **code of conduct**: agreed standards for how a teacher
should behave toward students, colleagues, parents, and the wider community — for example,
fairness in assessment, honesty, protecting students from harm, and respecting confidentiality.
Carr (2000) argues that teaching is a fundamentally *ethical* practice, because almost every
classroom decision affects the interests and development of children. Unit 2 examines these
codes and ethical frameworks in detail.

## Accountability, autonomy, and collegiality in professional practice

The third dimension has three parts that must be held in balance:

- **Accountability** — answering for the quality of your work to students, parents,
  employers, and standards bodies.
- **Autonomy** — the room to use professional judgement about how to teach a class, rather
  than following a rigid script.
- **Collegiality** — working with, learning from, and being answerable to fellow teachers.

Too much accountability with no autonomy pushes teaching back toward the industrial model. Too
much autonomy with no collegiality leaves each teacher isolated. Professional practice needs
all three.

## Role of professional associations and accreditation bodies

Professions organise themselves through **associations** (which represent members and often
maintain a code) and **accreditation bodies** (which judge whether a training programme or an
institution meets agreed standards). In Pakistan, teacher-education programmes are accredited
against national criteria, and bodies such as the National Accreditation Council for Teacher
Education set expectations for how teachers are prepared. Belonging to such structures is part
of what makes an occupation a profession rather than a private arrangement between a worker and
an employer.

## Influence of sociocultural and policy contexts on teacher professionalism and identity

What "being a professional teacher" means is not the same everywhere. It is shaped by the
society and the policy environment: the status teachers hold, how they are paid, how much they
are trusted, the language and cultural background of their students, and the national policies
and standards they work under. Beijaard, Meijer, and Verloop (2004), reviewing research on
teacher identity, stress that identity is formed in relation to this context, not in
isolation.

> Example: A teacher in an under-resourced rural school in Sindh, working across three grades
> in one room with few materials, is forming a professional identity under very different
> pressures from a teacher in a well-equipped city school — even though both follow the same
> national standards.

## How personal beliefs and values shape teacher identity

Your **teacher identity** is your evolving sense of who you are as a teacher — your purposes,
your values, and the kind of relationships you want with students. It is shaped strongly by
your own experience as a student: the teachers you admired or feared, what you came to believe
about how learning happens, and why you chose teaching. Two trainees can complete the same
course and still teach differently, because they bring different beliefs about what matters.

## Reflection and self-awareness in teacher-identity formation

Identity does not settle once and then stay fixed. It keeps developing through **reflection** —
deliberately looking back at your teaching, noticing the gap between what you intended and what
happened, and asking what that says about your assumptions. Brookfield (2017) describes the
critically reflective teacher as one who examines their practice from several viewpoints,
including their students' and their colleagues', rather than trusting first impressions.
Suarez and McGrath (2022), writing for the OECD, similarly treat teacher identity as
something that has to be actively developed and supported, especially during periods of
change.

## Reflecting on "who am I becoming as a teacher?"

A useful habit from the very start of training is to ask, and keep returning to, one question:
*who am I becoming as a teacher?* Answering it honestly — in a journal, in conversation with a
mentor, after a difficult lesson — is itself a professional practice. It turns experience into
learning instead of letting it pass by.

> Example: After a practicum lesson that did not go well, a trainee writes a short note: what
> she wanted, what actually happened, and one belief of hers that the lesson challenged. Six
> such notes across a term show her how her teaching is changing.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A profession is just a well-paid, respectable job."** Pay and status are not the test.
  The test is specialised knowledge, extended training, a code of conduct, and accountability
  to a professional community.
- **"Teaching is a profession only because the government calls it one."** Professional status
  comes from the knowledge base, the ethical code, and the standards teachers hold themselves
  to — policy recognises this; it does not create it.
- **"Reflective practice just means keeping a diary."** A diary is a record. Reflection is
  using that record to find and question your assumptions, and to change what you do next.
- **"Professionalism is mainly about dress, punctuality, and discipline."** Those matter, but
  professionalism is centrally about knowledge, ethical judgement, and continuing to learn.
- **"Once you know your subject, the professional part is finished."** Subject knowledge is
  one dimension of four; a teacher also needs pedagogical knowledge, ethical judgement, and
  the habits of accountability and reflection.

## Key ideas in this unit

- A profession is defined by specialised knowledge, extended training, a code of conduct, and
  accountability to a professional community — not by pay or prestige.
- Professionalism (how one teacher acts) and professionalisation (how the field organises
  itself) are related but different.
- Ideas about teaching have shifted from an industrial, compliance-focused model toward a
  reflective, inquiry-based one; both still exist in real schools.
- Teacher professionalism has four dimensions: specialised knowledge, an ethical code,
  accountability–autonomy–collegiality in balance, and organised professional bodies.
- Teacher identity develops through reflection and is shaped by personal beliefs and by the
  social and policy context you work in.
