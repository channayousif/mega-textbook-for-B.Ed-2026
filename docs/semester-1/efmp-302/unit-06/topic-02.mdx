# Topic 6.2 - The stages of a teaching career

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U6-3" kind="timeline" width="900" height="400" src="/img/figures/efmp-302/unit-06/fig-U6-3.svg" alt="Timeline of a teaching career across the preservice, new teacher and experienced teacher stages, with each transition labelled by what changes and each stage labelled with what it most needs." />

Three teachers sit in the same in-service session on questioning technique.

Sana is on practicum. She has taught nine lessons in her life and is still working out how to
stand. The session's advice about wait time is interesting and she has no room in which to try it,
because she is concentrating on getting through the material without losing the class.

Imran is in his second month of employment. He knows about wait time. He also has a Class 8 that
talks over him, and everything he has been given assumes a room that listens.

Mrs Aslam has taught for nineteen years. She has heard this session, or something close to it,
five times. She could deliver it. What she has not had in nineteen years is one person watching
her teach and telling her something true.

The same session. Three entirely different needs, one of which it meets.

## Explanation

### The preservice stage

The preservice stage covers initial teacher education up to qualification: your own position now.

**What changes at its end** is responsibility. A trainee observes, assists, and teaches under
supervision with someone else accountable for the class. A qualified teacher is accountable.

**The characteristic difficulty** is that almost everything is new at once, so attention is
consumed by mechanics: where to stand, how to start, how to get thirty books collected without
losing ten minutes. Sana is not failing to apply wait time; she has no spare attention with which
to apply it.

**What this stage needs** is structured practice with feedback, in a real classroom, on a small
number of things at a time. Not more content. Day (1999), writing about teacher development across
a working life, situates initial preparation as the beginning of a long process rather than as a
loading of everything required.

**What is usually offered instead** is a heavy content curriculum and a short practicum, which is
the pattern in most systems including this one. The practical response is to use your practicum
deliberately: the transfer tasks in this course exist because a practicum used for observation
alone teaches much less than one used for structured trials.

### The new teacher stage

The new teacher stage covers roughly the first three to five years.

**What changes at its beginning** is that you are alone with a class and responsible for what
happens in it. This transition is abrupt in teaching in a way it is not in most professions: a new
doctor is supervised for years; a new teacher frequently has the same load as a colleague of
twenty years' standing from the first week.

**The characteristic difficulty** is survival. The demands are immediate and continuous, and the
teacher has not yet built the automatic routines that later make room for thought. Imran knows
about wait time, and his problem is not knowledge. It is that he cannot use it in a room he cannot
yet hold.

This is where two of Unit 5's findings become concrete. New teachers absorb the elastic work
because they do not yet know they may decline it, and burnout risk is high at this stage for
exactly that reason.

**What this stage needs** is a mentor, reduced load, and help with the specific thing that is
actually blocking them, which is usually classroom management rather than technique. Ordinary
survival help.

**What is usually offered instead** is the same generic in-service programme given to everyone,
plus, frequently, the hardest classes, on the reasoning that senior teachers have earned the
better ones.

### The experienced teacher stage

<Figure id="fig-U6-4" kind="table" width="900" height="350" src="/img/figures/efmp-302/unit-06/fig-U6-4.svg" alt="Table of the preservice, new teacher and experienced teacher stages showing the characteristic difficulty of each, the support that helps, and the support usually offered instead." />

The experienced teacher stage covers the long remainder, and it is the least served.

**What changes** is that the mechanics have become automatic. Attention is freed. A teacher at
this stage can do things that were impossible earlier: notice an individual pupil mid-explanation,
change course in a lesson, hold a class while thinking about something else.

**The characteristic difficulty** is that nothing external requires any further change. The
classes run, the results hold, nobody complains. This is where Topic 6.1's drift operates, and it
is slow enough to be invisible from inside.

**What this stage needs** is genuine challenge: someone who will observe and say something true,
a problem worth solving, a role that requires learning something new, contact with practice
outside the school. Mrs Aslam's nineteen years without a single honest observation is the common
case, not an unlucky one.

**What is usually offered instead** is the same generic session for the fifth time, or an
administrative promotion that removes them from teaching altogether.

A misconception belongs here: **experienced teachers have finished developing.** The stage model
says the opposite. The experienced stage is the one with the most capacity for development,
because attention is free, and the least external pressure toward it. Capacity and demand are
inversely arranged, which is why development at this stage has to be self-driven or it does not
happen.

**A caution about stage models generally.** These stages describe a common pattern, not a law.
Teachers move at different rates, a change of school or subject can return an experienced teacher
to something like the new-teacher stage, and long service is not the same as being in the
experienced stage in this sense. Use the model to locate what you need, not to categorise people.

## Activity: Three teachers, one session

Work in groups of three, taking one of Sana, Imran and Mrs Aslam each.

For your teacher, write what the questioning session actually offered them, what they needed
instead, and one thing the session's organiser could have done to make it useful for your teacher
specifically without abandoning the other two.

Read all three out. Then answer as a group: is a single session for all three stages salvageable,
or is the mixed-stage room the fundamental problem? Give reasons.

Allow 25 minutes.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the three stages and state what changes at each transition. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why the new teacher transition is more abrupt in teaching than in most professions.
   *(Understand)*
3. Sana and Imran both know about wait time and neither uses it. Explain why, and show that the
   reasons differ. *(Apply)*
4. Explain why the experienced stage has the most capacity for development and the least pressure
   toward it. *(Analyze)*
5. Give, for any two stages, the support that helps and the support usually offered instead.
   *(Remember)*
6. Why should a stage model be used to locate a need rather than to categorise a teacher?
   *(Understand)*

## Summary

A teaching career passes through three broad stages. The preservice stage ends when responsibility
transfers; its difficulty is that everything is new at once, so attention goes to mechanics, and it
needs structured practice with feedback rather than more content. The new teacher stage begins with
an abrupt transition to full responsibility; its difficulty is survival, and it needs a mentor, a
reduced load and help with what is actually blocking the teacher, usually classroom management.

The experienced stage is the longest and least served. Mechanics have become automatic and
attention is free, so capacity for development is at its highest, while external pressure toward
it is at its lowest. Its difficulty is that nothing requires change, which is where drift operates.
It needs genuine challenge and honest observation, and is usually offered a repeat session or an
administrative promotion.

At every stage the support offered tends to mismatch the support needed. Stage models describe a
common pattern rather than a law, and are for locating a need, not for categorising a person.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can name the three stages and what changes at each transition.
- [ ] I can state the characteristic difficulty of each stage.
- [ ] I can explain why two teachers who know the same technique may both be unable to use it.
- [ ] I can explain the inverse arrangement of capacity and pressure in the experienced stage.
- [ ] I can locate myself and a colleague on the model without treating it as a label.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find a teacher at each stage in your practicum school if you can: someone in their first years and
someone with long service.

Ask each the same question: "What would actually help you get better at this?"

Write half a page comparing the answers against what this topic says each stage needs. The
answers are often more specific and more modest than the topic predicts, and where they differ
from it, the teachers are the better evidence.

## Summative task

Write 400 to 450 words.

> A district has budget for one professional development intervention, for all teachers, once a
> year. Using the stage model, argue what it should spend it on.

Your answer must use all three stages, acknowledge that one intervention cannot serve them
equally, and reach a defended choice. Address the objection that spending on new teachers
abandons the experienced majority, or that spending on the experienced abandons those most at
risk of leaving.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong response does |
|---|---|
| Use of the stage model (Apply) | Applies all three stages with their specific needs, not a general plea for more training |
| Recognition of the constraint | Accepts that one intervention cannot serve all three, rather than proposing three |
| **Defended choice (Evaluate)** | **States the criterion the choice rests on and answers the objection from the group not chosen** |
| Realism | The proposal is deliverable once a year on a district budget |
