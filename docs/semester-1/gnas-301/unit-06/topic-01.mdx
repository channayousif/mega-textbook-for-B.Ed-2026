# Topic 6.1 - What toxicology studies: substances and their classes

## A real classroom situation

At a village health day near Tharparkar, a trainee teacher holds up two bottles for the
Class 8 pupils: one is a pesticide from the cotton fields, the other is the salt from
their own kitchen. "Which one is the poison?" she asks. Every hand points at the
pesticide. Then she reads the label of a poisoning case the health worker told her
about: a child hospitalized after drinking the salty water of a hand pump, and another
treated for pesticide rash. "Both bottles hurt someone this month," she says. "The
difference is the dose."

That is the door into <Glossary term="Toxicology">toxicology</Glossary>, and this topic
opens it.

## Explanation

### Introduction to toxicology

Toxicology is the study of the adverse effects of chemicals on living organisms. It asks
four questions about any substance: what is it, how does it enter, what does it do, and
how much does it take? The last question is the founding one: <Glossary term="Dose">dose</Glossary> is the amount that reaches a sensitive site, and for most substances the
harm depends on it. Water in excess harms; the pesticide diluted a million-fold may not.
The classic saying, the dose makes the poison, is the discipline's first law and its
first caveat, because some agents, lead and the genotoxic class of Topic 6.2, carry no
safe level at all.

### Classification and properties of toxic substances

Toxic substances classify usefully along three axes, by origin, by use, and by the
effect they produce, and the third axis belongs to Topic 6.2. By origin: **anthropogenic**
poisons, made or concentrated by people, pesticides, industrial solvents, lead in paint;
and **natural** poisons, made by living things or geology, snake venom, plant alkaloids,
arsenic in rock that dissolves into groundwater. By use: pesticides, drugs at the wrong
dose, household chemicals, industrial chemicals, and cosmetics. The properties that
matter most to behaviour are the ones Unit 5 met: solubility, where it parks, and how
long it stays.

<Figure id="fig-U6-2" src="/img/figures/gnas-301/unit-06/fig-U6-2.svg" alt="Table of six classes of toxic substances with examples, origin and one property that matters for each" width={780} height={470} />

**Anthropogenic and natural poisons.** The village bottles show the axis. The pesticide
is anthropogenic, designed to kill, and the Sindh cotton season exposes the sprayers.
Arsenic is natural, dissolved from rock into the Indus basin's groundwater, and the
twenty-four-district study of Unit 5 found it in the water families drink (Alibhatti et
al., 2017). Snake venom, another natural poison, is the health unit's monsoon visitor.
The axis matters because controls differ: an anthropogenic poison can be banned or
replaced, a natural one can only be managed, tested, treated, avoided. The WHO's
chemical-safety programme spans both, natural and manufactured, for exactly that reason
(WHO, n.d.).

**A worked example.** Run the four questions on the kitchen salt. What: sodium chloride,
essential to life. Route: by mouth, in food and water. Effect: in excess it raises blood
pressure and, in the Thar child's case, the salty well pushed the body's water balance
past its limits. Dose: the kitchen pinch is nutrition, the well is a poisoning. Toxicology
does not divide the world into safe and dangerous substances; it asks how much, by which
door, to whom.

**Common misconception.** "Natural means safe, synthetic means dangerous." Both halves
fail. Arsenic and snake venom are natural and lethal; the synthetic class includes
life-saving medicines, whose toxicity is a matter of dose and purpose. The honest axis
is not origin but information: what the substance is, at what dose, by which route, to
whom.

## Activity: Two bottles, four questions

Work in pairs. Your teacher gives each pair two substance cards, one natural, one
anthropogenic, drawn from the table. For each card, answer the four toxicology questions
in one line each, then decide which of the two your pair would rather have spilled in a
classroom, and defend the choice with the dose idea. Allow 20 minutes, then three pairs
present.

## Check your understanding

1. Define toxicology and name its four questions. *(Remember)*
2. Classify these as anthropogenic or natural: pesticide, arsenic, snake venom, lead
   paint. *(Understand)*
3. Why do controls differ for an anthropogenic poison and a natural one? *(Analyze)*
4. The kitchen salt and the salty well are the same substance. Which question of the
   four separates nutrition from poisoning? *(Apply)*

## Summary

Toxicology studies the adverse effects of chemicals on living organisms through four
questions, the agent, the route, the effect and the dose, with dose the founding one and
its caveat, the no-safe-level agents. Substances classify by origin, anthropogenic like
pesticides and lead paint versus natural like arsenic and snake venom, by use, and by
effect. The origin axis decides the control: made poisons can be banned, natural ones
only managed. Toxicology divides the world not into safe and dangerous substances but
into informed answers.

## Try this at your practicum school

List the six toxic substances closest to the pupils: the cleaner in the toilet block,
the chalk dust, the pesticide in the garden, the water source, whatever is really there.
For each, note the class, the likely route of exposure for a child, and the one question
you would need answered before calling it safe. Do not name the school.
