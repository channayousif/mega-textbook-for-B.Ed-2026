# Topic 6.2 - Effects of toxic substances

## A real classroom situation

Two patients share a district hospital ward. The first is a farm worker carried in an
hour after spraying, sweating, blurred, weak. The second is a woman of fifty from the
same village, in for a biopsy, who has drunk the same well water for thirty years. The
nurse tells the trainee teacher on placement, "Same village, same chemicals, different
clocks." The worker's clock is an hour; the woman's is a lifetime.

The two clocks name the two great classes of effect, and behind them wait the special
classes this topic adds.

## Explanation

### Acute and chronic effects; the special classes

An <Glossary term="Acute effect">acute effect</Glossary> appears quickly after a single
or short exposure, the sprayer's hour. A <Glossary term="Chronic effect">chronic
effect</Glossary> develops slowly over long, often low-level exposure, the thirty-year
well. The distinction organises everything: acute poisoning is an emergency with an
antidote clock; chronic disease is a public-health programme with a testing clock.

Behind them sit the special classes, named for what they attack:

- <Glossary term="Genotoxic agent">Genotoxic agents</Glossary> damage DNA, the cell's
  instruction manual; the damage can then be copied forward.
- <Glossary term="Mutagen">Mutagens</Glossary> cause mutations, changes in the DNA
  sequence; most mutagens are genotoxic, and the effect may wait a lifetime to appear.
- <Glossary term="Teratogen">Teratogens</Glossary> harm the developing unborn child,
  causing birth defects; the timing of exposure, not only the dose, decides the harm,
  which is why pregnancy warnings carry their own logic.
- <Glossary term="Carcinogen">Carcinogens</Glossary> cause cancer, the uncontrolled
  growth that begins with one damaged cell; asbestos and tobacco smoke are the classic
  examples, and the WHO's chemical-safety work lists its major concerns (WHO, n.d.).
- <Glossary term="Sensitizer">Sensitizers</Glossary> train the immune system to
  overreact: the first exposure teaches, the later ones attack, which is why a worker
  fine with a chemical for years can suddenly develop a rash that never leaves.

<Figure id="fig-U6-3" src="/img/figures/gnas-301/unit-06/fig-U6-3.svg" alt="Table of the effect classes of toxic substances with definition, one example and detection or prevention for each" width={780} height={470} />

The dose-response relationship ties all the classes to a picture. For most effects,
plot dose against harm and the line rises from a **threshold**, a dose below which the
body's repair handles the exposure. For the genotoxic-carcinogenic class the line is
drawn without a known safe threshold, which is why regulation treats them with no-level
strictness. Note the picture's honest limit: it summarises populations, and the
individual, the unborn child, the sensitized worker, can sit far from the average.

<Figure id="fig-U6-4" src="/img/figures/gnas-301/unit-06/fig-U6-4.svg" alt="Diagram of two dose-response curves, one with a threshold and safe zone, one without a known safe level, as drawn for the genotoxic class" width={780} height={470} />

**A worked example.** Read the ward through the classes. The sprayer's collapse is
acute, organophosphate poisoning, Unit 5's bioactivation at speed. The woman's biopsy
question is chronic, thirty years of low-dose exposure, and if the well carries a
genotoxic carcinogen, arsenic is one, her clock had no threshold to hide behind. The
same village, the same chemicals: the classes are the difference between an antidote
and a screening programme.

**Common misconception.** "A small dose of anything is harmless." True for the
threshold class, false as a general law: the no-threshold agents break it, and the
sensitizers break it differently, the first small dose being the lesson the immune
system never forgets. The dose idea of Topic 6.1 needs this topic's classes to finish
the sentence.

## Activity: Class the case

Work in groups of three. Your teacher gives each group four case cards, real exposure
scenarios on one side. For each, name the effect class, the clock it runs on, and the
one control that would have prevented it, at the source, the pathway or the receptor.
Then each group reads its most instructive card aloud. Allow 25 minutes.

## Check your understanding

1. Define acute and chronic effects with one example of each. *(Remember)*
2. What makes a teratogen's harm depend on timing as well as dose? *(Understand)*
3. Why are genotoxic carcinogens regulated with no safe level? *(Analyze)*
4. A worker develops a rash after years of safe use of a solvent. Which class, and what
   does it mean for her return to that job? *(Apply)*

## Summary

Effects divide first by clock, acute after one exposure, chronic over years, then by
target: genotoxic agents damage DNA, mutagens change it, teratogens harm the unborn,
carcinogens start cancers, sensitizers train the immune system to overreact. The
dose-response curve pictures harm rising from a threshold for most effects and from no
known safe level for the genotoxic class. The classes decide the response: antidote and
emergency for the acute clock, testing and prevention for the chronic one.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask the school's first-aider about the two most common chemical complaints, a headache
from the store room, a rash from the cleaner. Class each one by effect class and clock,
and note the one control the school already has in place for it. Do not name the school.
