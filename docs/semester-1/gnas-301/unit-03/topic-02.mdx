# Topic 3.2 - Noise pollution

## A real classroom situation

A school in Karachi sits where two main roads meet. By afternoon, the Class 6 teacher has
stopped shouting over the traffic; she uses a hand signal and the pupils copy notes from
the board in silence. A visitor asks whether the noise bothers them. "Bother?" says the
teacher. "We have stopped noticing it. That is what worries me."

She is right to worry. Not noticing is not the same as not being harmed. This topic is
about the pollution people cannot see, often stop noticing, and are still injured by.

## Explanation

### Noise pollution: sources, human health impacts and control measures

Sound becomes <Glossary term="Noise pollution">noise pollution</Glossary> when it is loud
enough, or persistent enough, to harm health or wellbeing. Loudness is measured in
decibels, dB, on a logarithmic scale: every 10 dB is a tenfold increase in sound energy.
A whisper is about 30 dB, normal conversation 60, a busy Karachi road 85 to 95, a pressure
horn at the window over 110. Sustained exposure above about 85 dB damages hearing; a
single very loud event can do it instantly.

<Figure id="fig-U3-3" src="/img/figures/gnas-301/unit-03/fig-U3-3.svg" alt="Diagram of a decibel ladder from a quiet library at 30 dB to a pressure horn above 110 dB, with the hearing-damage zone marked above 85 dB" width={780} height={470} />

The sources in Pakistan are the ones you can list from any window: road traffic, pressure
horns, construction and generators, loudspeakers, workshops and industry. The health
impacts go beyond the ear. Hearing loss is the clearest: it is gradual, painless and
permanent. The World Health Organization reports that about 430 million people, including
34 million children, already need rehabilitation for disabling hearing loss, and names
loud noise exposure among its causes (WHO, 2026). But sustained noise also raises blood pressure and stress hormones, disturbs
sleep, and, in classrooms, measurably slows learning: a teacher shouting over traffic is
spending her voice and the pupils' attention at the same time.

<Figure id="fig-U3-4" src="/img/figures/gnas-301/unit-03/fig-U3-4.svg" alt="Table of five noise sources with health impact, who is exposed and one control measure for each" width={780} height={470} />

Control measures, once again, sit at the three links. At the source: silencers, horn
bans that are actually enforced, quieter machinery, maintenance (a loose part is a loud
part). In the pathway: distance, barriers, green belts, and siting, the decision that
keeps schools away from main roads, which is the cheapest control of all. At the
receptor: ear protection for workers, quiet rooms and, for a school, double glazing and
classroom scheduling that keeps the sensitive hours away from peak traffic.

**A worked example.** The school at the crossroads measures its courtyard with a free
sound meter app: 72 dB in the morning, 88 dB at the evening rush. The 16 dB difference is
not "a bit louder"; it is about forty times the sound energy. The school moves its
listening exercises to the morning, plants a hedge along the wall, and writes to the
union council about the pressure horns. Three links, three actions, two of them free.

**Common misconception.** "Noise is annoying but not a health hazard." Annoyance is the
smallest of its effects. Hearing loss is permanent, cardiovascular effects are documented,
and children in noisy schools lose learning time daily. Noise is pollution by the same
standard as smoke: a physical agent that harms at a dose.

## Activity: Measure three places

Work in pairs. Using a free sound meter app on one phone, measure three spots for one
minute each: the classroom in silence, the corridor at break, and the nearest road at
dismissal time. Record the three numbers, compare them with the ladder figure, and write
one sentence per spot naming who is exposed and for how long. Allow 20 minutes, including
walking.

## Check your understanding

1. What does it mean that decibels are logarithmic? Use a 10 dB example. *(Understand)*
2. Name four health impacts of noise pollution, only one of which is hearing loss.
   *(Remember)*
3. The corridor measures 70 dB and the road 90 dB. Explain why this is not "twice as
   loud" in energy terms. *(Analyze)*
4. Your school is beside a main road. Name one control at each of the three links, with
   the cheapest first. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Noise pollution is sound at levels that harm health: hearing loss that is gradual and
permanent, plus raised blood pressure, stress, disturbed sleep and lost learning. Decibels
are logarithmic, so small number jumps are large energy jumps, and the damage zone begins
around 85 dB. Sources are traffic, horns, construction, generators and loudspeakers.
Controls act at the source, in the pathway, where distance and siting are cheapest, and at
the receptor.

- [ ] I can name four health impacts of noise beyond annoyance.
- [ ] I can measure sound with an app and place the reading on the ladder.
- [ ] I can name one noise control at each link of the chain.

## Try this at your practicum school

With permission, take three one-minute readings at the school: a quiet classroom, the
courtyard at break, and the gate at dismissal. Note the time of day for each. Bring back
the three numbers and one recommendation you could make at a staff meeting, with the
cheapest option first.

## Summative task

Write 150 to 180 words on the following.

> The head teacher says, "We cannot move the road, so there is nothing to do about the
> noise." Using the three links and the evidence of health impacts, write the reply you
> would give, ending with the two cheapest actions the school could take this term.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong response does |
|---|---|
| Links used (Analyze) | Shows that the road is only the source, not the whole problem |
| Health evidence (Understand) | Names impacts beyond annoyance, correctly |
| Actions chosen (Apply) | Two feasible, cheap, this-term actions, not a bypass petition |
| Clarity and register | Plain sentences for a head teacher; within the word count |
