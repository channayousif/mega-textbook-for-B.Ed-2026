# Topic 3.1 - Air pollution

## A real classroom situation

Every winter, schools in Lahore watch the sky turn the colour of dishwater. In November,
Miss Farzana's cousin, a headteacher there, checks the air quality index each morning the
way coastal people check the tide. On a bad day the AQI passes 300, "hazardous", and the
government closes the schools. Her pupils stay home, and the news argues about whose fault
the smog is: India's crop burning, the brick kilns, the traffic, the weather.

This topic gives you the tools to read that argument properly: what is in the air, where it
comes from, what it does to people, and what can actually be done about it.

## Explanation

### Air pollution: causes, effects and control strategies

<Glossary term="Air pollution">Air pollution</Glossary> is the presence of substances in
the air at levels that harm people, other living things or materials. Its logic is the same
chain you met for water in Unit 2, with air as the pathway.

<Figure id="fig-U3-1" src="/img/figures/gnas-301/unit-03/fig-U3-1.svg" alt="Diagram of the air pollution chain from sources through the air to receptors, with control strategies marked at source, pathway and receptor" width={780} height={470} />

**The pollutants that matter.** Particulate matter, PM10 and the finer PM2.5, is soot,
dust and droplets small enough to reach deep into the lungs; it is the pollutant most
linked to the health burden. Nitrogen dioxide and sulfur dioxide come from burning fuel at
high temperature, in engines and furnaces. Carbon monoxide is the silent one: colourless,
odourless, and it takes up the seat in blood that oxygen needs. Ground-level ozone is not
emitted directly; sunlight cooks it out of other pollutants, which is why it peaks in hot
afternoons. Lead, from some fuels and paints, damages the developing brain; there is no
safe level.

<Figure id="fig-U3-2" src="/img/figures/gnas-301/unit-03/fig-U3-2.svg" alt="Table of six major air pollutants with main source, health effect and one control strategy for each" width={780} height={470} />

**How big is the problem?** The World Health Organization estimates ambient, outdoor, air
pollution caused 4.2 million premature deaths worldwide in 2019, and that 99 per cent of
the world's people breathe air exceeding its guideline levels (WHO, 2024). Its 2021 global
air quality guidelines set the levels the table's controls are trying to reach (WHO, 2021).

**The worked case: Lahore's winter smog.** A 2026 open-access study combined satellite
observations over winter seasons (2019 to 2021) with a survey of the people who work
outdoors in smog season, labourers, street vendors and traffic police, and found winter
smog critical for both environment and public health in Lahore district (Anwar et al.,
2026). A temperature inversion, cold air trapped under warm, holds the city's own
emissions over it. The causes are shared: vehicle exhaust, brick kilns,
industry, waste burning and crop-residue burning across the region. No single owner, no
single fix.

**Control strategies sit at the three links.** At the source: cleaner fuel, upgraded kilns,
engine standards, an end to open burning. In the pathway: dispersion helps only a little;
the honest answer is that the pathway cannot be cleaned, only shortened by reducing
emissions. At the receptor: masks (a good one, worn properly), indoor air filtration,
school closures on the worst days, and asthma plans for the vulnerable. A teacher's tools
are mostly at the receptor end: the AQI check, the morning timing of sports, the classroom
with windows shut on a hazardous day.

**Common misconception.** "Clean air means air you cannot see," and its winter cousin,
"smog is a winter problem only, and only in Lahore." Both halves of both claims fail.
Lahore's smog is visible, but the most dangerous pollutant there, PM2.5, is invisible
particle by particle; a clear sky can still carry an unhealthy index. And the same
pollutants burden Karachi's air and the summer afternoons' ozone, so the eye and the
calendar are not measuring instruments either. The AQI is.

## Activity: Read the index

Work in pairs. Your teacher puts one week of AQI readings for a Pakistani city on the
board. For each day, name the band (good, moderate, unhealthy, hazardous), and write one
sentence of school advice for that day. Then answer: which day would you cancel the sports
day, and which would you move it to the morning? Allow 20 minutes.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the six pollutants in this topic's table with their main sources. *(Remember)*
2. Why is PM2.5 more dangerous than PM10? Use the idea of where each can reach in the
   body. *(Understand)*
3. Explain why "the wind will clean it" is a weak control strategy, using the chain. *(Analyze)*
4. A headteacher asks what to do when the AQI reads "unhealthy". Give two actions and name
   the link of the chain each one acts on. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Air pollution runs the same source-pathway-receptor chain as water pollution, with six
pollutants that matter: particulates, nitrogen dioxide, sulfur dioxide, carbon monoxide,
ozone and lead. The WHO estimates 4.2 million premature deaths a year from outdoor air,
and nearly everyone breathes air above its guidelines. Lahore's winter smog, trapped by
inversions and fed by vehicles, kilns and burning, is the national case. Controls act at
the source, weakly in the pathway, and at the receptor, where a teacher's AQI check and
timing decisions live.

## Try this at your practicum school

For one week, record the AQI for the school's city every morning at the same time, and one
thing the school actually did in response, even if that was nothing. Bring back the seven
readings and one sentence on whether the school's response matched the index. Do not name
the school.

## Summative task

Write 160 to 200 words on the following.

> A parents' committee asks: "The smog is India's fault, so why should we change anything?"
> Using the chain, the pollutant table and the Lahore case, give an honest answer that names
> what is shared, what is ours, and what a school can still do.
