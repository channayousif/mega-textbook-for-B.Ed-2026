# Topic 4.3 - Safety management, regulation and internal control

## A real classroom situation

A new safety officer at a Lahore workshop posts the required rules on the wall. Within a
month the sheet is yellow and the foreman still has the wire guard off the lathe, because,
he says, "it slows the job and the target is the target." The safety officer writes a
report; the owner reads it and does nothing. Two months later the guard is still off, and
the rules are still on the wall.

The sheet was regulation. The foreman's calculation was management philosophy. What stood
between them, or failed to, was internal control. This topic is about those three layers.

## Explanation

### Safety management: regulations of health, safety and environment

Safety management is the discipline of running the Unit 4.2 system day to day, and
regulation is the state's part in it. The instruments of regulation form a ladder, from
the broadest to the most specific: the **act** (parliament's law, for example factory and
labour legislation), the **rules and regulations** made under it (the enforceable detail),
the **codes of practice** (accepted good methods), and **guidance** (advisory, not
binding). Each instrument answers a different question: acts say what must be achieved,
regulations say how, codes say what counts as doing it well, and guidance helps where the
situation is unusual.

<Figure id="fig-U4-6" src="/img/figures/gnas-301/unit-04/fig-U4-6.svg" alt="Table of the four instruments of HSE regulation, act, rules and regulations, codes of practice and guidance, with what each does and a question it answers" width={780} height={470} />

Enforcement is the state's half of the bargain: inspections, improvement notices, and
penalties when the rules are broken. The Karachi site study's method, community
consultation beside site inspection, is a reminder that enforcement works best when the
people exposed are part of it (Abbasi et al., 2022).

### Internal control and management philosophy

<Glossary term="Internal control">Internal control</Glossary> is the set of measures an
organisation uses to make sure its own rules are actually followed: authorisation, records,
supervision, reconciliation, review. In safety terms it is a stack of layers, each catching
what the one above misses.

<Figure id="fig-U4-5" src="/img/figures/gnas-301/unit-04/fig-U4-5.svg" alt="Diagram of internal control as five stacked layers, management philosophy, policies, procedures, supervision and records, each catching what the one above misses" width={780} height={470} />

The top layer is the one everything else inherits: **management philosophy**, what the
organisation's leadership actually rewards. If the foreman's target is output and the
owner ignores the report, the guard stays off, whatever the sheet on the wall says. If
production stops when a guard is missing, the guards stay on. The philosophy is read off
what gets rewarded, not what gets written.

**A worked example.** Return to the workshop and add one layer at a time. A procedure that
says "guard off, machine off"; supervision with the authority to stop the line; a
near-miss book the owner reads weekly; and a philosophy that counts a stopped line as the
system working. With all four, the foreman's calculation changes, because the target now
includes the guard. Internal control did not make him careful; it made carelessness
expensive for the right person.

**Common misconception.** "Regulation is what makes workplaces safe." Regulation sets the
floor and the penalty, but the floor is only as real as the internal control that lives on
it. The world's best act does not unbolt a guard; a supervisor with authority and a
philosophy that backs him does. Both halves are needed, which is why this topic carries
them together.

## Activity: The ladder and the stack

Work in groups of three. Your teacher gives you four workplace situations, for example "a
farm wants to know the legal limit for pesticide re-entry" or "a mill wants to prove its
dust controls meet the standard". For each, decide which instrument of the regulatory
ladder answers it. Then take one situation and design the internal-control stack for it:
philosophy, policy, procedure, supervision, record. Allow 25 minutes.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the four instruments of regulation from broadest to most specific. *(Remember)*
2. Why do codes of practice sit between regulations and guidance? *(Understand)*
3. The foreman keeps the guard off because "the target is the target". Which layer of
   internal control is failing first, and why? *(Analyze)*
4. Design the two cheapest internal-control layers a ten-worker workshop could add this
   month. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Safety management runs the system daily, and regulation supplies the ladder of
instruments, act, regulations, codes and guidance, each answering a different question,
with enforcement as the state's half. Internal control is the organisation's half:
philosophy, policy, procedure, supervision and records, stacked so each layer catches what
the one above misses. The philosophy layer, what leadership actually rewards, decides
whether the others function. Regulation sets the floor; internal control makes it real.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find one rule the school has on paper, for example the fire drill. Trace its internal
control: who supervises it, what is recorded, who reads the record, and what would happen
if the head teacher cancelled it without cause. Write four sentences naming the layers.

## Summative task

Write 150 to 200 words on the following.

> An owner argues, "We follow the Factory Rules; the rest is our business." Using the
> regulatory ladder and the internal-control stack, explain what the law cannot reach and
> what the workshop must build itself, ending with the one layer that matters most.
