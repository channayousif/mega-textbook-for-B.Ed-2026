# Topic 4.5 - Principles, measures and models of HSE management

## A real classroom situation

A student asks the safety officer from topic 4.3's workshop why he keeps the near-miss
book at all. "Nothing in it is an accident," she says. He shows her the page: a dropped
spanner, a slipped ladder, a smoke smell. "The pyramid says the accidents live under
these," he says. "Count the small ones and you have counted the big one's shadow."

This topic is the theory behind his notebook: the principles that connect management to
safety, the measures that make it visible, and the models that explain why small things
are big things in disguise.

## Explanation

### Key principles of management and HSE; measures and models

Management runs on four functions, plan, organise, lead, control, and HSE borrows them
wholesale. Applied to safety: **plan** the objectives and controls (Topic 4.2's PDCA),
**organise** people, equipment and authority around them, **lead** by what is rewarded
(Topic 4.3's philosophy layer), and **control** by measuring and correcting. The
principles that tie the functions to HSE are old and reliable: safety is a line
responsibility, not a staff department; every hazard gets an owner; and what is measured
is what gets managed.

<Figure id="fig-U4-9" src="/img/figures/gnas-301/unit-04/fig-U4-9.svg" alt="Concept map linking the four management functions, plan, organise, lead and control, to a central safe workplace node and the outcomes safe people, safe plant, safe procedures and safe place" width={780} height={470} />

The **measures** are the indicators of Topic 4.2, led by the leading kind, plus audits and
safety-climate surveys that ask the workers what management's philosophy actually is. The
**models** give the measures their meaning.

- The <Glossary term="Incident pyramid">incident pyramid</Glossary>: for every serious
  injury, a large number of minor injuries and a very much larger number of near-misses
  and unsafe acts sit beneath it. The exact ratios are debated; the shape is not. The
  spanner and the smoke smell are the base of a pyramid whose apex is the fatality.
- The <Glossary term="Swiss cheese model">Swiss cheese model</Glossary> of accident
  causation: defences are slices of cheese with holes, and harm passes through when the
  holes line up. The Karachi fire's locked door, missing alarm, stored combustibles and
  unaudited housekeeping were four holes lining up. Every model of control is an attempt
  to keep the holes from aligning.
- Near-miss reporting, formalised, is the cheapest measure in the set: the pyramid's base
  is where the next apex is visible.

### Organizational environment

The <Glossary term="Organizational environment">organizational environment</Glossary> is
the workplace's own climate: how work is actually divided, how pressure is applied, how
bad news travels. It is the medium the models live in. A mill where the near-miss book is
read has a different organisational environment from one where it is used to blame, and
the two will produce different pyramids from identical machinery. The environment is
measurable, by the questions workers answer about who gets punished for reporting, and it
is manageable, by the same philosophy layer as ever.

**A worked example.** Read the uncle's mill through the models. The near-misses, the
dropped bale, the unreported cough, are the pyramid's base. The defences, the guard, the
mask rule, the annual medical, are cheese slices with holes (the guard off for
convenience, the mask rule unenforced). The near-miss book, if the officer from 4.4 opens
one, is the measure that shows both. Nothing new is needed except the reading.

**Common misconception.** "The pyramid's ratios mean small accidents cause big ones."
Near-misses do not cause fatalities; they share causes with them. That is the point: the
base and the apex grow from the same soil, so gardening the base, fixing the small
failures, shrinks the apex you never see coming.

## Activity: Find the holes

Work in pairs. Your teacher gives you the Karachi fire's four failures as cards: locked
exits, no alarm, stored combustibles, no housekeeping audit. Draw four cheese slices,
place one hole per slice, and show the arrow of harm passing through. Then add a fifth
slice of your own design that would have stopped the arrow, and name which control level
it sits at. Allow 20 minutes.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the four management functions and match each to one HSE action. *(Remember)*
2. What does the shape of the incident pyramid tell a safety officer to count, and why?
   *(Understand)*
3. The Swiss cheese model calls defences "slices with holes". Explain the Karachi fire in
   those terms. *(Analyze)*
4. A near-miss book exists but is empty. Is that good news? Use the pyramid and the
   organizational environment in your answer. *(Analyze)*

## Summary

HSE management borrows management's four functions, plan, organise, lead, control, and
binds them to safety by principles: safety is a line responsibility, every hazard has an
owner, and what is measured is managed. The measures are led by leading indicators and
near-miss reporting; the models, the incident pyramid and the Swiss cheese model, explain
why the small and the serious share causes and why defences fail by alignment. The
organizational environment, how pressure and bad news actually travel, is the medium that
decides whether the models are used or ignored.

- [ ] I can explain the pyramid's shape and what to count because of it.
- [ ] I can redraw a real case as cheese slices and add a working slice.
- [ ] I can explain why an empty near-miss book is not good news.

## Try this at your practicum school

Start a one-week near-miss book for the school: any event that could have hurt someone
but did not, one line each, no names. At week's end, read the entries aloud without
comment and count them. Bring back the count and one sentence on what the pyramid says
about it.

## Summative task

Write 160 to 200 words on the following.

> A director says, "We have had no accidents for two years, so we are safe." Using the
> incident pyramid, the leading-versus-lagging distinction and the organizational
> environment, write the reply, ending with the two measurements you would start this
> month.

| Criterion | What a strong response does |
