# Topic 2.2 - Water pollution and industrial effluents

## A real classroom situation

In a settlement on the edge of Hyderabad, a hand pump stands twenty paces from an open drain.
The drain carries grey water from houses and, twice a week, a dark discharge from a small
textile unit upstream. A grandmother tells the health visitor, "The pump water looks clear,
but three of my grandchildren have had loose motions this month." The visitor takes a sample
bottle from her bag. Clear, she says, is not the same as clean.

That drain and that pump are the whole of this topic in one picture: a source, a pathway, a
receptor, and a health effect waiting to be measured.

## Explanation

### Water pollution: sources, types, impacts and remedies

<Glossary term="Water pollution">Water pollution</Glossary> is the addition of substances or
heat to water at a level that harms its use by people or by living things. Its logic is
always the same chain: a **source** releases a pollutant, a **pathway** carries it, water in
a river, canal, drain or aquifer, and a **receptor** receives it: a drinking well, a fish, a
swimmer, a crop. Every remedy in this topic is a cut made at one link of that chain.

<Figure id="fig-U2-3" src="/img/figures/gnas-301/unit-02/fig-U2-3.svg" alt="Flowchart of the water pollution pathway from source through pathway to receptor to health effect, with remedy points marked at each arrow" width={780} height={470} />

The main pollutant types, with their usual sources:

- <Glossary term="Pathogen">Pathogens</Glossary>, disease-causing organisms from human and
  animal waste. The world's heaviest water burden: the WHO estimates about 505,000
  diarrhoeal deaths each year from microbiologically contaminated drinking water (WHO,
  2023).
- **Organic matter**, food, slaughterhouse and dairy waste, that decays in water and uses up
  its oxygen, suffocating fish.
- **Nutrients**, nitrogen and phosphorus from fertiliser run-off and detergent, which
  over-fertilise water. The algal bloom that follows, and the oxygen collapse when it dies,
  is <Glossary term="Eutrophication">eutrophication</Glossary>.
- **Heavy metals**, chromium, lead, arsenic, from industry and old geology; they persist and
  accumulate.
- **Heat and suspended solids**, less discussed but real: hot discharge and silt both change
  what a water body can support.

<Figure id="fig-U2-4" src="/img/figures/gnas-301/unit-02/fig-U2-4.svg" alt="Table of five water pollutant types with usual source, impact and one remedy for each" width={780} height={470} />

Remedies sit at the three links. At the **source**: latrines and sewerage, phasing
phosphorus detergents, keeping waste out of the drain. At the **pathway**: treatment
(chemical dosing, filtration), protecting wellheads, and <Glossary term="Sanitation">sanitation</Glossary> figures show
what source control is worth: the WHO attributes 1.4 million deaths a year to inadequate
water, sanitation and hygiene (WHO, 2024). At the **receptor**: boiling or chlorinating
drinking water, and closed wells with proper sealing.

### Industrial effluents: sources, impacts, treatment and recycling

An <Glossary term="Effluent">effluent</Glossary> is the liquid waste a facility discharges.
Textile dyeing, tanning, sugar, paper and steel each produce a characteristic one: tanneries
carry chromium, dye houses carry colour and salts, sugar mills carry a heavy organic load.
Untreated, the effects arrive down the chain: a drain that kills fish, land that burns
crops irrigated with it, and groundwater that carries the metals for decades.

Treatment runs in stages, each removing a different kind of load.

- **Primary treatment** screens and settles: grit and a share of solids drop out. It is
  cheap and removes the visible load.
- **Secondary treatment** puts bacteria to work on the dissolved organic matter, in tanks or
  trickling filters, and settles their remains. This is the step that restores oxygen to the
  receiving water.
- **Tertiary treatment** polishes what is left: filtration, adsorption and special steps for
  metals, salts or nutrients, so the water can be reused or discharged safely.

**Recycling closes the loop.** A factory that treats its own effluent can return it to
washing, cooling or irrigation instead of the river, which cuts both its draw on fresh water
and its discharge. Pakistan's National Environmental Quality Standards for municipal and
liquid industrial effluents are the yardstick a treatment plant is built to meet. For each
parameter they set a maximum concentration in milligrams per litre, with separate columns for
discharge into inland waters, into sewage treatment and into the sea; chromium, for example,
is capped at 1.0 mg/L in every column, and effluent may not be diluted with fresh water to
meet a limit (Pakistan EPA, 2000).

**A worked example.** Take the Hyderabad drain. The textile unit is the source; primary
treatment would settle its fibre bits, secondary would eat its dye organics, tertiary would
treat its salts and colour. The household grey water needs a different, cheaper chain, and
the well needs a sealed head and a test. One drain, three links, different remedies at each.

**Common misconception.** "Water that looks clear is safe." The most dangerous pollutants are
invisible: pathogens by the million, dissolved metals, nitrate. Clear is a colour, not a
test. Boiling or chlorinating kills pathogens, but it does not remove dissolved metals or
nitrate, and boiling can even concentrate them. Only a test shows whether those are present,
and only treatment built for that pollutant removes them. Trust clear water only when it
comes from a protected source, has been boiled or disinfected against germs, and, where
metals or nitrate are suspected, has been tested and treated for them.

## Activity: Trace the drain

Work in pairs. Your teacher gives each pair a sketch map of a neighbourhood with one canal,
six buildings (houses, a workshop, a clinic) and two hand pumps marked. Mark every possible
source of water pollution on the map, draw the pathway arrows to the canal and the pumps,
and mark who or what receives each. Then choose the single cheapest remedy that would cut
the most arrows. Allow 20 minutes, then compare maps with another pair.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the three links of the pollution chain and give one example of each from your own
   surroundings. *(Remember)*
2. Which pollutant type causes eutrophication, and what happens in the water when it does?
   *(Understand)*
3. A tannery discharges chromium. Which treatment stage is built for it, and what remains if
   that stage is skipped? *(Apply)*
4. Explain why "the water looks clear" is not evidence that a well is safe. Use two different
   pollutant types in your answer. *(Analyze)*

## Summary

Water pollution runs source, pathway, receptor, effect, and every remedy cuts one of those
links. The pollutant types, pathogens, organic matter, nutrients, heavy metals, heat and
silt, each pair a usual source with a matching impact and remedy. Industrial effluents carry
a concentrated mix and are treated in stages, primary for solids, secondary for organics,
tertiary for metals, salts and colour, after which the water can be recycled instead of
discharged. Clear water is not tested water: the deadliest pollutants are invisible.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find the school's nearest drain or watercourse. Walk ten minutes along it, note what enters
it (pipes, open channels, dumped waste) and where it goes. Draw the chain, source, pathway,
receptor, for the two biggest inputs, and name one remedy for each that a neighbourhood
committee could actually ask for. Do not name the school or any person.
