# Topic 2.3 - Solid and hazardous waste

## A real classroom situation

A trainee teacher on practicum in Karachi walks past the school gate at 7 a.m. The sweeper is
burning the day's rubbish in a corner of the lane: paper, plastic bags, food scraps and, on
top, a cardboard box with a red stripe that has come loose from the clinic next door. The
smoke drifts across the assembly line. The trainee has just read about the waste hierarchy,
and she now sees three failures in one heap: waste that should have been reduced, waste that
should have been separated, and clinical waste that should never have been in the lane at
all.

This topic gives you the principles that heap breaks: how a city manages its solid waste, and
how the hazardous part, hospital and industrial waste above all, is kept apart.

## Explanation

### The principles of municipal solid waste management

<Glossary term="Municipal solid waste">Municipal solid waste</Glossary> is the everyday waste
of households, shops, schools and offices: food scraps, paper, plastic, glass, metal, garden
waste and dust. Managing it is a system with five stages: generation, storage at source,
collection, transport, and disposal or recovery. A city fails at whichever stage it neglects;
the burning heap at the school gate is a storage and collection failure before it is anything
else.

The ordering principle for the whole system is the waste hierarchy, a staircase from best to
worst.

<Figure id="fig-U2-5" src="/img/figures/gnas-301/unit-02/fig-U2-5.svg" alt="Flowchart of the waste hierarchy as a descending staircase, reduce, reuse, recycle, recover, dispose, each with a school-scale action" width={780} height={620} />

- <Glossary term="Waste hierarchy">Reduce</Glossary> comes first because waste never made
  needs no bin, no truck and no landfill. This is Unit 1's strongest step, restated for a
  city.
- **Reuse** gives an object a second life without reprocessing it.
- <Glossary term="Recycling">Recycle</Glossary> turns material into material, and depends on
  the recovery chain buying clean, separated stock: paper, cardboard, metal, some plastics.
- **Recover** extracts what is left, compost for organic waste, or energy where a proper
  facility exists.
- **Dispose** is the last resort, and even then a lined, managed site, not an open heap.

The stakes are real. A study of Pakistan's metropolitan dumpsites estimated decades of
methane emissions and leachate from Lahore's disposal systems alone at about 108 million
tonnes of carbon-dioxide equivalent, and leachate, the liquid that drains from waste, is a
direct threat to groundwater (Ilmas et al., 2026). The WHO reports that 44 per cent of
household wastewater worldwide is discharged without safe treatment (WHO, 2024); the solid
waste story is the same shape. Segregation at source, keeping the streams apart while they
are still clean, is the step that makes every later step possible.

### Hazardous waste management: hospital and industrial waste

<Glossary term="Hazardous waste">Hazardous waste</Glossary> is waste whose properties make it
dangerous to people or the environment: infectious, toxic, flammable, corrosive or sharp. The
cardinal rule is that it never mixes with the ordinary stream, because a bag of hospital
waste in a municipal heap turns the whole heap hazardous.

Hospital, or clinical, waste sorts into two streams. The World Health Organization reports
that about 85 per cent of health-care waste is general, comparable to household waste, while
the remaining 15 per cent is hazardous, infectious, toxic, flammable or corrosive (WHO,
2024). The **general** stream, offices and kitchens, behaves like municipal waste. The
**risk** stream is what the rules exist for: sharps (needles, blades), infectious waste
(dressings, laboratory cultures), pathological waste, pharmaceutical waste and chemical
waste. The WHO's key elements for handling it are to minimise the waste, segregate it
properly, handle, store and transport it safely, and treat it before final disposal. Where
feasible, the WHO favours autoclaving, microwaving, steam or chemical treatment over
incineration, because open burning and low-temperature incineration can release dioxins and
furans (WHO, 2024). The detailed rules, such as which container colours to use, how sharps
are boxed and who may collect and treat the waste, are set by provincial law and the
facility's own waste plan, so follow the local rules rather than a general list. When the
system fails, the WHO notes that waste handlers, and people who scavenge or sort waste by
hand, are at immediate risk of needle-stick injuries and of exposure to toxic or infectious
material (WHO, 2024).

Industrial hazardous waste, solvent sludges, electroplating liquids, chemical drums, follows
the same logic as good practice: know what the waste is, minimise it, store it in contained,
labelled space, and keep a record of where it goes until it is treated or disposed of. The
exact paperwork and who may receive the waste are matters for provincial environmental law,
not for this general outline.

<Figure id="fig-U2-6" src="/img/figures/gnas-301/unit-02/fig-U2-6.svg" alt="Table comparing hospital clinical waste and industrial hazardous waste with typical items, the hazard and the handling rule" width={780} height={470} />

**A worked example.** Return to the burning heap. The paper and food scraps belong lower on
the hierarchy, separated for the recovery chain and for compost. The plastic bags are a
reduce failure from the day they were issued. And the red-striped box is the sharpest lesson:
clinical waste in the municipal stream, burning at a school gate, exposing the sweeper, the
pupils and the whole lane to smoke from infectious material. Three streams, three rules, one
heap that broke all of them.

**Common misconception.** "Waste management means collection." Collection is one stage of
five, and it is not the first. The first is what household and shop decide to generate and
separate. A city cannot recycle, compost or safely dispose what arrives mixed and dirty; the
sorting that happens at the bin decides the fate of everything after it.

## Activity: Bin audit, three streams

Work in groups of three. With permission, and only after your teacher has checked that the
bins hold no clinical or sharp material, examine one day of your classroom block's waste.
Never touch the waste with bare hands: use tongs or gloved hands and a spread sheet. If you
find anything sharp, clinical or unsafe, stop and tell your teacher. If the waste is not
safe to handle, do a visual audit instead, from photographs of a sorted heap. Sort the waste
into three streams on the spread sheet: recoverable (clean paper, cardboard, metal), organic (food,
garden), and residual (the rest, including anything soiled). Weigh or count each stream,
then answer: what share never needed to be generated? Allow 30 minutes, including washing
hands afterwards.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the five steps of the waste hierarchy in order, from best to worst. *(Remember)*
2. Why does reduce sit above recycle, and what does recycling depend on that reduce does
   not? *(Understand)*
3. A clinic's dressings end up in the municipal bin. Name the rule that was broken and two
   groups of people put at risk. *(Apply)*
4. Your city has money for one change: a fleet of trucks, or a segregation campaign with
   bins. Use the hierarchy and the dumpsite evidence to argue for one. *(Analyze)*

## Summary

Municipal solid waste runs through five stages, generation, storage, collection, transport,
disposal or recovery, ordered by the waste hierarchy: reduce, reuse, recycle, recover,
dispose. Pakistan's open dumpsites show the cost of skipping the hierarchy in methane and
leachate. Hazardous waste, hospital risk waste and industrial waste above all, must never
enter the municipal stream: minimise it, segregate it at the point of generation, store and
transport it safely, and treat it before final disposal, following the local rules. Waste
handlers and people who sort waste by hand are at immediate risk when these rules fail.

## Try this at your practicum school

With your cooperating teacher's agreement, run the two-bin classroom separation from Unit 1
for one week, then extend it by one stream: a covered bin for anything wet or soiled, kept
out of the recoverable bin. Count the three streams at week's end and report the shares.
Note one thing the school could change so that the residual stream shrinks.
