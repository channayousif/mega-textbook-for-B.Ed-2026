# Topic 1.2 - People, activities and environmental problems

## A real classroom situation

In a boys' high school in lower Sindh, the science teacher takes his Class 9 pupils to the
edge of a field near the school. The crop stands in patches: some plants green, some yellow
and dying. An old farmer tells them, "Our fathers grew good wheat here. Now the water stands
too long in some parts and the soil has turned white in others." Back in the classroom, the
teacher writes two words on the board: waterlogging and salinity. "This field," he says, "is
not just a farming problem. It is a story about us."

The field is a story about people changing their environment, and the environment answering
back. This topic is about that two-way relationship, which the course guide calls the nexus
between environmental problems and anthropogenic activities, and about how such problems
appear at three scales: global, regional and local.

## Explanation

### The nexus between environmental problems and human activities

<Glossary term="Anthropogenic">Anthropogenic</Glossary> means caused by people. An
anthropogenic environmental problem is one that either did not exist before human activity
created it, or existed only mildly and was made worse by it. The nexus is the two-way link:
activities change the environment, and the changed environment returns the harm to people,
sometimes to the same people, often to different and poorer people, and sometimes years later.

The chain usually runs in four steps. An activity, such as farming, industry, transport,
energy use or waste disposal, takes something from the environment or puts something into it.
The environment responds: water tables rise, air goes grey, forests thin. The response
crosses back into human life: crops fail, illness rises, floods damage homes. And the human
response to that harm often starts the chain again, as when a failing crop pushes farmers to
clear more land.

<Figure id="fig-U1-3" src="/img/figures/gnas-301/unit-01/fig-U1-3.svg" alt="Concept map linking five human activities on the left, farming, industry, transport, energy use and waste disposal, through a central anthropogenic nexus hub, to five environmental problems on the right, waterlogging and salinity, air pollution, deforestation, water pollution and climate change" width={780} height={470} />

**A worked example, fully traced.** Take the yellow field near the Sindh school. Step one,
the activity: farmers in the last century expanded canal irrigation across lower Sindh to
grow more food. Step two, the environmental response: canals seeped, fields were watered more
than the soil could drain, the water table rose, and the salts naturally present in the soil
were drawn up and left on the surface as the water evaporated. Step three, the harm returned:
waterlogged and salt-affected soil yields less, and the white crust you can see is the
evidence. Step four, the response: some farmers abandon the land or dig tube wells, which can
draw up even saltier water. One chain, four steps, every link human and environmental at once.

The same four-step structure fits the other big activities. Industry and transport burn fuel
and load the air with pollutants. Energy choices, from coal to firewood, drive both local
smoke and global greenhouse gases. Waste disposal, when it means open dumping or burning,
moves the problem from the bin to the air, the soil and the water. The United Nations Decade
on Ecosystem Restoration describes the present situation plainly: there has never been a more
urgent need to restore damaged ecosystems (UN, 2021).

### Global, regional and local issues and their current status

Environmental problems are usually sorted by how far their causes and effects reach.

- **Global issues** affect the whole planet, whatever their source. Climate change is the
  clearest case: greenhouse gases mix through one shared atmosphere, and the World Health
  Organization reports that about 3.6 billion people already live in places highly
  susceptible to its effects (WHO, 2023). The loss of biodiversity is the second: the WHO
  also reports that around one million species face extinction risk (WHO, 2025). Ozone
  depletion, now slowly recovering thanks to a global agreement, is the third.
- **Regional issues** cross borders or cover a region. South Asia shares the smog that builds
  each winter, the waters of the Indus and its tributaries, and the Himalayan glaciers whose
  melt feeds the rivers. The Indus system's stress is a regional issue: what happens upstream
  in one country changes the flow downstream in another.
- **Local issues** are tied to a place, though they repeat everywhere. A neighbourhood's
  burning garbage heap, a village's contaminated hand pump, a school's dusty courtyard, one
  city's traffic noise.

<Figure id="fig-U1-4" src="/img/figures/gnas-301/unit-01/fig-U1-4.svg" alt="Table comparing global, regional and local environmental issues with an example, its current status and who feels it" width={780} height={470} />

The scales are connected, which is the point of showing them together. A local burning heap
adds carbon to the global atmosphere. A global rise in temperature shows up locally as
heatwaves in Karachi. When you analyse an issue, name its scale, but also ask how it crosses
to the others.

**Common misconception.** Many people think global problems are the "real" environmental
problems and local ones are too small to count. The reverse error also appears: that only
local problems matter because they are the ones we can see. Both are wrong. Global problems
are built from millions of local activities, and local people suffer global problems first
and worst. The scales are one system, not a competition.

## Activity: Headline scale sort

Work in groups of three. Your teacher will give you one week of environmental headlines from
a Pakistani newspaper. Sort each headline into global, regional or local. For two headlines,
one that surprised your group and one you argued about, write one sentence naming the human
activity behind the problem and one sentence naming a connection to a different scale. Allow
25 minutes, then each group presents its two headlines.

## Summary

Environmental problems and human activities are linked in a two-way nexus: activities change
the environment, and the changed environment returns harm to people. The chain runs activity,
environmental response, harm returning, human response. Irrigation-induced waterlogging and
salinity in lower Sindh is one complete worked chain. Issues appear at three scales, global
such as climate change and biodiversity loss, regional such as the Indus system and winter
smog, and local such as a burning waste heap, and the scales connect in both directions.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find the nearest environmental problem to the school gate, a drain, a heap, a dusty road, a
smoky stove. In half a page, name the scale you would call it, the human activity that drives
it, and one way it connects to a bigger scale. Ask one person who lives nearby how long it
has been there. Do not name the school or the person.

## Summative task

Write 150 to 200 words on the following.

> Choose ONE local environmental issue you have personally seen in Sindh. Trace it through
> the four nexus steps, then explain one connection it has to a regional or global issue.
> Finish with one sentence naming who bears most of its harm.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong response does |
