# Topic 1.1 - What environmental science studies, and the planet it studies

## A real classroom situation

Miss Farzana teaches Class 8 in a government girls' school in Karachi. It is November, and
the city's air has turned grey. Before the annual sports day, she checks the air quality
index, or AQI, on her phone the way other teachers check the weather. The reading is
"unhealthy". She moves the races to the morning, shortens them, and tells the girls with
asthma to sit out the long-distance events. A colleague asks her, half joking, "Since when
are you a scientist?" Miss Farzana answers, "Since the air started deciding whether my pupils
can run."

Her decision used a measurement, a standard, a health effect, and a judgement. That is
environmental science in one classroom moment. This topic defines the field, maps its scope,
and describes the planet it studies.

## Explanation

### What environmental science is, and why it matters

<Glossary term="Environmental science">Environmental science</Glossary> is the study of the
natural environment and the way people interact with it. It asks three linked questions: what
is in our surroundings, how do human activities change those surroundings, and how do the
changes come back to affect people? It is not one discipline but a meeting point of several:
chemistry for pollutants, biology for living things, physics for energy and climate,
geography for where things happen, and the social sciences for why people act as they do.

The scope of the subject is easiest to see as five areas of questioning. The table below
shows each area with one question a teacher in Sindh might actually ask.

<Figure id="fig-U1-1" src="/img/figures/gnas-301/unit-01/fig-U1-1.svg" alt="Diagram of planet Earth showing the four spheres, atmosphere, hydrosphere, lithosphere and biosphere, each labelled with a Pakistani example, with a school building placed inside the biosphere to show that people are part of it" width={780} height={470} />

The importance of the subject is practical. Pakistan faces real environmental pressures:
city air that repeatedly crosses unhealthy levels, a river system under strain, and a climate
that is warming. A teacher who understands the subject can read an AQI number, explain why a
canal smells, and answer pupils' questions with evidence instead of guesses. The World Health
Organization reports that almost the whole world's population, 99 per cent, breathes air that
exceeds its guideline levels (WHO, 2024). That single fact explains why this course exists.

A note on the word itself: <Glossary term="Natural environment">the natural
environment</Glossary> means everything around us that is not made by people: air, water,
soil, rocks, plants, animals and the climate, together with the processes that link them. A
classroom is not part of the natural environment, but the air its windows open onto is.

### The natural environment and the structure of planet Earth

Scientists describe the natural environment as four great spheres that meet and overlap.

- The **atmosphere** is the envelope of gases around the Earth. Its lowest layer, where
  weather happens, holds the air we breathe and the pollutants we add to it. The monsoon
  winds that water Sindh's crops are atmosphere at work.
- The **hydrosphere** is all the water: oceans, rivers, lakes, groundwater, glaciers and
  ice. The Indus river system, the Arabian Sea and the water under the Thar desert are all
  hydrosphere.
- The **lithosphere** is the solid outer part of the Earth: rocks, soil and the landforms
  they make. The soil that grows Sindh's wheat and the coal seams beneath Thar sit in the
  lithosphere.
- The <Glossary term="Biosphere">biosphere</Glossary> is the thin zone where life exists,
  where the other three spheres meet: the mangrove forests of the Indus Delta, the fish in
  Keenjhar Lake, the crops in the fields, and the people in the cities.

Beneath the biosphere, the Earth itself is layered: a thin crust, a thick mantle, and a core
of iron. The crust matters most for this course, because that is where soil forms, water is
stored, minerals sit and everything we build stands. When we speak of "using natural
resources", we almost always mean taking something from the crust, the hydrosphere or the
biosphere faster than it can be replaced.

<Figure id="fig-U1-2" src="/img/figures/gnas-301/unit-01/fig-U1-2.svg" alt="Table listing the five scope areas of environmental science, air, water, land, living things and human systems, each with a guiding question and a Sindh classroom example" width={780} height={470} />

**A worked example.** Place a cup of tea on the desk. The water came from the hydrosphere,
boiled by gas from the lithosphere. The tea leaf grew in the biosphere, in soil weathered
from the lithosphere, watered by the hydrosphere, under the atmosphere's rain. The cup, the
gas stove and the price of the tea belong to human systems, which environmental science also
studies, because people are part of the environment, not visitors to it. One object, four
spheres, one human system. That is the analytical habit this topic wants you to build.

**Common misconception.** Many people think "the environment" means nature far away from
people: mountains, forests, wild animals. The four-sphere picture corrects this. People live
inside the biosphere, drink the hydrosphere, farm the lithosphere and breathe the atmosphere.
There is no "away". A city street is as much an environment as a mangrove creek, and the
subjects of this course, from air quality to waste, are mostly about the environments people
live in every day.

## Activity: Sort the questions

Work in pairs. Your teacher will give you eight short newspaper headlines from a Pakistani
paper, for example "Smog closes Lahore schools", "Indus flows low at Kotri", "New coal plant
approved", "Fish deaths reported at Keenjhar". For each headline, decide two things: which
scope area it belongs to (air, water, land, living things, human systems), and which spheres
are involved. Be ready to defend one answer that surprised you. Allow 20 minutes.

## Check your understanding

1. Define environmental science in one sentence, in your own words. *(Remember)*
2. Name the four spheres and give one example of each from your own district. *(Understand)*
3. A pupil says, "Environmental science is just about pollution." Give two reasons why this
   is too narrow. *(Understand)*
4. Place these three things in their sphere: the Indus river, Thar coal, the Indus Delta
   mangroves. Then explain why the mangroves belong to more than one sphere. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Environmental science studies the natural environment and the human-environment interaction
across five scope areas: air, water, land, living things and human systems. The natural
environment is organised into four overlapping spheres: atmosphere, hydrosphere, lithosphere
and biosphere, with people living inside the biosphere. The Earth beneath is layered into
crust, mantle and core, and the crust is where most resources sit. The subject matters in
Pakistan because its pressures, unhealthy city air, a strained river system and a warming
climate, are already inside every classroom.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can define environmental science and name its five scope areas.
- [ ] I can name the four spheres and give a Pakistani example of each.
- [ ] I can explain why people are part of the biosphere, not separate from it.
- [ ] I can take one everyday object and trace it through the four spheres.

## Try this at your practicum school

Walk once around the school building with a notebook. Find one example of each sphere inside
or beside the school grounds: the air over the courtyard, the water source or water tank, the
soil in the garden or the dust on the windowsill, and any living thing from a tree to a
sparrow. Write four sentences, one per sphere, naming what you found and one human activity
that affects it. Do not name the school or any pupil.
