# Topic 5.2 - Fate of toxins: detoxification and bioactivation

## A real classroom situation

A farm worker in lower Sindh sprays cotton without gloves. That evening his hands tingle
and his vision blurs at the edges. The health worker who sees him is puzzled by one
thing: the pesticide container says the chemical is "weak", yet the man is sicker than
the label promised. The puzzle's answer is inside his liver: the body, trying to
disarm the chemical, turned it into something more poisonous first.

The body has a waste-treatment plant, and it is honest work to learn its plumbing. This
topic is that plumbing.

## Explanation

### Fate of absorbed toxins and xenobiotics, including detoxification and bioactivation

A <Glossary term="Xenobiotic">xenobiotic</Glossary> is any chemical foreign to the body,
pesticides, drugs, industrial solvents, pollutants. Once absorbed, every xenobiotic
follows the same four-stage journey.

<Figure id="fig-U5-3" src="/img/figures/gnas-301/unit-05/fig-U5-3.svg" alt="Flowchart of the fate of a xenobiotic, absorption, distribution, biotransformation with its two outcomes detoxification and bioactivation, then excretion or storage" width={780} height={470} />

1. **Absorption**: entry by the lung (fastest, Unit 5.1's door), the gut, or the skin,
   the farm worker's door.
2. **Distribution**: the blood carries the chemical everywhere, but each has its
   favourite parking places, the liver, the fat, the bones, the brain.
3. <Glossary term="Biotransformation">Biotransformation</Glossary>: the liver's chemical
   workshops, and the body's main defence, rework the chemical to make it easier to
   export. Usually this works. The reworked product is less harmful and more
   water-soluble, and the kidneys can send it out: this outcome is
   <Glossary term="Detoxification">detoxification</Glossary>. But the same machinery can
   also produce, mid-process, a product more poisonous than the original: this outcome is
   <Glossary term="Bioactivation">bioactivation</Glossary>, and it is the answer to the
   health worker's puzzle.
4. **Excretion or storage**: the finished products leave by kidney, breath or bile; the
   stubborn ones park in fat and bone, sometimes for years.

<Figure id="fig-U5-4" src="/img/figures/gnas-301/unit-05/fig-U5-4.svg" alt="Table comparing detoxification and bioactivation with what the liver produces, a worked example and the risk each carries" width={780} height={470} />

**A worked example, two of them.** Detoxification: the grandfather's CO from Topic 5.1
does not stay bound forever; the lungs slowly breathe it back out once he is in clean
air, and the body's own repair clears the rest. That is detoxification by exit.
Bioactivation: some organophosphate pesticides, the cotton-field kind, are weak in the
container and are converted by the liver into their active, nerve-attacking form, which
is why the sprayer's evening symptoms outran the label. The WHO's chemical-safety work,
through its programme on chemical safety, exists precisely because the container's label
is not the body's biography (WHO, n.d.).

**Common misconception.** "The liver detoxifies, so a chemical becomes harmless once it
is inside." Two errors hide in that sentence. Biotransformation is not always
detoxification, as the sprayer learned; and even successful detoxification takes time,
during which the dose is doing its work. The liver is a treatment plant with a queue,
not a shield.

## Activity: Journey map

Work in pairs. Your teacher gives each pair one substance card: CO, a pesticide, lead,
alcohol. Draw the four-stage journey for your card, naming the entry door, the parking
place, the liver's likely outcome, and the exit. Mark the stage where the harm actually
happens. Then compare maps with the pair who had the same card. Allow 20 minutes.

## Check your understanding

1. Define xenobiotic and name the three doors of absorption. *(Remember)*
2. What is the difference between detoxification and bioactivation? One sentence each.
   *(Understand)*
3. Why did the sprayer get sicker than the label promised? Name the stage responsible.
   *(Analyze)*
4. Lead parks in bone for years. Which two stages of the journey does that fact connect?
   *(Apply)*

## Summary

Every absorbed xenobiotic follows the same journey: absorption by lung, gut or skin;
distribution by the blood to its parking places; biotransformation in the liver, whose
outcome is usually detoxification, a less harmful, exportable product, but sometimes
bioactivation, a more poisonous one, as the cotton-sprayer's organophosphate showed; and
excretion by kidney, breath or bile, or storage in fat and bone. The liver is a
treatment plant with a queue, not a shield, and the label is not the body's biography.
