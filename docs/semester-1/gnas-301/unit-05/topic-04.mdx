# Topic 5.4 - Global frameworks: MEAs, SDGs and environmental law

## A real classroom situation

A trainee teacher reads two news items on the same morning. The first: another round of
global climate talks ends with a promise to "pursue efforts" toward limiting warming.
The second: a union council in her district cannot say which law, if any, covers the
drain behind the school. She asks her tutor the fair question: what do the world's
agreements have to do with that drain?

This topic is the answer: the same risk management she already knows, run at planetary
scale, and the laws that carry it down to the drain.

## Explanation

### Risk management

Risk management at environmental scale is Unit 3's cycle enlarged: identify the hazard
(arsenic in a district's wells, PM2.5 in a city's air), assess the risk (who is exposed,
at what dose, with what severity), control it (Unit 3's hierarchy still applies: remove
the exposure, treat the water, clean the air), and monitor and review (test again,
measure again). The management options have names worth learning: **avoid** the exposure
altogether, **reduce** it by treatment or control, **transfer** the burden, insurance,
compensation, and **accept** it knowingly when the cost of removing it exceeds the harm.
A school near a busy road can avoid (relocate the playing time), reduce (filtration,
green belts), transfer (health cover for asthmatic staff), or accept (with the AQI
watch running), and the honest choice is usually a mix.

### Multilateral environmental agreements

A <Glossary term="Multilateral environmental agreement">multilateral environmental
agreement</Glossary>, or MEA, is a treaty among many states that commits them to manage
a shared environmental problem. States negotiate, sign, ratify, and then report against
their commitments, which is what makes an MEA more than a press release. The major ones
a teacher should recognise:

<Figure id="fig-U5-7" src="/img/figures/gnas-301/unit-05/fig-U5-7.svg" alt="Timeline of multilateral environmental agreements from Stockholm 1972 to Paris 2015, six marked points each with what it controls" width={780} height={470} />

- **Stockholm 1972**, the first world environment conference, where the framework began.
- **Basel 1989**, controls the cross-border trade in hazardous waste.
- **Rio 1992**, the Earth Summit, produced the climate convention (UNFCCC) and the
  biodiversity convention.
- **Stockholm POPs 2001**, bans or restricts persistent organic pollutants, the
  long-lived chemicals that build up in bodies; researchers note its monitoring
  challenge, that POPs harm even at low levels, which is exactly why it exists
  (Guardans & Castro-Jimenez, 2013).
- **Minamata 2013**, controls mercury, named for the Japanese bay whose poisoning
  taught the world the word.
- **Paris 2015**, the climate agreement: hold warming well below 2 degrees and pursue
  1.5, through five-yearly national pledges, adopted by 195 parties (UN, n.d.).

### Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)

The <Glossary term="Sustainable Development Goals">Sustainable Development Goals</Glossary>,
adopted by all United Nations member states in 2015, are seventeen goals for 2030, and
this course lives inside seven of them: SDG 3 (health), 6 (water and sanitation), 7
(clean energy), 11 (sustainable cities), 12 (responsible consumption), 13 (climate
action) and 15 (life on land) (UN, 2015). Each goal carries targets and indicators,
which is what turns a wish into something a ministry can be asked about.

### Environmental laws, policies, guidelines and strategies related to public health

Pakistan's framework carries the same architecture down to the drain. The **Pakistan
Environmental Protection Act 1997** is the statute, with the **National Environmental
Quality Standards (NEQS)** as its enforceable limits, the yardstick Unit 2's effluent
treatment aimed at. Under it sit the provincial environmental protection acts and
agencies since the 2010 devolution, and the **National Climate Change Policy**, which
sets the strategies, mitigation and adaptation, that the Paris pledges report against.
Policies state goals, laws make them enforceable, guidelines tell how, and strategies
sequence the work; the drain behind the school is covered by the NEQS and the
provincial agency's complaint desk, which is the practical answer to the trainee's
question.

**A worked example.** Run the full stack on arsenic. The hazard is identified
(Alibhatti et al., 2017, twenty-four districts); the risk is assessed (who drinks the
water); the controls are the hierarchy (safe-source substitution, treatment, testing);
and the governance stack above it, SDG 6's water target, Pakistan's drinking-water
quality standards, the provincial agency's testing duty, is what makes the control
someone's legal job rather than a family's private misfortune. Risk management without
law is advice; law without risk management is paper; the course needs both.

**Common misconception.** "International agreements are toothless talk shops." An MEA
has no police, which is a real limit; but its reporting cycles, its lists of banned
chemicals and its funding mechanisms change what states do, and the POPs banned under
Stockholm 2001 are measurably fewer in bodies than they were. The honest statement is
slower and stronger than the cynicism: MEAs work at the speed of reporting cycles, not
at the speed of headlines.

## Activity: The wall timeline

Work in groups of four. Build the MEA timeline on the classroom wall from six cards
your teacher shuffles, each naming an agreement and its year without its subject. Place
them, then match each to its subject, then add one card of your own: the next agreement
you would want, its year, and the problem it would control. Allow 25 minutes, then a
gallery walk.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the four steps of the risk management cycle and the four management options.
   *(Remember)*
2. What makes an MEA more than a press release? *(Understand)*
3. Why does the Stockholm POPs convention exist, in terms of what the chemicals do in
   bodies? *(Analyze)*
4. The drain behind the school: name the statute, the standard and the office that
   answer the trainee's question. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Risk management at environmental scale is the same cycle, identify, assess, control,
monitor, with the options avoid, reduce, transfer, accept. Multilateral environmental
agreements commit states to manage shared problems through negotiation, ratification and
reporting: Stockholm 1972, Basel 1989, Rio 1992, Stockholm POPs 2001, Minamata 2013 and
Paris 2015 are the markers. The SDGs turn the goals into targets and indicators, half of
them this course's own ground. Pakistan's stack carries it down: PEPA 1997, the NEQS,
the provincial agencies and the climate policy, which is how a global agreement reaches
a drain.

## Try this at your practicum school

Take the school's nearest environmental complaint, the drain, the dust, the noise. Find
out which office owns it, the union council, the provincial EPA, the water board, and
which law or standard that office would cite. Write the three-line answer: problem,
office, instrument. Do not name the school.

## Summative task

Write 170 to 200 words on the following.

> Your district's groundwater carries arsenic. Using the risk management cycle and the
> governance stack, argue which single instrument, an MEA, an SDG target, or a national
> or provincial law, would do the most to reduce the exposure, and name what the other
> two contribute that your choice cannot.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong response does |
