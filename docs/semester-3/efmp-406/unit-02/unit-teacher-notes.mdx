# Teaching notes: Contemporary Forms of Literacy

## Teaching strategies and sequencing

The course guide names discussion, question-answer, brainstorming, group work, individual assignments and presentations. These notes apply those strategies to Unit 2. They add no new guide topics. Begin with the Unit 1 idea that literacy is used for purposes in social settings. Then teach Topic 2.1 before Topic 2.2: pupils need practice supporting a judgement before they compare several modes at once. The unit is organized by guide content, not an invented week allocation.

For a short opening discussion, show the fictional app claim from Topic 2.1. Ask what the sentence actually says before asking whether pupils agree. Write two headings on the board, "evidence in the text" and "questions still open". This helps the class keep uncertainty visible. A pupil who dislikes the advertisement may still need help identifying which words support a criticism. A pupil who likes it may offer a useful alternative interpretation. Both should explain their reasoning.

Use question-answer to model the steps in the worked example. Ask one question at a time: who made the claim, whom does it address, what support is offered, whose experience is missing? Avoid giving pupils a list of labels before they have read the text. When a learner names an omission, ask how including that voice might change the message. This moves discussion from detecting a problem toward creating a response.

For Topic 2.2, make the information question explicit before showing several sources. The book-exchange question is deliberately small enough to answer without a live search. One pair can identify the rule, another can compare the sources, and a third can inspect how design affects the rule's prominence. When the groups meet, they should explain how their findings relate. The group inspecting layout must still preserve the confirmed rule when it proposes a revision.

## Likely misconceptions and responses

If a learner says, "Critical literacy means finding something wrong", ask for a message they could agree with after checking it. Return to the difference between judgement and automatic rejection. A fair, clear invitation can receive a positive evaluation. The learner should name the evidence that earns that judgement.

If a learner says, "The seller paid for it, so it must be false", separate production context from truth. Payment may influence what is selected, but a particular claim still needs examination. Ask what evidence would help evaluate the recommendation. Do not replace trust in advertising with automatic distrust of advertising.

If a learner says, "She cannot use a phone, so she has no digital literacy", describe separate strengths and needs. The learner may be able to judge a source while needing help with an operation. Conversely, a confident operator may omit a date. Ask pupils to identify the evidence of each skill in the case, rather than rank people with one broad label.

If a learner says, "The words are accurate, so the poster is clear", cover the small print briefly and ask what the large heading and image suggest. Then restore the complete text and compare the readings. The exercise concerns the design's effect, not a claim that all readers miss small print. Invite a revision that makes the condition easier to retrieve.

If a learner says, "Add every mode to make it better", return to purpose and audience. An extra soundtrack cannot make a date easier to find if the words remain unclear. Ask which part of the message each mode carries and how a reader gains access to it. Remove a decorative feature if nobody can explain its reading purpose.

## Practical work

Use the two topic activities as group assignments. Roles should rotate, especially the operator and reporter roles. A class sharing one device can still discuss several paper drafts while waiting for a turn. Accept drawings, described layouts and handwritten notices. These alternatives preserve the literacy decisions under study while reducing dependence on ownership of materials.

For an individual assignment, use a reflective journal entry after the group revision. The trainee records the original interpretation, one piece of peer feedback and the resulting change. The useful evidence is the connection between feedback and revision, rather than the length of the entry. Keep the journal focused on the fictional text so pupils need not disclose family income, language practices or access to devices.

For presentations, ask groups to show the original and revised versions together. The presenter explains a problem, points to the relevant evidence and defends a change. Another member states what the group still does not know. Classmates can then compare the reasons without voting on which poster is prettiest. Use oral explanations to support pupils who need help expressing the same reasoning in writing.

## Assessment and feedback

The topic checks are formative retrieval prompts. Ask learners to close the explanation before responding, then reopen it to inspect gaps. The self-assessment statements should follow evidence from the activity. A checked box without an example is a prompt for more practice, rather than a grade by itself.

The topic mini-rubrics and unit-end ERQ rubrics assess evidence, relationships and justified choices. They do not assess family device ownership or artistic skill. A strong response can be drawn by hand. Feedback should identify the next move: distinguish observation from inference, preserve the condition in a summary, name the original source or explain how the image relates to the words.

The course guide lists participation and reflective journals at 10%, the mid-term written test at 20%, the inclusive lesson-plan project at 30%, and the final written examination plus presentation at 40%. It also lists class tests, mid-term tests, assignment evaluation, attendance and participation as assessment criteria. Those course arrangements are reported as given. They are separate from the unit's default 40% formative and 60% summative balance, and these notes do not rename the guide's project.
