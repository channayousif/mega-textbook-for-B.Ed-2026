# Unit 2 assessment

## Unit summary

Contemporary forms of literacy extend the work of reading and writing into the purposes, settings and modes of messages. Critical literacy asks how a text represents people, what interests it serves and whose voices are missing. Its conclusions need evidence. Digital literacy connects practical operations with judgement about finding, creating and communicating meaning. A person can be confident with a device while needing support to judge the information on it.

Media and information literacy connects information needs with sources, messages, production and participation. A message may be accurate in part and still incomplete for the reader's decision. Repeated copies are not necessarily independent support. Visual and multimodal literacy asks how words, images, layout, colour, speech and sound work together. A clear redesign considers both the intended meaning and the people who need access to it.

These forms overlap in everyday reading. You can analyze a poster critically, check its details against the organizer's information and revise the relationship between its words and image. You can then decide whether a paper, digital or spoken version best serves the audience. The quality of the response depends on the reasoning and evidence, rather than the number of technologies or modes used.

## Summative assessment

Attempt the questions before consulting the guidance at the end. All cases and messages here are fictional. MCQs 1–5 and RRQs 1–5 address Topic 2.1; MCQs 6–10 and RRQs 6–10 address Topic 2.2. ERQs 1–2 address Topic 2.1, ERQs 3–4 address Topic 2.2, and ERQ 5 integrates the unit. Bloom tags describe the main thinking each question demands. The public guidance supports self-study; it is separate from any graded course quiz bank.

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. Which question most directly examines representation in a school story? *(Remember)*
   - A. How many pages does the story contain?
   - B. Which characters get to make decisions?
   - C. How long does the story take to read?
   - D. Which words have more than three syllables?

2. What is the main aim of critical literacy? *(Understand)*
   - A. To reject persuasive texts before examining them
   - B. To replace reading words with personal reactions
   - C. To examine meaning, interests and voice using evidence
   - D. To identify the fastest way to repeat a message

3. A pupil downloads a notice quickly but shares it without its event date. Which learning need is most evident? *(Apply)*
   - A. Preserving essential information when communicating
   - B. Increasing the speed of opening a familiar app
   - C. Adding more decorative effects to a screenshot
   - D. Replacing the notice with a longer video file

4. An advertisement shows one smiling pupil and claims that every learner needs its app. Apply the rule that one example cannot establish a claim about all learners. Which statement follows? *(Apply)*
   - A. The picture establishes the claim for all learners.
   - B. The seller's purpose establishes that the app is useless.
   - C. The pupil's smile establishes that the app caused success.
   - D. The picture does not establish the claim for all learners.

5. Which choice best shows purposeful digital creation for families with limited connectivity? *(Apply)*
   - A. Choose a long video because it contains more effects.
   - B. Choose a small clear notice with the confirmed details.
   - C. Choose an image without dates to reduce its text.
   - D. Choose a private pupil profile to make the notice personal.

6. What does an information need identify? *(Remember)*
   - A. The number of messages already received
   - B. The format with the most attractive design
   - C. The question that the reader needs answered
   - D. The person who most recently forwarded a file

7. Five messages repeat the same screenshot of a school event. What do they establish about confirmation? *(Understand)*
   - A. Five separate observers have checked the details.
   - B. The event organizer has approved the details.
   - C. The date becomes more reliable with every copy.
   - D. The copies may still come from one unverified source.

8. A poster says "one book per pupil", while its image shows a pupil receiving a large pile. Using the multimodal reading step of comparing words with the image, which question should a reader ask? *(Apply)*
   - A. Whether the image and wording invite different readings
   - B. Whether the image contains more colours than the words
   - C. Whether the poster could be printed on larger paper
   - D. Whether the pupil in the image has a familiar name

9. A class makes a spoken invitation for a book exchange. Which addition best makes the essential speech available to a reader who cannot hear it? *(Apply)*
   - A. Background music between each sentence
   - B. A longer pause before the event date
   - C. A written version of the spoken details
   - D. A colourful border around the audio control

10. Why does media literacy extend beyond identifying false news? *(Understand)*
    - A. It treats the popularity of a message as its main evidence.
    - B. It also examines production, representation and participation.
    - C. It asks readers to avoid producing their own messages.
    - D. It limits classroom work to messages about public events.

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

Answer each in two to five sentences, staying within its stated scope. Each item carries four marks.

1. Explain one difference between criticizing a text automatically and reading it critically. Include one possible outcome other than rejection. *(Understand)*
2. A notice says, "Only caring families attend the morning meeting." In three or four sentences, identify one assumption, explain one likely exclusion and suggest one fairer wording or other way to respond. *(Analyze)*
3. A trainee can upload a file but cannot explain why that file suits the audience. Name the practical skill shown and one digital judgement still needed, then give one example of what could go wrong without that judgement. *(Understand)*
4. An invented school announcement contains a pupil's phone number. Give two checks a trainee should make before creating a version for a family group, and explain each briefly. *(Apply)*
5. Separate observation from inference in this case: a story shows boys leading a project, and a reader says its author believes girls cannot lead. State one observation, one inference and one justified revision suggestion. *(Analyze)*
6. A parent asks whether a book must be brought to an exchange. Name the exact information need, a suitable confirming source and one reason that source fits. *(Apply)*
7. Explain in two or three sentences why three identical copies of an old notice do not confirm a current event date. Name one next step. *(Understand)*
8. A large "FREE" heading sits above tiny print saying "bring one book to exchange". Explain how two design features combine to affect meaning. *(Analyze)*
9. A video contains the exchange date only in spoken words and the entry point only in a silent gesture. Suggest one way to make each detail accessible in another mode, and explain how each change helps a viewer who cannot hear the speech or see the gesture. *(Apply)*
10. Explain why a poster with many images and colours can communicate less clearly than a simple notice. Give one specific design choice that would improve clarity. *(Understand)*

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

Write about 300–400 words per response. Each item carries twelve marks. Use evidence from the supplied case; do not invent research results or real school policies. For designs, describe a layout in words or draw it by hand. Drawing skill and access to a device are not assessed.

1. A fictional tutoring advertisement reads, "Successful children speak only English at home." It shows one family reading an English book and asks parents to buy a course. Analyze the representation, audience and evidence. Consider an alternative interpretation of the image. Propose a fairer message and explain what you can and cannot conclude about the course from this advertisement. *(Analyze)*

2. A trainee must share a fictional reading-club invitation with families who have different levels of device access. The draft is a long video with the date spoken once, no written summary and a pupil's contact details visible. Design a more suitable communication plan. Justify the format, the checks before sharing, a privacy change and an offline route. Explain how your plan shows digital literacy beyond operating a device. *(Create)*

3. A fictional school magazine page recommends buying an expensive reading pack. A small line says a stationery seller paid for the page, and the page offers no comparison with borrowed or shared materials. Evaluate how a teacher could use it for media and information literacy. Identify the reader's information need, the relevance of sponsorship and sources that could help answer the question. Explain why sponsorship is relevant without treating it as proof that the pack is poor. *(Evaluate)*

4. Design a multimodal book-exchange notice for a fictional government primary school. Confirmed case details are: 18 April, 9–11 a.m., school courtyard, one usable book to exchange for one book, school office for questions. Explain how at least two modes work together, how the condition receives clear prominence, and how essential meaning remains available if someone cannot see the main image or hear an audio version. Analyze one possible misreading your design prevents. *(Create)*

5. An invented digital post about a school reading day says, "Every good parent brings a new book." It shows a smiling family holding several new books, places the date in small print and says nothing about borrowed books or families without a phone. No organizer's confirmation is supplied. Analyze the post using critical literacy and visual or multimodal literacy. Then explain an information check and a digital sharing choice before creating a fairer version. Clearly separate confirmed case details, reasonable questions and details that still need checking. *(Evaluate)*

## Answers and marking guidance

These are public self-study examples. Equivalent, supported answers are acceptable. The fictional cases do not establish facts about actual schools or families. Award marks for the stated reasoning, including reasonable conclusions that differ from the examples. Do not reward an unsupported assertion merely because it agrees with the teacher. For self-study, mark the evidence in your own response before comparing the total.

### MCQ answer key

1. **B.** Who makes decisions concerns the roles and voice given to characters. The other options count or describe surface features without examining representation.
2. **C.** Critical literacy examines how meaning, interests and voice are shaped, using evidence. Automatic rejection and unexamined personal reaction both bypass that work.
3. **A.** The pupil demonstrates an operation but loses a detail needed for the audience's decision. Faster use or more decoration does not repair the missing date.
4. **D.** One smiling pupil does not establish a claim about all learners. Selling an app also does not establish that the app is useless; both extreme conclusions exceed the evidence.
5. **B.** A small clear notice can serve the stated audience while preserving essential information. The other choices either add an access burden or remove necessary information or privacy.
6. **C.** An information need names the question that must be answered. Neither the appearance nor the circulation of a message defines that need.
7. **D.** Repeated copies can share a single source. The question provides no evidence of separate checking or organizer approval.
8. **A.** Multimodal reading compares the meaning invited by the words and image. Counting colours or changing paper size does not address that relationship.
9. **C.** A written version makes the spoken details available without hearing the audio. Music, pauses and border colour do not carry those details in another mode.
10. **B.** Media literacy also examines how messages are produced, whom they represent and how people participate. It does not treat popularity as evidence or forbid creating messages.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

Each scheme totals four marks. The model is an example, not required wording. Give credit for a distinct equivalent point. Do not count the same idea twice just because it is repeated in another sentence.

1. A critical reader examines specific choices and their effects before judging. Automatic criticism rejects the text without that examination. A supported reading might agree with a fair, well-evidenced school notice or suggest a limited revision. **Marks:** 1 for evidence-based examination; 1 for contrasting automatic rejection; 1 for a non-rejection outcome; 1 for explaining why that outcome follows from examination.

2. The wording assumes that caring can be measured by attendance at one time. It may exclude working parents or carers unable to attend that morning. A less judgemental invitation could offer another way to respond. **Marks:** 1 for identifying the attendance-caring assumption; 1 for naming a relevant circumstance; 1 for explaining how the words judge or exclude that group; 1 for a fairer wording or other way to respond that removes the judgement. Merely saying "the notice is bad" earns none of these points.

3. Uploading shows a practical operation. The trainee still needs to judge whether the audience can access and understand the file and whether its content serves the purpose. For example, a large video may be unsuitable where a short text would communicate the same date. **Marks:** 1 for the operation; 1 for a relevant judgement; 1 for connecting it to purpose or audience; 1 for a relevant example of what could go wrong. Typing speed alone does not answer the judgement part.

4. Check whether the event details are current and confirmed, so the group receives useful information. Check whether the pupil's number is needed or appropriate for that audience; remove it from the shared version and use the school's approved contact route. **Marks:** 1 for each relevant check and 1 for explaining each, to a maximum of 4. Other well-explained checks about source, permission or audience can earn credit. Two versions of the same privacy check count as one check.

5. "The story shows boys leading" is an observation about the supplied text. "The author believes girls cannot lead" is an inference about a belief that the case does not establish. A revision could show girls sharing decisions or leading another part of the work. **Marks:** 1 for the observation; 1 for the inference; 1 for recognizing the limit on knowing intention; 1 for a relevant revision connected to representation.

6. The parent needs to know whether bringing a book is a condition and whether an alternative exists. The organizer or school office can confirm the current exchange arrangements because it manages the event. A forwarded image alone may omit the condition. **Marks:** 1 for the condition; 1 for the relevant confirming source; 1 for why that source fits; 1 for noting currency, an alternative arrangement to ask about or the limitation of a forwarded copy. Do not require the learner to invent an alternative policy.

7. The copies repeat the same underlying claim rather than independently checking it. An old notice may describe a different year. Contact the organizer or find its current confirmed notice before sharing a date. **Marks:** 1 for common origin; 1 for the date or year problem; 1 for a suitable next step; 1 for explaining how that step addresses the uncertainty.

8. The large heading attracts attention toward an unconditional offer, while the small print makes the exchange condition easy to miss. Together they can invite a misleading impression even though the condition is written somewhere. **Marks:** 1 for the large heading's effect; 1 for the small print's effect; 1 for relating the two; 1 for a likely misreading or a justified revision. Naming only the two features earns at most 1 mark.

9. Add the date as a clear written caption or transcript. Describe the entry point in spoken words and in a written instruction, rather than relying only on the gesture. The caption lets a viewer who cannot hear read the date, and the spoken and written instruction lets a viewer who cannot see the gesture still find the entry point. **Marks:** 1 for each detail transferred to another appropriate mode; 1 for explaining how each transfer addresses the named barrier. An answer that simply says "add more pictures" without carrying the details earns no transfer marks.

10. Many images and colours may compete with the main rule and make essential details hard to find. Making the event title and condition clear, then placing time and location together, can improve retrieval. **Marks:** 1 for identifying competition or clutter; 1 for its reading effect; 1 for a specific improvement; 1 for explaining why it helps the intended audience. Personal preference for a particular colour is not enough.

### ERQ rubrics

For each criterion below, award **0** when absent or contradicted by the case, **1** for a relevant assertion without supporting detail, **2** for an accurate explanation with some case evidence, and **3** for a well-supported explanation that handles the stated distinction or limitation. Four criteria give a total of twelve marks. These levels apply to each criterion, not only to the response as a whole. Where a criterion asks for a design, assess the design's meaning and the reason for the choice, not artistic finish.

1. **Tutoring advertisement.**
   - **Representation and audience (Analyze):** links "successful", "only English" and the chosen family image to the picture of success being offered. At level 3, explains whose experience is left out without asserting that all readers respond alike.
   - **Evidence and limits (Analyze):** distinguishes the image and slogan from support for a universal claim. At level 3, states that the case cannot establish either the course's effectiveness or the maker's private intentions.
   - **Alternative reading (Evaluate):** considers that the image might show one useful activity rather than all legitimate home literacy. At level 3, explains why that possibility does not support the word "only".
   - **Revision and justification (Evaluate):** proposes an invitation that values learning without ranking families by home language. At level 3, explains how the revision addresses the exclusion while preserving an appropriate educational purpose.

2. **Reading-club communication plan.**
   - **Purpose and audience (Analyze):** identifies the essential event details and different access conditions. At level 3, explains why the original format may prevent retrieval of a date or exclude some families.
   - **Format and offline route (Create):** offers a workable small notice, written summary, spoken version or equivalent, plus a non-internet route. At level 3, each choice serves the stated audience rather than adding modes for decoration.
   - **Checks and privacy (Apply):** checks current information with the organizer and removes the pupil's unnecessary details. At level 3, describes an appropriate school contact route without inventing consent or authority to publish.
   - **Digital literacy reasoning (Evaluate):** explains how the plan joins operations with judgement. At level 3, links at least two decisions to purpose, access, evidence or responsibility, rather than claiming that any use of a phone proves literacy.

3. **Sponsored magazine page.**
   - **Information need (Apply):** frames a clear question such as whether the pack serves a learning need at a reasonable cost. At level 3, names relevant comparison information rather than asking only whether the page is popular.
   - **Production context (Analyze):** identifies the sponsor's commercial relationship and its possible relevance to selection. At level 3, explains a possible influence without treating sponsorship as proof of falsehood.
   - **Source comparison (Evaluate):** suggests sources suited to the question, such as sample materials, teacher evaluation and information about shared alternatives. At level 3, explains the contribution and limitation of each instead of merely listing websites.
   - **Teaching and participation (Create):** proposes a classroom comparison or revised recommendation with evidence. At level 3, learners explain and defend a judgement, including uncertainty, rather than repeat the teacher's opinion about buying.

4. **Multimodal exchange notice.**
   - **Fidelity to confirmed details (Apply):** preserves the date, time, place and one-for-one condition. At level 3, includes the office contact route without adding unsupported rules or promises.
   - **Relationship among modes (Analyze):** explains how words and an image or speech work together. At level 3, points to a specific relationship such as a one-book-each image reinforcing the exchange condition.
   - **Prominence and misreading (Evaluate):** gives the condition clear prominence and analyzes the misunderstanding it prevents. At level 3, explains the effect of size or position rather than describing the design as attractive.
   - **Access (Create):** includes a meaningful text description and a written equivalent of essential audio where audio is used. At level 3, the design retains the essential message without relying only on image, sound or colour.

5. **Integrated reading-day post.**
   - **Critical reading (Analyze):** examines how "good parent" and "new book" link care with a purchase. At level 3, considers missing family circumstances and avoids claiming to know the maker's intention.
   - **Multimodal relationship (Analyze):** links the smiling family, several new books and small date to likely impressions and retrieval problems. At level 3, explains how at least two features combine rather than listing modes.
   - **Information and digital response (Evaluate):** seeks current organizer confirmation, asks about acceptable books and chooses an accessible sharing route. At level 3, separates supplied details from unconfirmed requirements and avoids presenting a proposed route as an authorized announcement.
   - **Fairer creation (Create):** offers a clear invitation that avoids judging families by purchases and preserves uncertainty where needed. At level 3, explains how the changes address evidence, representation and access together. Do not require an invented free-book scheme; asking the organizer is a sound response to a missing arrangement.
