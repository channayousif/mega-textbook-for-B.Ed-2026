# Topic 2.2 - Media, information, images and many modes

## A real classroom situation

In this fictional Sukkur classroom, pupils compare two posters announcing a school book exchange. Both give the same date, but one makes "FREE" very large and puts "bring a book to exchange" in tiny print. Its photograph shows a child receiving a tall pile of books. Several pupils expect to take books home without bringing anything. The teacher asks how a poster can contain accurate words and still invite a misleading impression.

## Explanation

### Media and information literacy

**Media** are forms and channels through which messages reach people, such as newspapers, radio, television, posters and online services. **Media literacy** involves understanding and judging how messages are made and circulated, and taking part through thoughtful creation and communication. **Information literacy** involves recognizing what you need to know, finding relevant information, judging it and using it responsibly. Together, **media and information literacy**, often shortened to **MIL**, connects the message, the information need, the provider and the reader's response.

These forms overlap. When you examine who paid for an advertisement, you are asking a media question about its production. When you decide what evidence you need to answer a question, you are making an information choice. When you make your own poster and identify the source of its details, you practise both. The distinction helps you name what a lesson teaches; it does not divide real reading into separate boxes.

Alcolea-Díaz, Reig and Mancinas-Chávez (2020) examine the place of media structures in UNESCO's teacher curriculum. Their analysis draws attention to the relationship between how media are organized and the messages people receive. For this unit, that suggests a useful question: who can choose what gets published and what interests may shape those choices? A message's context deserves examination alongside its wording. The [journal article](https://doi.org/10.3916/c62-2020-09) provides the research basis for that connection.

Suppose a local stationery shop pays for an invented school magazine page about revision. The page recommends buying several expensive notebooks. The recommendation might be useful, but the payment is relevant to how we read it. Ask whether the page clearly identifies its sponsor, whether it explains why the items are needed, and whether it considers cheaper ways to organize revision. The commercial relationship is a reason to inspect the evidence. It is not proof that every recommendation on the page is false.

**The misconception "media literacy is only about spotting fake news" is too narrow.** A reader also needs to understand advertisements, entertainment, public notices and everyday images. A true report can leave out a viewpoint. A useful announcement can be hard to access. A pupil can create a clear, responsible message without investigating a false story. Media literacy includes those decisions and participation, not only detecting falsehoods.

To work with information, begin by making the question precise. "Find something about the book exchange" gives little guidance. "What must a family bring, and when does the exchange begin?" names the need. This helps you decide which information matters and when you have enough. A poster that shows smiling readers may be attractive, but it may not answer the question about what to bring. An attractive result is not necessarily a relevant result.

**Worked example: answering a family's question.** A parent asks a trainee, "Can my child collect a book without bringing one?" The trainee has the two fictional posters from the opening and a short organizer's note. The note says that pupils should bring one usable book to exchange, and that the school has a small separate box for pupils who cannot bring one. The trainee identifies the exact information need: the exchange rule and any alternative. She compares the posters with the note, checks that they concern the same event, and asks the organizer to confirm the arrangement before replying. She then explains both the rule and the alternative without naming families who may need it.

The example involves access, evaluation, use, creation and participation. Access means obtaining the material needed, whether through paper, speech or a screen. Evaluation means judging its relevance and support. Use means applying it to the actual question. Creation means shaping a message for an audience. Participation means contributing, asking and responding in a shared setting. These are teaching moves for this unit, rather than a claim that every task must follow one fixed sequence.

What makes a source appropriate depends on the question. The event organizer is well placed to explain the event's rule. A pupil can report how the poster was understood, but cannot necessarily confirm the rule. A shop's advertisement may tell you the price it offers, but not provide an independent comparison of all shops. A reader should ask whether the source has direct knowledge of the specific matter. Expertise in one area does not automatically answer a different question.

A **claim** is a statement presented for acceptance. **Evidence** is information offered to support it. A **source** is where the claim or evidence comes from. Keep the three separate. "The exchange starts at nine" is a claim. A current, confirmed organizer's schedule could support it. The school office could be the source. "My cousin forwarded it" explains how it reached you, but does not yet identify who knows the event details.

Comparing sources can strengthen a judgement, but counting copies is not enough. Five group messages may reproduce the same mistaken screenshot. They are five copies of one claim, rather than five independent confirmations. Find out whether each source gathered information separately or repeated the first. For an ordinary school notice, this may be as simple as checking the organizer's current version. The aim is enough relevant support for the decision, not an endless search.

The date also has a purpose. A notice may have been accurate when issued but refer to last year's event. A historical photograph can still be useful when studying that year. Do not reduce evaluation to "new is good, old is bad". Ask whether the material is current enough for the question you are answering. Record the event year and the publication date separately if both are available. When either is missing, say that it needs checking.

Responsible use includes preserving meaning when summarizing. If the organizer says that a small reserve box exists, do not rewrite that as "unlimited free books for everyone". That would create a new claim. A summary should retain conditions that matter to the reader's decision. It should identify where important details came from. If you are unsure, ask or state the limit rather than filling the gap with a plausible guess.

Participation also involves how people can reply. A notice with a contact route can let a family ask about access. A teacher can invite pupils to suggest clearer wording. A class can present a revised poster and explain its evidence. These are acts of literacy as social practice, connecting this topic to Unit 1. They show why evaluating information matters: it helps people make decisions and contribute, rather than merely earn a score on a source-checking worksheet.

### Visual and multimodal literacy

**Visual literacy** means interpreting and creating meaning through images and visual arrangement. **Multimodal literacy** means interpreting and creating texts that combine more than one way of making meaning. A **mode** is one such way, for example written words, images, sound, movement or gesture. Layout arranges the parts on a page or screen. Colour, size and position are visual choices that can influence what we notice. You do not need to settle a strict classification of every feature to explain what it does in a text.

A school poster combines words with image and layout. A short video might combine speech, written captions, moving images, music and gesture. A teacher's presentation may combine spoken explanation with a chart and pointing. These combinations can support one another, divide the work of explanation or create tension. The reader needs to ask how the parts work together. Naming the parts is only the beginning of multimodal reading.

UNESCO's [media and information literacy curriculum](https://www.unesco.org/mil4teachers/sites/default/files/medias/fichiers/2023/10/Part_1_CI%20MIL_CURRICULUM.pdf) treats literacy across print, audiovisual and digital environments. Its introduction provides the broader reason for examining both a paper poster and a digital clip. The detailed classroom readings below are original teaching examples. They show how to inspect a combination of modes without claiming that every reader will respond in the same way.

**The misconception "pictures do not need to be read" overlooks selection and arrangement.** Someone chose what to include in a photograph, what to leave outside the frame and how large to display it. These choices affect what readers can see. A picture can provide information that the words omit, or encourage an impression the words do not support. Looking at an image carefully is a reading task. It does not require assuming that the picture is deliberately dishonest.

**Worked example: the book-exchange poster.** Begin with the words. The large word "FREE" suggests no cost, while the small line states a condition: bring a book to exchange. Next, inspect the image. A child receiving a pile of books might suggest that visitors can take many books. Then inspect the layout. The large word sits at eye level and the condition is far below in tiny type. Together, those choices make the condition easier to miss. The teacher can conclude that the design risks an incomplete understanding, even if the small print is accurate.

Now revise the poster. Make "School book exchange" the main heading. Put "Bring one usable book to swap" directly beneath it in clear type. Use a simple picture of two pupils exchanging one book each. Add the confirmed date, time and contact route. Explain the reserve arrangement separately and respectfully, after confirming how the school wants families to ask about it. The revision changes the relationship among words, image and layout. Merely adding more words at the bottom would not solve the reading problem.

Do not claim that a visual choice has one meaning everywhere. Red can draw attention, but a red border does not automatically mean danger in every text. Size can mark importance, but readers also use language, position and prior experience. A teacher should ask, "What in this poster draws your attention, and how does it affect your reading?" Pupils may notice different things. Require a reason grounded in the design rather than a memorized rule about colours.

Consider a second invented example: a class presentation about library use. The speaker says, "All grades use the library equally." The chart beside the speaker shows Grade 4 borrowing twice as many books as Grade 5. A listener must compare speech and image. Perhaps the speaker means that all grades have equal access, while the chart reports actual borrowing. The teacher should clarify the claim instead of declaring that one mode must be wrong. Different modes may answer different questions unless the message explains their relationship.

A visual comparison also depends on what is counted. Imagine a chart showing 8 books borrowed by Group A and 4 by Group B. It may appear that Group A reads more. But the chart does not tell us whether the groups have equal numbers of pupils, whether pupils read books at home, or how long the books are. Visual literacy includes reading labels and noticing what the display cannot establish. This is a literacy example about evidence, not a demand for advanced statistics.

Sound can change the way a message is framed. In a fictional video invitation to a reading club, a calm voice explains the meeting arrangements while music suggests celebration. The music can encourage interest, but it cannot confirm that the arrangements are accurate. A sudden loud sound may attract attention without providing information. Ask what the spoken words state, what the soundtrack adds and whether the two support the same purpose. A transcript preserves the words, but may need a short note about an important sound cue.

Gesture and movement can also carry meaning. A presenter who points to a date helps connect speech to the poster. A demonstration of exchanging books can clarify a rule for pupils who find the written language difficult. Yet a video with only a pointing hand and no spoken or written explanation may exclude someone who cannot see the gesture. When creating a multimodal text, consider which information is carried only by one mode and how to make the essential meaning available another way.

Accessibility means arranging access to the intended meaning for people with different needs. A clear text description can communicate the important content of a picture. Captions or a transcript can carry spoken words for readers who cannot hear them. Adequate contrast and readable type can make a condition easier to find. Do not put essential meaning only in a colour difference. If a chart uses red for one group and blue for another, label the groups too. These are design choices that serve understanding, rather than decorative additions after the lesson is finished.

A good multimodal explanation names both a feature and its effect. "The poster has a picture" names a feature without explaining it. "The picture shows one book moving each way, which helps explain exchange" connects the feature to meaning. "The image is nice" is a preference. "The large heading is easy to notice, but the condition is too small to find quickly" is an analysis of reading. Model the second kind of sentence before asking pupils to analyze independently.

The modes can agree, add different information or conflict. In agreement, the sentence "bring one book" and a picture of one book reinforce each other. In addition, words may give the date while a simple plan shows where to enter. In conflict, the words may say that participation is open to all while the image shows only one group receiving books. The reader should identify the relationship and explain its likely significance. Do not simply count how many modes appear.

Creating with many modes is not automatically better than using fewer. A short, clear notice can serve its audience better than a crowded video full of moving text. Choose the modes the purpose needs. For the book exchange, the condition and date must be easy to retrieve. A decorative soundtrack does little to help someone find the date. A spoken version may be valuable where families prefer listening. The choice should follow the audience and information need.

Finally, combine the forms of literacy. Critical literacy asks whose experiences the book-exchange poster represents. Digital literacy asks how to prepare and share an appropriate version. Media literacy asks about the maker, purpose and circulation. Information literacy asks what the family needs to know and which source can confirm it. Visual and multimodal literacy ask how design and modes communicate the message. One text can support all these questions. The forms are different tools for making a well-supported judgement about meaning.

## Activity: Redesign the message, defend the choices

Work in pairs for about 25 minutes. One partner plays a family member asking about the exchange; the other plays a trainee preparing a notice. Start by recalling the difference between a claim, evidence and a source without looking back. Then list the exact details the family needs. Use the fictional organizer's note in the worked example. Treat any missing detail as unknown rather than inventing a school policy.

Draft a paper poster or a plan for a short audio-and-image message. Give the exchange condition clear prominence. Use two modes that serve a stated purpose. Add a text description of the main image and a written version of any essential spoken information. Exchange roles. The new family reader explains what they understand about bringing and receiving books. If their reading differs from your intention, identify the design choice that may have led to it.

Join another pair and compare designs. Each pair must defend one choice with reference to the audience and the organizer's information. The other pair suggests one change that would improve access without changing the rule. Revise and present the result. Report the original problem, the change and the reason. Assess the clarity of the information and reasoning, rather than the cost of materials or drawing skill.

## Check your understanding

1. Explain the difference between a source and the channel through which you receive its message. Give a school example. *(Understand)*
2. From memory, name three modes or visual resources that can contribute to a message. *(Remember)*
3. A poster gives its main condition in tiny print beneath a large "FREE" heading. Propose two changes that make the condition easier to understand and explain each. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Media and information literacy connects an information need with sources, evidence, production and participation. Visual and multimodal literacy examines how different parts of a text combine into meaning. Accurate small print does not guarantee a clear overall message. Readers and creators should compare the modes, consider access and explain their judgements with visible or audible evidence.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can identify what information a reader needs and which source could confirm it.
- [ ] I can explain why repeated copies of a message are not separate confirmations.
- [ ] I can analyze how at least two modes work together or create tension.
- [ ] I can revise a message so its essential details are available in more than one way.

## Try this at your practicum school

With your cooperating teacher, inspect a public poster or a teaching chart that has no private pupil information. Record its intended audience and the first detail you notice. Describe how words and one visual feature work together. Propose one revision, including a short description that would convey the main image's meaning to a reader who cannot see it. Ask the teacher whether the change preserves the intended message. Work from the fictional book-exchange poster if no suitable classroom text is available. You do not need to photograph pupils or collect family information.

## Summative task

Prepare a redesigned book-exchange notice and a 300–400 word commentary. State the reader's information need, identify the source for the event rule and show how two modes support the message. Discuss one likely misreading of the original and one access barrier your design addresses. Include a text description of the main image. You may draw by hand; artistic polish is not part of the score.

|---|---|---|---|
| Source and information fit (Apply) | Rule invented or source absent | Relevant details with a named source | Details preserve the rule and distinguish confirmed from missing information |
