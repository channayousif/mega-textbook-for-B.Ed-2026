# Topic 2.1 - Reading the world critically and digitally

## A real classroom situation

In this fictional government primary school near Hyderabad, a trainee brings a printed advertisement for a learning app. It says, "Every successful child studies online every night." Some pupils share one family phone, while others have no connection at home. One pupil asks whether the advertisement means that children without phones cannot succeed. The trainee must help the class examine the claim without shaming families or turning the lesson into an argument about brands.

## Explanation

### Critical literacy

**Critical literacy** means examining how texts represent people and interests, whose voices they include, and whose voices they leave out. A text can be a story, a notice, an advertisement or a video. Reading the words accurately matters. Critical literacy adds questions about the choices behind those words and their effects on readers. It also asks what a reader could do when a message treats a group unfairly.

Minott (2011) connects critical literacy teaching with reflection on one's assumptions and reasoned judgement. This supports a classroom habit: before judging a message, inspect both the message and your own first response. Perhaps you trust an advertisement because its English sounds confident. Perhaps you reject it because you dislike the company. Neither reaction is enough evidence about its claim. A teacher can model slowing down and explaining the basis of a judgement. [Minott's article record](https://hull-repository.worktribe.com/output/4127637) describes this reflective connection.

**Purpose** is what the maker wants a text to achieve. **Audience** means the people it is aimed at. **Representation** is how a text shows a person, group or place. These are useful reading questions, not labels to memorize without examples. In the app advertisement, the likely purpose is to encourage families to use or buy the app. Its audience may include parents who worry about school success. Its picture of a successful child may leave out pupils who learn well through books, conversation or shared materials.

The important word in the claim is "every". It makes a statement about all successful children. The teacher can ask what evidence could support such a wide statement. A photograph of one happy child using a tablet cannot establish it. A company's wish to sell a service does not prove its material is bad, either. The question is whether the claim has enough support and whether the representation is fair. Keep the judgement tied to the actual message.

**Worked example: reading an advertisement fairly.** First, the trainee reads the sentence aloud and asks the class to restate it. It appears to say that nightly online study is necessary for success. Next, she identifies the maker as an app seller, so she expects a persuasive purpose. Then she checks the evidence supplied: the invented advertisement gives a picture and a slogan, but no explanation of how success was measured. Finally, she considers who is missing: children using library books, children learning with grandparents, and children sharing a device. She concludes, "This advertisement has not shown that online study is necessary for every child. We can examine the app's features without accepting that claim."

Notice the limits of this conclusion. The trainee has not shown that the app never helps. She has not shown that the company meant to hurt anyone. She has shown that a very broad claim is unsupported in the text provided. A careful conclusion says what the evidence allows. It also says what remains unknown. This habit protects critical reading from becoming guessing about other people's motives.

Now consider a second invented text: "Parents who care attend the school meeting at 10 a.m." The words connect caring with attendance at one time. A parent who works that morning may care deeply and still be absent. Ask who can attend easily, whose circumstances are ignored, and how the sentence might affect a pupil whose parent cannot come. A fairer notice would invite families, name the time, and offer another way to contribute. It need not assume that missing a meeting means a lack of care.

This is an example of **power** in a text: the ability to shape what people see as normal or acceptable. A school notice can influence how pupils describe their families. A textbook story can repeatedly assign leadership to one group and helping roles to another. Look for patterns and specific choices. One character doing a household task does not by itself prove that a story excludes a group. Ask who gets to speak, decide and explain across the whole text.

**The misconception "critical literacy means criticising everything" confuses careful enquiry with automatic rejection.** A critical reader can agree with a message after examining it. If a school notice gives a clear date, a fair invitation and an accessible way to reply, the reader can explain why it works. The aim is an informed response. Respectful disagreement, revision and supported agreement are all possible outcomes.

A teacher also needs to separate a textual observation from an inference. "The poster shows only boys carrying tools" is an observation if those figures are visible. "The maker believes girls cannot use tools" is an inference about belief. The picture may invite that interpretation, but it does not let us read the maker's mind. A stronger statement is, "This choice could suggest to readers that tool use belongs to boys. Adding girls in active roles would broaden the representation." The revised wording links evidence, possible effect and action.

Use the same discipline with classroom discussion. A pupil may say, "This story is unfair." Ask which sentence or picture led to that judgement. Another pupil may read the story differently. Invite the second pupil to point to evidence too. Compare the explanations. Do not reward agreement with the teacher as if it were proof of reading. A thoughtful minority view may expose something the class overlooked.

There are several possible actions after reading critically. You might ask the maker for more information, write a response, suggest a fairer title or create an alternative notice. The action should fit the evidence and the setting. A trainee can revise a fictional poster without identifying a real family. If a sensitive issue arises, discuss the wording rather than asking pupils to reveal personal experiences. No child should have to defend their family's income or access to technology.

### Digital literacy

**Digital literacy** means using digital tools to find, judge, create and communicate meaning purposefully and responsibly. It includes practical operations, but it also includes choices. Opening an app is an operation. Deciding whether the app serves the learning task is a judgement. Sending a message is an operation. Deciding what to send, to whom, and with what supporting information is a literacy decision.

[Jisc's individual digital capabilities framework](https://digitalcapability.jisc.ac.uk/what-is-digital-capability/individual-digital-capabilities/) includes practical skills alongside critical use, creation, communication, learning and identity. Its breadth helps correct a common classroom assumption. **Digital literacy is not the same as knowing how to use a computer.** A pupil who types quickly may still copy an unsupported claim. A pupil who needs help finding a button may reason carefully about the information on screen. Teach the operation and the judgement together, while noticing which kind of help is needed.

Consider three pupils working with a fictional school reading notice. Sara knows how to take a screenshot but cuts off its date. Bilal checks that the notice is current but cannot find the download control. Meena explains the message clearly and asks permission before sharing it in a family group. These pupils show different strengths. Saying simply "Sara is digitally literate" hides the missing date. Saying "Bilal is not digitally literate" ignores his useful judgement. Describe the task and the evidence of learning instead.

A practical routine begins with a purpose. What does the learner need to know or communicate? A trainee might need the date and place of a school book exchange. She should look for the organizer's current notice, read beyond the preview, and record where the information came from. She can then create a short message that includes the date, place, audience and source. Before sharing, she should check whether the message contains anyone's private information. The details of particular apps may change, but this sequence remains useful.

**Worked example: making a useful digital message.** The trainee has a fictional school notice dated 5 March. It announces a book exchange in the school courtyard on 12 March, from 9 to 11 a.m., for enrolled families. An older image in a group says 10 March. First, she identifies her purpose: tell current families when and where to bring books. She compares the dates and asks the school office which notice is current. In this invented case the office confirms 12 March. She writes a short message with the confirmed details and identifies the office as her source. She removes a pupil's phone number from the photograph before sharing the message through the teacher's approved channel.

The teacher can now name the learning visible at each step. Comparing notices shows attention to currency, meaning whether information is current enough for the purpose. Contacting the office checks the source directly. Writing a clear summary shows creation for an audience. Removing the phone number shows attention to privacy. None of these decisions is measured by how quickly the trainee touches the screen. Speed can help a user complete a task, but it does not establish the quality of the result.

What if the office cannot be reached? The trainee should preserve uncertainty: "The date is not confirmed; please wait for the school's current notice." She should avoid turning the most recent-looking screenshot into an official announcement. A message can be easy to forward and still be incomplete. Acting responsibly sometimes means delaying a post. For a classroom exercise, pupils can practise this choice on paper by writing a draft reply rather than sending anything.

A **digital identity** is the picture of a person that their digital actions and information create. It may include a profile, public comments, shared work and information posted by others. A trainee can discuss this idea without asking pupils to open private accounts. Use invented profiles instead. Compare a profile that shares a full home address with one that shares only the information needed for the school task. Ask which details the audience needs and which can be left out.

Digital creation also involves considering access. A short text notice may reach a family whose connection cannot load a long video. A voice message may help a listener who finds the written language difficult, while a transcript helps someone who cannot hear it. There is no single format that solves every barrier. Ask what the actual audience needs. Where the class shares one device, rotate the roles of reader, operator, checker and reporter. The pupil holding the device should not be the only pupil doing the thinking.

The relationship between critical and digital literacy becomes clearer through a contrast. Suppose a trainee forwards the app advertisement perfectly, with its image sharp and its link working. She has performed the operation well. Yet she may still have passed on an unfair message without examining it. Another trainee questions its claim thoughtfully but cannot save a revised version. She has shown critical reading and needs practical support. A lesson can develop both strengths by asking pairs to analyze a message and then create a better version using a suitable tool.

A digital task does not always need a live connection. The teacher can print a search-results page, provide two saved notices or draw a phone screen on the board. Pupils can compare a title, source, date and description before discussing which result they would inspect. This rehearses judgement. It does not replace every experience of operating a real device. Be clear about the difference: the paper task practises reading and choosing; later supervised use practises the controls as well.

The source of a message and the technology carrying it are different. A family messaging group is a channel. The school office might be the source of a notice shared there. A search engine is a tool for finding material; it is not automatically the author of the material it lists. A screenshot can hide the original source. Asking "Who actually made this claim?" connects critical reading with digital judgement. Record the answer when you can, and say when you cannot.

For trainee teachers, an enduring understanding is that digital literacy is tied to purposes and people. A good choice for one audience may be poor for another. Before preparing a digital literacy task, name the intended meaning, the audience and the evidence you will look for. If the lesson aims to teach source comparison, do not grade pupils mainly on decorative fonts. If it aims to teach clear communication, check whether the reader can find the needed details. Assess the literacy move the task actually requires.

## Activity: Two readers, one message

Work in groups of four for about 25 minutes. Use the fictional app advertisement from the opening. No device or personal account is required. First, close the explanation and recall four critical questions together. Write them on scrap paper. Assign roles: reader, evidence finder, alternative-view proposer and recorder. Rotate roles halfway through so that one confident English reader does not speak for everyone.

Read the advertisement and make two columns: "what the text shows" and "what we infer". Identify its likely audience, the evidence it provides and one voice missing from the message. The alternative-view proposer must offer a fair reason someone might value the app. The evidence finder must then explain why that reason does or does not support the word "every". Agree on a conclusion that stays within the available evidence.

Create a replacement message for families in a Sindh school. Offer a useful learning choice without judging families by device ownership. Choose a digital or paper format and explain the choice. Exchange messages with another group. Ask whether the reader can identify the purpose, whether the message makes a supported claim, and whether anyone is excluded. Revise one sentence after the feedback. Present the change and the reason, rather than just displaying the finished message.

## Check your understanding

1. Define critical literacy in your own words and name two questions a critical reader asks beyond identifying the main idea. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why a pupil who can forward a video quickly may still need digital literacy teaching. Give one example of the judgement involved. *(Understand)*
3. A school meeting notice says, "Good parents attend at 10 a.m." Rewrite the invitation for working families and explain one choice in your wording. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Critical literacy examines representation, purpose, voice and power through evidence in texts. It can lead to supported agreement as well as disagreement. Digital literacy joins practical operations with purposeful, responsible judgement about creating and communicating meaning. Both forms require attention to people who may be excluded and to the limits of the information available.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can support a judgement about a text with a specific word, image or omission.
- [ ] I can separate an observation from an inference about the maker's intention.
- [ ] I can explain a digital choice in terms of purpose, evidence, audience and privacy.
- [ ] I can revise a message without assuming that all families own devices.

## Try this at your practicum school

With your cooperating teacher, choose one public school notice that contains no personal pupil information. Work from a copy or your own notes. Identify its purpose and audience, then suggest one way to make it clearer or more inclusive. Explain how you would share the revised information if some families lacked internet access. You are proposing a revision, not issuing an announcement for the school. Record the teacher's feedback and the sentence you would change. If no suitable notice is available, use the fictional meeting notice from this topic.

## Summative task

Write 350–450 words analyzing an invented advertisement for an online reading service. Include its exact claim, audience, evidence and one missing perspective. Propose a fairer message and a practical way to share it with families who have different levels of access. Explain one thing you cannot conclude from the advertisement. You may use the opening example or create a clearly labelled fictional one.
