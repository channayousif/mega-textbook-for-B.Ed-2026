# Concept and Evolution of Literacy - Assessment and Review

## Unit summary

Literacy is the ability to take meaning from texts and to put meaning into them. It has three parts: decoding, comprehension and composing. It usually includes numeracy as well. Literacy is a matter of degree, not a yes-or-no label. It depends on the language and script, on the kind of text, and on the purpose for which a person reads or writes. Functional literacy is literacy that is good enough for the demands of a person's daily life and work.

Two views of literacy shape how teachers and governments think about it. The traditional view, which Street called the autonomous model, treats literacy as a fixed set of skills that are the same everywhere and can be tested and counted. It is clear and easy to measure, but it can label people as lacking and miss the literacies they already have. The contemporary view, which Street called the ideological model, treats literacy as a social practice: there are many literacies, learned at home, in religious settings, at work and on screens as well as in school, and they are shaped by the values and power of a community. Signing a name or completing primary school is not enough evidence that a person is literate.

Literacy practices have changed through history. Oral tradition kept knowledge in memory. Manuscripts made reading the work of a small group of specialists. Print spread texts widely, made spelling more standard and encouraged private reading. Mass schooling set out to teach every child and began to count literacy in censuses. Screens added new modes, such as images, sound and layout, to reading and writing. At every stage new tools added new skills, and older practices continued alongside the new ones. Contemporary literacy adds to reading and writing; it does not replace them.

The same pattern appears across a single life. Literacy grows from childhood through school, work, community life and older age. New roles, new tools and new kinds of text keep demanding new learning, and skills fade without use. Literacy is therefore a lifelong learning process. For a teacher this has a practical meaning: build on the literacies pupils bring from home, do not assume that any pupil "already knows how to read", and treat every subject as a place where literacy is taught.

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

Choose A, B, C or D. Each item is worth 1 mark.

1. Which of these best describes comprehension? *(Remember)*
   - A) Turning written symbols into spoken words
   - B) Understanding the meaning of what is read
   - C) Copying a sentence neatly from the board
   - D) Reading aloud quickly and without errors
2. What does the word "functional" add to "functional literacy"? *(Understand)*
   - A) Literacy in a functional language such as English
   - B) Literacy learned only through formal schooling
   - C) Literacy good enough for the demands of a person's daily life and work
   - D) Literacy that is measured by a national examination
3. Which statement belongs to the traditional (autonomous) view of literacy? *(Understand)*
   - A) Literacy is a fixed set of skills that is the same in every place.
   - B) Literacy is shaped by the values of the community that uses it.
   - C) Children bring literacies from home that school should build on.
   - D) There are many literacies, such as religious and workplace literacy.
4. A man in a village in Thar can sign his name on a register but cannot read a letter from the bank. Which conclusion is best supported? *(Apply)*
   - A) He is literate, because signing his name is a literacy skill.
   - B) He has a memorised skill but is not yet functionally literate for this task.
   - C) He is illiterate, because he never went to school.
   - D) He needs only numeracy, not reading.
5. A teacher in Hyderabad brings bus timetables and electricity bills into a Class 7 reading lesson. Which view of literacy does this choice mainly reflect? *(Apply)*
   - A) The autonomous model, because the texts are printed
   - B) The traditional view, because the lesson takes place in school
   - C) The social-practice view, because it uses texts from learners' daily lives
   - D) The oral tradition, because pupils read the texts aloud
6. Which stage of literacy history made books rare and reading the work of a small group of specialists? *(Remember)*
   - A) Oral tradition
   - B) Manuscript
   - C) Mass schooling
   - D) Screens
7. Which change is most directly linked to printing? *(Understand)*
   - A) Knowledge was kept only in memory.
   - B) Texts could be copied in large numbers and spelling became more standard.
   - C) Every child was required by law to attend school.
   - D) Images and video were added to written messages.
8. In the history of literacy, what does "each new stage added to the older ones" mean? *(Understand)*
   - A) Older practices continued alongside the newer ones.
   - B) Each new stage made older practices disappear.
   - C) Each stage lasted exactly as long as the one before.
   - D) Only the newest practice is real literacy.
9. A Class 9 pupil reads Urdu stories fluently but struggles with the tables and word problems in her science textbook. Which idea best explains this? *(Apply)*
   - A) She has not yet learned to decode letters.
   - B) Reading to learn in a subject needs new literacy beyond learning to read.
   - C) Literacy is complete at the end of primary school.
   - D) Science textbooks do not require reading.
10. A 60-year-old shopkeeper in Larkana starts taking payments through a mobile app and has to learn to read its menus and messages. What does this example best illustrate? *(Apply)*
    - A) Contemporary literacy replaces reading and writing.
    - B) Literacy is a lifelong process triggered by new tools.
    - C) Older people cannot learn new literacies.
    - D) The oral tradition has disappeared.

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

## Answers and marking guidance

Use this section only after attempting the questions. It explains the reasoning behind each answer so that you can check and improve your own. Your teacher may use different marks for formal assessment.

### MCQ answer key

1. **B.** Comprehension is understanding meaning. A describes decoding, C describes encoding by copying, and D describes fluent reading aloud, which can happen without comprehension.
2. **C.** "Functional" points to use: literacy that lets a person meet the demands of daily life and work. It is not tied to one language, one setting or one examination.
3. **A.** The autonomous model treats literacy as a fixed, context-free set of skills. B, C and D belong to the social-practice view.
4. **B.** Signing a name is a memorised pattern; it does not show that he can read a new text for a real purpose. C wrongly equates literacy with schooling, and D ignores the reading the task needs.
5. **C.** Using real texts from learners' lives is the core of the social-practice view. Printing (A) and the school setting (B) do not decide the view; reading aloud (D) is not the main point.
6. **B.** Hand-copying made books rare and expensive, so reading belonged to scholars, religious teachers, officials and the wealthy.
7. **B.** Printing allowed many copies and encouraged standard spelling. A describes oral tradition, C mass schooling and D the screen stage.
8. **A.** Older practices, such as oral recitation and handwritten letters, continued after newer tools arrived.
9. **B.** She has learned to read but now needs subject literacy: reading tables, diagrams and word problems to learn science.
10. **B.** A new tool triggered new learning late in life. This shows literacy as a lifelong process, not a replacement of reading by technology (A).

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. **Model:** Encoding is turning spoken words into written symbols, for example writing words from dictation. Composing is putting one's own ideas into writing for a reader, for example writing a note to a friend about a holiday. **Marks (3):** 1 for a clear distinction (copying or transcribing vs creating meaning); 1 for each accurate example (2).
2. **Model:** A person can read some texts well and others badly, for example a bus board but not a newspaper editorial. Literacy also depends on language and script: a child in Sindh may read Sindhi and Urdu but not English. So "literate" or "illiterate" hides real differences. **Marks (3):** 1 for each valid reason (2); 1 for a relevant Pakistani example.
3. **Model:** Literacy as a social practice means that reading and writing are always used by particular people, for particular purposes, in ways their community values. For example, a shopkeeper's *khata* uses brief notes and numbers that only the shopkeeper and customer need to understand; it builds trust. A school reading test would not count it. **Marks (3):** 1 for the idea of shared, purposeful use; 1 for the role of community values; 1 for an apt example from the learner's community.
4. **Model:** Completing primary school shows that a person attended school, not what they can do with texts. In their published study abstract, Shakir, Lone and Zafar (2012) tested people who had completed only primary school and found that the primary grades did not show whether they had reached a useful level of literacy; they suggested Class 6 as a firmer standard. **Marks (3):** 1 for the attendance-vs-ability distinction; 1 for accurate reference to the study's finding; 1 for a clear conclusion.
5. **Model:** The pass rate tells us that most pupils can do what the test measures, probably decoding and short comprehension. It cannot tell us whether pupils can read texts from their daily lives, write for their own purposes, or read in subjects such as science. It also says nothing about pupils' home literacies or other languages. So the claim is too broad: it shows skill on one test, not "excellence at literacy". **Marks (4):** 1 for what the result does show; 2 for two things it cannot show; 1 for a reasoned judgement on the claim.
6. **Model:** Print allowed many copies, so more people could own and read texts. It also made spelling and forms more standard, and encouraged silent, private reading. **Marks (3):** 1.5 for each accurate, explained change (any two of: wider access, standard spelling, private reading).
7. **Model:** When a whole country teaches every child, it needs a simple way to measure progress. Skills such as decoding can be tested and counted in large numbers, so censuses and examinations defined literacy as those skills. For example, governments counted literacy rates in censuses to report national progress. **Marks (3):** 1 for the link to national schooling; 1 for the measurability argument; 1 for an example.
8. **Model:** Learning to read is the early stage of learning letters, sounds and words, as in a Class 1 Urdu lesson on the alphabet. Reading to learn is using reading to study a subject, as when a Class 7 pupil reads a science chapter on plants. **Marks (3):** 1 for the distinction; 1 for each example (2).
9. **Model:** In the manuscript stage a *munshi* or the trader himself wrote amounts by hand in a ledger; this needed handwriting, numbers and the ability to read one's own records, and many people relied on a scribe. In the screen stage a shopkeeper may keep the record on a phone app; this still needs reading and numeracy, plus navigating menus, typing and protecting passwords. The purpose is the same, but the skills widened. **Marks (4):** 1 for each stage described accurately (2); 1 for the skills named; 1 for analysis of what changed and what stayed.
10. **Model:** The claim is mistaken because phones add new kinds of reading rather than removing old ones. Pupils must read menus, messages, warnings and prices. Judging whether a forwarded news story is true needs careful reading and comparison of sources. For example, a fake prize message can be spotted only by reading it closely. **Marks (4):** 1 for rejecting the claim with reason; 2 for explaining how old skills are still needed; 1 for a fitting example.

### ERQ rubrics

Each task uses four criteria worth **0-3 marks each**, totalling 12. Across criteria, 3 means accurate, developed and supported; 2 means mostly accurate with an incomplete link; 1 means a relevant point with little explanation; 0 means absent, wrong or unrelated. Plain, short sentences can earn full marks; a fluent answer with weak reasoning cannot.
