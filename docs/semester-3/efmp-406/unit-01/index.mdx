# Concept and Evolution of Literacy

Most people think they already know what literacy is: being able to read and write. This unit asks you to look again. A shopkeeper who keeps accounts in a notebook, a grandmother who reads the Quran but not a newspaper, and a pupil who sends voice notes but avoids writing all use literacy in some way. By the end of the unit you should be able to explain what each of them can and cannot do, and why the answer matters to a teacher.

## Unit learning outcomes

By the end of this unit, you should be able to:

- Define literacy and explain its nature as both a set of skills and a social practice. *(Understand; SLO:EFMP-406-1-1)*
- Compare the traditional skills view and the contemporary social-practice view of literacy, using one Pakistani classroom example. *(Analyze; SLO:EFMP-406-1-1, SLO:EFMP-406-1-2)*
- Describe how literacy practices developed from oral tradition to manuscripts, print, mass schooling and screens. *(Understand; SLO:EFMP-406-1-2)*
- Explain why literacy is a lifelong learning process and give examples from different stages of life. *(Apply; SLO:EFMP-406-1-2)*
- Judge how a lesson or a literacy programme reflects a particular view of literacy, and justify the judgement. *(Evaluate; both outcomes)*

## Prerequisite knowledge

This is the opening unit of the course. You need no earlier study of literacy theory. You do need your own experience of learning to read and write, at home and at school, in one or more languages. Before you begin, write three lines in your notebook: one thing you read every day, one thing you write every day, and one text you still find hard to read.

## How to use this unit

Read the two topics in order. Each one starts with a real school situation, then explains the ideas and shows a worked example before asking you to use them. Do the pair or group activity with classmates. Answer the "Check your understanding" questions without looking back, then check your answers against the explanation.

The [unit assessment](./unit-assessment) has practice questions and, at the end, a public self-study guidance section. Try the questions before you read that guidance. In formal course assessment the default weighting is 40% formative and 60% summative; your teacher will tell you which work is collected. Teachers will find planning help in the [teacher notes](./unit-teacher-notes).

Keep your reflection journal open while you read. The course guide asks you to keep one, and the questions in this unit are a good place to start it.
