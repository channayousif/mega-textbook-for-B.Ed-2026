# Topic 1.2 - How literacy practices developed, and why literacy never ends

## A real classroom situation

In this illustrative teaching case, Bilal is doing his practicum at a government girls' high school in Sukkur. In a Class 8 lesson on communication, he asks the pupils to interview an older relative about reading and writing. Next day the stories pour in. Hira's grandmother learned the Quran by heart from a teacher in the village mosque, and learned to read Urdu only at the age of fifty, at a women's literacy class. Ayesha's grandfather was a *munshi* (clerk) who wrote letters for people in the bazaar who could not write. Mariam's mother says that she was "fully educated" when she passed matric, but she had to learn again when her bank moved to a phone app.

One pupil asks, "Sir, if my mother finished school, why does she still have to learn?" Another asks, "Will phones make books disappear?" Bilal realises that his pupils are asking two big questions. How have the ways people read and write changed over time? And does anyone ever finish learning to be literate?

## Explanation

Topic 1.1 showed that literacy is both a set of skills and a social practice. Practices change when the tools, the institutions and the needs of a society change. This topic follows those changes through history, and then follows them through one person's life. The two stories have the same lesson: literacy keeps moving, so learning it never stops.

### Historical development of literacy practices

The history below is told in five broad stages. Dates are approximate and different regions moved through the stages at different times. The figure above shows the stages in order. Its dashed line makes an important point: **each new stage added to the older ones; it did not wipe them out.**

#### Stage 1: Oral tradition

For most of human history, knowledge was kept in memory and passed on by speech. Stories, poems, family histories, laws, prayers and practical knowledge were learned by listening and repeating. In our region, poetry was sung and memorised, and storytellers carried long tales from village to village. Oral cultures were not "empty" cultures. They had demanding skills of memory, performance and careful listening.

Oral practices are still alive today. Children learn nursery rhymes and *naats* by heart before they can read. Many people memorise the Quran. A teacher who asks pupils to recite a poem is using an oral practice inside a print-based school.

#### Stage 2: Writing and manuscripts

Writing systems appeared when societies needed to keep records of trade, taxes, property and law. The seals found at Mohenjo-daro in Sindh carry signs of the Indus script, which scholars have still not been able to read. That fact is itself a lesson: a writing system is useful only while a community shares the knowledge to read it.

For many centuries every book was a **manuscript**: a text copied by hand. Copying was slow and expensive, so books were rare and reading belonged to small groups: scholars, religious teachers, officials and wealthy families. Skilled scribes and calligraphers were respected. Muslim societies developed fine traditions of calligraphy for copying the Quran and other works. Most people relied on someone else to read and write for them, like Ayesha's grandfather the *munshi*. Literacy was a specialist skill, not something every person was expected to have.

#### Stage 3: Print

Printing with movable type spread in Europe from the middle of the fifteenth century, and printing presses later spread across the world. In South Asia, printing in local languages expanded greatly during the nineteenth century, and newspapers, textbooks and religious books in Urdu and other languages became cheaper and more widely available.

Print changed literacy practices in three ways.

1. **More texts reached more people.** A printed book could be made in hundreds of copies.
2. **Spelling and forms became more standard.** When the same text is printed many times, one spelling and one layout become the norm.
3. **Silent, private reading grew.** A person who owns a book can read it alone, at their own pace.

#### Stage 4: Mass schooling

Once texts were common, governments began to expect every citizen to read and write. Over the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, many countries made schooling compulsory and set up national systems to teach literacy to every child. Governments also began to **count** literacy in censuses and surveys, so that they could plan and report progress.

Pakistan's Constitution now places this duty on the state. Article 25A says that the state shall provide free and compulsory education to all children aged five to sixteen (Government of Pakistan, 2012). Mass schooling is also the stage when the traditional skills view became powerful: when a whole nation is taught and tested, literacy is easiest to define as skills that can be counted. In their published abstract, Shakir, Lone and Zafar (2012) show the limits of this approach in Pakistan, where completing primary school is often taken as proof of literacy even though the primary grades alone do not show whether a person can read and write well enough for daily life.

#### Stage 5: Screens

From the late twentieth century, computers, the internet and then mobile phones changed literacy again. Today a pupil in Sukkur may read a textbook, a cricket score on a phone, a meme, a voice note transcript and a YouTube caption in the same afternoon. Researchers introduced the idea of **multiliteracies** in the 1990s to describe this change: reading and writing now happen across several languages and several modes, such as words, images, sound and screen layout (Papen, 2023). Papen also warns that as the level of literacy a society expects keeps rising, "new illiteracies" can appear: people who were literate for one stage can find themselves left behind in the next (Papen, 2023).

#### Worked example: tracing one practice through the stages

Take a single purpose, **sending news to a family member far away**, and see how the practice changed.

| Stage | How the news was sent | Skills needed |
|---|---|---|
| Oral tradition | A traveller or relative carried the message in memory | Remembering and retelling accurately |
| Manuscript | A *munshi* wrote a letter for a fee; someone at the other end read it aloud | The writer's literacy; the family needed only to speak and listen |
| Print | Printed postcards and forms; postal services | Writing an address and a short letter oneself |
| Mass schooling | Each family member writes and reads their own letters | Composing, spelling, reading handwriting |
| Screens | A WhatsApp message, a voice note or a photo | Typing, choosing between voice, text and image, judging privacy, reading quickly |

Notice two things. First, the purpose stayed the same, but the skills changed at every stage. Second, older practices did not disappear: families still send verbal messages through relatives, and some still write letters. This is the pattern you should look for whenever you study literacy history: **same purpose, new tools, new skills, old practices still present.**

#### Correcting a common misconception

Some people believe that **contemporary literacy replaces reading and writing**. They imagine that screens, images and voice notes make traditional reading and writing less important. The worked example shows the opposite. Every new stage needed the old skills and added new ones. Using a phone well still needs reading: menus, messages, warnings, prices. Judging whether a forwarded news story is true needs careful reading and comparison. Contemporary literacy is reading and writing **plus** more, not reading and writing **minus** something. The answer to the pupil who asked "Will phones make books disappear?" is: phones have added new kinds of reading; they have not removed the need to read.

### Literacy as a lifelong learning process

**Lifelong learning** means learning that continues across the whole of life, in school and outside it, in childhood and in adulthood. Literacy is a clear example. A person does not "complete" literacy at the end of primary school. They keep learning new literacies as their life brings new texts, new roles and new tools.

#### Literacy across the life course

The diagram above shows literacy as a rising spiral. Each turn of the spiral returns to reading and writing, but at a higher and wider level.

- **Childhood.** Children meet literacy before school: stories told by grandparents, religious texts recited at home, signs and packets, a parent's phone. These early experiences shape how ready a child feels for school reading.
- **School.** In the early classes children **learn to read**: they learn letters, sounds and words. In later classes they **read to learn**: they use reading to study science, history and mathematics. Each subject has its own vocabulary and its own kinds of text, such as tables, diagrams and word problems.
- **Work.** Adults read instructions, fill in forms, keep accounts, write reports and send messages. A new job often brings new texts. A farmer who starts selling through a mobile app, or a nurse who records patient data on a tablet, has to learn a new literacy.
- **Community and citizenship.** Citizens read notices, newspapers and ballot papers, check their identity card details and follow public health messages. Taking part in public life depends on these literacies.
- **Older age.** Older people read medicine labels and doctors' instructions, learn to use phones to stay in touch, and often help grandchildren with homework. Many adults, like Hira's grandmother, learn to read for the first time late in life.

#### Why literacy is lifelong

There are three reasons why literacy cannot be learned once and finished.

1. **Life roles change.** Each new role, such as worker, parent, voter or patient, brings texts a person has not met before.
2. **Tools change.** As the history above showed, new tools keep arriving. A person who was fully literate for a print world must learn new skills for a screen world. Papen's (2023) warning about "new illiteracies" describes exactly this.
3. **Skills fade without use.** A person who leaves school early and rarely reads may lose some of the skill they had. Literacy needs to be used to stay strong.

Adult literacy work is built on this idea. Papen (2023) describes the **real literacies** approach, developed for adult literacy teaching in countries of the Global South. It starts from the texts in adult learners' own lives, such as signboards, official forms and religious texts, because adults learn best when the literacy they study is the literacy they need (Papen, 2023). The same idea works with children: reading becomes meaningful when it connects to texts they already care about.

#### Worked example: mapping one person's literacy life

Here is how to map a person's literacy across their life. Take Mariam's mother from the opening situation.

| Life stage | Texts she used | New literacy she had to learn |
|---|---|---|
| Childhood | Quran recitation, Urdu storybooks | Decoding Arabic and Urdu script |
| School to matric | Textbooks, exam papers, essays | Reading to learn; writing answers in English and Urdu |
| Married life and work | Utility bills, school notices for her children, recipes | Checking bills; reading her children's school reports |
| Now | Bank app, WhatsApp, online forms | Navigating menus, passwords, digital payment, spotting fraud messages |

The map shows that her matric certificate marked one stage of her literacy, not the end of it. At each stage she needed something new. When you map a person's literacy life, look for the **trigger** that made new learning necessary: a new role, a new tool, or a new kind of text.

#### Correcting a common misconception

A common belief is that **literacy is learned once, in primary school, and is then complete**. The life-course view shows why this is wrong. Primary school gives the foundation: decoding, basic comprehension, basic writing. But every later stage of life asks for more. In their published abstract, Shakir et al. (2012) argue that in Pakistan completing the primary grades does not by itself show that a person is literate, and they call for more focus on practical literacy skills, which suggests that even the foundation needs further building. For a teacher, the lesson is practical: do not assume that a pupil in Class 6 or Class 9 "already knows how to read". Every subject teacher is also a literacy teacher, because every subject brings new texts.

## Activity: The literacy life interview

Work in pairs. About 40 minutes, plus time to interview at home.

1. With your partner, write five interview questions to ask an older relative or neighbour about their literacy life. Include at least one question about each of: childhood, school, work, and a new tool such as a phone.
2. Each partner interviews one person before the next class. Note their answers briefly.
3. In class, each partner completes a life-map table like the worked example for the person they interviewed.
4. Compare your two tables. Mark on each table: one oral practice, one manuscript or print practice, and one screen practice. Discuss which life event or tool forced each person to learn something new.
5. Share with another pair: what does your evidence suggest about the claim "literacy is complete after primary school"?

## Check your understanding

1. Name the five stages of literacy history in this topic, in order. *(Remember)*
2. Explain one way in which print changed literacy practices. *(Understand)*
3. What is the difference between "learning to read" and "reading to learn"? Give an example of each. *(Understand)*
4. A friend says, "Now that everyone has phones, children do not need to read books." Write two sentences in reply, using the idea of new tools needing old skills. *(Apply)*
5. Give one example of a new literacy that an adult in your community has had to learn in the last five years, and name the trigger that made it necessary. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Literacy practices have changed through oral tradition, manuscripts, print, mass schooling and screens. At each stage new tools brought new skills and new groups of readers, but older practices continued alongside the new ones. Contemporary literacy adds to reading and writing; it does not replace them.

The same pattern appears in one person's life. Literacy grows from childhood stories through school, work, community life and older age. New roles, new tools and new texts keep demanding new learning, and skills fade if they are not used. That is why literacy is a lifelong learning process, and why every teacher is a literacy teacher.

In one lesson you observe at your practicum school, note every kind of reading and writing the pupils are asked to do: copying, reading aloud, silent reading, answering questions, reading a diagram or table, using a device. For each, note whether it is "learning to read" or "reading to learn". Ask your cooperating teacher: "Which subject texts do your pupils find hardest to read?" ## Summative task

Write 400-500 words on the question: "Is a person who passed matric twenty years ago literate today?"

1. Use at least two stages of literacy history to explain how the demands of literacy have changed.
2. Use a life-course map of one real or invented person to analyse which literacies they have and which they may lack.
3. Reach a judgement on the question and justify it with evidence from your analysis.

*(Analyze, Evaluate)*

**Mini-rubric (10 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong (3-4) | Partial (1-2) | Missing (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Use of literacy history (3 marks) | Explains accurately how two stages changed literacy demands | Names stages with little explanation | No historical stages used |
| Life-course analysis (4 marks) | Maps the person's literacies and identifies specific gaps and the triggers behind them | Describes the person's literacy without analysing gaps | No person or no analysis |
| Judgement and justification (3 marks) | Reaches a clear, qualified judgement supported by the analysis | States a judgement with general reasons | No judgement |

The life-course and judgement criteria need analysis: show how the person's situation leads to your conclusion. A general essay on "the importance of literacy" does not meet them.
