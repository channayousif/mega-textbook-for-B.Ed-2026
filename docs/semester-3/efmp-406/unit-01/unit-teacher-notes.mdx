# Concept and Evolution of Literacy - Teacher Notes

These notes support the teacher educator who teaches this unit. They contain no assessment items. The course guide names lecture, discussion, question-answer and brainstorming as teaching strategies, together with group discussions and seminars, role-plays and simulations, case study analysis and reflection journals. The suggestions below use those strategies.

## Sequencing

Teach the two topics in order. Topic 1.1 builds the vocabulary (decoding, comprehension, composing, functional literacy, literacy practice) and the two views of literacy. Topic 1.2 uses that vocabulary to read history and a single life. The guide gives no week allocation for its six units, so plan the time to suit your own timetable.

A workable sequence is:

1. Open with the Sana and Imran case from Topic 1.1 before any definition. Let trainees argue about who is "more literate".
2. Introduce the three parts of literacy and functional literacy through a short lecture with question-answer.
3. Run the "Literacy practices in our town" activity, then introduce the two views as a way of naming what the groups found.
4. Move to Topic 1.2 with the interview homework set a week ahead, so that trainees bring real stories to class.
5. Teach the five historical stages with the "same purpose, new tools" worked example, then the life course.
6. Close with a seminar on the question "Is literacy ever complete?"

## Discussion prompt and a likely wrong answer

**Prompt:** "A person in your village can recite long passages of poetry and the Quran from memory but cannot read a newspaper. Is that person literate?"

**Likely wrong answer:** "No, because they cannot read." Trainees often treat oral skill as the absence of literacy.

**How to work with it:** Do not simply correct the answer. Ask: "What skills does the person have? What practices do they take part in? Which of the three parts of literacy are missing, and for which texts?" Lead the group to a more exact statement, such as: "The person has strong oral and memorisation skills and takes part in a valued religious practice, but is not yet able to decode and understand printed Urdu." This is the move from a yes-or-no label to a description by degree, language and purpose.

## Likely misconceptions and how to handle them

- **"Literacy only means reading and writing."** Accept that reading and writing are central, then ask what reading and writing are *for*. The *khata* example in Topic 1.1 works well.
- **"A person who can sign their name is literate."** Use a role-play: one trainee plays a landowner asking another to sign a document they cannot read. The risk becomes obvious.
- **"Literacy is learned once, in primary school."** The interview activity in Topic 1.2 usually produces counter-examples from trainees' own families. Collect them on the board.
- **"Contemporary literacy replaces reading and writing."** Ask trainees to list everything they read on their phones in one day. The list is always long.

Some trainees will feel that the social-practice view criticises the skills they were taught. Make clear that the unit does not reject phonics, spelling or grammar. It asks teachers to teach those skills while also seeing and using the literacies pupils already have.

## Practical work

The guide lists group work, group assignments, individual assignments and presentations. Suggested tasks:

- **Group work:** the "Literacy practices in our town" analysis, presented as a two-minute report.
- **Group assignment:** a wall display of a town's literacy practices, each with a photograph or a drawing and the five-question analysis (text, skills, language, purpose, value). Ask permission before photographing people or private documents.
- **Individual assignment:** the literacy life interview from Topic 1.2, written up as a life-course map with a one-paragraph reflection.
- **Presentation:** a five-minute talk on one stage of literacy history, using a local example.
- **Reflection journal:** the guide weights class participation and reflective journals at 10% of the course. Ask trainees to begin their journal with the three lines suggested in the unit opening and to add one entry for each topic.

Visits to inclusive schools are among the guide's strategies. If a visit is possible during this unit, ask trainees to note which texts pupils with different needs use, and how the school adapts them. This prepares the ground for later units on equity and inclusion.
