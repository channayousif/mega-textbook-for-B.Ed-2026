# Topic 1.1 - What literacy means, then and now

## A real classroom situation

In this illustrative teaching case, Nadia is a trainee teacher at a government primary school in Tando Allahyar. In her Class 4 Urdu lesson she asks pupils to read a short passage aloud. Sana reads every word smoothly and quickly. When Nadia asks what the passage was about, Sana is silent. Imran reads slowly and stops at long words. After school, Nadia sees Imran at his father's small shop. He reads the labels on packets, checks the prices on a supplier's list and writes the day's sales in a notebook. He also reads an SMS from a supplier on his father's phone and replies to it.

Nadia's mentor teacher says, "Sana is our best reader. Imran is weak." Nadia is not sure. Sana can sound out print but did not make meaning from it. Imran struggles to read aloud but uses reading and writing every day to get real work done. Which of them is more literate? The answer depends on what we think literacy is. That is the question of this topic.

## Explanation

Teachers use the word "literacy" every day, in timetables, in policy documents and in conversations with parents. Yet the word carries two different ideas. The first idea is about **skills**: what a person can do with letters, words and numbers. The second idea is about **practices**: what people actually do with texts in their lives, and why. This topic explains both ideas and shows why a teacher needs both.

### Meaning and nature of literacy

At its simplest, **literacy** is the ability to read and write. A **text** here means anything written or printed that carries a message: a book, a shop sign, a form, a text message or a medicine label. A literate person can take meaning from a text and can put meaning into one.

That simple definition already contains three parts.

1. **Decoding and encoding.** Decoding is turning written symbols into words. Encoding is turning words into written symbols. A child who sounds out "ک ت ا ب" and says "کتاب" is decoding. A child who writes "کتاب" from dictation is encoding.
2. **Comprehension.** Comprehension is understanding the meaning of what is read. Decoding without comprehension is like reading aloud in a language you do not know. Sana, in the situation above, decoded well but did not show comprehension.
3. **Composing.** Composing is putting your own ideas into writing so that a reader can understand them. Copying a sentence from the board is encoding. Writing a note to a friend is composing.

Most definitions also include **numeracy**: the ability to understand and use numbers in daily life, such as reading a price list, checking a bill or measuring medicine. Imran's notebook of sales uses literacy and numeracy together.

**Functional literacy** is literacy that is good enough to meet the demands of a person's daily life and work. A person is functionally literate if they can read the instructions on a fertiliser bag, fill in a bank form or follow a notice from the school. The word "functional" points to use: literacy is measured by what it lets a person do.

#### The nature of literacy: four features

Literacy is not one thing that a person either has or lacks. Four features describe its nature.

- **It is a matter of degree.** People are more or less literate, not simply literate or illiterate. A person may read a bus destination board easily but struggle with a newspaper editorial.
- **It depends on the language and the script.** Many Pakistani children meet three or more languages: a home language such as Sindhi, Saraiki, Pashto or Balochi, then Urdu, then English. A child may read Urdu well and English poorly. Literacy in one script does not transfer automatically to another.
- **It depends on the kind of text.** Reading a story, a timetable, a map and a WhatsApp message each need slightly different knowledge. A good reader of stories may be a poor reader of tables.
- **It is used for purposes.** People read and write to do something: to pray, to trade, to keep in touch, to vote, to learn. The purpose shapes which skills matter.

The last feature is the key to the second idea of literacy. When we look at purposes, we see that literacy is also a **social practice**.

#### Literacy as social practice

A **literacy practice** is a shared, regular way in which a group of people uses and values reading and writing. Papen (2023) describes the work of the New Literacy Studies, a group of researchers who, from the 1980s, studied how literacy is actually used in homes, workplaces and communities. They argued that literacy is a social and cultural practice. It is shaped by the values and beliefs of a community and by who holds power in it (Papen, 2023).

Some examples from Pakistan make the idea concrete.

- A family reads the Quran together after Fajr prayer. The text is sacred, it is read aloud in Arabic, and accuracy of pronunciation matters more than speed.
- A shopkeeper in Hyderabad keeps a *khata* (credit notebook) for regular customers. The writing is brief, uses numbers and abbreviations, and only the shopkeeper and the customer need to understand it.
- A young man in Larkana fills in an online job application on his phone. He needs to read instructions in English, type his details and upload a photo of his certificate.
- A mother in Quetta writes a short note to her child's teacher in Urdu to explain an absence.

Each of these uses reading and writing. But each has its own rules, its own language, its own purpose and its own idea of what "good" reading or writing is. That is what it means to call literacy a social practice.

#### Worked example: describing a literacy practice

Here is how to analyse one literacy practice using the four features above. Take the *khata* notebook.

| Question | Answer for the *khata* |
|---|---|
| What text is used? | A handwritten notebook of names, dates and amounts |
| What skills does it need? | Writing names and numbers, adding and subtracting, reading one's own handwriting |
| Which language or script? | Often Urdu or Sindhi script for names, with numerals |
| What is the purpose? | To remember who owes how much, and to build trust with customers |
| Who values it, and why? | The shopkeeper and customers value it as a record both sides accept |

Notice what the analysis shows. The *khata* needs real literacy and numeracy skills. But a school reading test would never ask about it. A shopkeeper who keeps a perfect *khata* might still "fail" a reading test based on a story. When you describe a literacy practice, always ask about text, skills, language, purpose and value. You will use this method in the activity.

#### Correcting a common misconception

A common belief is that **literacy only means being able to read and write**. This belief is not wrong, but it is incomplete. It describes the skills and leaves out the purposes and practices. Under this belief, Sana is "literate" because she reads aloud well, and Imran is "weak". Once we add comprehension and practice, the picture changes. Sana needs help to make meaning from texts. Imran needs help to read school texts with confidence, but he already uses literacy in demanding ways. A teacher who sees only the skills will miss what Imran brings to the classroom and will not notice what Sana is missing.

### Traditional and contemporary views of literacy

Over the last century, thinking about literacy has moved from a **traditional view** to a **contemporary view**. The two views do not disagree about whether children should learn letters, spelling and grammar. They disagree about what literacy is, where it is learned, and how it should be measured.

#### The traditional view: literacy as a set of skills

The traditional view treats literacy as a fixed set of mental skills: recognising letters, decoding words, spelling correctly and writing grammatical sentences. Papen (2023) calls this the "skills view". The researcher Brian Street called it the **autonomous model** of literacy, because it treats literacy as a skill that stands on its own, the same everywhere, separate from the people and places where it is used (Papen, 2023).

The skills view has real strengths. It is simple to explain. It gives teachers a clear sequence: letters, then words, then sentences. It makes literacy easy to test and count across a whole country. These are good reasons why governments and examination boards rely on it.

It also has weaknesses. Papen (2023) points out three.

1. It turns literacy into a "simplified" idea that ignores how literacy is used in real life.
2. It treats literacy as something an individual owns, so people who do badly on tests are labelled as lacking, and that label often follows lines of class, ethnicity and region.
3. It can impose one culture's idea of literacy on other cultures, so the literacies people already have are not counted.

In Pakistan, the traditional view is visible in how literacy is often measured. In their published abstract, Shakir, Lone and Zafar (2012) note that completing primary school is commonly treated as a sign that a person is literate. When they tested people in selected rural and urban areas who had completed primary school but gone no further, they found that the primary grades alone did not show whether a person had reached a useful level of literacy, and suggested that Class 6 might be a firmer standard (Shakir et al., 2012). Their point is important: a certificate or a yes-or-no count tells us little about what a person can actually do with texts.

#### Correcting a second misconception

A related belief is that **a person who can sign their name is literate**. Signing a name is a useful skill. It lets a person sign a form or a register. But it is a single memorised pattern. It does not show that the person can decode new words, understand a notice or write a message. A person who signs a land document they cannot read may be cheated. Signing is therefore a very small part of literacy, not proof of it. When you hear "she is literate, she can sign her name", ask: what else can she read and write, and for what purposes?

#### The contemporary view: literacy as social practice

The contemporary view grew from the New Literacy Studies. It accepts that skills matter, but it adds that literacy is always used by real people, for real purposes, in real places. Street called this the **ideological model** of literacy, because it recognises that literacy is tied to the values, beliefs and power of a community (Papen, 2023).

The contemporary view makes four claims.

- **There are many literacies, not one.** Religious literacy, workplace literacy, school literacy and digital literacy are different practices. A person can be strong in one and weak in another.
- **Literacy is learned in many places.** Children learn literacy at home, in the mosque or temple, in the market and on the phone, as well as at school.
- **Home literacies should be built on.** Papen (2023) reports that children whose home literacy practices match school practices, such as bedtime stories, start school with an advantage. Children whose home practices are different are often seen as having "no" literacy. Good teaching starts from what children already do with texts.
- **Literacy now includes more than print.** In the 1990s researchers introduced the idea of **multiliteracies**: literacy across several languages and several modes, such as images, sound, gesture and screen layout, as well as print (Papen, 2023). A child who reads a road sign, a cartoon and a phone screen is using more than one mode.

#### Worked example: comparing the two views in one classroom

Return to Nadia's class. Here is how each view would judge Sana and Imran, and what it would advise.

| | Traditional (skills) view | Contemporary (social-practice) view |
|---|---|---|
| Sana | Strong reader: fast, accurate decoding | Decodes well but does not yet make meaning; needs comprehension work |
| Imran | Weak reader: slow, makes errors | Uses reading, writing and numeracy at the shop; needs confidence with school texts |
| What the teacher should do | More reading-aloud practice for Imran | Bring shop labels and price lists into lessons as reading material; ask Sana questions about meaning, not only speed |

Which view is "right"? Neither is enough on its own. The skills view reminds Nadia that Imran must become a fluent reader of school texts, because examinations and further study depend on it. The social-practice view reminds her that Imran already has literacy she can build on, and that Sana's fluent reading hides a gap. A thoughtful teacher uses the skills view to plan what to teach and the social-practice view to decide how to teach it and what to count.

## Activity: Literacy practices in our town

Work in groups of four. About 30 minutes.

1. Each member names one literacy practice they have seen in their own family or town in the last week. Examples: a prayer book, a utility bill, a wedding card, a WhatsApp family group, a bus timetable, a madrassa *takhti* (wooden writing board).
2. As a group, choose two of the practices. For each, complete the five-question table from the worked example: text, skills, language or script, purpose, who values it and why.
3. Discuss: would a school reading test notice this practice? Would a person who does it well be counted as literate?
4. Prepare a two-minute report for the class that names one literacy that the traditional view would miss, and says why a teacher should know about it.

## Check your understanding

1. Define literacy in one sentence, then name its three parts. *(Remember)*
2. In your own words, explain the difference between decoding and comprehension. Use Sana's case. *(Understand)*
3. What does "functional literacy" mean? Give one example from rural Sindh or Punjab. *(Understand)*
4. A colleague says, "My aunt can sign her name, so she is literate." Write two sentences to explain to your colleague what this statement leaves out. *(Apply)*
5. Classify each statement as belonging to the traditional view or the contemporary view: (a) "A child who fails the reading test is illiterate." (b) "The market is a place where children learn to read." (c) "Literacy is the same in every country." *(Apply)*

## Summary

Literacy is more than the ability to read and write. It includes decoding, comprehension and composing, and it usually includes numeracy. It is a matter of degree, it depends on language, script and type of text, and it is always used for a purpose.

The traditional view sees literacy as a fixed set of skills that a person either has or lacks, and it measures literacy with tests or school certificates. The contemporary view sees literacy as a social practice: many literacies, learned in many places, shaped by community values and power, and now spread across print, images and screens. A good teacher needs both views: the first to plan skills, the second to see and build on what learners already do.

## Try this at your practicum school

During one practicum day, list every kind of text you see pupils use in and out of the classroom: textbooks, the board, notices, wrappers, phones, religious texts, sports scores. Mark each one "school text" or "out-of-school text". Then ask your cooperating teacher which of these texts are ever used in lessons. Write a short reflection in your journal: which literacies do pupils bring to school that the school does not use?

## Summative task

Choose one pupil you have observed, or a pupil from a case your teacher gives you. Write 400-500 words that:

1. Describe what the pupil can do with texts in school and out of school.
2. Analyse the pupil using both the traditional skills view and the contemporary social-practice view.
3. Judge which view gives the more useful picture of this pupil for a teacher, and justify your judgement with evidence from the case.

*(Analyze, Evaluate)*

| Criterion | Strong (3-4) | Partial (1-2) | Missing (0) |
