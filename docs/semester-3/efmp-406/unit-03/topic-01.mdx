# Topic 3.1 - Literacy, culture and power

## A real classroom situation

At a Government Boys High School in Khairpur, trainee teacher Tariq observes a Grade 6 social
studies class. The teacher asks the pupils to open their Urdu textbook and read aloud a passage
about agricultural trade in the Indus basin. Babar, an eleven-year-old pupil whose family farms dates
and wheat nearby, hesitates, mispronounces several formal Urdu vocabulary words, and stumbles over the
script. The classroom teacher sighs, tells Babar to sit down, and remarks to the class that Babar is
"barely literate" and needs to learn basic reading before attending middle school.

During the lunch break, Tariq visits the school office and notices Babar helping his uncle, who has
come to pay the school development fund. Babar fluently inspects the printed receipt, compares the
figures with a handwritten ledger (khata) kept in Sindhi, calculates the remaining balance on his
father's mobile phone, and explains the transaction clearly to his uncle. Babar is reading, interpreting,
calculating, and communicating across two languages and two scripts with precision and confidence.

The classroom teacher judged Babar as illiterate because Babar struggled with a single, formal,
school-sanctioned text. In his family and community, however, Babar routinely demonstrates complex
literacy competence. This contrast exposes the central question of this topic: who decides what
counts as literacy, and how do language and power shape that decision?

## Explanation

### Literacy as a social and cultural practice

For a long time, many people treated literacy as a single set of skills inside a person's head:
turning letters into sounds (decoding) and sounds into letters (encoding). Papen (2023) calls this the
"skills view" and notes that Brian Street named it the **autonomous model** of literacy. "Autonomous"
here means "standing on its own": the skill is thought to work the same way in every place and to
belong to the individual. Papen writes that this view is "pervasive, shaping educational policy and
public opinion".

From the 1980s, researchers in the New Literacy Studies (NLS) offered another view. Papen (2023)
explains that, beginning with the work of Brian Street, Shirley Brice Heath and others, they saw
literacy "as social and cultural practice: embedded in, shaped by and shaping values, beliefs and
power relations". Street called this the **ideological model**. "Ideological" here does not mean
political propaganda. It means that the way people use and value reading and writing always carries
the beliefs and power relations of a community. In this view, reading and writing take their meaning
from the activities in which people use them: a shop, a mosque class, a clinic, a school.

The two views are not simply opposites. Papen (2023) notes that social accounts of literacy "complement
cognitively oriented research". Children still need to learn to decode. The social view adds that
what they read, why they read it and who judges their reading also matter.

To study literacy as a social practice, this textbook uses two working terms that are common in NLS
research. Papen (2023) describes literacy practices as "shared cultural ways of using and valuing
literacy in different contexts"; the explanation of literacy events below is the textbook's own.

1. **Literacy events**: These are observable occasions in daily life where a written text plays a
   meaningful role in human interaction. A teacher writing on a blackboard, a mother reading a doctor's
   prescription, a shopkeeper recording a credit entry in a red cloth ledger (khata), an elder reading
   an invitation card, or a youth sending a text message on WhatsApp are all literacy events. You can
   see the people, the physical text, and the actions taking place.
2. **Literacy practices**: These are the shared habits, values and relationships that give meaning
   to literacy events. You cannot photograph a literacy practice directly.
   It includes who is considered authorized to interpret a text, what counts as good writing, why a
   particular script is respected, and what social status attaches to reading. When a family gathers to
   hear a letter from an overseas worker read aloud by the eldest daughter, the visible gathering is the
   literacy event; the shared family trust, gender roles, and mutual solidarity represent the literacy
   practice.

Consider a concrete Pakistani worked example.

**Worked example: Analyzing a literacy event into practice**
- *The observed situation*: In a village in Badin, farmers gather around the village head's veranda.
  A young college student reads aloud a government flood rehabilitation compensation form. The farmers
  listen attentively, ask the student to explain the bureaucratic criteria, debate what information
  must be entered, and affix their thumb impressions or signatures while discussing the local irrigation
  canal.
- *Step 1: Identify the literacy event*. The event is the physical gathering, the printed government
  form, the student reading the Urdu text aloud, and the farmers affixing their marks.
- *Step 2: Identify the participant roles and communicative modes*. The student acts as a literate
  mediator, translating formal bureaucratic Urdu into colloquial Sindhi; the farmers contribute local
  geographical knowledge and community memory.
- *Step 3: Analyze the underlying literacy practice*. The practice is communal, collaborative, and
  oral-print blended. Literacy is not an individual isolated act; it is shared community work. Authority
  is shared between the formal decoder (the student) and the experienced elders who verify whether the
  form reflects their true land claims.

**Naming and correcting the misconception: "Literacy is neutral and means the same thing everywhere."**
Many trainee teachers enter the profession believing that literacy is identical whether a child is in
Karachi, Gilgit, London, or rural Balochistan. This is incorrect. School literacy prioritizes silent,
individual, test-oriented reading of authorized textbooks. Community literacy in Pakistan often
prioritizes collective discussion, oral performance, religious devotion, trade accounting, and shared
interpretation. When teachers realize that literacy is culturally situated, they stop treating pupils
like Babar as if they bring nothing to school. Instead, they recognize that learners arrive with rich community
literacy traditions that can serve as building blocks for academic success.

### Language, identity, and power relations

Because literacy is part of social life, it is also connected with power. In every society, some
kinds of reading and writing are valued more than others. This textbook uses two working labels for
this difference:

- **Dominant literacies**: These are the forms of reading and writing set down in official rules,
  promoted, and demanded by official state institutions - schools, universities, courts, government
  bureaucracies, and formal corporate employers. Dominant literacies are associated with standard
  language varieties, formal examinations, and social gatekeeping.
- **Vernacular literacies**: These are the forms of reading and writing that originate in everyday
  community life, family traditions, informal marketplaces, and local cultural networks. They are
  often expressed in regional mother tongues, colloquial dialects, informal scripts, or forms that
  mix writing with pictures, gesture and speech.

In Pakistan, this distinction is charged with institutional power. Formal schooling historically
privileges standard Urdu and formal English as dominant literacies. A pupil who speaks Sindhi, Punjabi,
Pashto, Balochi, Saraiki, Hindko, or Shina at home finds that their mother tongue is frequently absent
from classroom materials or actively discouraged in school.

When a school treats only dominant school literacy as real literacy, it also sends a message about
**learner identity**, that is, how pupils see themselves and how others see them. A child's home
language is the language of family, early memories and friendships. When teachers call it broken or
an obstacle, many pupils hear that their family and community are not valued.

Papen (2023) notes that when literacy is seen only as a skill a person has or lacks, it becomes
connected to "how one is seen and positioned by others" and can be "linked to class, race and
ethnicity-related prejudices and negative stereotypes". She adds that when schools do not recognise the
literacy practices children bring from home, this can alienate children "from school curricula and
learning, setting them up for failure". In such classrooms, some pupils who find the school language
hard may become quiet or stop taking part. Responses differ from child to child. A teacher who treats
every language as a full language, with its own grammar and power to express ideas, makes it easier
for pupils to keep their confidence while they learn the school language.

**Naming and correcting the misconception: "A child who speaks Sindhi or Pashto at home is behind in intelligence."**
This common belief mixes up knowing a language with being able to think. A child who starts school
speaking Pashto or Sindhi well has already learned the sounds, words and sentence rules of a full
language. Such a child is not behind in intelligence; the child simply knows a different language
from the one in the textbook. If a teacher decides that a pupil who hesitates in formal Urdu is not
clever, the teacher has blamed the wrong cause. The difficulty usually lies in lessons that give the
pupil no bridge from the home language to the school language.

**Naming and correcting the misconception: "Only English literacy counts in modern Pakistan."**
While English carries undeniable economic prestige and administrative power in Pakistan, treating
English as the only valid form of literacy distorts education. It marginalizes the rich literary
heritage of Pakistan's regional languages - such as the poetry of Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, Rahman Baba,
Bulleh Shah, and Mast Tawkali - and disconnects schooling from the everyday communicative needs of
Pakistani society. Effective contemporary literacy education does not abandon English; it positions
literacy as an additive repertoire where pupils develop fluency across multiple languages and modes.

## Activity: Mapping community literacy practices

**Mode:** Collaborative pair and small-group inquiry.
**Time allocation:** Approximately 25 minutes.

1. **Form pairs.** Think of an actual primary or middle school pupil you have met during your practicum
   or in your own neighbourhood.
2. **Construct a 24-hour literacy timeline.** Track all the moments during a typical day when this child
   encounters or uses written text, symbols, numbers, or spoken language:
   - At home before school (e.g. reading a calendar, praying, looking at product packaging, mobile messages).
   - On the way to school (e.g. shop signboards, transport slogans, road signs).
   - In the classroom during lessons.
   - During break or after school (e.g. street games, community shop ledgers, religious classes, family chats).
3. **Classify each instance.** Label each moment as either a *dominant school literacy* or a
   *vernacular community literacy*.
4. **Design a pedagogical bridge.** Identify one vernacular literacy practice your child engages in outside
   school (for example, managing cricket scores, deciphering a family medicine schedule, or reciting folk
   tales). Write three specific steps a Grade 5 teacher could take to connect that out-of-school literacy
   practice to a textbook reading comprehension lesson.
5. **Share with another pair.** Compare your bridging strategies. How did your plan protect the child's
   linguistic identity while supporting formal reading development?

## Summary

Literacy is more than a neutral technical skill; it is also a social and cultural practice that is
part of everyday relationships. Decoding still matters, and the social view adds context to it. The New Literacy Studies demonstrates that reading and
writing take different forms across home, community, religious, and school domains. When schools
privilege dominant literacies while dismissing vernacular community practices, they can treat pupils
unequally and push some of them away from learning. Teachers who understand literacy as a social practice can build
constructive bridges from pupils' rich linguistic and cultural backgrounds into academic success.

## Try this at your practicum school

During your next practicum visit, conduct an informal linguistic and literacy inventory:
1. Spend 15 minutes in the school playground or canteen during recess. Observe and note down the languages
   and communication styles pupils use with each other when no teacher is directing them.
2. Observe a reading lesson in a primary classroom. Count how many times pupils are invited to use their
   home language to clarify a difficult concept, and how many times they are corrected or reprimanded for
   doing so.
3. Talk with one pupil who struggles with textbook reading. Ask them about their favourite activity outside
   school (such as helping in a family workshop, cooking, or sports). Identify what kinds of reading,
   numeracy, or sign interpretation they use in that activity.
4. Record your reflections in two paragraphs: how could the classroom teacher draw upon this pupil's real
   strengths to make the textbook accessible?

## Summative task

Read the following scenario and write an analytical response of approximately 350 to 500 words.

**Scenario**:
In a government primary school in rural Dera Ghazi Khan, Grade 4 teacher Master Aslam strictly enforces a
rule: "Only Urdu may be spoken in my classroom. Anyone speaking Saraiki will be fined five rupees and made
to stand in the corner. If you speak regional dialects, you will never learn to read or become civilized."
Several Saraiki-speaking children have become silent, refuse to participate in reading aloud, and have
begun missing school frequently.

**Task**:
Analyse Master Aslam's policy using the theoretical concepts from this topic:
1. Explain which model of literacy (autonomous or ideological) underlies Master Aslam's teaching philosophy.
2. Evaluate how his approach impacts the children's language, identity, and academic engagement.
3. Propose two concrete pedagogical alternatives Master Aslam could use to teach standard Urdu literacy
   while validating the children's Saraiki linguistic heritage.

**Mini-rubric** (10 marks):
- *Conceptual understanding (0-3 marks)*: Defines and applies the autonomous and ideological models and
  the dominant and vernacular labels.
  - 3: names the autonomous model, gives two features of Master Aslam's rule that show it, and uses
    the dominant/vernacular labels correctly.
  - 2: names the correct model with one supporting feature, or uses the terms with one small error.
  - 1: names a model with no reason, or confuses the two models.
  - 0: no relevant concept.
- *Analysis of language and power (0-4 marks - **Analyze**)*: Analyses how the rule may affect identity,
  participation and fairness, using evidence from the scenario.
  - 4: links the fine, the "civilized" remark and the children's silence and absence to identity and
    to unequal treatment of Saraiki speakers; notes that other causes of absence are possible.
  - 3: makes two clear links between the rule and its effects, with some reference to the scenario.
  - 2: describes effects in general terms without linking them to the rule or the scenario.
  - 1: states that the rule is harmful with no explanation.
  - 0: no relevant analysis.
- *Pedagogical recommendations (0-3 marks)*: Proposes two realistic strategies that use pupils' home
  language to support Urdu literacy.
  - 3: two practical strategies, each with a clear classroom step and a stated link to Urdu reading.
  - 2: two strategies, one vague; or one strong strategy.
  - 1: a general suggestion such as "be kind to Saraiki" with no classroom step.
  - 0: no relevant recommendation.
