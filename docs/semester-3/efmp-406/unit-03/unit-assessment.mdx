# Unit 3 Assessment - Literacy, Language, and Society

## Unit summary

This unit examined literacy as a socially situated and culturally shaped practice within Pakistani
society. Moving beyond traditional autonomous views that treat literacy as a neutral mechanical skill,
the New Literacy Studies demonstrates that reading and writing are ideological practices bound to
social institutions, power dynamics, and human relationships. Everyday literacy events take varied
forms across homes, workplaces, marketplaces, religious settings, and formal schools.

In Pakistan's multilingual federation, language and literacy intersect directly with learner identity
and educational equity. When schools devalue children's regional mother tongues and value only
dominant institutional literacies, they can weaken pupils' confidence and participation. Civic
literacy, the ability to understand public laws, judge official claims and voice community needs,
helps citizens use the rights they already hold. Rather than suppressing
pupils' home languages, additive multilingualism and scaffolded translanguaging allow teachers to
bridge learners' cultural funds of knowledge into academic success and active democratic participation.

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. Which of the following statements best defines the autonomous model of literacy? *(Remember)*
   - A) Literacy is a neutral, universal cognitive technology of decoding and encoding independent of context.
   - B) Literacy is a socially situated practice embedded in cultural institutions and community values.
   - C) Literacy is solely determined by political power and state curriculum mandates.
   - D) Literacy is restricted to reading and writing in one's primary regional mother tongue.

2. A mother reads an immunization card aloud and discusses the next clinic date with a female health worker. In the New Literacy Studies, this observable interaction is classified as: *(Understand)*
   - A) A dominant literacy regulation.
   - B) A literacy event.
   - C) An autonomous skill acquisition.
   - D) A subtractive literacy deficit.

3. Brian Street contrasts the autonomous model of literacy with the ideological model. The ideological model claims that: *(Understand)*
   - A) Literacy is culturally neutral and produces identical cognitive gains in all societies.
   - B) Reading and writing practices are always shaped by social power relations and cultural values.
   - C) Only standardized national languages should be used for school instruction.
   - D) Mechanical decoding must precede any meaningful understanding of texts.

4. A grain merchant keeps daily credit records in Sindhi in a handwritten ledger (khata), while his son reads formal Urdu invoices from Karachi. What does this demonstrate about community literacy? *(Apply)*
   - A) Vernacular community literacies serve practical economic functions alongside formal literacies.
   - B) Informal ledgers are illiterate practices that must be replaced by English accounting.
   - C) The father possesses no functional literacy skills.
   - D) Traditional scripts prevent children from learning formal mathematics.

5. When a teacher punishes pupils for speaking their regional home language in class, what is the most likely impact on the learners? *(Understand)*
   - A) It rapidly increases the pupils' innate cognitive intelligence.
   - B) It can damage the pupils' sense of linguistic identity and suggest that their culture is inferior.
   - C) It guarantees fluent English pronunciation within a few weeks.
   - D) It strengthens the children's emotional connection to schooling.

6. Which characteristic distinguishes dominant school literacies from vernacular community literacies? *(Understand)*
   - A) Dominant literacies are validated and credentialed by formal state educational institutions.
   - B) Dominant literacies are learned exclusively in informal home environments.
   - C) Vernacular literacies carry higher legal prestige in civil service examinations.
   - D) Vernacular literacies never involve written symbols or numbers.

7. Which of the following is a primary component of civic literacy in a constitutional democracy? *(Remember)*
   - A) Memorizing dates of past political dynasties without understanding current laws.
   - B) Reading, comprehending, and critically evaluating public policies and democratic rights.
   - C) Following official government directives without questioning evidence or bias.
   - D) Limiting reading exclusively to entertainment and commercial advertisements.

8. A Grade 8 class examines a municipal flyer about road construction and asks why the budget figures do not state expenditure for village feeder roads. This classroom task exemplifies: *(Apply)*
   - A) Passive mechanical transcription.
   - B) Critical reading of civic texts for democratic accountability.
   - C) Subtractive language immersion.
   - D) Autonomous phonics instruction.

9. Which statement matches how Ashraf, Turner, and Laar (2021) describe the roles of languages in Pakistani education? *(Remember)*
   - A) Official language policy and classroom language use are fully aligned.
   - B) English is the dominant language, Urdu the national language, and local languages mark the speaker's identity.
   - C) The complete absence of regional languages from informal classroom interactions.
   - D) Universal mother-tongue medium of instruction across all private and public schools.

10. A Grade 4 teacher in Swat introduces the Urdu word for "evaporation", lets pupils discuss what it means in Pashto in pairs, and then asks each pair to write a two-line explanation in Urdu. Which practice is the teacher using? *(Apply)*
    - A) Subtractive language immersion.
    - B) An autonomous phonics drill.
    - C) Planned translanguaging, drawing on pupils' full linguistic repertoire to support understanding.
    - D) Strict monolingual instruction.

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

1. Define literacy as a social practice in two or three sentences, highlighting how it differs from a purely cognitive skill view. *(Understand)*

2. Contrast the autonomous model of literacy with the ideological model of literacy in three distinct dimensions. *(Understand)*

3. Differentiate between a literacy event and a literacy practice, providing one concrete Pakistani classroom or community example of each. *(Apply)*

4. Explain how a school's deficit framing of a pupil's regional mother tongue can undermine that learner's academic self-concept and classroom participation. *(Analyze)*

5. Identify three distinguishing features of dominant literacies compared to vernacular community literacies in Pakistan. *(Understand)*

6. Name and briefly describe the four core dimensions of civic literacy required for active democratic participation. *(Understand)*

7. A local government council issues a public notice about flood relief eligibility in dense official Urdu (an invented scenario). Give two reasons why mechanical decoding alone does not ensure civic access for rural residents. *(Analyze)*

8. Using Ashraf, Turner, and Laar (2021), outline the roles of English, Urdu and local languages in Pakistani education (the unit's "three layers" summary), describing the main social role of each. *(Understand)*

9. Differentiate between subtractive multilingualism and additive multilingualism, explaining their different effects on young children's foundational reading development. *(Understand)*

10. A Grade 5 science teacher in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa uses Pashto to explain the concept of photosynthesis before guiding pupils to write their summary in Urdu. Explain two pedagogical benefits of this translanguaging practice. *(Apply)*

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. In many rural Pakistani schools, educational authorities evaluate literacy strictly through mechanical reading of prescribed textbooks, ignoring community print practices. Drawing upon Uta Papen (2023) and the New Literacy Studies, analyze the theoretical limitations of this autonomous approach. Contrast it with an ideological model, and propose three ways a primary school can integrate community literacy practices into its formal reading curriculum. *(Analyze)*

2. Language choices in education are never neutral; they construct identity and reinforce social power relations. Critically evaluate how the marginalization of regional mother tongues (such as Sindhi, Punjabi, Pashto, Balochi, or Saraiki) in formal Pakistani education affects pupil identity, self-worth, and educational equity. Formulate an asset-based framework that teachers can adopt to validate diverse linguistic backgrounds in daily instruction. *(Evaluate)*

3. Article 25-A of the Constitution of Pakistan places a duty on the State to provide free and compulsory education to children aged five to sixteen. Yet many adults find official documents hard to read and judge. Analyze the relationship between literacy, democratic citizenship, and social justice. Detail four practical strategies middle-school teachers can use to teach critical civic literacy using authentic public documents. *(Analyze)*

4. Drawing upon Ashraf, Turner, and Laar (2021), evaluate the gap between official language policies and actual classroom communication in Pakistani schools. Weigh the arguments for and against starting English-medium instruction in the early grades. Then judge which approach would better serve early readers: an additive Mother-Tongue-Based Multilingual Education (MTB-MLE) model with planned translanguaging, or another approach you can defend. Justify your judgement with evidence and state its limits. *(Evaluate)*

5. You are invited by a district education officer to design an integrated 4-week literacy unit for Grade 6 in a multilingual, low-income community school. The community speaks a regional mother tongue at home, learns Urdu as the national language, and aspires to English literacy. Synthesizing the concepts of literacy as social practice, critical civic literacy, and translanguaging, design a comprehensive unit plan that includes: (a) learning outcomes, (b) authentic community and civic text selections, (c) scaffolded multilingual instructional activities, and (d) fair, asset-based assessment criteria. *(Create)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### MCQ answer key

1. A - The autonomous model defines literacy as an independent, neutral technical skill of decoding and encoding detached from social context.
2. B - A literacy event is an observable real-life occasion where written text is central to human interaction.
3. B - The ideological model views literacy as multiple, situated practices shaped by social power relations and cultural institutions.
4. A - Vernacular community literacies (such as trade ledgers) serve vital daily economic functions alongside formal dominant literacies.
5. B - Devaluing a child's home language attacks their self-concept, communicating that their cultural identity is inferior and unwelcome in school.
6. A - Dominant literacies are codified, regulated, and credentialed by formal state institutions like examination boards and courts.
7. B - Civic literacy involves reading, understanding, and critically evaluating public policies, rights, and democratic processes.
8. B - Interrogating what is present and what is missing in a public budget flyer is a practical exercise in critical civic literacy.
9. B - Ashraf et al. (2021) describe English as the dominant language, Urdu as the national language and lingua franca, and local languages as the language of the speaker's identity. Option A contradicts their finding of a conflict between policy and practice.
10. C - Moving on purpose between Urdu and the pupils' home language to build understanding, then back into Urdu writing, is planned translanguaging. Options A and D forbid the home language, and B concerns decoding only.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. **Model answer:** Literacy as a social practice views reading and writing not as isolated, mechanical decoding skills inside an individual's head, but as meaningful human activities situated within specific cultural and institutional contexts. It emphasizes that literacy always serves particular social purposes, reflects community values, and is embedded in human relationships.
   - **Marks (3):** 1 mark for moving beyond individual mechanical decoding; 1 mark for identifying literacy as culturally situated; 1 mark for connecting it to human relationships and social purposes.

2. **Model answer:** First, on definition: the autonomous model treats literacy as a neutral, universal technical skill, whereas the ideological model views it as multiple, culturally situated practices. Second, on context: the autonomous model claims literacy produces identical cognitive effects everywhere, while the ideological model recognizes that literacy is shaped by social institutions and power structures. Third, on pedagogy: the autonomous model tends to teach and test decoding skills on their own, whereas the ideological model also connects reading to learners' lived experiences and critical inquiry (decoding still matters in both).
   - **Marks (3):** 1 mark for each clear, valid contrasting dimension (definition, context/power, pedagogy).

3. **Model answer:** A literacy event is an observable occasion where written text plays a role in interaction (e.g. pupils reading aloud a biology chart during a science lesson, or a villager reading an electricity bill at the post office). A literacy practice consists of the invisible cultural values, attitudes, and power relations that give meaning to that event (e.g. the shared belief that scientific knowledge holds authoritative truth, or the cultural norm of seeking help from an elder to interpret state papers).
   - **Marks (3):** 1 mark for defining literacy event with an authentic example; 1 mark for defining literacy practice with an authentic example; 1 mark for clearly distinguishing observable action from underlying cultural meaning.

4. **Model answer:** When a school labels a pupil's home language as corrupted or uneducated, the child may feel that the school rejects their family and community, because language is closely tied to identity. Many pupils in this position become anxious about speaking and stop taking part in discussion. Taking part less means fewer chances to practise reading and talking about texts, which can slow literacy progress. Effects differ between children.
   - **Marks (3):** 1 mark for linking home language to personal/cultural identity; 1 mark for a plausible effect on feelings (for example anxiety, silence, alienation), stated as possible rather than certain; 1 mark for connecting this to reduced participation and fewer chances to practise reading.

5. **Model answer:** Dominant literacies are: (1) codified and regulated by official state authorities (e.g. textbook boards, examination councils, courts); (2) associated with standard language varieties (standard Urdu or formal English); and (3) used as institutional gatekeepers for academic credentials and formal employment. In contrast, vernacular literacies arise organically from everyday community, trade, and domestic life.
   - **Marks (3):** 1 mark for each accurate distinguishing feature of dominant literacies (institutional codification, standard language variety, credential/gatekeeping function).

6. **Model answer:** The four dimensions in this unit's teaching framework are: (1) Access and comprehension (reading and understanding laws, rights, notices, and voter procedures); (2) Critical evaluation (detecting bias, propaganda, and unverified claims in civic discourse); (3) Civic voice and expression (writing petitions, letters, and articulating community needs); and (4) Democratic collective action (participating in School Management Committees, local councils, and community problem solving).
   - **Marks (4):** 1 mark for each correctly identified and described dimension of civic literacy.

7. **Model answer:** Decoding alone is insufficient because: (1) Administrative texts use specialized bureaucratic register, archaic legal vocabulary, and institutional acronyms that cannot be understood merely by sounding out letters; and (2) Civic access requires critical comprehension - knowing what entitlement is being offered, what documents are legally required, how to challenge an unfair rejection, and who is accountable.
   - **Marks (3):** 1.5 marks for explaining the barrier of bureaucratic vocabulary/register; 1.5 marks for explaining the need for critical civic comprehension and actionable knowledge.

8. **Model answer:** (1) English - the dominant language, used with Urdu in bureaucracy and holding high status in education, law, government, science and higher education, so it works as a gatekeeper for good jobs; (2) Urdu - the national language and lingua franca between ethnic groups, widely used in public schooling; and (3) local languages such as Sindhi, Pashto, Punjabi and Balochi - the languages of home and community that mark the speaker's identity, with a limited place in formal schooling except where, as in Sindh, Sindhi-medium schools exist.
   - **Marks (3):** 1 mark for each language accurately named with its social and institutional role.

9. **Model answer:** Subtractive multilingualism occurs when the school adds a dominant language (such as English or Urdu) by pushing out the child's mother tongue. A child taught to read in a language they do not yet understand may learn to sound out words without grasping their meaning. Additive multilingualism develops reading and understanding in the child's first language while adding Urdu and English step by step, so that the child can link print to meaning from the start. Its benefits depend on good planning, materials and teaching.
   - **Marks (3):** 1.5 marks for subtractive multilingualism and a likely effect on early reading; 1.5 marks for additive multilingualism and its intended benefit, stated with an appropriate qualification. Half marks are awarded for a correct definition with no effect described.

10. **Model answer:** Two pedagogical benefits are: (1) Understanding the concept - explaining photosynthesis in Pashto lets pupils grasp the process (sunlight, leaves, food-making) in the language they understand best, so they are less likely to memorise words without meaning; and (2) Confidence and a bridge to Urdu - using the home language can make pupils less worried about asking questions, and the planned move to an Urdu summary helps them learn the Urdu terms for ideas they already understand.
    - **Marks (3):** 1.5 marks for explaining conceptual understanding beyond rote learning; 1.5 marks for explaining pupils' confidence and the planned bridge into Urdu writing. Half marks are awarded for a benefit named but not explained.

### ERQ rubrics

Each ERQ is marked out of 10. For every criterion, award the band whose description best fits the
answer; a band's top mark goes to an answer that fully meets it, its lower mark to one that mostly does.

1. **ERQ 1 Analytic Rubric (Autonomous vs Ideological Models):**
   - *Critique of the autonomous model (0-3 marks)*: Explains what a decoding-only test of prescribed textbooks misses.
     - 3: names at least two things the test misses (for example, purposes of reading, community texts such as ledgers or notices, who judges reading) and explains why each matters.
     - 2: names two limitations with thin explanation, or one well explained.
     - 1: says the approach is "narrow" with no specific limitation.
     - 0: no relevant critique.
   - *Analysis of the ideological model (0-4 marks - **Analyze**)*: Uses Papen (2023) accurately to analyse how literacy is tied to social context and how schooling can devalue some practices.
     - 4: uses an accurate point from Papen (2023), contrasts the two models on at least two dimensions, and recognises that decoding still matters.
     - 3: accurate contrast on two dimensions with general reference to the source.
     - 2: describes the ideological model without a clear contrast, or with a source claim Papen does not make.
     - 1: names the model only.
     - 0: no relevant analysis.
   - *Curricular integration strategies (0-3 marks)*: Proposes three concrete ways to bring community literacy into the reading curriculum.
     - 3: three distinct, practical strategies, each linked to a named community text and a reading goal.
     - 2: three strategies with one vague or repeated, or two strong strategies.
     - 1: one strategy, or general suggestions with no classroom step.
     - 0: no relevant strategy.

2. **ERQ 2 Analytic Rubric (Language, Identity, and Power):**
   - *Sociolinguistic power analysis (0-3 marks)*: Explains how schooling gives some languages more status than others.
     - 3: explains the different status of English, Urdu and regional languages with an accurate Pakistani example and notes regional variation such as Sindhi-medium schooling.
     - 2: explains the status difference with a general example.
     - 1: states that some languages are "higher" with no explanation.
     - 0: no relevant analysis.
   - *Evaluation of learner identity (0-4 marks - **Evaluate**)*: Judges the possible effects of marginalising home languages on identity, self-worth and equity.
     - 4: weighs at least two possible effects with reasons, uses cautious language ("can", "may"), considers which pupils are most affected, and reaches a clear judgement.
     - 3: a clear judgement on two effects, with some reasons but little qualification.
     - 2: lists effects without a judgement, or states them as certain for every child.
     - 1: a one-line opinion.
     - 0: no relevant evaluation.
   - *Asset-based framework (0-3 marks)*: Proposes teaching practices that value home languages and build the school language.
     - 3: at least three practical practices organised into a framework, each with a clear link to Urdu or English literacy.
     - 2: practical practices with no clear organisation, or with a weak link to the school language.
     - 1: general advice such as "respect all languages".
     - 0: no relevant framework.

3. **ERQ 3 Analytic Rubric (Civic Literacy and Democratic Citizenship):**
   - *Link between literacy and citizenship (0-3 marks)*: Analyses how literacy helps citizens use rights, keeping the text of Article 25-A separate from interpretation.
     - 3: states accurately what Article 25-A requires, separates this from the argument about civic purpose, and notes that citizenship does not depend on literacy.
     - 2: makes the link clearly but blurs the Article's text with interpretation.
     - 1: asserts that literacy matters for democracy with no reasoning.
     - 0: no relevant link.
   - *Analysis of public access barriers (0-3 marks - **Analyze**)*: Analyses how official language, misinformation and weak critical reading make public texts hard to use.
     - 3: analyses at least two barriers with an example of each and explains who is most affected.
     - 2: names two barriers with brief examples.
     - 1: names one barrier.
     - 0: no relevant analysis.
   - *Classroom civic strategies (0-4 marks)*: Describes four practical strategies using authentic public documents.
     - 4: four distinct strategies, each naming a document type (notice, budget summary, form, petition), a pupil task, and a way to check facts or sources.
     - 3: four strategies with one vague, or three well developed.
     - 2: two developed strategies.
     - 1: one strategy, or a list with no tasks.
     - 0: no relevant strategy.

4. **ERQ 4 Analytic Rubric (Multilingual Policy and Translanguaging):**
   - *Analysis of the policy-practice gap (0-3 marks)*: Uses Ashraf et al. (2021) accurately to analyse the gap between official policy and classroom language use.
     - 3: gives at least two accurate points from Ashraf et al. (2021) (for example, teachers in public schools using local languages or Urdu, the 2009 English policy without a plan) and explains the gap they show.
     - 2: one accurate point clearly explained.
     - 1: describes the gap with no source or with a claim the study does not make.
     - 0: no relevant analysis.
   - *Evaluation of early English-medium instruction (0-3 marks - **Evaluate**)*: Weighs arguments for and against starting English-medium teaching in the early grades.
     - 3: weighs at least one argument for (such as access to English) and one against (such as pupils not understanding lessons), using evidence, and reaches a reasoned judgement.
     - 2: presents arguments on one side only, with reasons.
     - 1: asserts a view with no reasons.
     - 0: no relevant evaluation.
   - *Justified recommendation (0-4 marks)*: Recommends MTB-MLE with planned translanguaging or another defensible approach, and states the limits of the evidence.
     - 4: a clear recommendation with staged steps, reasons tied to how children learn to read, conditions needed (teachers, materials), and an honest statement of what the evidence does and does not show. Any well-justified approach can earn full marks.
     - 3: a clear, reasoned recommendation that omits the limits or the conditions.
     - 2: a recommendation with general reasons only.
     - 1: a recommendation with no reasons.
     - 0: no relevant recommendation.

5. **ERQ 5 Analytic Rubric (Integrative Unit Design):**
   - *Learning outcomes and coherence (0-2 marks)*: Writes clear, checkable outcomes that join social practice, civic awareness and multilingual literacy.
     - 2: three or more measurable outcomes covering all three strands.
     - 1: outcomes that are vague or cover only one or two strands.
     - 0: no outcomes.
   - *Authentic text selection (0-2 marks)*: Chooses community, civic and school texts that suit the learners.
     - 2: names specific texts of each type and says why each suits Grade 6 pupils in this community.
     - 1: names texts of only one or two types, or with no reason.
     - 0: no texts.
   - *Multilingual instructional sequence (0-3 marks - **Create**)*: Designs a four-week sequence using the home language, Urdu and English.
     - 3: a week-by-week sequence in which home-language talk leads into Urdu and English reading and writing, with each step building on the last.
     - 2: a sensible set of activities with an unclear sequence or a weak bridge between languages.
     - 1: a list of activities with no plan for language use.
     - 0: no sequence.
   - *Assessment and equity (0-3 marks)*: Sets fair assessment criteria that reward understanding rather than penalising developing language.
     - 3: at least two formative and one summative check, with criteria that separate understanding from fluency in the school language.
     - 2: sensible checks with criteria that are only partly fair to emerging language users.
     - 1: mentions assessment with no criteria.
     - 0: no assessment.
