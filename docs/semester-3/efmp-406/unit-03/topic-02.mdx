# Topic 3.2 - Literacy for citizens in a multilingual country

## A real classroom situation

In a government middle school on the outskirts of Hyderabad, trainee teacher Saima observes a Grade 8
class during an election week. The local municipal council has distributed an official informational
brochure in the neighbourhood regarding local council development projects, public water supply rights,
and voter registration procedures. Saima brings several copies of the brochure into the classroom.

When she asks the pupils to read the brochure, she observes an immediate divide. The text is written
in dense, formal bureaucratic Urdu, accompanied by legal citations and official English acronyms
(such as "TMA", "WASA", and "ADLG"). Most pupils can mechanically decode the words aloud, but when
Saima asks what the document actually promises and how a local resident can file a complaint regarding
unclean drinking water, the classroom falls silent. A pupil named Faraz whispers in Sindhi to his desk
partner: "These words sound like court papers. My father received a similar notice about our shop, but
we had to pay a petition writer outside the deputy commissioner's office twenty rupees just to explain
what it meant."

Saima realizes that being able to sound out letters is not the same as being able to exercise citizenship.
To participate in community governance, citizens need the critical competence to analyze public documents,
verify claims, formulate collective demands, and navigate multiple languages.

## Explanation

### Literacy for citizenship and democratic participation

In a constitutional democracy, literacy is fundamentally tied to civic rights and popular sovereignty.
Article 25-A of the 1973 Constitution of Pakistan declares that the State shall provide free and
compulsory education to all children aged five to sixteen. The ultimate purpose of this constitutional
guarantee is not merely to train compliant workers, but to develop informed, autonomous citizens who
can take an active part in democratic self-governance.

**Civic literacy** refers to the knowledge, skills, and critical dispositions that enable individuals to
understand public institutions, evaluate civic information, engage in political discourse, and exercise
their democratic rights and duties. It encompasses four vital dimensions:

1. **Access and comprehension**: The ability to locate, read, and comprehend civic, administrative, and
   legal documents - including municipal notices, utility bills, public budget summaries, voter registration
   procedures, public health guidelines, and constitutional rights frameworks.
2. **Critical evaluation**: The capacity to question the credibility of public statements, detect
   political propaganda, identify commercial or partisan bias, and distinguish verified evidence from
   unsubstantiated claims in print and social media.
3. **Civic voice and expression**: The ability to articulate personal and community concerns through
   written petitions, formal letters to authorities, participation in town hall debates, and constructive
   public dialogue.
4. **Democratic collective action**: Using communication and collaborative inquiry to participate in local
   decision-making bodies - such as School Management Committees (SMCs), union councils, community welfare
   societies, and trade associations.

When literacy instruction is reduced to passive copying and memorization of textbook answers, schools
cultivate what educational philosopher Paulo Freire termed a "culture of silence". Learners acquire the
mechanical ability to read without developing the critical confidence to question authority or evaluate
the fairness of public policies. In contrast, when classrooms engage pupils in analyzing real community
texts, literacy becomes an empowering instrument of democratic citizenship.

Consider a practical classroom worked example:

**Worked example: Critical analysis of a civic public notice**
- *The text*: A public health flyer posted outside a basic health unit in a rural taluka states:
  "Clean drinking water is available at all filtration plants. Citizens must report private water theft
  immediately. Violators will face legal penalties under section 144."
- *Step 1: Examine the explicit message*. What does the text say on the surface? It asserts that clean
  water is available and warns against theft under threat of punishment.
- *Step 2: Interrogate the missing information and assumptions*. Who authored this text? (The local
  administration). What does it assume? (It assumes all filtration plants are functioning, and places
  the primary burden of water shortages on citizen theft). What is omitted? (It fails to state whether
  the municipal pumps have electricity, how frequently filters are changed, or what citizens should do
  if the plant is locked).
- *Step 3: Transform passive reading into civic agency*. The teacher guides the Grade 8 class to draft
  a polite, evidence-based inquiry letter to the Union Council Secretary asking for the maintenance
  log of their village filtration plant. Through this move, reading leads directly to constructive civic
  participation.

**Naming and correcting the misconception: "Citizenship has nothing to do with reading."**
Many people assume that citizenship is merely a legal status acquired at birth or recorded on a National
Identity Card (CNIC), requiring only occasional voting every five years. This view misunderstands modern
democracy. Democratic life operates through written laws, budgetary allocations, published policies,
legal rights, and digital public forums. An illiterate citizen, or a citizen possessing only mechanical
decoding skills, is forced to rely on intermediaries - lawyers, petition writers, or political patrons -
to exercise basic entitlements. Developing critical civic literacy in schools is therefore an essential
prerequisite for genuine democratic empowerment and social equity.

### Multilingualism and literacy development

Pakistan is one of the most linguistically diverse federations in South Asia, with over seventy living
languages. However, this natural linguistic wealth has often been treated as an administrative challenge
rather than an educational resource.

In their comprehensive empirical study of language practices in Pakistani education, Ashraf, Turner,
and Laar (2021) document a persistent **three-tier linguistic hierarchy**:

- **The elite tier (English)**: English functions as the language of constitutional jurisprudence,
  higher civil service examinations, corporate business, elite private schooling, and advanced science
  and technology. It acts as the primary socio-economic gatekeeper for power and upward mobility.
- **The national tier (Urdu)**: Urdu serves as the national language, the symbol of inter-provincial
  cohesion, the dominant language of national broadcast media, and the lingua franca across diverse ethnic
  groups. It possesses extensive print culture and institutional support.
- **The regional and community tier (Mother tongues)**: Regional mother tongues - including Sindhi,
  Punjabi, Pashto, Balochi, Saraiki, Hindko, Brahui, Shina, Balti, and others - represent the lived
  mother tongues and primary cultural heritage of the overwhelming majority of Pakistan's citizens.
  Except in Sindh, where Sindhi has enjoyed long-standing status as an official medium of school instruction,
  regional languages have often been excluded from formal school curricula.

This linguistic hierarchy produces what researchers describe as a **policy-practice contradiction**
(Ashraf et al., 2021). National education policies have repeatedly oscillated between imposing sudden
English-medium instruction or uniform Urdu-medium mandates. When primary schools enforce sudden English
immersion or prohibit the child's mother tongue, they create **subtractive multilingualism**: the child's
first language is suppressed and devalued in the attempt to replace it with the school language.

The cognitive consequences of subtractive language policies are severe. When young children are forced
to learn to read and write in a language they neither speak nor understand, they cannot connect printed
symbols to known meanings. Reading degenerates into rote phonological reproduction without comprehension.
Children experience intense linguistic anxiety, drop-out rates increase, and foundational literacy is
crippled.

In contrast, international research and local linguistic evidence strongly support **additive
multilingualism** and **Mother-Tongue-Based Multilingual Education (MTB-MLE)**. In an additive framework,
learning to read in one's mother tongue provides the cognitive architecture, phonological awareness,
and conceptual clarity that makes acquiring subsequent languages (Urdu and English) substantially faster
and more successful. Skills learned in the first language - such as predicting a story, identifying cause
and effect, and evaluating an argument - transfer naturally across languages.

### Translanguaging as an asset-based classroom strategy

In daily classroom life, teachers and pupils do not keep their languages in rigid, sealed boxes.
Ashraf et al. (2021) observed that even in schools designated as strict "English-medium", teachers
routinely switch between regional languages, Urdu, and English to explain difficult concepts, discipline
pupils, and build rapport.

This natural communicative practice is known as **translanguaging**. Translanguaging is the pedagogical
practice where teachers and learners deploy their full linguistic repertoire flexibly to make sense of
academic content. Far from being a sign of linguistic corruption or poor discipline, translanguaging
is a purposeful, research-grounded teaching tool.

In an effective multilingual Pakistani classroom:
- The teacher introduces a new scientific or historical concept using a textbook term in Urdu or English.
- The teacher invites pupils to discuss the concept in pairs using their home language (e.g. Sindhi,
  Pashto, or Saraiki).
- Pupils formulate their understanding, ask clarifying questions, and debate ideas in the language where
  their thinking is richest.
- The teacher then supports the pupils to summarize their findings and write their final answers in the
  required academic language (Urdu or English).

**Naming and correcting the misconception: "Mixing languages (translanguaging) in class is bad and shows poor teaching."**
Traditional teacher training often taught that teachers should strictly enforce monolingual immersion:
"If it is an English class, speak only English; if it is Urdu, never allow Sindhi or Pashto." This
dogmatic view has been thoroughly refuted by modern linguistics. Rigid monolingualism silences pupils
who are still building vocabulary in the target language. Translanguaging acknowledges the reality of the
child's mind: bilingual and multilingual speakers have one integrated linguistic system, not two separate
brains. When teachers strategically use translanguaging, they lower affective filters, eliminate fear,
and dramatically improve pupils' conceptual comprehension and academic literacy.

## Activity: Analyzing a civic text across languages

**Mode:** Collaborative small groups (3 to 4 participants).
**Time allocation:** Approximately 25 minutes.

1. **Review a sample civic document.** Examine the following excerpt from a government school admission
   notice displayed outside an urban primary school:
   *"All parents seeking admission under the Right to Free and Compulsory Education Act must furnish
   an attested B-form, computerized CNIC copies of the guardian, proof of municipal residence, and an
   income declaration duly signed by an officer of BPS-17 or above. Non-compliance within fourteen days
   shall result in forfeiture of eligibility."*
2. **Identify linguistic barriers.** List four specific words or phrases that an ordinary working-class
   parent (who may be literate in a regional language or possess basic functional literacy) would find
   confusing, intimidating, or inaccessible.
3. **Draft a plain-language civic rewrite.** Rewrite the admission notice in two versions:
   - Version A: Plain, accessible Urdu using straightforward everyday terms.
   - Version B: An equivalent message in your local regional language (e.g. Sindhi, Pashto, Punjabi, or
     Balochi).
4. **Develop a translanguaging role-play.** Prepare a 2-minute dialogue between a trainee teacher and a
   parent who visits the school office feeling anxious about the documents. Demonstrate how the teacher
   uses the parent's home language to explain the requirements warmly and accurately, while pointing to
   the official Urdu form so the parent learns to recognize the administrative headings.
5. **Class reflection.** Share your dialogues. Discuss: how does treating the parent's language with
   dignity strengthen the school's relationship with the community?

## Summary

Literacy is the gateway to democratic citizenship and active participation in society. In a constitutional
democracy like Pakistan, citizens require civic literacy to access public services, understand legal
rights such as Article 25-A, and evaluate public information critically. Pakistan's multilingual reality
demands an additive approach to education that honors children's mother tongues while developing competence
in the national language (Urdu) and official registers (English). Rather than suppressing home languages,
effective teachers use translanguaging as a powerful pedagogical scaffold that bridges community knowledge
with academic literacy.

## Try this at your practicum school

Investigate the linguistic landscape and civic visibility of your practicum school:
1. Walk around the school building and grounds. Document all visible written text on display: school rules,
   mottoes, signboards, library labels, notice boards, and student work displays.
2. Record what languages are used:
   - What percentage of signs are in English?
   - What percentage are in Urdu?
   - Are any signs or notices written in the regional mother tongue spoken by the majority of pupils?
3. Interview two Grade 7 or 8 pupils using a real school notice or government announcement. Ask them:
   - What does this notice ask you to do?
   - Are there words you find difficult to understand?
   - What language would make this notice clearest to you and your parents?
4. Write a brief reflection in your journal: what changes in the school's print environment would promote
   inclusive civic literacy and multilingual pride?

## Summative task

Read the scenario below and write an analytical essay of approximately 400 to 550 words.

**Scenario**:
The school education department in an administrative district has issued a directive declaring that all
government primary schools must switch immediately to "100% English-medium instruction from Grade 1
onwards" to eliminate the educational divide with elite private schools. Teachers are instructed to speak
only English during all classes, textbooks have been replaced with English editions, and children are
forbidden from using their mother tongue or Urdu in school. After four months, Grade 2 and 3 teachers report
that reading scores have collapsed: pupils can recite memorized alphabet rhymes phonetically, but cannot
read simple sentences with understanding, and classroom interaction has virtually ceased.

**Task**:
Provide a detailed critical evaluation of this policy and propose an evidence-based alternative:
1. Draw upon Ashraf, Turner, and Laar (2021) to analyze why sudden English immersion produces subtractive
   multilingualism and reading comprehension failure in early grades.
2. Explain how this policy contradicts the principles of equitable literacy development and civic inclusion.
3. Formulate an alternative, research-supported language and literacy model (such as Mother-Tongue-Based
   Multilingual Education with planned translanguaging) that enables children to build solid foundations
   in their home language while progressively mastering Urdu and English.

**Mini-rubric**:
- *Linguistic analysis (0-3 marks)*: Accurately explains subtractive versus additive multilingualism,
  language hierarchies, and the relationship between first-language concept formation and second-language
  reading acquisition.
- *Evaluation of equity and policy (0-4 marks - **Analyze**)*: Critically analyzes the policy-practice
  contradiction and explains how sudden immersion alienates working-class and linguistic-minority children.
- *Evidence-based proposal (0-3 marks)*: Formulates a realistic, coherent alternative model incorporating
  scaffolded translanguaging, bilingual resources, and staged language development.
