# Topic 3.2 - Literacy for citizens in a multilingual country

## A real classroom situation

In a government middle school on the outskirts of Hyderabad, trainee teacher Saima observes a Grade 8
class during an election week. The local municipal council has distributed an official informational
brochure in the neighbourhood regarding local council development projects, public water supply rights,
and voter registration procedures. Saima brings several copies of the brochure into the classroom.

When she asks the pupils to read the brochure, she observes an immediate divide. The text is written
in dense, formal bureaucratic Urdu, accompanied by legal citations and official English acronyms
(such as "TMA", "WASA", and "ADLG"). Most pupils can mechanically decode the words aloud, but when
Saima asks what the document actually promises and how a local resident can file a complaint regarding
unclean drinking water, the classroom falls silent. A pupil named Faraz whispers in Sindhi to his desk
partner: "These words sound like court papers. My father received a similar notice about our shop, but
we had to pay a petition writer outside the deputy commissioner's office twenty rupees just to explain
what it meant."

Saima realizes that being able to sound out letters is not the same as being able to exercise citizenship.
To participate in community governance, citizens need the critical competence to analyze public documents,
verify claims, formulate collective demands, and navigate multiple languages.

## Explanation

### Literacy for citizenship and democratic participation

Article 25-A of the Constitution of Pakistan states: "The State shall provide free and compulsory
education to all children of the age of five to sixteen years in such manner as may be determined by
law." The Article sets out a duty of the State. It does not state the purpose of that education.

The rest of this section gives the textbook's own interpretation, which you can discuss and question.
Many educators argue that one important purpose of schooling in a democracy is to prepare citizens who
can understand public decisions and take part in them. On this view, literacy helps people use rights
they already have.

**Civic literacy** means the knowledge and skills that help a person understand public institutions,
judge public information, and take part in public life. This textbook groups it into four working
dimensions. They are a teaching framework for planning lessons, not a fixed or official standard:

1. **Access and comprehension**: The ability to locate, read, and comprehend civic, administrative, and
   legal documents - including municipal notices, utility bills, public budget summaries, voter registration
   procedures, public health guidelines, and constitutional rights frameworks.
2. **Critical evaluation**: The capacity to question the credibility of public statements, detect
   political propaganda, identify commercial or partisan bias, and distinguish verified evidence from
   unsubstantiated claims in print and social media.
3. **Civic voice and expression**: The ability to articulate personal and community concerns through
   written petitions, formal letters to authorities, participation in town hall debates, and constructive
   public dialogue.
4. **Democratic collective action**: Using communication and collaborative inquiry to participate in local
   decision-making bodies - such as School Management Committees (SMCs), union councils, community welfare
   societies, and trade associations.

When reading lessons are mostly copying and memorising textbook answers, pupils may learn to read
the words without learning to ask questions about what they read. When pupils examine real community
texts and ask who wrote them, what they claim and what is missing, reading can become a tool they use
in public life.

Consider a practical classroom worked example:

**Worked example: Critical analysis of a civic public notice**

*The flyer below is invented for teaching. It is not a real notice and does not describe any law.*
- *The text*: A public health flyer posted outside a basic health unit in a rural taluka states:
  "Clean drinking water is available at all filtration plants. Citizens must report private water theft
  immediately. Violators will face legal penalties."
- *Step 1: Examine the explicit message*. What does the text say on the surface? It asserts that clean
  water is available and warns against theft under threat of punishment. It names no law, so a careful
  reader would ask which rule the penalty comes from and who issued the flyer.
- *Step 2: Interrogate the missing information and assumptions*. Who authored this text? (The local
  administration). What does it assume? (It assumes all filtration plants are functioning, and places
  the primary burden of water shortages on citizen theft). What is omitted? (It fails to state whether
  the municipal pumps have electricity, how frequently filters are changed, or what citizens should do
  if the plant is locked).
- *Step 3: Transform passive reading into civic agency*. The teacher guides the Grade 8 class to draft
  a polite, evidence-based inquiry letter to the Union Council Secretary asking for the maintenance
  log of their village filtration plant. Through this move, reading leads directly to constructive civic
  participation.

**Naming and correcting the misconception: "Citizenship has nothing to do with reading."**
Some people think of citizenship only as a legal status recorded on a National Identity Card (CNIC)
and used when voting. Citizenship does not depend on literacy: a citizen who cannot read has the same
rights as one who can. But much of public life now runs through written laws, budgets, forms, notices
and online portals. A person who cannot read these texts, or can only sound out the words, often has
to depend on others, such as petition writers or agents, to use their rights. Faraz's father in the
opening scene is an example. Teaching critical civic literacy in school is one way to reduce that
dependence.

### Multilingualism and literacy development

Ashraf, Turner, and Laar (2021), citing a standard language catalogue, describe Pakistan as a country
with speakers of 77 languages. Their study interviewed 24 school teachers from Punjab, Sindh, Khyber
Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan, two university teachers and three policymakers in 2017. They describe
schooling in which "English is the dominant language, Urdu the national language, and local languages
most accurately signify the speaker's identity". This textbook summarises that pattern as three layers
(the "three layers" label is the textbook's, not the authors'):

- **The elite tier (English)**: English functions as the language of the Constitution and higher
  courts, higher civil service examinations, corporate business, elite private schooling, and advanced
  science and technology. It acts as the primary socio-economic gatekeeper for power and upward
  mobility.
- **The national tier (Urdu)**: Urdu serves as the national language, the symbol of inter-provincial
  cohesion, the dominant language of national broadcast media, and the lingua franca across diverse ethnic
  groups. It possesses extensive print culture and institutional support.
- **The regional and community tier (Mother tongues)**: Regional mother tongues - including Sindhi,
  Punjabi, Pashto, Balochi, Saraiki, Hindko, Brahui, Shina, Balti, and others - represent the lived
  mother tongues and cultural heritage of most of Pakistan's citizens.
  Sindh is an important exception: Ashraf et al. (2021) note that Sindhi-medium schools exist alongside
  Urdu-medium schools. Elsewhere, regional languages have often had little place in the formal curriculum.

The subtitle of Ashraf et al. (2021) names a **conflict between policy and practice**. Policy has
promoted Urdu as the national language, and the National Education Policy (2009), as the authors report,
aimed at "implementing English language policy in the shortest possible time". Yet the authors found no
plan issued to carry this out, and they report that in most offices staff speak Urdu or a local
language while "official documents are released in English".

Language-education writers use two terms for what can happen to a child's home language at school.
In **subtractive multilingualism**, the school language is added by pushing the home language out:
the child is discouraged or punished for using it. In **additive multilingualism**, the child keeps
and develops the home language while adding Urdu and English.

What happens when children are taught in a language they do not yet understand? Teachers in the
Ashraf et al. (2021) study said it was difficult to teach primary pupils science and mathematics in
English "because students could not even recognize the English alphabet". A child in this position
may learn to sound out words without knowing what they mean. That study did not measure reading
scores or dropout, so this unit makes no claim about how large such effects are.

Many language-education specialists support **Mother-Tongue-Based Multilingual Education (MTB-MLE)**:
pupils first learn to read in a language they know, then move step by step into Urdu and English.
The reasoning is that a child who understands what a sentence means can learn how reading works, and
can then use skills such as predicting or finding cause and effect in a second language. How well this
works depends on trained teachers, suitable books and a planned move into the school languages. This
unit does not bind a study that measures these results, so treat MTB-MLE as a reasoned position to
examine and test, not a guaranteed outcome.

### Translanguaging as an asset-based classroom strategy

In daily classroom life, teachers and pupils do not keep their languages in sealed boxes. Ashraf
et al. (2021) report that public schools in their study had no rule about which language to speak,
and that most teachers "often communicate with their colleagues and students in local languages or in
Urdu". The private-school teachers in the study, by contrast, said their schools required pupils and
teachers to speak only Urdu or English. The authors also found that
teaching across sectors commonly explained English material "through national or local languages".

When a teacher plans this kind of language use on purpose, language educators call it
**translanguaging**: teachers and pupils use all the languages they know (their **linguistic
repertoire**) to understand and discuss new content. It is not a sign of poor discipline. Used with a
plan, it can be a deliberate teaching tool.

In an effective multilingual Pakistani classroom:
- The teacher introduces a new scientific or historical concept using a textbook term in Urdu or English.
- The teacher invites pupils to discuss the concept in pairs using their home language (e.g. Sindhi,
  Pashto, or Saraiki).
- Pupils formulate their understanding, ask clarifying questions, and debate ideas in the language where
  their thinking is richest.
- The teacher then supports the pupils to summarize their findings and write their final answers in the
  required academic language (Urdu or English).

**Naming and correcting the misconception: "Mixing languages (translanguaging) in class is bad and shows poor teaching."**
Some teacher training has taught a strict one-language rule: "If it is an English class, speak only
English; if it is Urdu, never allow Sindhi or Pashto." Many language educators now question this rule.
A strict rule can silence pupils who do not yet have the words they need in the school language. A
pupil who knows two or more languages draws on all of them when thinking. Planned translanguaging can
lower pupils' worry about speaking and help them understand new ideas, provided the teacher also plans
how pupils will move into the school language. It does not remove every difficulty, and its effect
depends on how well it is planned.

## Activity: Analyzing a civic text across languages

**Mode:** Collaborative small groups (3 to 4 participants).
**Time allocation:** Approximately 25 minutes.

1. **Review a sample civic document.** Examine the notice below. *It is a fictional notice written for
   this activity. It does not come from any real school or department, and it is not a statement of
   the law or of real admission rules.*
   *"All parents seeking admission under the Right to Free and Compulsory Education Act must furnish
   an attested B-form, computerized CNIC copies of the guardian, proof of municipal residence, and an
   income declaration duly signed by an officer of BPS-17 or above. Non-compliance within fourteen days
   shall result in forfeiture of eligibility."*
2. **Plan how to check the rules.** Before trusting a notice like this, a parent or teacher would need
   to check who issued it, which law or official admission policy it relies on, and whether each
   document is really required. List three questions you would ask and where you would check (for
   example, the school office, the district education office or the provincial education department's
   official website). Do not treat the fictional notice as a guide to real requirements.
3. **Identify linguistic barriers.** List four specific words or phrases that an ordinary working-class
   parent (who may be literate in a regional language or possess basic functional literacy) would find
   confusing, intimidating, or inaccessible.
4. **Draft a plain-language civic rewrite.** Rewrite the admission notice in two versions:
   - Version A: Plain, accessible Urdu using straightforward everyday terms.
   - Version B: An equivalent message in your local regional language (e.g. Sindhi, Pashto, Punjabi, or
     Balochi).
5. **Develop a translanguaging role-play.** Prepare a 2-minute dialogue between a trainee teacher and a
   parent who visits the school office feeling anxious about the documents. Demonstrate how the teacher
   uses the parent's home language to explain the notice warmly, says which points still need checking,
   and points to
   the official Urdu form so the parent learns to recognize the administrative headings.
6. **Class reflection.** Share your dialogues. Discuss: how does treating the parent's language with
   dignity strengthen the school's relationship with the community?

## Summary

Literacy makes it easier for citizens to use the rights they already hold. Civic literacy helps people
use public services, understand rights such as Article 25-A, and judge public information carefully.
Ashraf et al. (2021) show how English, Urdu and local languages play different roles in Pakistani
schools, and how official language policy and classroom practice often differ. This unit argues for an
additive approach that keeps children's home languages while they learn Urdu and English, and for
planned translanguaging as one way to link home knowledge with school learning. These are positions
to test in your own classroom, not guarantees.

## Try this at your practicum school

Investigate the linguistic landscape and civic visibility of your practicum school:
1. Walk around the school building and grounds. Document all visible written text on display: school rules,
   mottoes, signboards, library labels, notice boards, and student work displays.
2. Record what languages are used:
   - What percentage of signs are in English?
   - What percentage are in Urdu?
   - Are any signs or notices written in the regional mother tongue spoken by the majority of pupils?
3. Interview two Grade 7 or 8 pupils using a real school notice or government announcement. Ask them:
   - What does this notice ask you to do?
   - Are there words you find difficult to understand?
   - What language would make this notice clearest to you and your parents?
4. Write a brief reflection in your journal: what changes in the school's print environment would promote
   inclusive civic literacy and multilingual pride?

## Summative task

Read the scenario below and write an analytical essay of approximately 400 to 550 words.

**Scenario** (invented for this task):
The school education department in an administrative district has issued a directive declaring that all
government primary schools must switch immediately to "100% English-medium instruction from Grade 1
onwards" to eliminate the educational divide with elite private schools. Teachers are instructed to speak
only English during all classes, textbooks have been replaced with English editions, and children are
forbidden from using their mother tongue or Urdu in school. After four months, Grade 2 and 3 teachers report
that reading scores have collapsed: pupils can recite memorized alphabet rhymes phonetically, but cannot
read simple sentences with understanding, and classroom interaction has virtually ceased.

**Task**:
Evaluate this policy and propose an alternative:
1. Using this topic and Ashraf, Turner, and Laar (2021), analyse why this policy may have led to the
   results the teachers report. Say what other explanations are possible and what further evidence
   you would want.
2. Explain how far the policy fits the aims of fair literacy development and civic inclusion.
3. Propose and justify an alternative language and literacy plan (for example, Mother-Tongue-Based
   Multilingual Education with planned translanguaging, or another plan you can defend). State how
   the district could check whether your plan works.

**Mini-rubric** (10 marks):
- *Linguistic analysis (0-3 marks)*: Explains subtractive and additive multilingualism and the link
  between understanding a language and learning to read in it.
  - 3: explains both terms correctly, applies them to the scenario, and links the pupils' recitation
    without understanding to the language barrier.
  - 2: explains both terms but applies them to the scenario only briefly.
  - 1: explains one term, or uses the terms loosely.
  - 0: no relevant explanation.
- *Evaluation of equity and policy (0-4 marks - **Analyze**)*: Analyses the gap between policy and
  practice and who gains or loses from the policy, using Ashraf et al. (2021) accurately.
  - 4: uses an accurate point from Ashraf et al. (2021), identifies which pupils are most affected,
    weighs at least one other explanation, and reaches a reasoned judgement.
  - 3: makes a clear judgement with accurate evidence but does not weigh another explanation.
  - 2: describes problems with the policy with little evidence or with an inaccurate claim about the
    source.
  - 1: states an opinion with no reasons.
  - 0: no relevant evaluation.
- *Evidence-based proposal (0-3 marks)*: Proposes a realistic alternative with staged language
  development and a way to check its results.
  - 3: a coherent plan with stages, named classroom supports (such as bilingual word lists or planned
    pair talk) and a clear way to check whether pupils understand what they read.
  - 2: a sensible plan that lacks either the stages or the way to check results.
  - 1: a general recommendation with no plan.
  - 0: no relevant proposal.
