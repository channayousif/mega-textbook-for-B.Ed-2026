# Topic 6.1 - Literacy challenges and fair access in Pakistan

## A real classroom situation

In this teaching case, a trainee visits a government primary school near Thatta. Sana has returned after several weeks away and can tell a detailed story in Sindhi, but cannot yet read the Urdu task sheet. Bilal attends every day but cannot see the small print clearly. A colleague says that both children simply need to try harder. The trainee must decide what to change before the next reading lesson, and what support to request from the school.

## Explanation

### Literacy challenges in developing contexts

A literacy challenge is a condition that makes it difficult to gain, use or continue reading and writing for meaningful purposes. The phrase *developing contexts* draws attention to limits in resources and public services. It does not mean that children or families have less ability. It also does not mean that every school in a country faces the same conditions. A teacher begins with a specific learning situation, then asks which conditions are affecting it.

Ahmad's (2011) account of Pakistan's Education for All effort links limited progress to several kinds of obstacle, rather than to a single shortage. It is a historical account, not a description of every school today. Its useful lesson for this unit is to avoid one-cause explanations. An attendance problem, a teaching problem and a language problem may appear together. Each needs evidence and an appropriate response.

**Begin with what a measure means.** A *literacy rate* is the percentage of a defined population counted as literate under a stated definition. The age group, place, date and method matter. A figure based on adults cannot answer a question about current Grade 3 reading. A household response about literacy is different from observing someone read a short text and explain it. Before repeating a rate, ask what the source actually measured.

Rehman, Jingdong and Hussain's historical provincial comparison (2016) shows why national summaries need to be examined alongside differences within a country. We use it for that comparison principle, not for current provincial rankings or rates. A province is also too large a unit to describe a single school. A teacher needs local attendance and reading evidence as well as wider reports.

Consider an invented example. Two areas each count 60 of 100 people as literate. In the first, the counted population is adults aged 15 and above. In the second, it is children aged 10 to 14. Both yield 60%, but they answer different questions. You cannot conclude that the areas offer equally effective primary teaching. Even with the same age group, you would need the same definition, year and method before making a fair comparison.

A literacy rate also leaves many learning questions unanswered. Can people understand a medicine label? Can they compare two messages about a school closure? Can they write a request that gives a reason? These are different tasks. Contemporary literacy includes making and judging meaning in social, digital, media and visual settings. A single percentage is useful for a broad question, but cannot replace a lesson-level assessment of those practices.

Shakir, Lone and Zafar (2012), in their published abstract, question treating completion of primary school as sufficient evidence of literacy. Their study tested primary-only completers in selected rural and urban areas. This does not establish a national estimate. It does support a practical caution: **time spent in school and demonstrated reading ability are different kinds of evidence**. A register or completion certificate cannot show all that a child understands.

**Trace a barrier to a learning opportunity.** Poverty may affect the cost of materials, travel or time available for study. In an invented Hyderabad case, a boy uses the family's only phone after his parent returns from work. A homework task that closes online at six o'clock can exclude him even though he lives close to school. Calling him careless hides the actual barrier. A paper alternative and access during school hours would address the task more directly than a warning about discipline.

Distance can reduce safe and reliable access. A school may be physically near enough for one child and difficult to reach for another because the route, transport or mobility needs differ. A trainee should not prescribe a route or organise travel alone. The classroom action may be to provide a catch-up text and keep the learner in a group. The school action may be to discuss safe access with the family and responsible staff. A road or staffing problem needs action beyond a single teacher.

Gender barriers concern unequal opportunities connected with expectations about girls and boys. They are not evidence that one sex learns reading less well. In a teaching case, a girl misses a reading club because its timing conflicts with care responsibilities. A boy is discouraged from joining because friends call story reading unsuitable for boys. Both cases require attention, but their immediate barriers differ. Listen to the learners and families before selecting a response. Avoid turning one example into a claim about a whole community.

Language of instruction can hide what a learner knows. A child may understand a story when discussing it in Sindhi or another familiar language but struggle with unfamiliar written Urdu or English. The teacher must identify the task being assessed. If it is story reasoning, allow a familiar-language discussion to reveal the reasoning, then build the vocabulary needed for the written text. If it is reading a particular language, teach and assess that skill directly. Oral knowledge alone is not proof of reading, but difficulty in a new language is not proof of weak thinking.

A shortage of teachers or teaching time changes the opportunities learners receive. In a multi-grade room, one group may spend long periods copying while the teacher supports another. More worksheets will not necessarily solve the problem. A planned rotation can give one group a familiar independent reading task while the teacher works with a small group on decoding and meaning. Staffing support may still be necessary. The rotation is a bounded response to lost reading practice, not a substitute for adequate staffing.

Other disruptions can interrupt routines, including displacement, damage to buildings or illness. In an invented flood-affected community, pupils return with different gaps in attendance and different access to books. A single demand to complete every missed page may add to the difficulty. A short check of present reading needs gives a better starting point. The teacher can select essential texts, provide repeated opportunities and report the wider resource problem through the school. This example describes a possible response, not a measured claim about a particular disaster.

**Worked example: from blame to a testable explanation.** Return to Sana. The observation is narrow: she tells a story clearly in Sindhi and has difficulty reading a new Urdu sheet. We do not yet know whether the difficulty involves recognising words, understanding vocabulary, missing earlier lessons, or several things together. The phrase "she is weak" gives no useful teaching direction. The first move is to replace the label with a question that an ordinary lesson can answer.

The trainee chooses a short familiar story in an appropriate reading form. Sana first discusses its meaning, then reads a few lines and explains them. The trainee notes where help is needed. If Sana explains a sentence after one unfamiliar word is discussed, vocabulary support may be useful. If she cannot recognise familiar written words, she needs reading instruction as well. The teacher should not infer a diagnosis from one task. The aim is to choose a starting support and check it again.

Now trace the response: observed difficulty → possible barrier → teaching change → evidence. The possible barrier is unfamiliar written language after missed lessons. The change is a short catch-up group, with familiar-language talk followed by supported reading in the target language. The evidence is Sana's attempt to read and explain a comparable new passage later. Completing a copied page is not the same evidence. The new passage matters because recalling the practised story could hide the difficulty.

**Low literacy is not only a rural problem.** This misconception confuses one location with a cause. An urban learner can face crowded classes, interrupted schooling, language barriers or insecure access to materials. A rural learner can receive strong support from a teacher and family. Place helps identify questions to investigate; it does not give a complete answer. Compare actual opportunities and performance before deciding that a village or city label explains a result.

### Equity, access, and inclusion in literacy education

*Access* means being able to reach and use a learning opportunity. <Glossary term="Equity">Equity</Glossary> means fairness in the support and opportunities learners receive. *Inclusion* means working to remove barriers to being present, taking part and achieving. These distinctions follow the inclusion and equity framing in UNESCO (2017). They help a teacher ask more precise questions than "Did everyone receive the same sheet?"

Giving everyone the same printed page is an equal distribution of paper. It may still be unfair if some children cannot see its print or understand its instructions. A larger copy, a clearer layout or vocabulary teaching can help learners work towards the same worthwhile goal. Equity does not mean assuming that some children can never reach the goal. It means identifying the support they need and checking whether that support works.

*Out-of-school children* are school-age children who are not enrolled in school; an official count must state the ages and education levels it covers. This differs from a learner who is enrolled but absent on one day. It also differs from being present without learning. All three situations matter, but they require different evidence. A class register describes enrolled learners. By itself it cannot show every child in the neighbourhood who has never enrolled or has left school.

UNESCO's 2017 guide encourages examining barriers across the education system. A teacher can use three linked questions to apply that approach. Is the learner present and able to access the task? Does the learner have an active role in the work? What can the learner now read, explain or produce? These questions prevent a head count from being treated as proof of inclusion. They also prevent a lively group activity from being mistaken for demonstrated learning.

**Worked example: attendance is only the first question.** Bilal is in the classroom and has a textbook. That gives some evidence of access, but he leans close to the page and contributes little in the group. The teacher asks discreetly which format is easier to use, offers a clearer larger text and makes sure the light falls on the page. The trainee reports the observation to the cooperating teacher, who can follow school procedures for further support. The trainee does not announce a medical label.

Next the teacher gives Bilal a meaningful role: select the sentence that supports the group's conclusion and explain why. Another learner records the group's answer. Bilal must still engage with the text rather than only distribute paper. Finally, the teacher checks whether he can find and explain evidence in a comparable passage. If the adult merely reads the whole answer to him, the check no longer shows his reading. Format support and evidence of reading need to be planned together.

This corrects a second misconception: **inclusion does not mean placing every child in the same room and doing nothing different**. A learner can be seated in a group but excluded from the thinking. The teacher may need to change the materials, discussion roles or instruction. The learning goal remains visible. Good support makes the learner's own contribution possible; it does not make the learner disappear behind a partner who performs every step.

**Look for overlapping barriers.** Sana may face a language barrier and a gap in schooling. A child with a disability may also live far from school or have limited money for materials. These are possible combinations to investigate, not fixed identities. A plan based only on "girls" or "rural children" can miss the individual barrier. A plan based only on individuals can also miss a recurring school rule that excludes many learners. Keep both levels in view.

Compare three imagined responses to a reading task. Response A repeats the same instructions more loudly. Response B removes the learner from all reading work and asks them to decorate the display. Response C teaches key words, offers readable text and lets the learner practise finding a reason with a partner before answering alone. C is the stronger starting response when language and format are the observed barriers. A does not change those barriers. B gives participation in the room but removes the intended learning opportunity.

Support must match the goal. If the goal is to evaluate a message's claim, hearing a teacher read the message may allow a learner to show reasoning. If the goal is independent reading of that message, hearing the whole message changes what the task measures. A teacher can use both activities at different times, while recording the distinction. This avoids reporting reasoning support as proof that the learner has independently mastered reading.

**Use a small evidence plan.** For an imagined class of 30, suppose 24 attend a lesson, 18 speak during group work and 12 independently find a reason in the text. These counts describe three different things. They do not prove why the others were absent, quiet or unsuccessful. The teacher can check a few possible explanations through ordinary lesson observations and a short follow-up task. The numbers are a starting point for questions, not labels to attach to children.

The same caution applies when reporting improvement. Suppose the next task shows 16 learners finding a reason. Four more succeeded, but perhaps the second text was much easier. The teacher should use comparable tasks and record what support was offered. A brief note such as "shorter sentences and vocabulary rehearsal used" helps interpret the result. Evidence is more useful when the reader knows the conditions in which it was produced.

Fairness also involves dignity. Do not display a public list of "weak readers" or ask children to disclose household income during a class debate. Use invented cases in teacher education. If discussing a real class with the cooperating teacher, keep notes anonymous and focused on the teaching problem. A child should not have to accept humiliation in order to receive help. Group support can be organised around a current task rather than a permanent ability label.

**Match responsibility to the barrier.** A teacher can choose accessible materials, organise reading time and adjust feedback. A school can review club timing, shared resources and follow-up procedures. Responsible education authorities may need to address staffing, buildings or transport. Families and community members can contribute knowledge about language, schedules and useful reading purposes. None of these roles removes the public responsibility to provide education. A request for cooperation should not become a demand that families privately fund a missing service.

Here is a complete response in a fictional Karachi school. Homework requires downloading a video and posting an English summary. Some learners share phones; one understands the content but needs vocabulary support. The teacher identifies the learning goal as explaining the message and supporting a judgement with evidence. She supplies a printed transcript at school, discusses key words, and allows planning in a familiar language before the written summary. All learners then use evidence from the same message. She records who can justify a judgement and checks reading separately where needed.

The improvement is specific: the plan reduces dependence on home devices while keeping the literacy goal. It does not prove that every home-access barrier has disappeared. If a learner still struggles, the next question concerns the remaining barrier, not whether the child deserves support. This is how equity becomes a cycle of noticing, changing and checking. It is also how an apparently technical choice, such as requiring a phone, becomes a question about fair participation.

## Activity: Build and challenge a barrier-response plan

**Work in groups of four for about 25 minutes.** Use this invented case: a peri-urban Sindh school plans a phone-based evening reading club. A learner shares a phone with a working parent, another is new to the instructional language, and a third can attend but cannot use the small text. No one has yet checked what any learner can read independently.

- Give each member a role: learner advocate, teacher planner, evidence checker or critical friend. Begin with two minutes of individual recall of the differences between access, equity and inclusion, with the explanation closed.
- Make a three-column paper plan: observed barrier, matched response and evidence to check. Include a classroom response and one issue that needs school-level action. Do not invent a family attitude to explain a difficulty.
- Agree on one literacy goal. Explain which support changes access to the task and which performance will demonstrate the learner's own reading or reasoning.
- Swap plans with another group. Their critical friend must identify one unsupported assumption and one learner who might still be excluded. Revise the plan together and prepare a two-minute explanation of the revision.

Keep the revised plan for your reflection journal. State what changed because another group challenged your first explanation. A plan with a clear remaining question is stronger than a plan that claims to have solved every barrier.

## Check your understanding

1. Define literacy rate and name two details you need before comparing two reported rates. *(Remember)*
2. Explain the difference between access and inclusion, using a learner who attends but cannot take part in a reading task. *(Understand)*
3. An urban learner cannot submit phone-based homework because the device is shared. Suggest one change that preserves the reading goal and explain what evidence you would collect. *(Apply)*
4. A learner understands a story orally but cannot read its written form. Describe a short check that separates reading difficulty from knowledge of the story. *(Apply)*

Answer from memory first. Then reopen the explanation and revise any response that treated a hypothesis as a fact. Check that your example names what the learner will do, rather than only what the teacher will provide.

## Summary

Literacy barriers can occur together, and neither a place label nor a single rate explains a learner's opportunities. Access, participation and demonstrated learning need separate evidence. Equity means matching support to a barrier while preserving a worthwhile goal, and inclusion requires more than a shared seat in a classroom.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can explain why literacy measures need a definition, age group, date and method.
- [ ] I can trace an observed barrier to a response without blaming a learner or family.
- [ ] I can distinguish being present, taking part and demonstrating literacy learning.
- [ ] I can justify a support choice and name evidence that would test whether it helped.

## Try this at your practicum school

During an ordinary lesson, with your cooperating teacher's agreement, observe one reading task. Note the goal, the format and who gets an active reading or reasoning role. Do not record names or collect family information. Discuss one possible access barrier with the teacher and propose a small change. In a paragraph, distinguish what you observed from what you still need to find out. If a classroom observation is unavailable, analyse a sample task sheet with the same questions.

## Summative task

Write 350–450 words analysing a proposed response to low literacy in your own district, or a fictional district if you have no verified local proposal. A district team proposes sending the same online reading sheet to every household, then counting submissions as proof of improvement. Explain two barriers this plan may miss, propose one feasible improvement, and justify the evidence you would use to check access, participation and learning. Do not supply invented district rates. *(Evaluate)*

**Mini-rubric (10 marks):**

| Criterion | Strong evidence | Partial evidence | No evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Barrier analysis, 0–3 *(Analyze)* | Explains two plausible barriers and how each affects the task, 3 | Names barriers but explains only one link, 1–2 | Labels learners without analysing the task, 0 |
| Improvement, 0–3 *(Evaluate)* | Justifies a feasible change that preserves the literacy goal, 3 | Offers a relevant change with limited justification, 1–2 | No relevant change, 0 |
| Evidence plan, 0–4 | Separates access, participation and learning, with a comparable follow-up task and one limit, 4 | Provides some useful evidence but confuses a dimension or misses a limit, 1–3 | Counts submissions alone as mastery, 0 |
