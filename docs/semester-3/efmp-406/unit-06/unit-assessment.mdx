# Unit 6 assessment and review

## Unit summary

Literacy challenges arise through linked conditions, such as cost, language, materials, distance and the availability of teaching. These conditions do not define a learner's ability. A teacher should replace a broad label with an observable question: what can this learner read, explain or compose, and what is making the task difficult? Wider rates help frame a problem, but they need an age group, definition, date and method. They cannot replace direct evidence of a particular learner's literacy.

Access, equity and inclusion help distinguish different parts of a response. Access concerns reaching and using a learning opportunity. Equity concerns fair support. Inclusion requires attention to presence, participation and achievement. A learner may be enrolled without regularly attending, present without an active role, or active without yet showing the intended learning. Each situation calls for a different question and appropriate evidence. Giving everyone an identical resource does not establish that everyone can use it.

Teachers contribute through classroom instruction, school coordination, home knowledge and community purposes for reading and writing. They should make bounded improvements and identify needs that require wider action. A small teaching change can matter without proving a national effect. Reports should describe the task, support and evidence, and acknowledge limits. Families' knowledge can enrich learning without making a home device, paid resource or adult's formal literacy a condition of success.

Future directions should be judged by the same principles. A timeline of EFA, the SDGs and Pakistan's policy framework records commitments, not automatic achievements. A digital tool needs a learning purpose, fair access and evidence of the learner's own performance. Texts made with images, audio or AI still require interpretation and verification. Reading and writing remain central to making and judging meaning as formats change.

## Summative assessment

This bank is for public self-study. It is separate from a protected LMS quiz bank. Try each band before consulting the guidance at the end. The unit uses the 40% formative and 60% summative default; the course guide's separate course-wide assessment weights are unchanged. A teacher choosing formal assessment should state the selected tasks, marks and time in advance.

All school scenes, counts and proposals below are invented. They should not be quoted as district evidence. For MCQs, choose one best option and note a reason. For RRQs, stay within the stated bound. For ERQs, organise an argument or plan, use the scenario's evidence and acknowledge what you cannot conclude. No internet or device is needed.

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. What does a literacy rate describe? *(Remember)*
   - A) The number of books distributed to a school.
   - B) The percentage of a defined population counted as literate.
   - C) The proportion of lessons taught through a phone.
   - D) The average number of years teachers have worked.
2. Two reports give literacy rates for different age groups. What is the best first step before comparing them? *(Understand)*
   - A) Rank the places using the larger percentage.
   - B) Treat the younger group as the more accurate one.
   - C) Combine the percentages into a national estimate.
   - D) Check each definition, population, year and method.
3. A learner attends regularly but cannot use the small-print passage. Which response best applies equity while preserving the reading goal? *(Apply)*
   - A) Provide readable print and check reading on a comparable passage.
   - B) Give the learner the same page and count attendance as success.
   - C) Ask a partner to complete the whole response for the learner.
   - D) Move the learner to decoration work instead of reading.
4. A city school requires online evening homework, but some learners share a parent's phone. Which explanation best fits the observed difficulty? *(Apply)*
   - A) Urban residence removes material barriers to learning.
   - B) Missing a submission demonstrates weak reading ability.
   - C) Required timing and device access may limit participation.
   - D) A phone-based task gives each household equal opportunity.
5. Which evidence most directly shows a learner achieving a reading goal? *(Understand)*
   - A) A name appears in the class register.
   - B) The learner reads a new passage and explains its meaning.
   - C) The learner receives the same textbook as classmates.
   - D) A group displays a neatly copied reading chart.
6. Two teachers agree on reading rotations and share anonymous work samples. Which teacher role is most directly shown? *(Apply)*
   - A) Collecting private household messages for public display.
   - B) Replacing district staffing decisions with volunteer work.
   - C) Coordinating school-level literacy support and evidence.
   - D) Measuring the national literacy rate after a lesson.
7. What kind of statement is a policy target to improve literacy by a stated year? *(Understand)*
   - A) A commitment whose achievement needs evidence.
   - B) A completed outcome established by its publication.
   - C) A reading assessment of every learner in a school.
   - D) A guarantee that the intended changes will occur.
8. A proposed app gives scores for tapping an option. The goal is to justify a judgement about a message. What extra evidence best matches that goal? *(Apply)*
   - A) A total of app installations on household phones.
   - B) A count of screens viewed during the lesson.
   - C) A list of colours and sounds learners prefer.
   - D) An independent explanation using evidence from a new message.
9. A teacher wants family knowledge to support literacy without requiring an adult to read English. Which task best fits? *(Apply)*
   - A) Require every parent to complete an English reading test.
   - B) Invite an oral account of a public text, with a classroom alternative.
   - C) Require every family to purchase the same online story package.
   - D) Grade learners according to how many forms parents return.
10. Why does a polished AI-generated story still need literacy judgement? *(Understand)*
   - A) Its attractive layout establishes that its claims are reliable.
   - B) Its fluent language makes comparison with evidence unnecessary.
   - C) Its claims and assumptions still need interpretation and checking.
   - D) Its automatic production demonstrates the learner's composing skill.

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

1. In three sentences, explain why a national literacy rate cannot tell a teacher everything needed to plan a Grade 4 reading lesson. Name two missing details. *(Understand)*
2. In three or four sentences, distinguish an out-of-school child from an enrolled learner who is absent today, and explain why a class register cannot count both groups fully. *(Understand)*
3. A school gives every learner the same worksheet. One cannot see the print clearly; another needs support with its language. In four sentences, analyse why equal distribution is insufficient and propose one matched support for each learner. *(Analyze)*
4. A trainee says that a rural address explains weak reading. In three sentences, replace this conclusion with two questions about learning opportunities or task performance. *(Analyze)*
5. A learner can retell a story orally but struggles with its written form. In four sentences, propose a short check that distinguishes knowledge of meaning from independent reading. State one limit of the check. *(Apply)*
6. In four sentences, describe one classroom action and one school action that promote contemporary literacy. Explain how the two actions connect. *(Apply)*
7. In three sentences, explain why the EFA 2015 horizon, the SDG 2030 horizon and Pakistan's 2018 framework should not be labelled achieved literacy outcomes. *(Understand)*
8. A teacher says an app has improved reasoning because 25 pupils clicked through all its pages. In four sentences, analyse the weakness in this evidence and propose a better task. *(Analyze)*
9. In three or four sentences, propose a respectful home or community link for a school reading task. Include an alternative for a learner unable to use the home route. *(Apply)*
10. In four sentences, respond to the claim that future learners will need no reading because audio, images and AI will do everything. Give one literacy task that remains necessary and explain why. *(Analyze)*

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. **A district reading proposal.** A fictional district team proposes sending the same online worksheet to every household. It will count returned sheets as evidence of improved literacy. Write 450–550 words evaluating this proposal. Analyse two possible barriers in a Pakistani setting, distinguish access, participation and learning, and justify one feasible improvement. State which local evidence you would need before expanding the plan. Do not invent district rates. *(Evaluate)* (Topic 6.1)
2. **Inclusion beyond a shared room.** In a fictional class, a learner who has difficulty seeing the print is seated with a fluent reader. The partner reads everything and writes the response. The teacher records both learners as having mastered independent reading. In 400–500 words, analyse the claim, propose support that preserves the learner's own role, and design a comparable follow-up task. Explain when a read-aloud could appropriately support a different goal. *(Analyze)* (Topic 6.1)
3. **A teacher's bounded improvement plan.** Write a 450–550-word two-week plan for helping pupils judge the reliability of short public messages. Include classroom teaching, school coordination and a home or community connection with an alternative route. Identify one need beyond a class teacher's authority and how to refer it. Explain the evidence you would collect and what claims that evidence would not justify. *(Create)* (Topic 6.2)
4. **A future-facing resource decision.** A supplier offers AI-generated illustrated stories and automatic scores. The school has one shared device and uneven connectivity. In 450–550 words, evaluate whether to adopt, adapt, defer or reject the proposal for a specified literacy goal. Discuss a possible benefit, two access or learning risks, a viable offline route, and evidence of independent performance. Explain why reading and writing remain necessary. *(Evaluate)* (Topic 6.2)
5. **From commitment to classroom evidence.** A school presentation states: "EFA promised literacy by 2015, the SDGs promise it by 2030, so distributing tablets will achieve inclusion." In 500–600 words, evaluate the reasoning and create a better response for a fictional Sindh school. Connect a historical or policy commitment to a concrete literacy goal, a barrier-response plan, teacher responsibilities and evidence. Include a low-resource route, a review point and a limit on the claims you will make. *(Evaluate / Create)* (integrative)

## Answers and marking guidance

Use this guidance after attempting the questions. The model responses illustrate defensible reasoning; alternative responses can receive full credit when they answer the task with relevant evidence. Do not award credit merely for repeating a technical word. Check whether the explanation connects the concept to the scenario. These are intentionally public self-study materials, not protected assessment keys.

### MCQ answer key

1. **B.** A literacy rate is a percentage for a defined population and a stated literacy definition; books, devices and teacher experience measure other things.
2. **D.** Definition, age group, year and method determine what the percentages can fairly be compared to; combining unmatched rates would hide their differences.
3. **A.** Readable material addresses the observed format barrier, while a comparable reading task retains evidence of the learner's own performance.
4. **C.** Device availability and timing are plausible access barriers in the described city household; the missing submission alone does not establish reading ability.
5. **B.** Reading and explaining an unfamiliar but comparable passage provides direct evidence for the reading goal; a register or shared product cannot show each learner's understanding.
6. **C.** Shared routines and evidence are school coordination; they can improve support without replacing staffing responsibilities or establishing a national effect.
7. **A.** A target records an intended outcome and horizon. Separate evidence is needed to establish progress or achievement.
8. **D.** An independent explanation using evidence from a new message tests the stated reasoning goal more directly than clicks, views or preferences.
9. **B.** An oral account invites family knowledge without assuming English literacy; the classroom alternative keeps the opportunity open to every learner.
10. **C.** Fluency and polish do not establish accuracy or appropriateness. The learner and teacher still need to interpret claims and check support.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

Award **4 marks per item**. Accept clear equivalent wording. The four points are scored separately, so a partially sound response can earn partial credit. Examples may differ from the models if they preserve the distinction or teaching goal.

1. A national rate summarises a defined population under a particular literacy measure. It does not show which Grade 4 learners can read a chosen text or what help they need. The teacher needs a short check of word reading and meaning, and information about the language or format learners can use. **Marks:** 1 for the aggregate nature of the rate; 1 for recognising the missing class-level performance evidence; 1 each for two useful planning details, such as reading difficulty, language, format or support. Merely saying "more data" does not earn the detail marks.
2. An out-of-school child is school-age but not enrolled, under the age and level definition used. An enrolled learner absent today remains on the register. The register can show that learner's absence but may omit children who never enrolled or left school. **Marks:** 1 for non-enrolment; 1 for distinguishing temporary absence; 1 for identifying who the register includes; 1 for explaining the omitted population. Do not require a numerical age range because none was supplied in the task.
3. Identical distribution gives everyone paper but does not establish that everyone can use it. Offer a clear larger-print version to the learner who cannot see the print. Teach key vocabulary and provide supported language practice for the learner facing unfamiliar written language. Check each learner's reading on a comparable task rather than accepting receipt of a sheet as mastery. **Marks:** 1 for distinguishing equal distribution from usable access; 1 for each matched support; 1 for retaining the learning goal or performance check. Do not award the last mark for removing both learners from reading work.
4. A rural address describes location, not the complete cause of a reading difficulty. Ask how much supported reading time the learner receives and what the learner can read and explain in a familiar language and format. The answers can guide a response without assuming lower ability or family indifference. **Marks:** 1 for rejecting place as a sufficient explanation; 1 each for two specific diagnostic questions; 1 for explaining how those questions guide teaching. Accept questions about attendance, materials, travel or teaching when connected to an observed difficulty.
5. First discuss the story in a language the learner knows to check meaning without requiring reading. Then ask the learner to read and explain a short comparable written passage, noting help with words or vocabulary. Compare the performances to choose support. One brief check cannot establish a diagnosis or show mastery across all texts. **Marks:** 1 for an oral meaning check; 1 for a separate direct reading check; 1 for using the difference to plan support; 1 for a sensible limit. A familiar story read from memory alone is insufficient reading evidence.
6. In class, the teacher models how to identify a claim and check its support, then learners practise and answer independently. At school, teachers share a short reading check and ensure each group receives support time. The shared routine helps the classroom work continue and makes remaining needs visible. The school can use anonymous samples to review progress without publicly ranking pupils. **Marks:** 1 for a concrete classroom action; 1 for a concrete school action; 1 for a clear connection; 1 for a relevant learning purpose or evidence check. General statements such as "teachers should work hard" earn no action mark.
7. The two horizons state when intended outcomes were to be reached. The 2018 framework records policy priorities, not a test of every learner. Progress and achievement require separate evidence about participation and literacy performance. **Marks:** 1 for treating 2015 as a horizon; 1 for treating 2030 as a horizon; 1 for identifying the framework as a policy text; 1 for requiring outcome evidence. A date alone cannot demonstrate success or failure for a particular school.
8. Click completion shows that pupils moved through pages, but does not establish that they reasoned about the text. A teacher should ask each pupil to judge a new message and identify textual evidence for the judgement. Record the support offered and whether pupils can explain their own choices. A similar follow-up task can check whether the reasoning transfers. **Marks:** 1 for identifying the weak measure; 1 for connecting that weakness to the reasoning goal; 1 for a matched independent task; 1 for comparable conditions, transfer or support information. Simply requesting more clicks earns no task mark.
9. Invite learners to discuss a public notice with a family member and bring back an account of its audience and purpose. Do not require a purchase, home device or private message. Provide a teacher-prepared notice and partner discussion at school for anyone unable to use the home route. Check learning through each pupil's explanation of the text. **Marks:** 1 for a meaningful link; 1 for avoiding an unreasonable home requirement; 1 for a viable alternative; 1 for connecting both routes to the same literacy goal. Accept a school-approved community connection with equivalent safeguards and purpose.
10. Audio, images and automatically produced text still carry messages that need interpretation. A learner may need to check the source of a claim and compare it with evidence before forwarding it. Reading and composing help learners make and explain such judgements across formats. Automatic production does not demonstrate the pupil's own understanding. **Marks:** 1 for challenging the replacement claim; 1 for a concrete continuing literacy task; 1 for explaining why it matters; 1 for distinguishing tool output from learner performance. A general dislike of technology is not an analysis.

### ERQ rubrics

Each response carries **20 marks**, with four criteria worth **0–5** each. Apply the performance levels separately to each criterion: **5** means accurate, specific and justified with scenario evidence; **3–4** means mostly sound with a limited gap; **1–2** means a relevant point is present but asserted or weakly connected; **0** means absent or contrary to the task. Grammar matters only where it prevents meaning. Word ranges guide scope; do not reward repetition or penalise a concise response that meets the criteria.

1. **District proposal.** Score (a) analysis of two barriers and how they affect the online task, (b) distinction among access, participation and learning with appropriate measures, (c) evaluation and feasibility of the proposed improvement, and (d) an evidence-and-review plan with limits. For (a) and (c), full marks require causal reasoning or justified judgement, not a list. A strong response might replace home-only distribution with school reading opportunities and paper access, while retaining the goal. It would use independent work on comparable texts, investigate who remains excluded and avoid claiming a district literacy change from returned sheets. Accept different improvements if they address the stated barriers and name who can implement them.
2. **Shared-room inclusion.** Score (a) analysis of why the partner's work does not establish the learner's independent reading, (b) matched format or instructional support that preserves the learner's own role, (c) a comparable follow-up task and interpretation of its evidence, and (d) explanation of when a read-aloud can support a different goal. Full marks for (a) require identifying the mismatch between the claim and the evidence. For (d), a strong response distinguishes reasoning about a heard text from independent reading. It does not reject all read-aloud support. It may suggest clear larger print, appropriate positioning and consultation through the cooperating teacher without inventing a diagnosis.
3. **Two-week plan.** Score (a) a sequenced classroom plan with modelling, guided practice and individual performance, (b) connected school and home or community roles with an alternative route, (c) realistic responsibility and referral for a need beyond teacher authority, and (d) evidence, review and restrained reporting. Full marks for (a) and (d) require explaining how the chosen actions lead to a test of the intended literacy goal. A strong response could move from source checking to comparing two messages, then to a new message. It would record support, not treat a neat portfolio as proof of understanding, and avoid claiming that a class improvement establishes a national trend.
4. **Emerging resource.** Score (a) a clear goal and analysis of a plausible benefit, (b) analysis of two access or learning risks, (c) a justified adoption decision with a workable offline route and continued role for reading and writing, and (d) independent performance evidence with a limit. Full marks for (c) require weighing the benefit against conditions, rather than praising or rejecting technology in general. An acceptable decision may be to trial a checked teacher-controlled sample or to defer purchase. It should not assume the supplied score measures the chosen goal. A strong plan asks learners to interpret, compare or revise a message and explain the evidence for their choices.
5. **Integrative response.** Score (a) correction of the commitment-versus-achievement reasoning using the timeline, (b) a linked barrier, goal and response plan, (c) bounded teacher roles plus a low-resource implementation route, and (d) evidence, review point and a justified limit on claims. Full marks for (a) require explaining why policy horizons do not establish outcomes. Full marks for (b) and (d) require connecting the proposed action to learner performance and a review decision. A strong response might use a school-based message-reading project with accessible print, family knowledge as optional support and a comparable independent task. It would review who participated and learned before deciding whether to expand, rather than counting tablets as achieved inclusion.
