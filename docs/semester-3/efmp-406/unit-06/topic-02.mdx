# The teacher's role and where literacy is heading

## A real classroom situation

In this teaching case, a Hyderabad school receives an offer of an app that produces illustrated stories. One teacher calls it the answer to low literacy; another says teachers cannot change a problem this large. The trainee sees that several pupils can retell a story but struggle to explain the evidence for a message's claim. The school asks the trainee to propose a small literacy improvement that works with or without the app. The decision must connect daily teaching with longer-term literacy needs.

## Explanation

### Role of teachers in promoting contemporary literacy

A teacher promotes literacy by helping learners read, compose and judge meaning, then checking what they can do. Contemporary literacy adds questions about whose voice is heard, which information deserves trust, and how words, images and digital tools shape messages. These questions draw on Units 2–5 and on the course guide's outcomes. They do not remove the need to teach reading and writing. They give reading and writing purposeful work.

**Teachers cannot repair every social barrier alone, but their choices matter.** The misconception "teachers cannot change literacy rates" treats a national measure as if it were the only useful outcome. One teacher cannot credibly claim to raise a district rate after a short activity. The teacher can increase a learner's opportunity to read, teach a missing skill and check improvement. A school can sustain and coordinate that work. These are meaningful contributions even when a national trend takes longer to change.

The Government of Pakistan's *National Education Policy Framework* (2018) identifies school participation, learning quality and skills among its priorities. It is a dated policy framework, not proof that its intentions have been achieved. For teacher planning, the useful distinction is between announcing a priority and implementing it. A poster about literacy is an announcement. A routine that gives learners supported practice, checks progress and responds to difficulty is part of implementation.

**In the classroom: connect a purpose, a skill and evidence.** Begin with a clear literacy goal, such as "learners will identify the evidence for a claim in a short notice". Then choose a text they can work with, teach what the task requires and provide practice. Some learners may need help recognising words. Others may read the words fluently but accept a claim without checking its support. A single task can reveal different needs, but the teacher must notice the difference.

In a fictional Grade 5 lesson in Sukkur, learners read two short announcements about a school book fair. One states a time and place and names the school office as the source. The other is a forwarded message that changes the time but gives no source. The teacher thinks aloud: "I see two different times. I need to identify the source and check before passing the message on." She marks the claim, finds the named source and explains the next check. The model shows the reasoning instead of simply giving a verdict.

Learners then work in pairs on another message. One identifies the claim; the other asks what evidence supports it. They exchange roles before answering individually on a new example. The teacher checks whether each learner can explain a reason. A response such as "the teacher said so" may show recall of the lesson but not independent evaluation. A response that identifies the source, the missing detail and a sensible verification step shows more of the intended literacy practice.

Feedback should name the next move. "Good work" encourages, but does not tell a learner how to improve a weak explanation. "You found the claim; now point to the sentence that supports it" gives a usable direction. If the child cannot read that sentence, the next move may be supported word reading. If the child can read it but cannot connect it to the claim, the next move concerns reasoning. The teacher learns from the response rather than treating every error alike.

**At school: make support dependable.** A single excellent lesson is easier to lose than a shared routine. Teachers can agree on a short reading check, share locally meaningful texts and compare anonymised samples of pupil work. They can also identify who receives no active reading turn in group lessons. The purpose is to improve instruction, not to rank colleagues publicly. Different classes may need different starting points even when they share a broad goal.

In an invented multi-grade school near Mirpurkhas, two teachers notice that the same small group always reads aloud while others copy. They make a weekly rotation so each learner has supported reading time. They keep brief notes on text difficulty and the help offered. After two weeks they compare what learners can do on another passage. If the routine is too demanding for the available staffing, they adjust the rotation and report the remaining need. Collaboration makes the problem visible; it does not make a staffing shortage disappear.

School coordination also includes fair access to shared resources. If the only phone or audio device stays with the most fluent group, it may increase an existing gap. Teachers can plan turns, prepare paper versions and make sure the tool serves the same goal for all groups. A useful resource rule answers "who gets an opportunity to learn?" rather than only "who can finish quickly?" The rule should be easy enough to maintain during an ordinary school week.

**With homes: invite knowledge without assuming literacy or devices.** Families may know the learner's language, interests, reading purposes and daily schedule. Invite that knowledge through a respectful conversation or a simple school communication. Do not make a child's success depend on a parent reading an English instruction sheet. A family member may contribute an oral account, identify a useful local text or listen to a child explain a school task. These contributions do not require formal teaching credentials.

For example, a teacher asks learners to bring the idea of a notice they have seen, not a purchased book or a photographed family document. One describes a bus notice, another a shop sign and another a message shared with relatives. In class, pupils work with teacher-prepared versions and ask who each message addresses. The school provides the reading material needed for the lesson. Family knowledge enriches it without becoming a hidden requirement to buy resources or reveal private information.

A home connection should also have a school alternative. If a learner cannot speak with an adult after school, the teacher can arrange discussion with a classmate or use a prepared case. Avoid judging families as uninterested because a form was not returned. Ask what communication method is workable, and keep the child's learning opportunity open. The teacher's evidence concerns the learner's work, not a guess about the household's values.

**In the community: give literacy a real purpose.** A community connection might involve reading a public notice, comparing information about a local event or composing a request about a school concern. A teacher can coordinate with a library or an approved community resource where available. The aim is meaningful reading and writing. It is not to send children out to collect private stories, to advertise a commercial service, or to make unsupported claims about community need.

In an invented school case, pupils prepare a clear notice for a book-sharing day. They identify the audience, explain the time and place, and revise the wording after classmates identify an ambiguity. The teacher checks that the published version is accurate and follows school procedures. Pupils learn that writing has readers who need to act on it. They also learn that a message needs verification before it is circulated. No internet is necessary for this contemporary literacy task.

**Worked example: a small improvement with four connected roles.** The Hyderabad trainee chooses the goal "explain why a message can or cannot yet be trusted". She uses a short invented school notice and teaches a model of identifying the claim, checking the source and asking what evidence is missing. She provides readable print and familiar-language planning before the required written response. This is the classroom role: instruction plus access to the same thinking goal.

At school, she agrees with the cooperating teacher on one comparable follow-up task and on how shared materials will be available. At home, learners may discuss a public message with a family member, with a classroom alternative for anyone unable to do so. For the community link, pupils produce a school-approved notice for an actual class event. The roles reinforce one another. None asks children to share family messages publicly or to create accounts on an outside service.

The trainee collects anonymous class totals and a few work samples through the teacher's ordinary practice. Suppose 8 of 20 learners initially justify a judgement with textual evidence, and 13 do so later on a comparable message. This is an invented example of class evidence, not proof of a district change. She records which support was used and notes that familiarity, attendance or task difficulty could affect the result. The next decision is whether to continue, change the teaching or investigate a remaining barrier.

This model avoids two extremes. It does not excuse inaction because the national problem is large. It also does not promise that one lesson will eliminate low literacy. The claim remains proportionate to the evidence: more learners in this class demonstrated a specified practice under described conditions. Responsible reporting is itself a form of literacy. It connects a statement to evidence and acknowledges what the evidence cannot show.

### Future trends in literacy education

A *trend* is an observed direction of change; a *prediction* is a claim about what may happen. A *commitment* is a stated intention or target. Confusing these leads to weak planning. A government can commit to a literacy target without having achieved it. A tool can become widely discussed without showing that it improves learning. A teacher should ask which kind of statement is being made and what evidence would support it.

**Read policy history as a sequence of commitments.** Ahmad (2011) describes the renewal of Education for All at Dakar in 2000, with a 2015 target horizon. The <Glossary term="Sustainable Development Goals">Sustainable Development Goals</Glossary> followed in 2015 with a 2030 horizon. SDG 4 includes literacy and numeracy, equitable education and lifelong learning. Pakistan's 2018 framework is a national policy milestone within this wider period. These dates organise a timeline; they do not show a steady rise in every learner's skills.

The United Nations' Goal 4 targets distinguish learning outcomes, equal access and literacy. Target 4.6 concerns literacy and numeracy among youth and adults by 2030. Target 4.5 concerns disparities and access for vulnerable learners. For a teacher, their connection matters: teaching more literacy while leaving some learners without access is an incomplete response. A classroom project can contribute to these aims, but cannot claim that a global target has been reached merely because the project was completed.

<Figure id="fig-U6-4" src="/img/figures/efmp-406/unit-06/fig-U6-4.svg" alt="EFA's 2000 renewal and 2015 horizon precede the SDG period from 2015 to 2030, with Pakistan's 2018 policy framework marked as a commitment rather than evidence of achievement." caption="Original schematic: textbook.com.pk" />

In an invented presentation, a trainee writes "2000: EFA began; 2015: literacy achieved; 2030: all problems end." Each part needs correction. EFA had earlier roots; 2000 marks its Dakar renewal in this timeline. A deadline is not evidence of achievement. A future target is also not a guarantee. A stronger caption says what was promised, when it was intended to be reached, and which evidence would be needed to judge progress.

**Digital and multimodal texts will keep requiring judgement.** A multimodal text combines modes such as words, images and sound. This concept from Unit 2 helps teachers plan for texts pupils may meet on paper or screens. A short video can carry a persuasive claim through an image even when its spoken words sound neutral. A printed poster can do something similar through size, layout and selected details. The teaching question concerns how the message makes meaning, not only which device displays it.

A possible future direction is greater use of such texts in literacy lessons. Treat this as a planning possibility, not a promise that every classroom will receive the same equipment. A teacher can compare a poster and its written claim without connectivity. If audio is available, learners can compare what the voice adds. If it is unavailable, a transcript can support part of the task. The teacher should explain which features the alternative preserves and which it cannot reproduce.

**Artificial intelligence adds a reason to read carefully.** <Glossary term="Artificial Intelligence">Artificial intelligence</Glossary>, or AI, refers here to computer systems that produce outputs such as text or images from patterns learned from data. A generated story can look polished while containing an error, an unsuitable assumption or an unsupported claim. UNESCO's education guidance stresses inclusion, equity and a human-centred approach to AI. The relevant planning principle is that people remain responsible for judging the output and the learning it supports.

This does not require a school to adopt a particular product. In a teacher-made demonstration, present a short invented machine-style answer alongside two reliable classroom sources. Ask learners to locate one claim, trace its support and revise it where necessary. The same exercise can use a photocopy. Children need not create accounts or supply personal details for the lesson to teach verification. The aim is thoughtful reading, not the ability to obtain a fluent response from a tool.

A teacher should also ask which work the learner is doing. If a system writes the paragraph and the pupil copies it, a complete page may hide limited reading or composing. If the learner compares the output with sources, explains a correction and rewrites it, the task can make reasoning visible. In both cases, the teacher must check the content beforehand. New technology changes possible materials; it does not remove responsibility for the accuracy and purpose of the lesson.

**Future literacy will still need reading.** This corrects the misconception that images, voice messages or automatic tools will make reading unnecessary. A learner still needs to interpret instructions, judge evidence and compose a message for an audience. Even an audio or image task involves choices about meaning and trust. For pupils who use accessible formats, the route to a text may differ, while the thinking remains demanding. A future-facing lesson should widen participation in literacy, rather than reduce learners to passive viewers.

Another possible direction is more flexible combinations of school, home and community learning. Flexible provision can help only if learners can use it. A plan that assumes constant connectivity may exclude the learner it was meant to help. Ask whether there are school-based opportunities, accessible materials and an offline route. Then check whether the same central goal is reachable through those routes. Flexibility should concern access and support, not a lower expectation for particular groups.

**Worked example: judge an emerging tool before expanding it.** The app supplier promises illustrated stories and immediate scores. The trainee does not begin by counting attractive features. She specifies the learning problem: pupils need to use evidence when judging a message. She inspects a sample story and the scoring rule. If the score rewards only tapping a choice, she cannot use it alone as evidence that learners can explain their judgement. She needs a short independent response as well.

Next she asks who can use the sample. The app may need a phone, mobile data and a language some learners do not yet read. The school could use one teacher-controlled display or print an approved sample where permitted. Neither route should shift required costs to households. She asks whether an accessible version is available and whether the school can maintain the approach when the trial ends. A free demonstration can still lead to later costs or unsuitable dependence.

She then compares the app-supported lesson with a manageable paper lesson aimed at the same goal. She checks learners' explanations on a new message and notes the support and time each approach required. A simple classroom comparison cannot prove universal superiority. It can answer a local planning question more responsibly than a supplier's slogan: did this approach help these learners practise and demonstrate the intended skill, and who still could not take part?

Her decision could be to use a carefully checked sample, adapt it, defer adoption or reject the proposal. The reason matters more than novelty. If the paper version achieves the goal more fairly under present conditions, choosing it is a reasoned literacy decision. If the app adds a useful accessible feature and the school can support it, a limited trial may be defensible. Keep the decision open to new evidence rather than turning either technology or paper into a fixed belief.

**Use a decision record that can be reviewed.** A short note can state the goal, proposed benefit, access conditions, evidence and next step. For example: "Goal: justify a judgement with textual evidence. Benefit: several short texts can be compared. Condition: school access and checked print copies. Evidence: independent explanation on a new text. Next step: review after two lessons." This record makes the reasoning visible to another teacher. It also prevents a successful-looking display from becoming the only memory of the trial.

The longer-term direction should remain linked to equity and lifelong learning. A teacher cannot know every tool that will exist in ten years. The teacher can help learners form habits that travel: ask who made a message, connect a claim to evidence, compare accounts and explain a revision. These habits apply to a printed notice, a video and an automatically generated answer. Teaching them alongside foundational reading gives learners a stronger basis for adapting to change.

## Activity: Hold a school literacy planning meeting

**Work in groups of four for about 30 minutes.** Use the Hyderabad app proposal from the opening. Give members the roles of class teacher, learner-access advocate, school resource coordinator and evidence checker. Each member first recalls one principle from Topic 6.1 without looking back.

- Agree on one literacy goal and identify the problem the tool is supposed to address. Reject a goal stated only as "use the app".
- Design a two-lesson plan with a school-based offline route. Connect classroom teaching with one school role and one home or community role. State who owns a need the class teacher cannot meet alone.
- Ask the evidence checker to challenge the proposed success measure. Replace downloads, clicks or completed pages with a performance that shows the intended reading or reasoning.
- Conduct a short role-play meeting. Each member must question one assumption, then support or oppose a limited trial with reasons. Record a shared decision and one unresolved question.

Swap your record with another group. They must identify which statement is a commitment, which is a prediction and which is classroom evidence. Revise any sentence that claims more than the record shows. Keep the revised decision in your portfolio.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the four settings in which teachers can promote literacy and give one bounded action in each. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why a policy deadline and an achieved literacy outcome are different kinds of statement. *(Understand)*
3. An app awards a high score for tapping a choice. Propose one extra task that would show whether a learner can justify a judgement with evidence. *(Apply)*
4. A learner says automatic stories mean future pupils will not need reading. Respond with one example that shows why interpreting and checking a text still matters. *(Apply)*

Compare your responses with the worked cases after answering from memory. In the first response, check that no action quietly assumes a parent has a device or that a teacher can solve a staffing problem alone.

## Summary

Teachers contribute through instruction, school coordination and respectful links with homes and communities. Their claims should match the evidence they collect. Future tools and policy commitments need the same scrutiny as present materials: identify the learning goal, test fair access and check what learners can do, while keeping reading and writing central.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can plan a literacy improvement across the teacher's four settings without promising a national effect.
- [ ] I can distinguish a policy commitment, a prediction and evidence of learning.
- [ ] I can judge an emerging tool against a specific goal, access conditions and an independent task.
- [ ] I can explain why changing formats increase the need for thoughtful reading rather than remove it.

## Try this at your practicum school

Discuss one existing reading resource with your cooperating teacher during a normal planning conversation. It may be a textbook page, poster, recorded story or digital resource. Write an anonymous note naming its learning goal, one access condition and a task that would show learning. Suggest a feasible improvement and identify who could act on it. Do not install software, create learner accounts or run a trial without the school's own arrangements. If no discussion is possible, use a sample resource from college.

## Summative task

Write a 350–450-word recommendation about the Hyderabad story-app proposal. State a literacy goal, analyse one likely benefit and two possible barriers, and justify whether to adopt, adapt, defer or reject the proposal. Include an offline route, an independent evidence task and a short explanation of why reading remains necessary. Relate your recommendation to one EFA, SDG or Pakistan policy commitment without treating the commitment as an achieved outcome. *(Evaluate)*

**Mini-rubric (10 marks):**

| Criterion | Strong evidence | Partial evidence | No evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Benefit and barrier analysis, 0–3 *(Analyze)* | Connects a plausible benefit and two barriers to the chosen goal, 3 | Gives relevant features with some missing links, 1–2 | Lists features without a learning goal, 0 |
