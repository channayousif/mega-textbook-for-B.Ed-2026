# Unit 6 teacher notes

## Teaching strategies and sequence

The course guide names discussion and seminars, role-play, case analysis, inclusive-school visits, reflection journals and portfolios. It also names lecture, question-answer and brainstorming. Use these forms to help trainees move from a broad claim about literacy to a response whose learning purpose and evidence are clear. The following sequence adapts those strategies to the approved Unit 6 topics. It does not add a new course requirement or create a separate assessment bank.

Begin with a short brainstorming activity about conditions that affect reading opportunities. Record ideas as hypotheses rather than established local facts. Sort them into barriers that can be addressed through classroom instruction, through school coordination and through wider services. Keep a question column for claims that need evidence. A brief lecture can then establish the distinctions among access, equity and inclusion. Return to these distinctions when groups make their plans.

Teach Topic 6.1 before the resource decision in Topic 6.2. The first topic gives trainees a way to test whether a proposal reaches learners and preserves their own role. The second applies that reasoning to teacher work and emerging tools. Model one full analysis before asking groups to analyse another case. Point out where the model makes a tentative inference, chooses a teaching change and selects evidence. This supports trainees who otherwise jump straight from "the child struggles" to "give more homework".

Use the provided invented cases when discussing sensitive barriers. Do not ask trainees to identify children publicly by disability, household income or language status. A useful case discussion stays close to observable opportunities and work. Where a trainee makes a claim about a real district, ask for a dated, appropriate source or restate the example as hypothetical. No part of this unit requires a new survey of children or families.

## Discussion and likely misconceptions

A productive discussion prompt is: "Two learners receive the same task, but only one can use its format. What must stay the same, and what may need to change?" A likely first response is that changing the resource is unfair. Bring the group back to the learning goal. A clearer or larger text can preserve independent reading while removing an unnecessary obstacle. Let trainees explain the difference between support and doing the task on the learner's behalf.

Another likely response is that one teacher can do little about a district problem. Acknowledge the limit on the teacher's authority, then distinguish scale from responsibility. Classroom practice can improve a specified opportunity and provide evidence. Staffing, safe buildings and other system needs require referral and wider action. Do not imply that commitment or unpaid community effort excuses an unmet public responsibility. A good plan identifies both an immediate action and the need that remains.

When discussing technology, avoid organising the seminar as a competition between enthusiasts and opponents. Ask all groups to use the same literacy goal and decision questions. A resource can be useful for one goal and unsuitable for another. A tool's automatic score may measure task completion rather than reasoning. Ask trainees to inspect what the learner actually does. A low-resource option should preserve the intended thinking wherever possible, rather than merely keep pupils busy.

## Practical work

The guide requires group work, group assignments, an individual assignment and presentations. The two topic activities provide bounded group assignments: a barrier-response plan and a school planning decision record. Keep both in the trainee's portfolio alongside the revision made after peer challenge. A group product should show how members considered competing explanations, rather than just divide headings among members and join unrelated paragraphs.

For the guide's individual work, use the existing topic summative task or a selected unit-bank response. Avoid adding another assessment item merely to fill the portfolio. The individual response helps distinguish each trainee's reasoning from the group's presentation. A reflective journal entry can explain how a peer challenge changed the trainee's assumption, and what further evidence would be needed. This records learning from the discussion without pretending that reflection alone demonstrates classroom impact.

Use short presentations to share decisions and their limits. Ask the presenting group to state its goal, one observed or hypothetical barrier, a matched response and its evidence plan. Listeners should identify an assumption before offering an alternative. The presenter then explains whether the challenge changes the recommendation. This structure puts reasoning at the centre of participation and reduces the reward for simply speaking longest or making the most polished slides. Paper posters or spoken presentations are sufficient.

The guide also names visits to inclusive schools. Where the college already has an approved visit, focus observation on a normal literacy lesson: readable materials, active roles, teacher support and evidence of learning. Follow the host school's arrangements. Do not collect pupil names, photographs, diagnoses or family details for this unit. If a visit is unavailable, use the supplied case and sample materials. A simulated observation can practise analysis, but must not be recorded as an actual visit or a teacher dry-run.

## Connecting the course project

The guide's course-level project is titled "Developing an inclusive lesson plan" and carries 30% of the course assessment. Keep that title and weight. Unit 6 can contribute the barrier analysis, access choices and evidence plan for a literacy lesson within the project. It does not authorise replacing the project or redistributing the other course weights. The guide's remaining course components are participation and reflective journals 10%, mid-term written test 20%, and final written examination plus presentation 40%.

For unit practice, retain the default 40% formative and 60% summative balance. The topic checks are formative opportunities; the teacher selects formal evidence from the existing tasks and bank. Explain the selected tasks and criteria before collection. Do not treat attendance, a completed app screen or a group display as proof of an individual's literacy reasoning. Use the scenario-specific rubric to connect the judgement to what the trainee has shown.

## Materials and follow-up

Prepare readable print copies of short invented notices, the two planning tables and a sample message with a claim that needs checking. A board and paper are sufficient. If demonstrating a digital output, check it beforehand and keep a paper route to the same goal. The lesson should not require learner accounts or a commercial subscription. Four English schematic figures are placed in the topics and can support comparison and discussion. Their Urdu localisation belongs to the separate translation job.

After teaching, review which distinction remained difficult: barrier versus assumption, access versus learning, or commitment versus outcome. Use the relevant worked case again before introducing a new context. Record unresolved source or policy questions for the course owner rather than improvising a new authoritative rule. These notes do not establish expert review, Urdu review or the course's practicing-teacher gate. Those stages require their own evidence and responsible owners.
