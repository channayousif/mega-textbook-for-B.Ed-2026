## A real classroom situation

At a teacher education college in Hyderabad, a practicum group prepares a digital reading activity. One trainee copies a story from a website, adds a pupil's photograph and forwards the file to a large group. Another trainee asks who wrote the story, whether the image should be shared and how families without data will receive the task. The group needs a routine for making those decisions before the next message leaves the classroom.

## Explanation

### Online communication and social media

Online communication is the exchange of messages through connected digital services. A message may be private, sent to a selected group or made public. **Social media** are services through which people create and exchange content and respond to one another. These services can carry stories, comments, pictures, sound and links. The literacy questions remain familiar: who is speaking, to whom, for what purpose and with what evidence? The setting adds questions about reach, storage and further sharing.

The misconception **"social media writing is not real writing"** overlooks the choices a writer makes for readers. A short announcement can require selecting facts, ordering information and anticipating questions. A comment can use evidence or merely repeat a claim. A voice message also needs a clear purpose and sequence, although it does not demonstrate written spelling. Treat each mode accurately. Valuing a spoken contribution does not mean pretending it measures every writing skill.

Greenhow and Robelia (2009) studied social networking among eleven older teenagers from low-income families in the United States. They found examples of social support and creative communication alongside social learning. Their small qualitative study helps teachers notice literacy practices beyond formal essays. It does not establish that public social media improves attainment, that all platforms work alike or that young children should create accounts. Use the insight to examine communication, not to prescribe a service ([study](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1083-6101.2009.01484.x)).

A **qualitative study** examines meanings and experiences through material such as interviews, observations and texts. It can show how people use a service in a particular setting. Its purpose and evidence differ from a numerical comparison of learning outcomes. When a trainee says "this study proves social media raises examination marks," ask whether marks were measured and whether the research design supports that claim. A thoughtful use of research respects the question it actually investigated.

For classroom practice, separate three decisions. First, what communication should pupils learn? Second, what audience can provide a useful response? Third, what setting can provide that audience safely and fairly? The answer may be a paper noticeboard, a teacher-managed exchange or a draft addressed to a fictional reader. Teaching online communication does not require children to join a public platform. A simulated conversation can let them rehearse evidence and tone before using any connected service.

#### Purpose and audience shape a message

Suppose a school reading club wants pupils to return borrowed books. A reminder to known club members can refer to the club, but a message for all families must explain which pupils it concerns. A message that says "Bring them back soon" leaves both the object and the time unclear. A clearer draft names the books, return place and date. These are decisions about shared knowledge: what does the writer know that the reader still needs to be told?

Tone is the attitude a reader hears in the words. Compare "You people never return anything" with "Please return reading-club books to the class shelf by Thursday so another pupil can borrow them." The second states an action and a reason without blaming an entire group. A writer can be clear about a problem without humiliating readers. Ask pupils to revise a message while preserving its purpose, rather than simply replacing a strong statement with something vague.

Audience also affects language choice. A teacher might prepare a brief notice in Sindhi and Urdu so that more carers can understand it, while an English class studies how the English version is organised. The goal and audience determine what support is useful. Avoid judging a family's commitment by whether it responds in English or types fluently. Offer a route through school for questions rather than require children to act as the only interpreters of important instructions.

A message may travel beyond its intended audience. A recipient can forward it, copy its text or capture a screen image. For a classroom exercise, write as though an unintended reader could see the message. This is not a reason to avoid all communication. It is a reason to keep unnecessary personal information out of the first draft and to select a channel with the supervising teacher. A small group should not be treated as a guarantee of permanent privacy.

#### Responding is a literacy practice

A good response identifies the part of a message it addresses and adds something useful. "I disagree" gives little information. "The notice says Tuesday, but the timetable we received says Wednesday; which is current?" identifies a conflict that can be checked. Teach pupils to ask for clarification when evidence is incomplete. A question can be responsible participation, especially when a confident answer would rest on guessing.

A classroom discussion also needs room for quieter readers. Give everyone a short private planning time, then ask pairs to compare responses. A child who needs longer to compose should not lose the chance to contribute because the fastest pupil posts first. If devices are shared, rotate the writer and checker. A group message should represent an agreed idea, and the teacher should still collect some individual evidence of understanding.

Feedback must address the text or reasoning rather than a person's worth. Ask pupils to identify a claim, explain what is unclear and suggest a useful next step. For example: "You say every pupil can download the story, but the scenario says some families have no data. Can you add a school-based route?" This response connects a criticism to evidence and invites revision. It models the kind of discussion a teacher can assess without rewarding hostility.

When a harmful message appears, the literacy task includes choosing a response. Do not ask pupils to forward it to prove they have seen it. In a practice case, stop circulation, avoid public argument and ask the supervising teacher how to report it through school procedures. Children should know whom to approach. Teaching a reporting route is more useful than telling them merely to "be careful" while leaving them alone with a problem.

#### Read the message and the system around it

Alcolea-Díaz, Reig and Mancinas-Chávez (2020) examine the place of media ownership and underlying interests in teachers' media and information literacy education. Their work supports looking beyond a message's surface: ask who produces and distributes it, and whose interests might shape it. This does not mean that every commercial message is false. It means that source and purpose deserve examination alongside the words ([article](https://doi.org/10.3916/c62-2020-09)).

Imagine a fictional page headed "Best books for every child." It links only to one seller and gives no explanation of how books were selected. A careful reader can identify a possible commercial purpose. That is not enough to declare every listed book unsuitable. The next step is to inspect the reasons and compare an independent source. Distinguish an observed feature, a possible explanation and a conclusion. This prevents suspicion from becoming another unsupported claim.

Similarly, a popular post with many reactions is evidence that people responded, not evidence that its claim is accurate. A newly shared screenshot might contain an old notice with its date cropped out. A careful trainee asks for the original source and current context before treating it as an instruction. A spelling mistake alone does not prove a message false, just as polished design does not prove it true. Use checks connected to the claim.

#### A worked message-checking example

A fictional class group receives a screenshot saying the school library will be closed "tomorrow." It has no visible date and no source. The trainee's first move is to identify the action the message asks readers to take: avoid visiting the library. The missing date matters because "tomorrow" changes with the time of sending. The missing source matters because a forwarded image may not reflect the school's current arrangement.

The trainee does not circulate the screenshot with a confident warning. She checks through the supervising teacher or the school's established contact route. If confirmation is unavailable, she labels the information unverified and postpones any class announcement. If a current notice is confirmed, she drafts a new message with the actual date, the relevant group and the approved contact for questions. She does not attach private conversation screenshots simply to demonstrate that she checked.

Notice the reasoning. The trainee has neither accepted the claim because a friend sent it nor rejected it because it arrived on social media. She has identified what would make it usable information. This is the same evidence habit used in reading a printed notice. Digital communication makes the consequences of a careless forward easier to see, but the underlying literacy principle is to connect a claim to a trustworthy source and enough context.

### Ethical, legal and responsible use of digital resources

Responsible use requires several separate judgments. **Accuracy** asks whether a claim is well supported. **Permission** asks whether a planned use is authorised. **Credit** identifies whose work or words are being used. **Privacy** asks whether personal information should be exposed to this audience. **Access** asks whether the intended learners can take part. A resource can pass one check and fail another. Do not let an attractive or accurate resource conceal the remaining questions.

<Glossary term="Copyright" /> concerns rights in creative work, including permissions for copying and adapting it. <Glossary term="Plagiarism" /> means presenting another person's words or ideas as your own. The two concerns overlap but are not identical. A writer can acknowledge a creator and still lack permission for a particular reuse. A writer can have permission to use a passage and still mislead a reader by presenting it as original writing. For classroom planning, check both rather than assume a reference settles every issue.

The misconception **"anything on the internet is free to copy"** confuses the ability to see a work with permission to reuse it. A download button does not, by itself, explain what uses are permitted. Look for the creator, licence or permission statement attached to the original work. A **licence** states conditions under which a work may be used. If those conditions are unclear, seek help from the supervising teacher, request permission or choose a different resource with clear terms.

Creative Commons explains several standard licences. CC BY permits reuse and adaptation with attribution; other licences can add conditions such as sharing adaptations under matching terms, restricting commercial use or prohibiting distribution of adaptations. Check the exact licence and the planned use, including translation or editing. For this unit, the practical rule is to read the terms rather than assume every "CC" label grants the same permission ([Creative Commons](https://creativecommons.org/cc-licenses/)). This explanation is a resource-checking routine, not a ruling on a particular Pakistani copyright dispute.

Do not guess a legal exception from the word "education." If you cannot establish a suitable permission for a copied story or illustration, select material the teacher created, obtain permission or choose a clearly licensed alternative. If a legal question remains, refer it to an appropriate school authority rather than ask pupils to settle it. The literacy skill being taught is identifying the missing information and choosing a responsible next step.

#### Credit that lets a reader trace the source

A useful credit names the creator, work and source, and records the licence or permission when relevant. It should let another reader find the original and understand whether changes were made. The exact form may differ between a handout, a slide and a written assignment. The purpose stays the same: do not hide where borrowed material came from. Keep a record while collecting resources, since tracing an unlabeled picture later can be difficult.

For example, a trainee uses a fictional drawing called "Reading under a tree" by fictional creator Sana Ali, supplied for an exercise under CC BY 4.0. A classroom credit could name the title and creator, give the supplied source and licence, and state that the trainee cropped it. This is a model of the fields to retain, not a claim that such a drawing exists. In real work, replace every field with verified details from the actual source before using the image.

A search engine is a way of finding a resource, not usually the resource's creator or permission giver. A picture result may lead to a copied image without its original terms. Open the original source where possible and check the specific item. If the origin cannot be established, the teacher can choose an original classroom drawing instead. Do not invent a creator or attach a licence because the image would be convenient for the lesson.

Paraphrasing means expressing an idea in your own words while retaining its meaning and acknowledging its source. Replacing a few words in a copied sentence is not enough to show your own explanation. A useful routine is to read, close the source, explain the idea for your audience and reopen the source to check accuracy. Then add the reference. The teacher can ask the writer to explain the meaning orally to check that the text is understood.

A quotation uses the source's exact words and needs clear quotation marks and credit. In this course, use your own explanation and keep any quotation short. Neither paraphrasing nor quoting removes the need to consider permission for the planned use. In the classroom, a pupil's own example can often make a concept clearer than a long copied passage. The goal is understanding, not filling the page with impressive language from elsewhere.

#### Privacy is a separate check

A creator's permission to use a worksheet does not grant permission to expose the children who use it. A photograph can reveal a face, a name badge, a location or private work on a desk. A screenshot can reveal group members' numbers or messages. Inspect the whole item, not only the feature you intend to discuss. The safest teaching example is often a fictional reconstruction that contains no real child's details.

Use school safeguarding procedures and the supervising teacher's guidance for any proposed recording or sharing. Permission should relate to the intended use and audience. Agreement to take part in a class activity should not be treated as blanket agreement to public posting. A child should have a meaningful alternative that does not reduce the quality of the learning task. Do not ask a practicum class to provide personal images for this unit's exercises.

Ask whether the information is needed at all. To show that a pupil revised a sentence, a teacher can describe the change in anonymised words. To practise reading a timetable, the teacher can create a fictional timetable. To discuss respectful replies, the class can work with invented messages. These alternatives preserve the teaching goal without creating a store of unnecessary personal material. Remove detail because it serves no learning purpose, not only after somebody complains.

Access is another responsibility. A task should not require a pupil to post publicly or buy mobile data in order to be counted as participating. Provide the essential material in class and an equivalent paper or oral route. If an assignment asks learners to analyse online messages, offer printed examples whose sources and permissions have been checked. A child should not need to search through unsuitable material to demonstrate the analysis skill.

#### A worked resource decision

Return to the Hyderabad practicum group. They need a short reading text and an image to discuss with Grade 4 pupils. Their first choice is an online story with no clear reuse terms. They can read the webpage, but they cannot establish permission to reproduce the whole story in a forwarded file. They stop that reuse and select a teacher-written passage. This solves the permission problem for the text without making an unsupported legal claim about the first site.

Their second choice is an image with a clear CC BY licence and an identifiable creator. They retain the creator, title, source and licence, and note any permitted changes. They then notice that their planned file also contains a real pupil's photograph. The image licence does not address that separate photograph. They remove it and use a fictional drawing. Each check concerns the particular material being used; permission for one item cannot be transferred to every other item in the file.

Finally, they consider the audience. A supervised in-class exchange is enough for pupils to receive comments on their reading response. The file does not need to go to a large public group. The trainees prepare a printed copy with the same passage and question. Their decision therefore includes permission, attribution, privacy and access, rather than ending when they find something that looks suitable. They record the source choices for their supervisor to inspect.

#### Use a stop-and-check routine

Before use or sharing, ask: who made this, what do we know about it, what use is permitted, how will we credit it, who might be exposed and who can participate? These questions are not a rigid race to a final tick. New information can send the decision back. An image may be permitted but unsuitable for the learners; a useful story may lack clear permission for copying. Stop, replace, seek help or narrow the use when a necessary check remains unresolved.

The routine also helps when someone makes a mistake. If a draft contains an uncredited passage, correct the source use and explain what changed. If private material has been shared, tell the supervising teacher promptly and follow the school's response procedure. Avoid circulating the material again in a public apology. The aim is to address the harm and learn a better process, rather than turn another person's information into a classroom spectacle.

A good teacher models uncertainty honestly. "I have not yet checked whether we may adapt this" is a responsible statement when it is followed by checking or choosing an alternative. It is better than adding an invented licence or assuming that a small audience makes every use acceptable. Pupils learn from the decision process the teacher demonstrates, including when the teacher decides not to use a tempting resource.

## Activity: Resource and message clinic

Work in groups of three for about 25 minutes. Allocate writer, source checker and audience checker roles, then rotate. Use these fictional cases on paper: a copied story with no permission information; an accurately credited image with a clearly stated CC BY licence; a timetable screenshot showing real contact details; and a class reminder that only says "submit tomorrow." Do not collect actual messages or photographs.

For each case, identify the learning purpose, what is known, what is missing and the next action. Distinguish a permission problem from a credit or privacy problem. Then draft a reading-club reminder for a chosen audience and exchange it with another group. Give one evidence-based comment about clarity and one about responsible sharing. Revise the reminder and write a brief reason for the change. Your task is to make a defensible decision, not to find a way of forwarding every item.

## Check your understanding

1. Define plagiarism and copyright in your own words, then state one difference between them. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why a social media message can involve real literacy even when it is short. Name two choices a writer makes. *(Understand)*
3. A correctly credited worksheet contains a pupil's name and private comment. Describe what you would do before a class presentation and why credit alone is insufficient. *(Apply)*
4. A forwarded notice says "tomorrow" but shows no date or source. State the checks you would make before passing it to families. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Online communication requires decisions about purpose, evidence, audience and response. Social media can contain meaningful reading and writing, but a study of one group does not justify a platform recommendation for every class. Responsible resource use separates accuracy, permission, credit, privacy and access. When a necessary check is unresolved, the next step is to stop, seek help or choose a suitable alternative.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can explain the literacy choices in a short online message.
- [ ] I can separate a source's popularity from evidence for its claim.
- [ ] I can distinguish permission, credit and privacy using one classroom resource.
- [ ] I can identify missing licence information without inventing a legal conclusion.
- [ ] I can give respectful feedback and revise a message for a defined audience.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your supervising teacher to show you a permitted, non-personal resource used in a lesson. Do not copy private messages or pupil records. Record its purpose, creator or source, available permission information and intended audience. If a detail is missing, record the question rather than assume the answer. Suggest a fictional or teacher-created alternative if needed. Discuss how pupils without home internet would receive the same essential learning material.

## Summative task

Write a 450–550-word review of this proposed Grade 4 activity: pupils must join a public group, copy a story with no licence information, add their own photographs and reply "good" to two posts. The stated goal is to write a clear, evidence-based response to a story. Identify the strongest literacy opportunity, analyze the problems and redesign the activity for a teacher-managed classroom audience. Include useful peer feedback, a justified revision, traceable source information and an equivalent offline route.

Mini-rubric, 12 marks: explains audience and a genuine reading or writing purpose (0–3); distinguishes and analyzes permission, attribution and privacy problems using scenario evidence (**Analyze**, 0–3); provides a feasible redesign with fair access and appropriate sharing (0–3); models specific feedback and explains a resulting revision (0–3). For each criterion, 0 means absent, 1 means named without application, 2 means applied with a material gap, and 3 means specific, coherent and justified. Unsupported legal certainty does not earn credit for responsible judgment.
