## Unit summary

Digital literacy involves making meaning and exercising judgment, as well as operating a device. A teacher starts with a literacy goal, chooses a resource and support that serve it, and collects evidence of what pupils understand. Enjoyment can encourage participation, but it cannot substitute for evidence of comprehension. A child who can explain an event with supporting details has shown something different from a child who can tap through a lively story.

The screen-reading research discussed in Topic 4.1 concerns young children and particular book designs and teaching conditions. Its findings should inform questions about a resource, not become a universal ban or promise. A careful lesson plan identifies relevant features, possible distractions, adult support and a comparable route for pupils without devices. A local classroom observation can inform the next lesson without proving that a device caused an outcome.

Readers of digital texts set a purpose, inspect the source, read a manageable section and check meaning. They keep track of the difference between a stated detail and an inference. Digital writers plan for an audience, draft, add useful links where needed, share with an appropriate reader, receive focused comments and revise. A writing assessment should recognise improvements in meaning and audience awareness rather than reward typing speed alone.

Online messages also involve literacy choices. A short message can be carefully composed or confusing. A popular post can still lack evidence. A respectful response identifies a claim, gives a reason and invites clarification where information is missing. Sharing with a partner can meet a learning goal without public posting or child accounts. Research on older teenagers in one setting does not establish that every platform benefits every class.

Responsible use separates several questions. An accurate source may lack permission for copying. A credited image may still expose a child. A permitted resource may require data that some families cannot afford. Check the creator, evidence, permission, attribution, privacy, audience and access. Where a necessary check is unresolved, seek help, narrow the use or select a suitable alternative. Keep these distinctions visible in both lesson planning and feedback.

## Summative assessment

Attempt the questions before consulting the final guidance. The examples are fictional. No account, purchase, public post or personal image is needed. MCQs carry 1 mark each; RRQs carry 4 marks each; ERQs carry 10 marks each. This gives 100 practice marks. Your teacher may select tasks for assessment; the unit's default formative/summative balance does not replace the course guide's course-level scheme.

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. What is the main purpose of checking comprehension after a digital story? *(Remember)*
   - A) To count how often pupils touch the screen.
   - B) To find out what meaning pupils have understood.
   - C) To compare how brightly the pictures appear.
   - D) To identify which device pupils prefer to hold.

2. A teacher selects an animation that explains the event pupils must discuss. What most directly justifies this choice? *(Apply)*
   - A) It makes the lesson file larger than the print version.
   - B) It replaces the need for pupils to explain the event.
   - C) It serves the identified story-understanding goal.
   - D) It shows that every animated resource is effective.

3. Which statement best respects the scope of Furenes and colleagues' study? *(Understand)*
   - A) Its findings concern young children and the conditions studied.
   - B) Its findings establish the best medium for every age group.
   - C) Its findings show that enjoyment measures understanding.
   - D) Its findings remove the need to inspect digital book design.

4. A pupil opens several pages and forgets the reading question. Which action best restores a purposeful reading path? *(Apply)*
   - A) Open another page with a more attractive heading.
   - B) Copy the longest passage from the current page.
   - C) Choose the page with the largest number of pictures.
   - D) Restate the question and return to a relevant heading.

5. A partner says a reminder lacks a return date. Which revision best responds to this feedback? *(Apply)*
   - A) Replace every short word with a longer word.
   - B) Add the date clearly where readers need it.
   - C) Add an unrelated picture beside the message.
   - D) Repeat the existing reminder in a larger font.

6. What does plagiarism mean? *(Remember)*
   - A) Asking a writer to clarify an unclear sentence.
   - B) Recording a source while preparing an assignment.
   - C) Presenting another person's words or ideas as one's own.
   - D) Revising an original draft after receiving comments.

7. A teacher credits an image but cannot establish permission for the planned adaptation. What should happen next? *(Apply)*
   - A) Check the terms or choose a clearly permitted alternative.
   - B) Treat the credit as permission for any adaptation.
   - C) Remove the credit so the origin is less noticeable.
   - D) Ask pupils to distribute the adaptation privately.

8. A popular screenshot says a library closes "tomorrow" and shows no date. Which check is most useful before announcing the closure? *(Apply)*
   - A) Count the reactions beneath the latest forward.
   - B) Compare the colour of its heading with another notice.
   - C) Ask which pupil received the screenshot first.
   - D) Confirm the current date and instruction through the school.

9. Which peer comment most directly supports responsible revision? *(Understand)*
   - A) "Your writing is poor and everyone will notice."
   - B) "The place is missing, so readers cannot find the meeting."
   - C) "I like the colour, so no changes are necessary."
   - D) "Use my version because I finished before you."

10. A story has suitable reuse permission, but its attached photograph reveals a pupil's private information. What remains necessary? *(Apply)*
   - A) Increase the font size of the creator's credit.
   - B) Share the photograph only after the story is read.
   - C) Resolve the privacy issue before the proposed sharing.
   - D) Add another link to the resource's permission page.

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

1. In three sentences, distinguish enjoyment from comprehension. Give one observable classroom indicator of each. *(Understand)*
2. In four sentences, analyze why a finding about young children's digital storybooks does not settle how college students should collaborate on a shared document. Identify the different learners, task and evidence needed. *(Analyze)*
3. Give four steps for reading a digital school notice purposefully. Include one action for recovering the source after following a link. *(Apply)*
4. In three or four sentences, propose a shared-device arrangement for 32 pupils and one teacher phone. Preserve individual evidence and an equivalent route if the battery fails. *(Apply)*
5. A draft says "Bring it tomorrow." In no more than four sentences, identify two reader problems and explain two corresponding revisions. *(Analyze)*
6. In three sentences, explain two literacy choices in a short social media message and why brevity does not remove the need for those choices. *(Understand)*
7. In four sentences, distinguish permission and credit. Use a case in which naming a creator does not settle whether a planned adaptation is permitted. *(Analyze)*
8. A trainee thinks a notice must be accurate because it has many reactions. Give a three-step checking plan and explain in one sentence why reactions are insufficient. *(Apply)*
9. In three or four sentences, explain why a licence for a worksheet does not settle whether a screenshot of pupils' responses can be posted publicly. State a suitable alternative. *(Analyze)*
10. Write a respectful two-sentence response to a group message that gives a submission deadline but no date or source. Then explain, in two sentences, which information you would confirm before forwarding. *(Apply)*

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. A Grade 2 teacher plans to show a digital story with unrelated games and judge success by the number of taps. Analyze the plan in 400–500 words. State a comprehension goal, evaluate the chosen feature, propose adult support and specify evidence of understanding. Use the screen-reading study with its scope clearly stated. *(Analyze)*
2. Design a 35-minute Grade 5 writing lesson for 40 pupils sharing two devices. In 400–500 words, include purpose, audience, a model draft, feedback and revision. Explain what a paper route preserves and how you will distinguish writing quality from device operation. *(Create)*
3. Evaluate this claim in 400–500 words: "Because social media involves writing, every primary pupil should post publicly for homework." Discuss literacy opportunities, research limits, audience, access and a feasible teacher-managed alternative that still teaches meaningful communication. *(Evaluate)*
4. A trainee proposes forwarding an online story with no clear reuse terms, a credited picture and a screenshot of a named child's reading difficulty. In 400–500 words, analyze each item separately. Set out a defensible use-or-replace decision, source records, privacy arrangements and the point at which help is required. *(Analyze)*
5. A school wants a phone-supported reading project, but some families have no data. In 500–600 words, design and defend a sequence that combines a reading goal, source checking, a written response, peer feedback and responsible sharing. Include a paper route, individual evidence, permission and privacy checks, and a justified response to screen-reading evidence. *(Create)*

## Answers and marking guidance

These models show defensible reasoning, not the only acceptable wording. Accept equivalent examples that preserve the distinctions taught. Do not award points for unsupported claims that a particular technology always improves learning or that credit automatically grants permission. For a missing legal or policy detail, identifying the gap and seeking an appropriate decision is stronger than inventing certainty.

### MCQ answer key

1. B - comprehension concerns the meaning understood, rather than visible device activity.
2. C - the feature is justified by its connection to the literacy goal.
3. A - the study's learners and conditions limit the scope of its findings.
4. D - restoring purpose and location helps the reader recover relevant evidence.
5. B - adding the missing date addresses the reader's stated problem.
6. C - plagiarism misrepresents the origin of another person's ideas or words.
7. A - permission must be established for the planned use; credit alone is insufficient.
8. D - a current school confirmation resolves missing date and source information.
9. B - the comment identifies a specific omission and its consequence for readers.
10. C - permission for a resource does not remove a separate privacy concern.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. Model: enjoyment concerns interest or preference; comprehension concerns meaning. A pupil may ask to replay a story because it is enjoyable. Explaining why a character acted, using an event as support, provides evidence of understanding. **4 marks:** distinguishes the two concepts (2, one each); gives an observable enjoyment indicator (1); gives a relevant comprehension indicator (1).

2. Model: young children reading stories differ from college students collaborating on a document. The reading study addresses book comprehension and design conditions, not the quality of collaborative argument. A college task needs evidence about contributions, source use and revisions. The reading findings can suggest questions about distraction without answering that different research question. **4 marks:** identifies learner difference (1); task difference (1); suitable new evidence (1); explains the limit on the inference (1).

3. Model: state the question; locate the heading, source and date; read a relevant section; explain the meaning and check the supporting sentence. After following a link, return to the original page and locate the heading again before using its evidence. **4 marks:** purpose (1); source inspection (1); meaning check (1); workable return-to-source action (1). Reading a section may be combined with the inspection step; do not penalise a coherent four-step organisation.

4. Model: use rotating reader, operator, recorder and checker roles in small groups. The teacher phone can demonstrate one feature while groups work from the same printed passage. Each pupil produces a brief explanation so group performance does not hide individual gaps. If the battery fails, the teacher or partner reads the passage and groups use the same questions. **4 marks:** workable sharing arrangement (1); role rotation or equivalent participation protection (1); individual evidence (1); backup preserving the goal (1).

5. Model: "it" does not name the item, and "tomorrow" gives no stable date. Replace "it" with the intended book or task. Add the actual date once confirmed. The revisions supply information the reader cannot safely infer from the draft. **4 marks:** two distinct reader problems (2, one each); two matching revisions (2, one each). Do not require invented real arrangements.

6. Model: a writer selects the essential facts for a reader and chooses a tone suited to the relationship. A short reminder still needs a clear action and enough context. Its small size does not prevent misunderstanding. **4 marks:** two valid choices (2, one each); explains a consequence for meaning (1); explains why brevity does not remove the need (1).

7. Model: credit acknowledges the creator; permission concerns what use is authorised. Naming a photographer does not show that translating labels or editing an image is allowed under its terms. The trainee checks the specific licence or obtains permission. If the intended use remains unclear, the trainee chooses a suitable alternative. **4 marks:** defines credit (1); defines permission (1); applies the distinction to adaptation (1); proposes a responsible next step (1).

8. Model: locate the original notice; check the date and issuing source; confirm the instruction through the school's established route. Reactions measure responses to the post, not whether the current instruction is correct. **4 marks:** original source (1); date and issuer (1); relevant confirmation route (1); explains the limit of popularity (1). Accept an equally coherent order.

9. Model: the worksheet's licence concerns use of that work, while the screenshot contains pupils' personal information. Names or private comments may be visible even when the worksheet itself is permitted. The teacher should use a fictional reconstruction or anonymised description and follow school procedures for any proposed recording. **4 marks:** separates resource rights from personal information (1); identifies a concrete exposure (1); names an appropriate school decision route (1); gives an alternative preserving the goal (1).

10. Model response: "The message gives a deadline, but I cannot see the date or issuing source. Could we confirm those through our teacher before circulating it?" Confirm the current date and authoritative instruction. Forward only the relevant confirmed information to the intended audience, without exposing private conversation details. **4 marks:** respectful and specific question (1); identifies date (1); identifies source/confirmation (1); states a responsible forwarding decision (1).

### ERQ rubrics

Use the four criteria for each item below. Each criterion earns 0 for absent or fundamentally mistaken reasoning, 1 for a relevant but incomplete response, and 2 for accurate, specific and coherent application to the case. Add 0–2 for the item's justification criterion: 0 for an unsupported conclusion, 1 for a reason with a material gap, and 2 for a conclusion supported by case evidence and a recognised limit or trade-off. Total: 10 per item. Length guides scope; do not reward repetition or penalise concise completeness.

1. **Goal and evidence (0–2):** an observable comprehension goal and a check that requires meaning, not tapping. **Feature analysis (0–2):** distinguishes a relevant support from unrelated games and connects the distinction to attention and the goal. **Teaching support (0–2):** includes workable pauses, discussion and a comparable paper route. **Research scope (0–2):** accurately names young children and avoids a universal conclusion. **Justification (0–2, Analyze):** uses the case's mismatch between taps and comprehension to defend a revised plan. A strong response rejects the proposed measure of success without claiming every digital story is ineffective.

2. **Audience and purpose (0–2):** a defined reader and clear writing job. **Model and sequence (0–2):** demonstrates drafting, useful feedback and an actual revision in a feasible 35-minute sequence. **Access (0–2):** rotating participation and a paper route with equivalent thinking. **Evidence (0–2):** individual writing evidence separated from device skill. **Justification (0–2, Create):** explains why the chosen sequence addresses shared access and reader understanding. A strong design uses limited device time for a clear purpose and does not require public posting.

3. **Literacy opportunity (0–2):** explains authentic choices about message, audience and feedback. **Research limits (0–2):** distinguishes a small study of older teenagers from a universal prescription for primary pupils. **Responsibility (0–2):** addresses appropriate audience and privacy rather than assuming public visibility is necessary. **Access and alternative (0–2):** a teacher-managed task with an equivalent route for pupils without data. **Justification (0–2, Evaluate):** weighs the educational opportunity against the proposed compulsory public homework. A strong response rejects the inference in the claim while retaining a meaningful communication goal.

4. **Item-by-item analysis (0–2):** separately examines the story, credited image and named child's screenshot. **Permission and credit (0–2):** checks intended use and records traceable source details without treating credit as blanket permission. **Privacy (0–2):** removes or replaces identifying material and refers to school procedures. **Decision route (0–2):** identifies when to stop, seek help or substitute teacher-created material. **Justification (0–2, Analyze):** explains why passing one check does not settle the others. A strong answer does not assume that the credited picture is either automatically permitted or automatically forbidden; it checks the missing terms.

5. **Reading and evidence (0–2):** an explicit comprehension goal, appropriate support and individual evidence. **Writing cycle (0–2):** a response for a defined reader with focused feedback and explained revision. **Responsible resource use (0–2):** source, permission, credit and privacy checks attached to specific actions. **Access (0–2):** essential content available without home data and a comparable paper route. **Justification (0–2, Create):** integrates both topics, uses research within scope and explains a feasible trade-off. A strong response may use a phone only for a brief demonstration if that choice best serves the class; more screen time is not a scoring criterion.
