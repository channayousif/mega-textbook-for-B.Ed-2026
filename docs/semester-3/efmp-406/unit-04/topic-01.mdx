## A real classroom situation

At a government primary school near Tando Allahyar, Ms Samina brings one charged phone and a printed story to Grade 2. The children enjoy tapping the phone's pictures, but some cannot explain why the main character changes her mind. Another group hears the teacher read the printed story and discusses the character's choice. Samina must decide what the phone should contribute to the next lesson and how every child will take part.

## Explanation

### Technology and literacy development

Technology is a tool people use to do a job. For literacy, the job might be hearing the sounds in a word, understanding a story, finding information or revising a message. A digital tool stores or processes information electronically. A phone can carry a story, record an explanation or display a draft. None of these functions tells us, on its own, whether a child has learned. Start with the literacy goal, then ask what a tool helps the learner do.

Consider two goals. A teacher wants pupils to explain why a story character leaves home. A recorded reading may help pupils who are still learning to read the words. The teacher also wants pupils to practise reading the words themselves. For that goal, listening alone does not show the needed skill. The same recording can support one goal while failing to provide evidence for another. Match the task and the evidence to the goal, rather than treating the presence of a device as success.

A useful planning question is: what would be difficult without this feature? Enlarged text might make a passage easier for a child to see. An offline recording might let a pair hear an unfamiliar word again. A shared draft might make a writer's changes visible. Each feature has a teaching purpose. By contrast, a spinning decoration beside every sentence may keep children busy without helping them understand the sentence. Being busy, interested and learning are related possibilities, not interchangeable observations.

Furenes, Kucirkova and Bus (2021) reviewed research with children aged one to eight. When paper and digital books differed only in their medium, comprehension favoured paper. Digital additions connected to a story could help, whereas unrelated features could distract. Adult support also mattered. These findings concern the conditions studied; they do not prove that every child learns better from paper or that an older learner should avoid screens. Their practical value is to make us inspect design and teaching, rather than assume a device improves understanding ([study](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654321998074)).

A **meta-analysis** brings numerical findings from several studies together. Its result describes a pattern across those studies. It is not a prediction for every classroom. A trainee should ask who took part, what they read, what support they received and what outcome was measured. For example, evidence about young children's story comprehension cannot by itself settle whether college students collaborate well in a shared document. That requires another question and appropriate evidence.

The misconception **"children read better on screens because they like them"** confuses preference with understanding. A child may prefer a lively book and still miss the reason for an event. Another child may dislike a small screen because the words are hard to see. Ask pupils to retell, explain, connect and point to evidence. Their responses tell you more about comprehension than smiles or time spent touching the device.

Here is a worked decision for Samina's class. Her goal is that pupils explain a character's decision using two events from the story. She checks the phone version before class. It has a relevant audio reading and an unrelated matching game. She chooses the audio, avoids the game and pauses twice to ask what the character knows at that point. Pupils then explain the decision to a partner. Samina listens for a link between an event and a reason, not merely a repeated sentence.

She also prepares a paper route. A partner reads aloud from the same story, and both pupils use the same two discussion prompts. Children without a phone are therefore doing the same thinking. The routes need not look identical. What should remain comparable is the goal, access to the necessary content and the evidence used to judge understanding. It would be unfair to assess the phone group on explanations and the paper group only on neat copying.

#### Access is part of the lesson design

The misconception **"digital literacy needs expensive equipment, so it does not apply to government schools"** makes a different mistake. Learning to judge a link, plan a clear message or explain a sharing decision does not require each child to own a device. Pupils can practise on printed examples or a chalkboard model. One teacher-controlled phone can demonstrate a feature. These arrangements teach important decisions while avoiding an assumption about household income.

Paper simulation has limits. A drawing of a screen can teach where a heading appears, but it cannot show whether a particular button is easy to operate. If the goal includes using an actual interface, pupils eventually need supervised practice with that interface or an accessible equivalent. Be clear about which part of the skill the simulation provides and which remains to be taught. Do not mark a paper discussion as proof that every learner can operate an unfamiliar application.

Plan the class around shared access. In a group of four, one child reads, one operates the device when needed, one records evidence and one asks the checking question. Rotate the roles. If the quickest pupil controls the device throughout, the teacher sees one child's performance and may mistakenly record group competence. Role changes make participation more visible. They also stop a literacy lesson becoming a demonstration watched by everyone else.

A low-bandwidth plan uses small files prepared beforehand and avoids requiring live downloads during a short lesson. Check that the file opens without an account. Keep a printed passage or a board version ready in case the battery fails. A backup should preserve the learning task. Replacing a planned explanation task with unrelated copying because the internet failed does not preserve it. Decide the alternative before class, while there is time to choose well.

Think about language too. A pupil may understand a story in Sindhi but find its English instructions difficult. Explain the task in a familiar language while keeping the target reading goal clear. If the goal is understanding the story, a spoken explanation in Sindhi may reveal comprehension. If the goal is writing an English sentence, that spoken explanation is useful preparation but not the final evidence. State which skill you are assessing so that language support does not hide or distort the target.

Visibility, hearing and movement also affect participation. Ask whether text can be enlarged without losing words, whether a recording has a text alternative and whether a child can participate without precise tapping. These are questions for inspecting a resource, not promises about every device. Consult the learner and supervising teacher about suitable support. A lesson should not require children to disclose private information to prove why they need a different route.

#### Distinguish the resource from the teaching

A resource provides material. Teaching organises what learners notice, do and explain with that material. A good story file can still be used badly if pupils rush through it without discussion. A simple paper story can support demanding reasoning when the teacher asks for evidence. When reviewing a lesson, record both the resource features and the teacher's actions. Otherwise, a successful lesson may be attributed to a phone when the important change was a better question.

For example, Samina compares two plans rather than claiming to run a scientific experiment. In Plan A she shows the story continuously and asks for a title at the end. In Plan B she pauses at the turning point and asks pupils to justify a prediction. If Plan B produces fuller explanations, both the reading support and the task changed. She can use the observation to improve teaching. She cannot honestly conclude that the device alone caused the difference.

A small classroom check can still be useful. Before the lesson, select a question requiring a reason. During the task, note which pupils use an event from the story. Afterwards, ask a similar question independently. Record a few anonymised examples of reasoning, including incomplete explanations. Use these to decide what to reteach. Avoid publishing a child's response or name simply to demonstrate that the technology worked.

### Digital reading and writing practices

**Digital reading** means making meaning from text presented through a digital device. The text may be a story, a notice, an article, a message or a set of linked pages. Reading still requires attention to words and meaning. The reader may also need to choose links, follow headings, move between pages and judge which text belongs to the source. A link is a connection that opens another location; following it is a reading decision because it can change the path through the information.

A useful reading routine has four moves: set a purpose, inspect the page, read a manageable section and check meaning. These moves can be modelled on paper first. The aim is not to teach pupils to click quickly. It is to make the decisions around a digital text deliberate. A reader who pauses to explain why a link is useful may be doing more thoughtful reading than a reader who opens many tabs without remembering the question.

**Set a purpose.** Before opening a page, write the question you want to answer. A Grade 5 class preparing a school garden notice might ask when seedlings should be watered. That question helps pupils notice relevant information and leave unrelated material aside. Without a purpose, they may copy the first sentence they find. A purpose also helps them know when they have enough information to stop searching and begin writing.

**Inspect the page.** Locate its heading, creator or organisation, date and main text. Notice whether a button is part of the information or an advertisement. Check the whole destination of a link when the device permits it. A familiar picture or official-looking design is not enough to establish a source. If pupils cannot identify the source, the teacher can choose a better example rather than ask them to make an unsupported judgment.

**Read a section.** Move through enough text to understand the idea before opening another page. Ask pupils to pause at a heading and state its meaning in their own words. If they use a word aid or audio support, bring them back to the sentence. A feature is useful when it helps the pupil return to the reading goal. Opening a definition without connecting it to the passage is an action, but not yet evidence of understanding.

**Check meaning.** Close or cover the passage and explain the main point. Reopen it to check a detail and distinguish that detail from your own inference. An <Glossary term="Inference">inference</Glossary> is an explanation drawn from clues rather than a statement directly written in the text. In a story, wet shoes might support an inference that a child walked through rain. The reader should say which clue supports the idea, rather than present a guess as a fact.

Navigation means finding your way through the material. A paper reader may use page numbers and physical position. A screen reader may use a contents list, search function, headings or a back button. Neither route guarantees understanding. Teach the route the particular text requires. If a pupil loses a passage after opening a link, model returning to the original page and locating the heading again. This is a small but useful literacy lesson in keeping track of evidence.

#### A worked digital reading example

Suppose a trainee has prepared a fictional school notice for practice. It says that the library opens at 8:30 on Tuesday and that borrowed books should be returned by Friday. The page also contains a colourful button for a book sale. The question is: when should a pupil return a borrowed library book? These details are invented for the exercise; pupils should not treat them as actual school instructions.

The trainee models her thinking aloud. "My purpose is to find the return day. The heading identifies a library notice. I read the sentence about borrowed books. Friday is the day I need. The sale button is unrelated to this question, so I will not open it." She then asks pupils to cover the notice and explain the answer with its supporting sentence. The model shows selection and justification, rather than a list of clicks to imitate.

Now change the question to: can we say the library opens at 8:30 every day? The notice only states Tuesday. A careful reader separates the stated information from a wider assumption. This distinction matters in digital reading because short extracts can look complete when they are not. Ask pupils what further information would settle the question. Teaching them to name a gap is more valuable than rewarding a confident but unsupported answer.

#### Writing for an audience

Digital writing is composing a text with a digital tool, often with opportunities to edit, link, combine modes and exchange responses. A mode is a way of making meaning, such as words, an image or sound. A school notice may combine a heading, a date, a picture and a short explanation. The writer chooses each part for a purpose. Adding a picture does not improve a message if the picture obscures the date readers need.

The misconception that social media writing is not real writing will be examined in Topic 4.2. Here, keep a simpler distinction in mind: a digital text can have a real audience even when it is short. A message to a head teacher needs enough context and an appropriate tone. A reminder to a known class group may be shorter. Both require decisions about meaning. The standard is whether readers can understand and act appropriately, not whether the text fills a long page.

<Figure id="fig-U4-2" kind="diagram" src="/img/figures/efmp-406/unit-04/fig-U4-2.svg" alt="A digital writing cycle connects planning, drafting, linking, sharing with a chosen audience, receiving comments and revising." caption="Original teaching schematic, textbook.com.pk." />

Work through a writing cycle before asking pupils to produce a finished message. **Plan:** name the reader, purpose and essential information. **Draft:** put those ideas into words. **Link:** add a useful source or connected text when needed and check that it supports the claim. **Share:** choose a suitable audience, which may be only one partner. **Receive comments:** ask readers a specific question. **Revise:** change the text in response to a useful comment and explain the change.

Sharing is not the same as public posting. A pair can exchange a saved draft on one device, or swap printed versions. A classroom can use a teacher-managed display without creating children's accounts. The literacy goal is to learn from another reader's response. Choose the smallest audience that can provide that response. Public visibility adds risks and responsibilities without necessarily improving the writing task.

#### A worked digital writing example

A fictional Grade 5 class in Mirpurkhas is preparing a message about a book exchange. The audience is parents and carers. Its first draft reads: "Bring it tomorrow. We will exchange." The writer knows the plan, but a reader may not know what "it" means, where to come or which date "tomorrow" refers to. A message can look complete to its author because the author carries information that the reader does not share.

A partner gives focused feedback: "I know there is an exchange, but I cannot tell which item to bring or when." The writer revises: "Our Grade 5 class will exchange used storybooks in the classroom on Thursday 12 November. Please send one book your child is willing to exchange." These are fictional arrangements, not a live invitation. The revision replaces an unclear pronoun, adds a date and names the item and place. The writer can explain the purpose of each change.

A second partner asks whether every family has a spare book. The class adds a teacher-approved route for children who cannot bring one, instead of treating non-participation as lack of interest. This is another kind of revision: the readers' situation changes the plan the message communicates. Writing quality involves checking whether the text creates a fair and workable action, as well as correcting spelling.

If the class uses a source, its link should help the reader. A link labelled "school book-exchange information" is clearer than an unexplained string or "click here" repeated several times. Check that the destination is appropriate and does not ask for unnecessary personal details. If an essential instruction is available only through a link, families without data may miss it. Put the essential instruction in the message itself and offer a paper copy.

A comment should help the writer make a decision. "Bad writing" names no problem. "The return date is missing, so I cannot plan" points to a reader's need. A writer need not accept every comment. A partner might suggest adding a home address that is unnecessary and private. The writer should be able to decline that suggestion with a reason. Feedback is evidence for revision, not an order to surrender judgment.

#### Keep a record of learning

Ask pupils to retain an initial draft and a revision, with one sentence explaining the change. On paper, they can draw a line between the changed phrases. On a device, they can save two appropriately named versions if the application permits it. The important record is the reasoning: what problem did a reader notice, and how did the writer respond? The number of edits alone says little about whether meaning improved.

A fair classroom rubric distinguishes content, organisation, audience and revision from typing speed. A pupil who dictates a clear message to a partner may demonstrate strong planning even if the pupil is still learning keyboard use. Record that achievement accurately and teach keyboard skills separately when needed. Conversely, a quickly typed message with missing instructions is not automatically strong writing. Decide which evidence belongs to each skill before marking.

Digital reading and writing can also connect. Readers gather relevant evidence, writers use it to make a claim, and partners check whether the claim follows. In a school garden task, pupils might read a teacher-prepared passage, write a two-sentence advice note and link the advice to one detail. Another pair checks that the detail actually supports the advice. This keeps source use visible and prepares pupils for responsible resource use in the next topic.

## Activity: Redesign the shared-phone lesson

Work in groups of four for about 25 minutes. Use paper; a phone is optional. Your case is a Grade 3 class with 36 pupils, one teacher phone and a printed story. The planned task is to watch a story, tap every animation and copy its last sentence. The stated goal is to explain why one character helps another.

First, each member recalls one reading or writing move without consulting the explanation. Then agree on what evidence would show the goal. Allocate reader, planner, access checker and evidence checker roles. Draw a revised sequence with two purposeful pauses, a question requiring a reason and an equivalent paper route. Include a short message pupils will draft for a partner about the character's choice.

Exchange plans with another group. Ask them to identify a feature that serves the goal and a feature that might distract. Revise one step and explain why. Your group should submit the original plan, the revised plan and its reason, so the teacher can see the thinking rather than only the neat final sheet. Do not claim that your proposed lesson has proved a research finding.

## Summary

A digital feature is useful when it serves an identified literacy goal and pupils can access the task. Reading on screens requires deliberate choices about purpose, navigation and checking meaning. Digital writing develops through planning for an audience, receiving focused responses and making justified revisions. Judge understanding and writing through evidence, while recognising what a study or classroom observation cannot establish.

- [ ] I can connect a digital feature to a literacy goal and identify a possible barrier.
- [ ] I can explain the screen-reading evidence without turning an average finding into a rule for every child.
- [ ] I can model a reading routine that returns to the question and supporting text.
- [ ] I can show how reader feedback leads to a useful revision.
- [ ] I can design an equivalent task for pupils without devices or home data.

## Try this at your practicum school

With your supervising teacher, choose one short, permitted text already used in class. Observe how pupils locate a detail and explain its meaning. If appropriate, model the same reading purpose with a teacher-controlled device and a paper copy; otherwise use a paper screen simulation. Record the goal, support provided and two anonymised patterns in pupils' responses. Do not record names, photographs or account details. Discuss one change to the task with your supervisor and state the limits of your observation.

## Summative task

Prepare a 450–550-word lesson proposal for a Grade 2 or Grade 3 class with one phone. Use a story-comprehension goal, one digital feature and an equivalent paper route. Explain how you will model reading, obtain individual evidence and teach a short writing revision. Compare one tempting but unsuitable feature with the one you select. Use Furenes and colleagues cautiously, naming the age range and one limit of transfer.

Mini-rubric, 12 marks: goal and observable comprehension evidence (0–3); analysis of how selected and rejected features affect the task, supported by a justified comparison rather than preference (**Analyze**, 0–3); feasible access arrangements and paper route (0–3); modelled writing, focused feedback and a meaningful revision (0–3). For each criterion, 0 means absent, 1 means named but not workable, 2 means workable with a gap, and 3 means specific and well justified.
