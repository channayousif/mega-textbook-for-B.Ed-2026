# Topic 5.1 - Teaching literacy with the learner at the centre

## A real classroom situation

The classroom scenes, named teachers and pupils, and worked examples in this topic are fictional teaching examples. They are not empirical case studies or official school inspection records.

Two Class 5 teachers at a government school in Mirpurkhas teach the same Urdu textbook lesson, a chapter on clean drinking water.

In Section A, Mr Akbar reads the chapter aloud. The pupils repeat difficult words after him. He writes five questions on the board, and the pupils copy the answers from the textbook into their notebooks. The room is quiet and the lesson ends on time.

In Section B, Ms Rubina starts with a question: "Where does the water in your home come from, and is it safe to drink?" Pupils talk in pairs. Several say their family buys water from a tanker; one says her grandmother boils water from the hand pump. The pupils then read the chapter in small groups to find out what makes water unsafe. Each group writes three questions they still have. Over the next two weeks, the class reads a health department leaflet, interviews the school chowkidar about the water tank, and makes a wall poster in Urdu and Sindhi called "Safe water in our school".

Both teachers taught reading and writing. Ms Rubina's pupils talked, chose, read several texts and made something for real readers. Some teachers in the staff room say her lesson was "noisy" and "not finishing the syllabus". This topic asks what it means to put the learner at the centre of literacy teaching, and whether it is worth the effort.

## Explanation

In every literacy lesson someone does most of the talking, choosing and thinking. In a **teacher-centred** lesson that person is the teacher. In a **learner-centred** lesson the learners do much more of it, while the teacher plans, guides and checks. This topic explains learner-centred literacy instruction first, then two approaches that put it into practice: inquiry-based learning and project-based learning.

### Learner-centred literacy instruction

**Learner-centred literacy instruction** is teaching reading, writing, speaking and listening in a way that starts from what learners already know, gives them an active part in the work, and uses texts and purposes that matter to them. It does not mean that learners decide everything. It means that the lesson is designed around what learners need to do in order to learn.

Four features describe it.

1. **It starts from the learner.** The teacher finds out what pupils already know and do with texts, in and out of school. In Unit 1 you saw that pupils bring home literacies to school, such as reading prices at a shop or messages on a phone. A learner-centred teacher builds on these. Ms Rubina's opening question did this: it brought pupils' knowledge of water at home into the reading lesson.
2. **Learners are active.** Pupils read, talk, question and write for most of the lesson. They do not only listen and copy. Talk matters because pupils make sense of a text by explaining it to someone else.
3. **Learners have some choice.** Pupils may choose which question to explore, which book to read from a box, or how to present what they found. Choice increases effort, because pupils care more about work they have shaped.
4. **Learners think about texts, not only read them.** Pupils are asked what a text means, who wrote it, why, and whether they agree. This links to the critical literacy you met in Unit 2.

#### What the teacher does

Learner-centred teaching asks more of the teacher, not less. The published abstract of Minott (2011) links critical literacy with reflective teaching and lists teacher tasks such as the following. This topic draws only on that abstract, not on the full article:

- building time into the lesson for pupils to think carefully about a text and judge it;
- guiding pupils' judgements and criticisms with care;
- encouraging pupils to question what they read;
- asking pupils to read a text from several points of view;
- letting pupils keep journals in which they compare different viewpoints, and holding discussions based on those journal entries.

Every item on this list is something the teacher plans and manages. The teacher chooses the text, sets the question, groups the pupils, watches, prompts and corrects. The difference is that the teacher's skill goes into setting up thinking for the pupils, rather than doing the thinking for them.

#### Worked example: turning a teacher-centred lesson into a learner-centred one

Take Mr Akbar's lesson on clean water. Here is how one part of it changes, step by step.

| Step | Teacher-centred version | Learner-centred version | What changed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening | Teacher reads the title aloud | Teacher asks, "Where does your water come from?"; pairs talk for three minutes | Starts from what pupils know |
| Reading | Teacher reads; pupils repeat words | Groups read one section each to answer "What makes water unsafe?" | Pupils read with a purpose |
| Questions | Five questions from the teacher | Each group writes two questions of its own, then answers one from another group | Pupils ask as well as answer |
| Writing | Copy answers from the book | Each pupil writes three lines: "At home we can keep water safe by ..." | Pupils compose, not copy |
| Checking | Teacher marks notebooks after school | Teacher walks around, listens to groups and reads two lines from each pupil | Checking happens during the work |

The same textbook chapter is used. The time is the same. What changed is who does the reading, talking and writing.

#### Correcting a common misconception

A common belief is that **learner-centred means the teacher does nothing**: the teacher stands back and the pupils "discover" everything. This is wrong. In Ms Rubina's lesson she chose the opening question, chose the texts, decided the groups, set the task for each group, watched for pupils who were lost, and stopped the class to explain the word "germs". Minott's list of teacher tasks above shows the same thing: guiding, encouraging and facilitating are active work. A learner-centred lesson with no teacher guidance is not learner-centred; it is unplanned. It usually fails the weakest readers first, because they cannot find their own way into a difficult text.

### Inquiry-based and project-based learning

Two approaches put learner-centred literacy teaching into practice over more than one lesson.

**Inquiry-based learning** is learning that starts from a real question and moves through finding, reading and judging information to reach an answer. The learners do the investigating. **Project-based learning** is learning organised around making a product over several days or weeks, such as a poster, a booklet, a radio script or a class newspaper, for a real audience. The two overlap. Most good projects contain an inquiry, and most inquiries end with something to share.

#### Where the approaches come from

Gasque (2016) traces both approaches to the American educator John Dewey, who argued in the early twentieth century that pupils learn best through real problems connected to their own experience. Dewey criticised a rigid curriculum that ignored learners' interests. According to Gasque (2016), Dewey set four conditions for a project to be truly educational:

1. it grows from the pupils' interest;
2. it is worth doing for its own sake in life;
3. it raises problems that make pupils curious and send them to search for information;
4. it lasts long enough to be done properly, as one connected piece of work and not a string of separate facts.

Gasque (2016) also explains why these approaches matter for literacy. An inquiry project makes pupils search for, read, judge and use information. This is **information literacy**: knowing when you need information, and being able to find it, judge it and use it well. Gasque reports that teaching these skills through projects, with time to reflect on how information was found and used, makes learning more meaningful than teaching them as a separate set of skills.

#### The stages of a literacy inquiry project

Gasque (2016) lists the usual parts of a research project: identifying a problem, setting objectives, searching for information, collecting and analysing data, and drawing conclusions. For a primary or middle-school literacy class, these can be put into seven plain stages.

<Figure id="fig-U5-1" src="/img/figures/efmp-406/unit-05/fig-U5-1.svg" alt="Flowchart of a literacy inquiry project in seven stages, ask, find, read, judge, make, share and reflect, with an example from a Class 5 project on safe water under each stage and an arrow from reflection back to new questions." />

| Stage | What pupils do | Literacy they practise |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Ask | Agree on a question worth answering | Speaking, forming questions |
| 2. Find | Collect texts: textbook, leaflet, newspaper, interview | Locating information |
| 3. Read | Read and take short notes | Comprehension, note-making |
| 4. Judge | Compare sources: who wrote this, and can we trust it? | Critical and information literacy |
| 5. Make | Write and design a product | Composing, editing, layout |
| 6. Share | Present to real readers or listeners | Speaking, writing for an audience |
| 7. Reflect | Discuss what worked and what was hard | Thinking about one's own learning |

Harada and Yoshina, as summarised by Gasque (2016), describe features of inquiry-based learning that a teacher can check for: pupils help decide the direction of the work, learning is social, pupils learn by doing, assessment is continuous, and the learning is authentic, meaning it connects to real life.

#### Worked example: planning a short literacy project

Here is how a teacher plans a two-week project for Class 6 in a school in Sukkur, step by step.

1. **Choose a question from pupils' lives.** "What do the signs and notices in our bazaar tell people, and are they easy to read?"
2. **Check Dewey's four conditions.** Pupils walk past the bazaar daily (interest). Clear notices help shopkeepers and buyers (worth doing). Pupils will need to read, photograph or copy signs and compare them (curiosity and search). Two weeks of short daily sessions are enough (time).
3. **Plan the texts.** Signs copied by hand or photographed on a parent's phone; the textbook chapter on public notices; two printed notices the teacher brings.
4. **Plan the product and audience.** Each group writes and designs one clear notice for a real shop, in Urdu or Sindhi, and presents it to the shopkeeper.
5. **Plan the checking.** Short notes in a project notebook, a checklist for a clear notice (purpose, short words, big letters, correct spelling), and a two-minute group presentation.

Notice that every stage asks pupils to read or write something real. That is what makes this a literacy project and not only an outing.

#### Correcting a second misconception

Some teachers believe that **project work is only for science**: projects mean models, experiments and charts of plants. This belief leaves literacy teachers out. A project is any extended piece of work that answers a real question and ends in a product. Urdu, Sindhi and English lessons can run projects such as a class newspaper, a booklet of family stories collected from grandparents, a radio-style news report, a survey of favourite books with a written report, or a set of safety notices for the school. Gasque (2016) describes projects as a way to organise learning across subjects, and the information skills they build are the reading and writing skills of a language class. A language teacher who never runs a project misses one of the best ways to give pupils real reasons to read and write.

## Activity: Redesign a lesson

Work in groups of three or four. About 35 minutes.

1. Each group takes one ordinary textbook lesson from a Class 4-7 Urdu, Sindhi or English book that a member has used or seen on practicum.
2. Describe how the lesson is usually taught, using the five rows of the worked-example table: opening, reading, questions, writing, checking.
3. Redesign each row to make it learner-centred. For each change, name the feature of learner-centred instruction it uses.
4. Then stretch the lesson into a one-week mini-project. Write a project question and check it against Dewey's four conditions.
5. Swap plans with another group. Each group writes one question the other group's teacher would need to plan for, such as "What will the weakest reader do in stage 3?".

## Summary

Learner-centred literacy instruction starts from what learners already know, keeps them active, gives them some choice and asks them to think about texts. It does not mean the teacher stands back: the teacher plans, guides, groups, watches and explains, and puts their skill into setting up thinking for pupils.

Inquiry-based learning starts from a real question; project-based learning ends in a real product for a real audience. Both grew from Dewey's idea that learning should begin from pupils' experience and real problems. Through stages of asking, finding, reading, judging, making, sharing and reflecting, pupils practise reading, writing, speaking and information literacy for real purposes. Projects belong in language classes, not only in science.

## Try this at your practicum school

Observe one language lesson at your practicum school. Every five minutes, note who is talking (teacher, one pupil, pairs or groups, or nobody) and what pupils are doing with text (listening, reading, copying, writing their own words). At the end, add up your notes. In your journal, write a short paragraph: how learner-centred was this lesson, and which one change would make the biggest difference for the pupils?

## Summative task

Choose one Urdu, Sindhi or English textbook unit for Classes 4-7. Write 400-500 words that:

1. Plan a one- or two-week literacy project based on that unit, with a project question, the texts pupils will read, the product and its audience.
2. Show how your project meets Dewey's four conditions and the seven stages.
3. Analyse one risk in your plan, for example weak readers being left out or the work drifting away from reading and writing, and explain how you would manage it.

*(Create, Analyze)*

**Mini-rubric (10 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong (3-4) | Partial (1-2) | Missing (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Project plan (3 marks) | Clear question, real texts, product and audience that fit the unit | Plan present but one part vague or unrealistic | No usable plan |
| Use of Dewey's conditions and the stages (3 marks) | Each condition and stage is shown in the plan, not only named | Some conditions or stages shown, others only listed | Conditions and stages missing |
| Analysis of a risk (4 marks) | Identifies a real risk, explains why it happens and gives a workable response linked to learner-centred teaching | Names a risk with a general response | No risk analysed |

The risk criterion needs analysis: explain the cause of the risk in your own plan, not a general list of problems with group work.
