# Topic 5.2 - Using ICT and assessing literacy

## A real classroom situation

The classroom scenes, named teachers and pupils, and worked examples in this topic are fictional teaching examples. They are not empirical case studies or official school inspection records.

A private school in Hyderabad receives a multimedia projector. Ms Hina, the Class 5 English teacher, is pleased. For a month she puts every textbook page on the wall as a slide and reads it aloud. Pupils copy from the wall instead of from the book. At the end of the month she gives the usual written test. The marks are no better than before.

In a government school in a village near Badin there is no projector, and the electricity often fails. Mr Sajjad, the Class 5 Sindhi teacher, has one smartphone. He records three pupils reading a short story aloud in September and again in December, and plays both recordings to each pupil. Pupils hear for themselves how much more smoothly they read now. He also uses the phone to record a grandmother telling a folk tale; the class listens, then writes the story down in their own words.

One school had more technology. The other used its technology for literacy. And only one teacher found out what her pupils could actually do as readers. This topic looks at both questions: how to integrate ICT into literacy teaching, and how to assess literacy skills fairly and usefully.

## Explanation

**ICT** (information and communication technology) means devices and services that store, show and share information, such as phones, computers, projectors, radio, television and the internet. In literacy teaching, ICT can bring new texts into the room and give pupils new ways to read, write and listen. Assessment then tells the teacher whether pupils are actually learning to read and write better. This topic treats the two together, because a good use of ICT, like a good lesson, is one whose effect on learning the teacher can see.

### Integration of ICT in literacy teaching

There is a difference between **using** ICT and **integrating** it. Using ICT means a device is present in the lesson. **ICT integration** means the device is chosen because it helps pupils do a specific literacy task better than they could without it, and the task is planned into the lesson. Ms Hina used a projector. Mr Sajjad integrated a phone.

A simple test helps. Ask: *What can pupils now do with text that they could not do before?* If the answer is "nothing, they still copy", the ICT has not been integrated.

#### What teachers need

Hafifah and Sulistyo (2020) surveyed 280 higher education English lecturers in Indonesia about their **ICT literacy**, meaning their own ability to use ICT confidently and well, and about how they used ICT in their teaching. Their findings concern Indonesian higher education, not Pakistani primary or middle schools. We use them cautiously as questions for local planning; pupils, resources and teaching conditions differ, so these findings do not establish what works in Pakistani schools.

- Teachers who had more ICT training had higher ICT literacy, and more frequent ICT use in teaching was associated with higher ICT literacy (Hafifah & Sulistyo, 2020).
- The biggest problem teachers reported was facilities: internet access and equipment, especially in rural areas. Lack of training, lack of institutional support and lack of time came next (Hafifah & Sulistyo, 2020).
- The authors note that technology now takes on some of the teacher's roles, such as providing materials, supporting learning and helping with assessment, so teachers need to be ICT literate themselves (Hafifah & Sulistyo, 2020).

Two lessons follow for a Pakistani classroom. First, a teacher's own confidence grows through regular, small uses of ICT, so it is better to start with one tool used well than to wait for a computer lab. Second, plans must fit the facilities the school really has. A plan that needs the internet in a school with no signal is not a plan.

#### Matching tools to literacy purposes

Low-cost tools can do a great deal if they are matched to a purpose.

| Literacy purpose | A low-cost ICT tool | How it helps |
|---|---|---|
| Hearing fluent reading | Teacher's phone, radio | Pupils hear a model reader, or hear their own reading played back |
| Reading more and wider | Offline e-books on a phone or tablet | A small library without buying many books |
| Writing and editing together | Projector, or a photo of a pupil's work | The whole class can see one text and improve it together |
| Collecting real texts | Phone camera | Photograph signs, notices and labels for reading lessons |
| Listening and retelling | Recorded stories, school radio programmes | Pupils listen, then summarise or retell in writing |
| Critical reading of media | A forwarded message or online news item shown to the class | Pupils check the source and judge whether it is true (see Unit 4) |

#### Worked example: from "using" to "integrating"

Ms Hina wants to fix her projector lessons. Here is how she redesigns one lesson on a textbook story.

1. **Name the literacy goal.** "Pupils will write a short summary of a story in their own words."
2. **Ask what pupils find hard.** They copy whole sentences from the book instead of choosing the main points.
3. **Choose the tool for that difficulty.** She projects one pupil's summary (with permission and no name) on the wall.
4. **Plan what pupils do with it.** The class reads it together and marks which sentences are copied and which are in the pupil's own words. In pairs, pupils rewrite two copied sentences.
5. **Check the effect.** Every pupil writes a summary of a new short story. Ms Hina compares these with the first summaries.

The projector is now doing a job that would be hard without it: letting thirty pupils look at and improve the same piece of writing at the same time. And step 5 builds assessment into the plan.

#### Correcting a common misconception

A common belief is that **ICT integration means showing slides**. Ms Hina's first month shows why this is wrong. Slides of textbook pages change where pupils look, not what they do. Pupils still listen and copy, so their reading and writing do not improve. Gasque (2016) reports a related finding from another study: teachers often thought of information literacy as just ICT skills, and did not teach pupils how to find, judge and use information. Integration means that the technology serves a literacy task: hearing, reading, writing, editing, searching or judging. A radio programme followed by a written retelling is better ICT integration than a hundred slides.

### Assessment and evaluation of literacy skills

**Assessment** is collecting evidence of what a learner knows and can do. **Evaluation** is making a judgement from that evidence, for example about a pupil's progress, a lesson or a whole programme. A teacher assesses when she listens to a pupil read; she evaluates when she decides that the pupil needs more help with long words, or that her new reading programme is working.

Assessment also has two main purposes.

- **Formative assessment** happens during learning, to guide the next step. Mr Sajjad's September recording told him which pupils needed help.
- **Summative assessment** happens at the end of a period of learning, to record what has been achieved, such as an end-of-term examination.

#### Types of assessment, from classroom to examination

The Early Grade Reading Assessment (EGRA) toolkit, a widely used guide for testing children's early reading, places assessments along a line (RTI International, 2016). At one end are **examinations**: long, formal tests given to all pupils to decide grades or promotion. In the middle are **assessment surveys**, which test samples of pupils to see how a whole system is doing. At the other end are **classroom assessments**: short, less formal and low-stakes checks that teachers use to find out what individual pupils know and to improve teaching (RTI International, 2016). Most of a literacy teacher's assessment belongs at the classroom end.

EGRA itself is an oral test given to one child at a time. It checks foundational skills such as identifying letters, reading familiar words, reading a short passage aloud and answering questions about it, and understanding a passage that is read aloud (RTI International, 2016). The toolkit makes two points every literacy teacher should know. First, its tasks can be adapted by teachers as a formative check on pupils' progress. Second, no single test can measure all the skills a pupil needs to read with understanding (RTI International, 2016).

#### Ways to assess literacy

Because no single method sees everything, a teacher combines several.

<Figure id="fig-U5-4" src="/img/figures/efmp-406/unit-05/fig-U5-4.svg" alt="Diagram with one learner's literacy at the centre surrounded by five ways to assess it, observation, reading record, portfolio, rubric and test, inside a ring showing that together they give a fuller picture than any one method." />

| Method | What it shows well | What it can miss |
|---|---|---|
| **Observation** | How a pupil reads, talks and writes during real work | Gives notes, not scores; needs a simple checklist to stay fair |
| **Reading record** (listening to one pupil read aloud and noting errors and self-corrections) | Decoding, fluency and the kinds of mistakes a pupil makes | Comprehension, unless questions are added |
| **Portfolio** (a collection of a pupil's work over time) | Growth across a term; writing for different purposes | Takes time to review; needs clear criteria |
| **Rubric** (a table of criteria and levels) | What "good" looks like for a task, shared with pupils | Only as good as its criteria |
| **Test** (the same questions for all pupils) | Comparison across a class; quick to mark | Talk, process, real-life reading and writing |

#### Worked example: a reading record

A **reading record** is one of the most useful tools a primary literacy teacher has, and it needs no equipment. Mr Sajjad does one like this with a Class 3 pupil, Zainab.

1. He chooses a passage of about 50 words from a book Zainab has not read.
2. Zainab reads aloud. On his own copy, he ticks each word read correctly, writes what she said above words she misread, and marks an "SC" where she corrects herself.
3. He counts: 50 words, 6 errors, 2 self-corrections. She read 44 of 50 words correctly, which is 88%.
4. He asks two questions: "What happened to the goat?" and "Why do you think the boy was afraid?" Zainab answers the first well but not the second.
5. He looks at the pattern: most errors are on long words with the same ending. Her self-corrections show she checks for meaning. She can find facts but not yet infer feelings.

His evaluation: Zainab decodes most words, needs practice with word endings, and needs teaching on reading "between the lines". A written test score would not have shown any of this.

#### Correcting a second misconception

Many teachers and parents believe that **a written test is the only way to assess reading**. A written test does show some things well, and examinations will continue to use them. But reading is also done aloud, in groups, for real purposes and over time. A written test cannot hear a pupil's fluency, see how a pupil works through a hard word, or show growth across a term. It also mixes reading with writing: a pupil who understands a story but writes slowly may score low. The EGRA toolkit itself warns that no single instrument measures all reading skills and that it should complement, not replace, written classroom tests (RTI International, 2016). Good literacy assessment combines methods, as in the diagram above, and uses each for what it shows best.

#### Assessing learner-centred and ICT-supported work

The approaches in this unit change what needs assessing. In a project from Topic 5.1, the teacher assesses the process (notes, judgements about sources, teamwork) as well as the product. Harada and Yoshina, summarised by Gasque (2016), describe assessment in inquiry learning as continuous. In practice this means a project notebook, short observation notes and a rubric shared with pupils at the start. ICT can help too: phone recordings of reading, photos of draft and final posters, and a simple folder of each pupil's work make a low-cost portfolio.

## Activity: Assessment toolkit for one lesson

Work in groups of four. About 35 minutes.

1. Each group is given (or chooses) one learner-centred literacy lesson or mini-project from Topic 5.1.
2. Decide what pupils should be able to do by the end. Write it as two "Pupils can ..." statements.
3. Choose two assessment methods from the table. For each, say what evidence it will give and what it may miss.
4. Write a four-row rubric for the product, with three levels in each row.
5. Add one low-cost ICT tool that will either help pupils learn or help you collect evidence. Apply the test: what can pupils now do that they could not do before?
6. Present your plan to another group, which must find one gap in it.

## Check your understanding

1. Define assessment and evaluation, and give one example of each from a reading lesson. *(Remember)*
2. Explain the difference between using ICT and integrating ICT in a literacy lesson, with one example of each. *(Understand)*
3. Name two problems teachers face in integrating ICT, according to Hafifah and Sulistyo (2020), and suggest one response a Pakistani school could make to each. *(Understand)*
4. A pupil reads 40 words of a 50-word passage correctly. What percentage of words did the pupil read correctly, and what else would you check before deciding how well the pupil reads? *(Apply)*
5. A head teacher says, "We assess reading only through the written paper at the end of term." Write three sentences explaining what this approach misses and what could be added. *(Apply)*

## Summary

ICT supports literacy only when it is integrated: chosen for a specific reading, writing, listening or judging task that pupils could not do as well without it. Low-cost tools such as one phone, a projector, radio and offline e-books can be enough. Teachers' ICT confidence grows through training and regular small uses, and plans must fit the facilities a school actually has.

Assessment collects evidence of what learners can do; evaluation judges from that evidence. Most literacy assessment belongs at the classroom end: low-stakes checks that guide teaching. Because no single method sees everything, a teacher combines observation, reading records, portfolios, rubrics and tests, and assesses the process of projects as well as the product.

- [ ] I can match at least four low-cost ICT tools to literacy purposes in a school with few resources.

## Try this at your practicum school

With your cooperating teacher's permission, do a reading record with two pupils from the same class, using a short passage they have not read. Count the words read correctly, note the kinds of errors and self-corrections, and ask two questions about meaning. Compare the two pupils. In your journal, write what you learned about each pupil that their last written test did not show, and what you would teach each of them next.

## Summative task

You are a Class 5 literacy teacher in a school with one smartphone, a radio and no internet. Write 400-500 words that:

1. Plan one ICT-supported literacy activity for a week, naming the literacy goal and showing how the tool is integrated, not only used.
2. Plan how you will assess the activity, using at least two methods, with one short rubric.
3. Evaluate your own plan: which pupils might it serve less well, what evidence would tell you it was working, and what would you change if it was not?

*(Create, Evaluate)*

**Mini-rubric (10 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong (3-4) | Partial (1-2) | Missing (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| ICT integration (3 marks) | Tool clearly serves a named literacy task that pupils could not do as well without it; fits the school's facilities | Tool is used, but the link to the literacy task is weak | Tool only displays text, or no tool |
| Assessment plan (3 marks) | Two or more suitable methods with a clear rubric matched to the goal | One method, or a rubric not matched to the goal | No assessment plan |
| Evaluation of the plan (4 marks) | Judges strengths and limits with specific reasons, names evidence of success and a realistic change | Gives general strengths or limits | No evaluation |

The evaluation criterion needs a judgement: name who the plan might fail, why, and what evidence would show it. A list of the plan's good points does not meet it.
