# Pedagogical Approaches to Contemporary Literacy - Teacher Notes

These notes support the teacher educator who teaches this unit. They contain no assessment items. The course guide names lecture, discussion, question-answer and brainstorming as teaching strategies, together with group discussions and seminars, role-plays and simulations, case study analysis and reflection journals. This unit is about teaching approaches, so the most useful thing you can do is teach it the way it recommends: let trainees experience a learner-centred lesson and a short inquiry before they analyse them.

## Sequencing

Teach the two topics in order. Topic 5.1 sets out learner-centred instruction and inquiry and project work; Topic 5.2 adds ICT and assessment to those approaches. The guide gives no week allocation for its six units, so plan the time to suit your own timetable.

A workable sequence is:

1. Open with a role-play: two trainees teach the same five-minute textbook extract, one in Mr Akbar's way and one in Ms Rubina's way. The class notes who talks and what pupils do.
2. Use the class notes to introduce the four features of learner-centred instruction and Minott's list of teacher tasks.
3. Run a one-week mini-inquiry with the trainees themselves, for example "What do pupils in our practicum schools read outside school?", moving through the seven stages.
4. Move to Topic 5.2 with a "using or integrating?" sort: give groups ten short descriptions of ICT use and ask them to sort and justify.
5. Model a reading record live with a volunteer reading a passage in a language they read less well, then let pairs practise.
6. Close with the "Redesign a lesson" and "Assessment toolkit" activities combined into one planning seminar.

## Discussion prompt and a likely wrong answer

**Prompt:** "A teacher has 70 pupils in one room and one textbook between three. Can learner-centred literacy teaching work here?"

**Likely wrong answer:** "No. Learner-centred teaching needs small classes, many books and technology." Trainees often think of the approach as a set of materials rather than a way of planning.

**How to work with it:** Ask trainees to list what pupils in that room could do instead of listening and copying: talk in pairs about a question, read one textbook between three with a clear task for each reader, write two lines of their own, listen to a reading on a phone. Lead the group to see that learner-centred teaching changes who does the thinking, and that this is possible with very few resources. Large classes need tighter planning and routines, not a different philosophy.

## Likely misconceptions and how to handle them

- **"Learner-centred means the teacher does nothing."** Ask trainees to list every decision Ms Rubina made in the opening situation. The list is long.
- **"Project work is only for science."** Collect project ideas from language textbooks on the board: newspapers, story collections, surveys, notices. Ask which reading and writing skills each needs.
- **"ICT integration means showing slides."** Use Ms Hina's case and the "what can pupils now do?" test. Ask trainees to rescue one slide lesson by changing what pupils do, not what they look at.
- **"A written test is the only way to assess reading."** After the live reading record, ask: "What did we learn that a written paper would not show?"

Some trainees will worry that learner-centred work "does not finish the syllabus". Acknowledge the pressure. Show that the worked examples use the same textbook chapter and the same time, and that projects can cover several textbook lessons at once.

## Practical work

The guide lists group work, group assignments, individual assignments and presentations. Suggested tasks:

- **Group work:** the "Redesign a lesson" activity, presented as a before-and-after table.
- **Group assignment:** a two-week literacy project plan for a practicum class, with its rubric, displayed for a gallery walk.
- **Individual assignment:** two reading records from the practicum school, with a short analysis of what each pupil should be taught next. Remind trainees to get the cooperating teacher's permission and to keep pupils' names private.
- **Presentation:** a five-minute demonstration of one low-cost ICT activity, explaining what pupils can do with it that they could not do before.
- **Reflection journal:** one entry for each topic, on a lesson observed or taught.

The course project is "Developing an inclusive lesson plan". The Grade 5 activity in the unit assessment (extended task 5) is a good first draft for it. Ask trainees to add how the activity includes pupils with different needs and home languages.
