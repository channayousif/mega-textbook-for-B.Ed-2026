# Pedagogical Approaches to Contemporary Literacy - Assessment and Review

## Unit summary

Learner-centred literacy instruction designs the lesson around what learners need to do in order to learn. It starts from what pupils already know and do with texts, keeps them reading, talking and writing for most of the lesson, gives them some choice, and asks them to think about texts as well as read them. It does not mean the teacher does nothing. The teacher chooses texts and questions, groups pupils, watches, prompts and explains. Minott (2011) lists such tasks for teachers of critical reading: building in time for careful thought and judgement, encouraging pupils to question texts, reading from several points of view, and using journals and discussion.

Inquiry-based learning starts from a real question; project-based learning ends in a product for a real audience. Gasque (2016) traces both to Dewey, who set four conditions for an educational project: it grows from pupils' interest, is worth doing in life, raises problems that send pupils to search for information, and lasts long enough to be done properly. A literacy inquiry moves through seven stages: ask, find, read, judge, make, share and reflect. Along the way pupils practise reading, writing, speaking and information literacy. Projects belong in language classes as much as in science.

ICT helps literacy only when it is integrated: chosen for a specific literacy task that pupils could not do as well without it. Showing textbook pages as slides is using ICT, not integrating it. Low-cost tools, such as one phone, a projector, radio and offline e-books, can serve real literacy purposes. Hafifah and Sulistyo (2020) found that teachers' ICT literacy grows with training and regular use, and that facilities, training, support and time are the main barriers, so plans must fit the school's real conditions.

Assessment collects evidence of what learners can do; evaluation is the judgement made from that evidence. Formative assessment guides the next step; summative assessment records achievement at the end. The EGRA toolkit places assessments on a line from examinations to classroom assessments and warns that no single instrument measures every reading skill (RTI International, 2016). A literacy teacher therefore combines observation, reading records, portfolios, rubrics and tests, and in project work assesses the process as well as the product.

## Summative assessment

Attempt every question before you read the final guidance section. Each MCQ has one best answer. For restricted responses, keep to the length stated. For extended responses, build an argument with evidence, not a list of definitions. All named pupils, teachers and schools below are invented teaching cases.

MCQs 1-5 and RRQs 1-5 address Topic 5.1; MCQs 6-10 and RRQs 6-10 address Topic 5.2. Extended tasks 1-2 address Topic 5.1, tasks 3-4 address Topic 5.2, and task 5 draws on both. Your course teacher decides which tasks to collect and how the course's 60% summative weighting is applied.

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

Choose A, B, C or D. Each item is worth 1 mark.

1. Which statement best describes learner-centred literacy instruction? *(Remember)*
   - A) Pupils decide what to learn and the teacher waits for questions.
   - B) The lesson is designed around what pupils need to do to learn, starting from what they know.
   - C) The teacher explains each text fully before pupils read it.
   - D) Pupils work alone on worksheets at their own speed.
2. According to Gasque (2016), which of these is one of Dewey's conditions for an educational project? *(Remember)*
   - A) It must use a computer or the internet.
   - B) It must be finished within one lesson.
   - C) It raises problems that make pupils curious and send them to search for information.
   - D) It must be marked by an external examiner.
3. In a literacy inquiry project, which stage comes directly after pupils read and take notes? *(Understand)*
   - A) Ask a question
   - B) Find sources
   - C) Judge the sources
   - D) Share with an audience
4. Ms Farzana's Class 6 pupils choose a question about litter in their mohalla, read a municipal notice and a newspaper report, and write a letter to the local councillor. Which approach does this mainly show? *(Apply)*
   - A) Project-based learning, because pupils make a product for a real audience
   - B) Teacher-centred instruction, because the teacher set the topic area
   - C) Rote learning, because pupils used printed texts
   - D) Summative assessment, because pupils wrote a letter
5. A head teacher tells a new teacher, "In learner-centred lessons you can relax, because the children do the work." Which response best corrects this? *(Apply)*
   - A) It is true for older pupils but not for younger ones.
   - B) It is true, but only in science lessons.
   - C) The teacher plans, groups, guides and checks, so the teacher's work changes but does not disappear.
   - D) Learner-centred lessons should be avoided in Pakistani schools.
6. What is the main difference between using ICT and integrating ICT in a literacy lesson? *(Understand)*
   - A) Integrating ICT means the lesson uses more than one device.
   - B) Integrating ICT means the tool is chosen for a literacy task that pupils could not do as well without it.
   - C) Integrating ICT means the internet is available.
   - D) Integrating ICT means the teacher shows every page as a slide.
7. Hafifah and Sulistyo (2020) found that the problem teachers reported most often in integrating ICT was: *(Remember)*
   - A) pupils' lack of interest
   - B) facilities, such as internet access and equipment
   - C) parents' objections
   - D) the cost of textbooks
8. Which of these is an example of evaluation rather than assessment? *(Understand)*
   - A) Listening to a pupil read a passage aloud
   - B) Collecting pupils' stories in a folder
   - C) Deciding from a term's evidence that a pupil needs extra help with word endings
   - D) Asking a pupil two questions about a story
9. A pupil reads 45 words of a 50-word passage correctly. What percentage of words did the pupil read accurately? *(Apply)*
   - A) 45%
   - B) 80%
   - C) 90%
   - D) 95%
10. A teacher wants to see how pupils' writing grows across a whole term. Which method suits this purpose best? *(Apply)*
    - A) A single end-of-term written test
    - B) A portfolio of each pupil's writing collected over the term
    - C) One reading record in the first week
    - D) A multiple-choice quiz on grammar rules

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

Answer each in the length stated.

1. In three or four sentences, explain two features of learner-centred literacy instruction, with a Pakistani classroom example for each. *(Understand)*
2. List three tasks Minott (2011) gives for teachers in a classroom where pupils read critically, and explain in one sentence why they show that a learner-centred teacher is active. *(Understand)*
3. In three or four sentences, explain the difference between inquiry-based and project-based learning, and how they overlap. *(Understand)*
4. A Class 7 English teacher says, "Projects are for the science teacher; my job is grammar and comprehension." In three or four sentences, explain why this view is mistaken, with one example of a literacy project. *(Analyze)*
5. A teacher's "project" asks pupils to copy ten facts about the Indus River from the textbook onto a chart. Using Dewey's four conditions, analyse in four or five sentences why this is a weak project and suggest one improvement. *(Analyze)*
6. In three sentences, explain why a teacher's own ICT literacy matters for integrating ICT, using the findings of Hafifah and Sulistyo (2020). *(Understand)*
7. A school near Thatta has no internet but each teacher has a phone. Describe two literacy activities that integrate the phone, and say what pupils can do in each that they could not do without it. *(Apply)*
8. In three or four sentences, explain the difference between formative and summative assessment, with one literacy example of each. *(Understand)*
9. Mr Sajjad's reading record for Bilal shows 50 words, 9 errors, most of them on long words, and 3 self-corrections. Bilal answers a factual question but not an inference question. In four or five sentences, analyse what this record shows and what Bilal should be taught next. *(Analyze)*
10. A parent says, "My daughter scored well on the written English paper, so she reads well." In three or four sentences, explain what the written paper may not show about her reading and what other evidence a teacher could collect. *(Analyze)*

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

Write a developed answer of about 300-400 words for each.

1. **Two lessons, one chapter.** Compare Mr Akbar's teacher-centred lesson and Ms Rubina's learner-centred lesson on clean water (Topic 5.1). Analyse what pupils do in each, what each teacher does, and what each lesson is likely to teach about reading and writing. Then judge which lesson better serves weaker readers, and justify your judgement. *(Evaluate)*
2. **A project for a language class.** Plan a two-week literacy project for a Class 6 Urdu or Sindhi class on a question from pupils' own community. Show how it meets Dewey's four conditions and the seven stages of inquiry, and explain how you would keep the weakest readers and writers fully involved. *(Create)*
3. **The new projector.** A school receives a projector, and teachers begin showing every textbook page as a slide. Evaluate this practice as ICT integration, using ideas and evidence from Topic 5.2, and propose a better plan that uses the projector for two specific literacy tasks. *(Create)*
4. **Is the written paper enough?** A school assesses reading only through a written paper at the end of each term. Evaluate this policy, using what you know about types of assessment, the EGRA toolkit's warnings and the strengths and limits of different methods. Recommend a combination of methods for one class and justify it. *(Evaluate)*
5. **Design a Grade 5 literacy activity.** Design a short literacy activity (one to three lessons) for a Grade 5 class in a school you know, using one approach from this unit: learner-centred instruction, inquiry or project-based learning, or ICT integration. State the literacy goal, describe what pupils and the teacher do, and plan how you will assess it with at least two methods, including a short rubric. Then justify why your chosen approach and assessment suit these pupils better than an alternative. *(Create)*

## Answers and marking guidance

Use this section only after attempting the questions. It explains the reasoning behind each answer so that you can check and improve your own. Your teacher may use different marks for formal assessment.

### MCQ answer key

1. **B.** Learner-centred instruction is designed around what pupils need to do to learn and starts from what they know. A describes an unplanned lesson, C a teacher-centred one, and D individual seatwork, which need not be learner-centred.
2. **C.** Dewey's third condition is that a project raises problems that arouse curiosity and require a search for information. None of his conditions requires a computer, a single lesson or an outside examiner.
3. **C.** After reading and taking notes, pupils judge the sources before they make a product. Ask and find come earlier; share comes later.
4. **A.** Pupils investigate a real question and make a product (a letter) for a real audience (the councillor). The teacher setting the topic area does not make the work teacher-centred (B); using print is not rote learning (C); and the letter is a product, not in itself a summative assessment (D).
5. **C.** In learner-centred lessons the teacher's work shifts to planning, grouping, guiding and checking. The approach is not limited by age (A) or subject (B), and there is no reason to avoid it (D).
6. **B.** Integration means the tool serves a literacy task that pupils could not do as well without it. Number of devices (A) and internet (C) do not decide it, and D describes using, not integrating.
7. **B.** Facilities, internet access and equipment were the most reported problem, followed by training, institutional support and time.
8. **C.** Evaluation is a judgement made from evidence. A, B and D are ways of collecting evidence, that is, assessment.
9. **C.** 45 ÷ 50 × 100 = 90%.
10. **B.** A portfolio collects work across the term and so shows growth. A and D give one snapshot; C is at the start only and assesses reading, not writing.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. **Model:** Learner-centred instruction starts from the learner: a teacher opening a lesson on water by asking where pupils' families get their water builds on home knowledge. Learners are active: pupils read a section in groups and write their own questions instead of copying answers. **Marks (4):** 1 for each feature correctly explained (2); 1 for each fitting Pakistani example (2). Other valid features: choice, thinking about texts.
2. **Model:** Any three of: building time for pupils to think carefully about and judge a text; guiding pupils' judgements with care; encouraging pupils to question what they read; reading texts from several points of view; using journals that compare viewpoints; leading discussions based on journal entries. Each is planned and managed by the teacher, so the teacher is active even when pupils do most of the talking. **Marks (4):** 1 for each task (3); 1 for the explanation.
3. **Model:** Inquiry-based learning starts from a real question and moves through finding, reading and judging information to an answer. Project-based learning is organised around making a product for a real audience over days or weeks. They overlap because most projects contain an inquiry and most inquiries end in a product to share. **Marks (3):** 1 for each definition (2); 1 for the overlap.
4. **Model:** The view is mistaken because a project is any extended piece of work that answers a real question and ends in a product; it is not limited to science. Projects give real reasons to read, write, speak and listen, which are the skills of an English class. For example, pupils could survey classmates' favourite books and write a short report and recommendations for the school library. **Marks (4):** 1 for rejecting the view with a reason; 2 for linking projects to literacy skills; 1 for a fitting example.
5. **Model:** The task does not grow from pupils' interest, because the teacher chose the facts. It is not worth doing in life, since a copied chart has no reader. It raises no problem, so pupils need not search for or judge information. It is one short copying task, not a connected piece of work. A better version would ask "Why does the Indus matter to our town?", send pupils to read the textbook, a newspaper item and an interview with an elder, and end with a short talk or booklet for another class. **Marks (5):** 1 for each condition analysed (4); 1 for a workable improvement.
6. **Model:** Teachers can only integrate tools they can use confidently. Hafifah and Sulistyo (2020) found that teachers with more ICT training had higher ICT literacy and that more frequent use made teachers more ICT literate. So training and regular small uses build the confidence teachers need to plan ICT into literacy lessons. **Marks (3):** 1 for the reason; 1 for accurate use of the findings; 1 for the link to classroom planning.
7. **Model:** (a) Record pupils reading aloud at the start and end of a term and play back both recordings; pupils hear their own progress, which they cannot do without a recording. (b) Photograph shop signs and notices in the bazaar and use them as reading texts; pupils read real local texts that could not be carried into class. Other valid uses: recording an elder's story for retelling in writing; offline e-books for group reading. **Marks (4):** 1 for each activity (2); 1 for each explanation of what the phone adds (2).
8. **Model:** Formative assessment happens during learning to guide the next step, for example a reading record in October that shows a pupil needs help with long words. Summative assessment happens at the end of a period to record achievement, for example the end-of-term reading paper. The difference is purpose and timing, not the type of task. **Marks (3):** 1 for each definition with an example (2); 1 for naming purpose and timing.
9. **Model:** Bilal read 41 of 50 words correctly, which is 82%. Most errors are on long words, so he needs practice breaking long words into parts and reading common endings. His three self-corrections show he notices when meaning goes wrong, which is a strength to build on. He can find facts but not yet infer, so he needs teaching on reading "between the lines", for example asking why a character acted as they did. **Marks (5):** 1 for the accuracy calculation; 1 for the decoding pattern; 1 for interpreting self-corrections; 1 for the comprehension pattern; 1 for a sensible next step.
10. **Model:** A written paper shows what she can do under test conditions in writing, but not her fluency, how she handles hard words, whether she reads for her own purposes, or how she reads other kinds of text. It also mixes reading with writing. A teacher could add a reading record, observation of her reading in class, and a portfolio of her reading responses. **Marks (4):** 1 for what the paper does show; 2 for two things it may miss; 1 for suitable extra evidence.

### ERQ rubrics

Each task uses four criteria worth **0-3 marks each**, totalling 12. Across criteria, 3 means accurate, developed and supported; 2 means mostly accurate with an incomplete link; 1 means a relevant point with little explanation; 0 means absent, wrong or unrelated. Plain, short sentences can earn full marks; a fluent answer with weak reasoning cannot.

**ERQ 1: Two lessons, one chapter**

| Criterion | 3 marks | 2 marks | 1 mark | 0 marks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy about the two approaches | Describes teacher-centred and learner-centred features accurately, including the teacher's active role | Describes both with minor gaps | Describes one approach only | Confuses the approaches |
| Analysis of the two lessons (**Analyze**) | Compares what pupils and teachers do in each lesson and what each teaches about reading and writing, with evidence from the case | Compares pupils' or teachers' actions, not both | Retells the lessons without comparison | No analysis |
| Judgement on weaker readers (**Evaluate**) | Reaches a clear judgement and supports it with specific reasons, including risks in both lessons | Judgement with general reasons | Judgement without reasons | No judgement |
| Use of sources | Uses Minott's teacher tasks or another unit idea accurately to support the argument | Mentions a source with a loose link | Names a source without use | None |
