# Topic 7.1 - The Muslim teacher's professional ethics

## A real classroom situation

Bilal is a B.Ed trainee on practicum at a government boys' high school in Larkana. In his first week he keeps a notebook of things that puzzle him.

On Monday the Class 8 English teacher arrives twenty minutes late, writes an exercise on the board and tells the boys to copy it while he drinks tea in the staffroom. On Tuesday a father visits the staffroom with a box of sweets and an envelope. He asks the science teacher to "take special care" of his son's practical marks. On Wednesday the head teacher announces a new duty roster in the assembly. Three senior teachers have no duties at all; the two youngest teachers have duties every day. Nobody was asked. On Thursday a teacher gives the class a fine talk on truthfulness. Ten minutes later his phone rings and he tells a pupil, "Say I am not here."

On Friday Bilal asks his cooperating teacher about the notebook. She shrugs. "That is professional business, beta. Islamiat is for the Islamiat period. Don't mix religion with the job."

Bilal is not sure. Every entry in his notebook seems to be about honesty, fairness or trust. Are those only "professional business", or does Islam have something to say about how a teacher does the job? That is the question of this topic.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What does a Muslim teacher owe, and to whom? What does Islam expect of the people who lead a school or a class? And why do pupils often learn more from what a teacher does than from what the teacher says? A short decision process at the end brings the three answers together.

### Ethical responsibilities of teachers

#### What professional ethics means

**Ethics** is the study of right and wrong conduct. **Professional ethics** is the set of standards of right conduct for the members of a profession while they do their work: how a doctor treats patients, how a judge hears a case, how a teacher treats pupils. Most professions write some of these standards down as rules or a code of conduct. A school, too, has rules about attendance, marking and the care of pupils.

In Unit 3 you studied *akhlaq*, the moral character Islam builds: truthfulness, justice, honesty and patience. Professional ethics, for a Muslim teacher, is *akhlaq* at work. It is the same character, shown in the classroom, the staffroom and the examination hall.

#### Teaching as an amanah

The key idea in this topic is ***amanah***. *Amanah* means a **trust**: something placed in a person's care, which they must look after and hand back as it is due. The word also names the quality of being trustworthy. A person with *amanah* can be relied on.

The Qur'an uses the idea in several ways.

- It describes a great Trust that the heavens, the earth and the mountains declined to bear, but that human beings took on (33:72). Being a responsible moral agent is itself a trust.
- It commands believers "to render trusts to whom they are due", and to judge between people with justice (4:58).
- It describes successful believers as those who "are to their trusts and their promises attentive" (23:8).
- It warns believers not to betray Allah, the Messenger or their trusts (8:27).

A teaching post is a trust in all these senses. Parents hand over their children for six hours a day. The government or the school owner pays a salary for work done. Pupils trust their teacher to teach them truthfully and to mark their work fairly. Each of these is an *amanah*, and Allah asks that each be rendered "to whom it is due".

The Qur'an also gives a short test for a good employee. In the story of Prophet Musa (peace be upon him), a young woman tells her father: "the best one you can hire is the strong and the trustworthy" (28:26). Two qualities are joined: **strength**, which for a teacher means competence (knowing the subject and how to teach it), and **trustworthiness**, which means honesty in using that competence. A clever teacher who cheats, and an honest teacher who does not prepare, each have only half of what the verse asks for.

#### Correcting a common misconception

Bilal's cooperating teacher holds a common view: **professional ethics is separate from religious ethics**. On this view Islam governs prayer and fasting, while punctuality, honest marking and fair duty rosters are just "the job".

Unit 1 showed why this view does not fit Islam. Islam is a *din*, a complete code of life, and its moral teaching reaches every area of life. A hadith makes the link to work very plain. The Prophet (peace be upon him) said that the signs of a hypocrite are three: when he speaks he lies, when he promises he breaks his promise, and when he is trusted he betrays the trust (*Sahih al-Bukhari* 33). None of these three is a ritual. All three are failures that happen every day at work: a false excuse for absence, a promised lesson never given, an exam paper shown to a favourite pupil.

Two things change when professional ethics is seen as part of a Muslim's faith.

1. **The motive.** A school rule is kept when an inspector is watching. An *amanah* is kept because a Muslim will answer to Allah for it, as you studied under Akhirat in Unit 1.
2. **The reach.** A written code covers what its writers thought of. The values of truthfulness, justice and trust also cover the cases no rule has named.

This does not make school rules unimportant. A Muslim teacher keeps the lawful rules of the school as part of keeping promises: "fulfill [every] commitment" (17:34). Islamic ethics adds to the rules; it does not replace them.

Research with Muslim teachers points the same way. Tambak and Sukenti (2020) surveyed 397 teachers of Islamic subjects in *madrasah aliyah* (Islamic senior secondary schools) in Riau Province, Indonesia. They report that teachers' Islamic behaviour, such as wisdom, courage, self-restraint and justice, was linked to stronger professional competence, and that it contributed more than the other factor they measured. They also argue that when teaching is understood as worship, it becomes more than a routine task or a way to earn money (Tambak & Sukenti, 2020). This is one survey in one province of another country, and its correlations concern teacher competence rather than a directly measured effect on pupils, so it supports the argument; it does not prove it for Pakistan.

#### The four circles of a teacher's amanah

To whom does a teacher owe this trust? The concept map below groups the answer into four circles.

<Figure id="fig-U7-1" src="/img/figures/giss-401/unit-07/fig-U7-1.svg" alt="Concept map with the teacher's amanah at the centre, linked to four groups the teacher is responsible to: learners, colleagues, parents and the institution, each with two examples of the duties owed." />

| Circle | What the teacher owes | A Qur'anic or hadith anchor |
|---|---|---|
| **Learners** | Prepared lessons, the full period taught, fair marking, equal respect for every pupil, care for their safety and dignity | Justice even towards people one dislikes (5:8); woe to those who give short measure (83:1-3) |
| **Colleagues** | Cooperation, sharing the workload fairly, checking a rumour before repeating it, no running down of others | Investigate news before acting on it (49:6) |
| **Parents** | Honest reports of progress, keeping family matters private, refusing gifts that buy favour | Render trusts to whom they are due (4:58) |
| **The institution** | Punctuality, care of school property and funds, keeping lawful rules and the hours paid for | Fulfil every commitment (17:34) |

Look at the verse about short measure. It was revealed about traders who take full measure for themselves and give less to others (83:1-3). A teacher who takes a full salary but teaches half a period, or who gives a pupil fewer marks than the work deserves, is giving short measure in the same way.

#### Worked example: tracing Bilal's notebook

Here is how a careful trainee would analyse the first two entries in Bilal's notebook against the four circles.

| Notebook entry | Which circle is wronged | Which value is broken | What keeping the amanah would look like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Teacher arrives late and sets copying | Learners (lost teaching) and the institution (paid hours not given) | Trust; fulfilling commitments (17:34) | Arrive on time; if delayed, inform the head and leave real work |
| Father offers sweets and an envelope for marks | Learners (other pupils marked unfairly), parents (this parent is misled) | Justice (5:8); rendering trusts (4:58) | Decline politely, explain that marks follow the work, and tell the head |

Notice the method. First name **who** is affected, then name **which value** is at stake, then describe the **action** that keeps the trust. You will use the same three steps in the activity.

### Islamic values in educational leadership

#### Who leads in a school?

When we hear "leader" we think of the head teacher. The head does carry the largest share. But in Islam leadership is wider. The Prophet (peace be upon him) said: "All of you are guardians and responsible for your wards" (*Sahih al-Bukhari* 893). The same hadith names the ruler, the head of a family and a servant looking after his master's goods, and says each is responsible for what is in his care. A class teacher is the guardian of forty pupils. A subject coordinator guides other teachers. A senior teacher leads by example in the staffroom. Each one is a leader for what is in their care, and each will be asked about it.

**Educational leadership** means guiding the people and work of a school, or of a part of it, towards good learning. This section looks at the Islamic values that should guide it.

#### Five values for leading a school

1. **Justice (*adl*).** Allah "orders justice and good conduct" and forbids oppression (16:90). Believers must stand firm for justice "even if it be against yourselves or parents and relatives" (4:135). For a leader, justice means the same rule for everyone: duties, resources and praise shared on fair grounds, not on friendship or seniority.
2. **Consultation (*shura*).** The Qur'an praises believers "whose affair is [determined by] consultation among themselves" (42:38). It tells the Prophet (peace be upon him) himself to "consult them in the matter" (3:159). A leader who consults hears the people who will carry out the decision, and gets a better decision.
3. **Trustworthiness and competence.** The test of "the strong and the trustworthy" (28:26) applies most of all to those who hold authority. A head must know the work and must use school funds, posts and marks honestly.
4. **Mercy and gentleness.** The same verse 3:159 says that if the Prophet (peace be upon him) had been "harsh in heart", people would have left him. Gentleness is not weakness. It is what keeps a team together.
5. **Accountability.** "Responsible for your wards" (*Sahih al-Bukhari* 893) means that a leader answers for the people in their care: to Allah, and also to the people themselves. A leader who admits a mistake and puts it right shows this value.

#### Correcting a common misconception

A second common belief is that **leadership means authority, not service**. On this view the head teacher's job is to give orders and the staff's job is to obey. Bilal's head teacher acted this way: he made the roster alone and announced it.

The Islamic picture is different. The Arabic word in the hadith, *ra'i*, is the word for a shepherd. A shepherd does have authority over the flock, but the authority exists for the flock's sake: to feed it, guard it and bring it home safely. Authority in Islam is a trust given for service, and the person who holds it is asked about the people in their care (*Sahih al-Bukhari* 893). The Qur'an joins leadership to consultation and gentleness (3:159), not to command alone. A head teacher who serves still decides. The difference is in how the decision is reached and whose good it serves.

#### Worked example: two ways to make the duty roster

Return to Bilal's school. Here are two ways the head teacher could have made the same roster.

| Step | The roster as it was made | The roster made with Islamic values |
|---|---|---|
| Gathering views | No one asked | The head asks every teacher for their free periods and any family constraint (consultation, 42:38) |
| Sharing the load | Senior teachers excused, juniors loaded | Duties shared by a clear rule, such as the number of free periods; exceptions only for a stated reason (justice, 4:135) |
| Hard cases | None considered | A teacher with a pressing family circumstance may get a lighter week under a stated rule; the family detail stays private unless the teacher agrees to share it (mercy, 3:159) |
| Announcing | Read out in assembly as an order | Shown to staff first, with a week to point out errors (accountability) |
| Review | Never | Reviewed after one month and changed if it is unfair (accountability, 893) |

Both rosters end with a decision by the head. The second one is still the head's decision, but it is made as a guardian, not as a boss. It is also more likely to work, because the teachers who must carry it out helped to shape it.

### Role modelling in education

#### The Prophet as the pattern

A **role model** is a person whose conduct others copy. The Qur'an presents the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) as the first role model for Muslims: "There has certainly been for you in the Messenger of Allāh an excellent pattern" (33:21). It praises his "great moral character" (68:4). The Prophet taught his companions mostly by living among them. They learned how to pray, how to trade, how to treat their families and how to forgive by watching him.

**Role modelling** in education means teaching values by showing them in one's own conduct. Every teacher is a role model, whether they intend to be or not. Pupils watch how the teacher speaks to the peon, whether the teacher keeps time, how the teacher reacts to a wrong answer and what the teacher does when no one is checking.

#### Correcting a common misconception

A third common belief is that **pupils learn from what teachers say, not what they do**. On this view, a good lecture on honesty is enough to teach honesty.

The Qur'an rejects this view firmly. "O you who have believed, why do you say what you do not do?" (61:2). The next verse calls this "greatly hateful in the sight of Allāh" (61:3). The Qur'an also asks the scholars of an earlier community: "Do you order righteousness of the people and forget yourselves?" (2:44).

Think again about Bilal's Thursday entry. The teacher's talk on truthfulness lasted ten minutes. The lie on the phone lasted five seconds. Which lesson did the pupil who carried the message learn? Most likely the second: that truthfulness is something adults talk about, not something they do. When word and action disagree, pupils often take the lesson from the action.

Tambak and Sukenti (2020) make a related point about professional teachers. They cite earlier studies reporting that a teacher's exemplary behaviour supports better teaching, and argue that teaching should aim to change pupils' bad habits, not only pass on information. A teacher can only do this by showing the good habit first.

#### What pupils see

Role modelling often happens in small, daily actions as much as in speeches. A teacher models:

- **truthfulness** by admitting "I do not know; I will find out and tell you tomorrow", and then doing it;
- **justice** by marking the head teacher's nephew by the same rubric as everyone else;
- **respect** by greeting the *chowkidar* and the cleaner as warmly as the visiting officer;
- **self-control** by staying calm when a pupil is rude, and dealing with it without insults;
- **keeping promises** by returning marked work on the day promised.

#### Worked example: rewriting a moment

Here is one ordinary moment from a Class 6 mathematics lesson, written two ways.

| | What the teacher does | What the pupils learn |
|---|---|---|
| **First version** | A pupil points out an error in the teacher's working on the board. The teacher says, "Don't be clever. Sit down," and quietly corrects it later. | Admitting a mistake is shameful; adults hide their errors; asking questions is risky. |
| **Second version** | The teacher looks again and says, "You are right, thank you. I made a mistake here. Let us correct it together." | Truthfulness includes admitting one's own mistakes; questions are welcome; the teacher is fair. |

The content of the lesson is the same in both versions. The values the pupils take home are opposite. That is role modelling at work.

### Putting it together: an ethical decision in school

Real situations rarely come labelled. A teacher needs a simple process for working out what to do when a duty is not clear. The flowchart below sets out five steps.

<Figure id="fig-U7-3" src="/img/figures/giss-401/unit-07/fig-U7-3.svg" alt="Flowchart of an ethical decision in school in five steps: identify the issue and who is affected, consult principles from the Qur'an, Sunnah and school rules, consult people such as the head or a trusted colleague, act, then reflect, with a loop back to the start if the action did not keep the trust." />

1. **Identify** the issue: what is happening, and whose trust is at stake (learners, colleagues, parents, the institution)?
2. **Consult principles**: which values from the Qur'an and Sunnah apply, such as *amanah*, justice and truthfulness? What do the school's lawful rules say?
3. **Consult people**: whom should I ask before acting? This is *shura* on a small scale: the head teacher, a trusted senior colleague, sometimes the parent.
4. **Act**: do what keeps the trust, kindly and openly.
5. **Reflect**: did the action keep the trust? What would I do differently next time? If the problem remains, go round the steps again.

#### Worked example: the envelope in the staffroom

Suppose the father with the envelope comes to Bilal instead of the science teacher.

1. **Identify.** A parent is offering money for higher practical marks. The other pupils' right to fair marking is at stake, and so is the school's trust in its examiners.
2. **Consult principles.** Marks are a trust to be given "to whom they are due" (4:58). Justice must not follow personal inclination (4:135). Taking the envelope would betray a trust (*Sahih al-Bukhari* 33). The school's rules forbid it too.
3. **Consult people.** Bilal is a trainee, so he tells his cooperating teacher and, with her, the head teacher, so that the school can respond as one.
4. **Act.** Bilal declines politely: "Uncle, I cannot accept this. Your son's marks will follow his work. If you want, I can show you how he can prepare for the practical." He offers real help in place of the favour.
5. **Reflect.** In his journal Bilal notes that he felt awkward, but that offering help kept the father's dignity. Next time he will speak to the head before the parent leaves, not after.

The process does not make every decision easy. It makes sure that the teacher has asked the right questions before acting.

## Activity: A staffroom code of trust

Work in groups of four. About 40 minutes.

1. Each member takes one circle of the teacher's *amanah*: learners, colleagues, parents or the institution.
2. On your own, for ten minutes, write two commitments for your circle that a teacher at a Pakistani school could keep every week. Each commitment must name the value it serves and one Qur'anic verse or hadith from this topic. For example: "I will return marked copies within three school days, because a promise is a trust (17:34)."
3. Read your commitments to the group. The group checks each one with two questions: is it specific enough to check, and does the verse or hadith really support it?
4. As a group, choose one dilemma from Bilal's notebook that you have not yet analysed (the duty roster or the phone call). Take it through the five steps of the ethical decision flowchart. One member plays the teacher, one plays the head teacher, and two observe and note which step was hardest.
5. Share your eight commitments and the hardest step with the class in a three-minute report.

## Check your understanding

1. State the meaning of *amanah* and give two Qur'anic references that use the idea. *(Remember)*
2. Name the four circles of a teacher's *amanah* and give one duty a teacher owes to each. *(Remember)*
3. Explain what the two qualities in 28:26, "the strong and the trustworthy", mean for a teacher. *(Understand)*
4. Explain, using the hadith "All of you are guardians" (*Sahih al-Bukhari* 893), why a class teacher is also a leader. *(Understand)*
5. A head teacher decides the examination timetable alone and announces it on the notice board. Name two Islamic values of leadership this decision does not show, and say how the head could show them next time. *(Apply)*
6. A teacher tells pupils to keep the classroom clean but leaves tea cups and paper on her own table every day. Use 61:2-3 to explain what the pupils are likely to learn, and suggest one change she could make. *(Apply)*

## Summary

A teaching post is an *amanah*, a trust from Allah, parents, pupils and the school. The Qur'an commands that trusts be rendered to whom they are due (4:58), and asks for workers who are both strong and trustworthy (28:26): competent and honest. For a Muslim teacher, professional ethics is not separate from religious ethics. It is *akhlaq* at work, kept because the teacher will answer to Allah, and it reaches four circles: learners, colleagues, parents and the institution.

Every teacher is a leader of what is in their care, and will be asked about it (*Sahih al-Bukhari* 893). Islamic leadership is a trust held for service. It is guided by justice, consultation, trustworthiness, mercy and accountability, and the Qur'an joins it to gentleness and *shura* (3:159; 42:38).

Pupils often learn more from what a teacher does than from what the teacher says, especially when the two disagree. The Prophet (peace be upon him) is the excellent pattern (33:21), and the Qur'an condemns saying what one does not do (61:2-3). When a duty is unclear, a teacher can identify the issue, consult principles, consult people, act and reflect.

## Try this at your practicum school

During one practicum week, keep a notebook like Bilal's. Note three moments where a teacher's conduct (not their words) taught pupils something about honesty, fairness, respect or keeping promises. At least one should be a good example. For each moment, write which circle of *amanah* it touched and what the pupils probably learned. Then note one moment from your own teaching that week when your words and your actions did not match, and what you will change. Do not name the teachers in your journal.

## Summative task

Read this case and write 400-500 words in response.

*At a government girls' middle school in Mirpurkhas, the head teacher must choose one teacher to attend a fully paid five-day training course in Karachi. She chooses her cousin, who teaches Class 6 Urdu, without telling the staff. Another teacher, who teaches Class 8 science and has asked for this training for two years, hears about the choice from the peon. In the staffroom she says loudly that the head "cannot be trusted with anything". Several pupils hear her.*

Your response should:

1. Analyse the case using the four circles of *amanah*: whose trust is affected, and how? Consider both the head teacher and the science teacher.
2. Evaluate the head teacher's decision against at least three Islamic values of educational leadership, with a Qur'anic verse or hadith for each.
3. Evaluate the science teacher's reaction as a role model for the pupils who heard her.
4. Use the five-step ethical decision process to propose what each of the two teachers should do now.

*(Analyze, Evaluate)*

**Mini-rubric (12 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Analysis of trust (3 marks) | Identifies the affected circles for both teachers and explains how each trust was affected (3) | Names circles without explaining the effect, or covers only one teacher (1-2) | No analysis |
| Evaluation of the decision (4 marks) | Judges the decision against three values, each supported by an accurate verse or hadith and by evidence from the case (3-4) | Names values with weak links to the case or to the sources (1-2) | No evaluation |
| Evaluation of the reaction (2 marks) | Judges the reaction as role modelling, with a reason drawn from this topic (2) | A judgement without a reason (1) | Not addressed |
| Proposal (3 marks) | Realistic next steps for both teachers that follow the five steps and keep each trust (3) | Steps for one teacher only, or steps that skip consulting people (1-2) | No proposal |

The analysis and evaluation criteria ask you to show how the evidence in the case leads to your judgement. Quoting more verses does not earn more marks unless each one is applied to the case.
