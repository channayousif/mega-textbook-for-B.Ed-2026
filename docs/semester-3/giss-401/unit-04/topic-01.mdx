# Topic 4.1 - The Islamic social system

## A real classroom situation

At a government primary school in Badin, a trainee helps prepare a reading afternoon. One child lives with her grandmother, another with both parents, and a third with an uncle while his parents work elsewhere. A colleague suggests inviting only fathers because they are the people who make decisions. Another proposes giving every family the same written notice, although some carers cannot read it. The trainee must decide what respect for families and fairness require in this situation.

## Explanation

### Family system in Islam

A **family system** is the network of relationships through which people provide care, belonging and responsibilities across generations. It includes spouses, parents, children and relatives. Studying this system means asking what people owe one another, not merely listing the people who live in one house. A household may contain several generations. A child may also be cared for by a grandparent or another guardian. A teacher should recognise that actual care arrangement without treating the child as less worthy.

The Qur'an describes marriage through tranquillity, affection and mercy in [30:21](https://quran.com/30/21). These ideas help us understand the quality of a relationship, rather than seeing family authority as permission to dominate. Affection involves concern for another person's wellbeing. Mercy matters when a person is weak, tired or unable to return a favour. This verse supports a relationship of care; it does not provide a school admissions procedure or settle every question of family law.

Care across generations has different forms. Parents and guardians care for dependent children. Children learn respect, gratitude and helpful conduct. Adult children may have responsibilities towards older parents. The Qur'an directs respectful treatment of parents in [17:23–24](https://quran.com/17/23-24). In a classroom example, a pupil can practise speaking kindly to a grandparent and helping with an age-appropriate household task. The teacher should not turn that example into an expectation that a young child must carry an adult's responsibilities.

An extended family can provide support, but its size does not prove that care is fair. Equally, living in a small household does not prove selfishness. Judge the relationship through what happens: is a dependent person heard, supported and treated with dignity? Do not ask pupils to compare which family is more religious. For school work, use invented situations so that learning about responsibility does not expose a child's private circumstances.

The Qur'an's discussion of infant care in [2:233](https://quran.com/2/233) includes provision, protection from harm, and consultation between parents. This is a specific context, not a complete description of all family duties. It nevertheless helps a trainee notice that care involves material support and joint consideration, not just kind feelings. A parent who purchases books but never listens to a child's difficulty has met one need while another remains. A caring response considers the child and the adults' real capacities together.

**Worked example: the reading afternoon.** First identify the task: the school needs to communicate with those responsible for each child's care. Next identify the people: some are fathers, some mothers, some other guardians. Then identify the need: every carer should receive and understand the invitation. A suitable plan invites the recorded carer, offers a spoken explanation where needed, and provides a way to ask questions. The plan supports relationships around the child. It does not require the teacher to decide who should control a household.

Notice the boundary of the example. A reading invitation is an ordinary school decision. A teacher should not use this unit to rule on custody, marriage, inheritance or an allegation of harm. Those questions require the appropriate qualified people and procedures. Here the trainee's professional task is narrower: recognise the person providing care, communicate respectfully and avoid making a child answer for adult circumstances.

### Rights and responsibilities

A **right** is a claim to treatment or provision that another person ought to respect. A **responsibility** is something a person ought to do. **Huquq ul-Ibad** means the rights of people. In this teaching vocabulary, **Huquq Allah** refers to duties owed to Allah, such as worship. The distinction helps organise learning. It does not divide life into two sealed areas: caring for people can also be part of obedience to Allah.

[Qur'an 4:36](https://quran.com/4/36) places worship alongside kindness towards parents, relatives, orphans, people in need, neighbours and travellers. The list reaches beyond a person's own household. A trainee can therefore ask both a close question, "Who needs care in this family?", and a wider one, "Who might our school overlook?" These are applications of the passage, not claims that the verse supplies a modern school policy.

Rights and duties are connected, but they do not form a simple exchange. A child does not lose the right to care because he has behaved badly. An elderly person who cannot help with household work still deserves respectful treatment. A responsibility should be considered in relation to capacity. A six-year-old can return a shared pencil; the adult is responsible for arranging the classroom so that resources are available and children are safe.

A common misconception says, **"Rights in Islam belong only to men."** This fails to recognise women as persons with claims and responsibilities. For example, [Qur'an 4:7](https://quran.com/4/7) names both men and women as having shares in inheritance. We use that reference to challenge a denial of women's rights, not to calculate shares here. Detailed family entitlements require careful study beyond this lesson. Equal dignity does not mean every legal responsibility has an identical form.

A teacher can respect this distinction without making a classroom discussion into a legal debate. If a girl is told her opinion cannot matter because she is a girl, invite her reason and assess it by the same learning criteria used for boys. If a boy struggles to explain his view, support him too. School participation should turn on the learner's needs and the task, not on an assumption that only one sex can think or contribute responsibly.

**Worked example: a borrowed book.** A pupil takes a classmate's book without asking and later apologises during a lesson on good conduct. Identify the affected right: the owner should control use of the book and receive it back. Identify the responsibility: the borrower should return it and address any damage. Identify the adult's role: help resolve the incident fairly, without humiliating either pupil. A spoken apology matters, but it does not return the book. This example distinguishes a feeling of regret from action that repairs another person's loss.

That reasoning also protects against favouritism. Suppose the borrower is the teacher's nephew. The relationship does not make the owner's claim disappear. [Qur'an 4:135](https://quran.com/4/135) calls for justice even where oneself, parents or relatives are involved, and rejects wealth or poverty as grounds for biased testimony. Applied to the example, the teacher should hear the facts rather than favour a relative or assume that the poorer child must be truthful. Fairness requires evidence as well as a good intention.

When discussing responsibility, name who can act. "Society must be kind" is too broad to guide the next step. "The borrower returns the book today, and the teacher checks that it is usable" identifies an action and an accountable person. If the book is missing, the adult should establish what happened before deciding on repair. Assigning responsibility is different from attaching a permanent label such as "dishonest child".

### Social justice and equality

**Equality** here means equal human dignity and fair consideration, rather than higher worth based on family, wealth, ethnicity or sex. [Qur'an 49:13](https://quran.com/49/13) addresses human diversity and points to righteousness, rather than ancestry, as the basis of honour before Allah. A teacher cannot measure another person's standing before Allah. In school, the useful application is to avoid treating a surname, language or social position as evidence of greater human worth.

**Social justice** concerns fair relationships and arrangements across a community: whose needs are recognised, who receives opportunities, and how unfair treatment is corrected. <Glossary term="Equity">Equity</Glossary>, in the sense used here, means responding to relevant differences in need so that people can participate fairly. A shared rule can support equality, but a rule applied without attention to barriers can also exclude. These are classroom applications of justice, not a claim that a modern educational term occurs in the verse.

The misconception **"Social justice means everyone gets the same"** confuses identical provision with fair access. Imagine that a school gives each pupil one ordinary-print worksheet. Most can read it, but a pupil with low vision cannot. Providing larger print changes the material while preserving the learning goal. The reason for the difference is an access need. Giving a larger prize to a head teacher's relative would also be different treatment, but without a relevant justification. Difference alone proves neither fairness nor unfairness.

[Qur'an 5:8](https://quran.com/5/8) requires justice even towards people one dislikes. This provides a demanding test for a classroom decision. Would you defend the same rule if the affected child were someone you found difficult? Would you hear a complaint from a family with whom the school recently disagreed? Justice is more than sympathy towards people we already like. It asks us to examine whether our reasons survive a change in the person's identity.

**Worked example: six reading-support places.** Twelve pupils apply for six places in a short reading group. Selecting only the children of regular donors would privilege wealth. Drawing names without considering the purpose would be impartial in one sense, but might miss those needing support most. A defensible procedure checks a recent reading task, selects those with the greatest relevant need, and explains the criteria privately to carers. If equally eligible pupils remain, the school can rotate places or use another stated fair method. It should also review progress rather than treat the first allocation as permanent.

Follow the reasoning, not just the outcome. The purpose is reading support, so reading need is relevant. A donation is not evidence of that need. The decision affects children, so avoid publicly ranking their families. A review date matters because needs can change. A response to a complaint matters because a fair-looking procedure may still use mistaken information. This is how an abstract principle becomes a decision that another person can question and improve.

Consider a language barrier too. A notice written only in a language a carer cannot read may be formally identical for all, yet practically inaccessible. A spoken explanation or a trusted translation can make the information usable. The school need not guess a family's ability from its address or clothing. It can offer everyone the option of an explanation. That combines a common standard of respect with flexible support.

A short decision record helps connect rights and social justice. State the purpose, the people affected, the relevant need, the proposed action and the reason. Add how someone can raise a concern. Such a record cannot guarantee justice, but it makes hidden assumptions easier to inspect. The test is not whether the decision sounds generous. Ask whether it protects a real claim, uses relevant evidence and leaves a way to correct an error.

## Activity: A fair invitation and support plan

**Work in groups of four. Allow about 20 minutes.** Use the fictional reading-afternoon case; do not describe classmates' families. Assign a reader, a rights checker, a practical planner and a questioner. Without opening the text, first explain the difference between a right and a responsibility to one another. Then reopen the explanation to check your definitions.

Your group must agree on an invitation process and a rule for six reading-support places. The reader states the purpose. The rights checker identifies whose claim might be missed. The planner proposes a workable action. The questioner tests whether the same reason would be accepted for an unfamiliar family. Record the initial proposal, one objection and a revision caused by that objection. Each member must explain one part of the final reasoning.

Exchange plans with another group. Ask whether a difference in treatment serves a relevant need or favours a relationship. Where you disagree, identify the missing fact rather than voting on which family deserves respect. Finish with a joint sentence explaining what your plan protects and one fact that could require its revision.

## Check your understanding

1. Define Huquq ul-Ibad and give one responsibility towards a person outside the family. *(Remember)*
2. Explain, in three sentences, why equal dignity does not require identical learning materials. Include the difference between an access need and favouritism. *(Understand)*
3. A school invites fathers only to a discussion about reading support. Some pupils' recorded carers are mothers or grandparents. Propose two changes that respect family care and make communication usable. Explain each change. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Family relationships involve care, mercy and responsibilities suited to people's circumstances and capacities. Rights of people extend beyond family membership and are not withdrawn because someone is weak or unpopular. Social justice combines equal dignity with attention to relevant needs, reasons and opportunities to correct unfair decisions.

- [ ] I can cite a passage and distinguish what it says from my school application.

## Try this at your practicum school

During one visit, examine a routine invitation or classroom resource arrangement. Record its purpose and one way it helps participation. Suggest one small improvement for a possible access barrier. Use no names, family details or income information. Discuss the proposal with your cooperating teacher as an idea for improvement; do not promise a change on the school's behalf.

## Summative task

Write 350–450 words analyzing this fictional decision. A school sends invitations only to fathers and gives reading-support places first to donor families. The head teacher says this respects the family system and treats everyone by one rule. Identify the claims affected, explain two weaknesses in the reasons, and propose a fairer procedure. Use at least two Qur'anic references from the explanation and distinguish your applications from the passages themselves.

| Criterion | 0 points | 1 point | 2 points |
|---|---|---|---|
| Family and rights | No relevant claim identified | One claim explained | Care arrangements and rights connected to responsible action |
| Analysis of the decision | Repeats the decision without testing it | Identifies one relevant weakness | Explains why both exclusions lack a relevant justification |
| Source use | No relevant reference | Relevant reference with unclear link | Two references used with applications clearly identified |
| Alternative procedure | No workable proposal | Suggestion without reasons or correction route | Workable criteria, access support and a way to correct errors |

A strong response explains why the proposed procedure is fairer. Simply replacing one preferred group with another does not resolve the problem.
