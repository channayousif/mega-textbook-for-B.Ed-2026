# Topic 5.2 - The political and administrative system of Islam

## A real classroom situation

During an observation visit to a government girls high school in Mirpurkhas, Sindh, a trainee teacher attends a staff meeting where significant friction emerges. The newly appointed headmistress announces a revised schedule for midterm examinations, setting major papers on dates that coincide exactly with the religious festival of Diwali. When a senior Hindu science teacher respectfully points out that more than fifteen percent of the school's pupils and several non-teaching staff observe Diwali and would face painful academic penalties or family conflict, the headmistress dismisses the objection: "In a public school, we follow the provincial term calendar. Administration cannot consult every faction before setting dates, and minority students must simply adjust to the institutional schedule."

The decision triggers widespread distress across the school community. Several parents visit the administration block feeling alienated and excluded, while teacher morale drops sharply. During the afternoon staff tea, an experienced teacher quietly remarks to the trainee: "True Islamic governance is never about imposing the arbitrary will of an administrator. In Islam, leadership is a trust (*amanah*), decisions require mutual consultation (*shura*), and the rights of non-Muslim citizens are protected by sacred covenants."

The trainee recognizes that this administrative conflict illustrates a profound educational need. She resolves to develop a teaching plan examining the political and administrative systems of Islam. She wants future educators to understand that Islamic governance is rooted in divine accountability, where consultation (*Shura*) eliminates autocratic arrogance, where justice (*Adl*) applies impartially to rulers and citizens alike, and where the rights of religious minorities are guaranteed by Islamic jurisprudence and the Constitution of Pakistan.

## Explanation

Political theory in secular traditions often views the state as the product of a social contract where human sovereigns possess supreme legislative authority. In Islamic political thought, governance is grounded in an entirely distinct theological foundation: the doctrine of **Hakimiyyah** (the ultimate sovereignty of Allah).

Because sovereignty belongs exclusively to the Creator, human rulers do not possess absolute, unchecked authority to legislate according to personal whims or majoritarian prejudices. Instead, political leadership is an exercise in human vicegerency (**Khilafat**) and a sacred trust (**Amanah**). A ruler, headteacher, or civil administrator is merely an executive trustee bound by higher moral and constitutional principles: upholding justice, consulting the community, protecting the vulnerable, and managing public resources with transparent integrity. Abdel Salam (2015) notes that Islamic political ethics establishes a constitutional framework that combines divine moral imperatives with participatory civic responsibility.

### Concept of Shura

The defining institutional mechanism of Islamic governance and administrative decision-making is **Shura** (mutual, institutional consultation). Far from being an optional courtesy or an informal recommendation that leaders may ignore at will, Shura is an obligatory constitutional imperative command in Islamic law.

#### Scriptural foundations of Shura

The Holy Qur'an establishes consultation as an essential characteristic of faithful community life alongside worship and financial responsibility. In Surah Ash-Shura, Allah describes the righteous believers as people whose affairs are decided by consultation among themselves and who spend from what He has provided (42:38).

Remarkably, the Qur'an commands the Prophet Muhammad himself - despite receiving divine revelation and possessing incomparable spiritual wisdom - to pardon his companions, ask forgiveness for them, and consult them in the matter (3:159).

If the Messenger of Allah was commanded to consult his followers, no subsequent political ruler, government minister, headteacher, or classroom educator can legitimately claim an autocratic right to impose unilateral decisions without consulting those affected.

#### Institutional function and participants in Shura

In classical Islamic jurisprudence, consultation operates through institutional councils comprising representatives of the broader community and qualified specialists known as **Ahl al-Hall wal-Aqd** (literally, "those who untie and bind" - people of recognized wisdom, legal expertise, professional competence, and moral integrity).

Abdel Salam (2015) highlights that Shura serves multiple crucial governance functions:
1. **Preventing Executive Despotism (Istibdad, rule by an unchecked executive):** By requiring leaders to deliberate with councils, Shura dismantles authoritarian impulses and ensures that policy reflects collective wisdom rather than personal vanity.
2. **Utilizing Specialized Knowledge:** Consultative governance brings technical, legal, and operational expertise into administrative decisions, ensuring policies are realistic, effective, and grounded in empirical reality.
3. **Fostering Civic Ownership and Social Cohesion:** When citizens and institutional staff participate meaningfully in deliberation, they develop psychological ownership over community decisions, transforming passive compliance into active cooperation.

#### Worked example: Implementing Shura in school management

To translate these political principles into educational administration, consider how a school headteacher can resolve a complex policy challenge through consultative governance:

*Scenario:* A high school faces an urgent problem with campus littering and damaged classroom desks. 
- *Autocratic approach (Non-Shura):* The headteacher unilaterally issues an administrative decree banning all snacks from campus and fining the entire student body five hundred rupees. Result: Student resentment, vandalism increases, and parents lodge complaints with the district education officer.
- *Consultative approach (Shura in practice):* 
  1. The headteacher convenes a consultative council comprising two senior teachers, two student council representatives from Grades 9 and 10, the school custodian, and two parent representatives from the school management committee (SMC).
  2. The headteacher presents the challenge transparently: desk repair costs exceed the school maintenance budget, and plastic litter is blocking drainage channels.
  3. The council deliberates collaboratively: students suggest placing recycling bins near the playground and appointing rotating pupil hygiene monitors; parents propose a volunteer Saturday repair workshop where local carpenters guide students in mending broken desks; the custodian requests specific daily bin-emptying routines.
  4. The headteacher synthesizes the recommendations into an agreed action plan, thanking participants for their constructive input.
  5. *Pedagogical outcome:* By practicing Shura, the headteacher upholds Islamic administrative ethics, models democratic participation, and secures authentic student commitment to campus care.

#### Correcting the misconception about Shura and democracy

**Misconception: "Shura is identical to Western majority voting, where majorities can vote on anything."**
A widespread point of confusion in modern political discourse is whether Shura is simply another word for parliamentary democracy, or whether the two systems are incompatible.

Scholars emphasize that while Shura and democratic governance share essential procedural mechanisms - such as representative deliberation, freedom of expression, institutional checks and balances, and the rejection of hereditary tyranny - they rest on distinct philosophical foundations:
- In a majoritarian democracy, the majority's decision is formally authoritative. Some constitutional systems, however, place limits on what a majority may decide by protecting individual rights and minorities; the comparison is between institutional designs, not a claim that all secular systems permit anything.
- In the Islamic framework, divine sovereignty (**Hakimiyyah**) establishes immutable moral boundaries. No parliament, council, or majority vote possesses the authority to overturn fundamental revealed commands: interest cannot be made halal, justice cannot be suspended, and the human rights of minorities cannot be voted away.

Where Shura operates dynamically is in the expansive domain of **ijtihad** (independent reasoning within Islamic law) and **maslahah mursalah** (public interest policies, meaning government action for the common good where the texts are silent): determining operational budgets, designing educational curricula, planning transport networks, building hospitals, and selecting competent administrative leaders. In these practical areas, consultation, empirical evidence, and collective consensus guide decision-making.

### Justice and governance

The legitimacy of political authority in Islam is measured neither by the military power of the state nor by the royal lineage of its rulers, but by its uncompromising commitment to **Adl** (impartial justice).

#### Adl as the supreme constitutional duty

In Islamic political thought, justice is not merely one administrative virtue among others; it is a central purpose for which divine scriptures were revealed and prophetic messengers were sent (Qur'an 57:25). The Holy Qur'an commands political leaders and judges to render trusts to their rightful owners and to judge between people with justice (4:58).

The Qur'an demands that justice transcend personal emotions, ethnic loyalties, tribal ties, and even religious differences. In Surah Al-Ma'idah, believers are commanded to stand firm as witnesses in justice and not to let the hatred of a people prevent them from being just (5:8).

#### Rule of law and judicial independence

A cornerstone of the Islamic political system is the absolute **rule of law**. No individual - regardless of whether they hold the office of head of state, military commander, wealthy landowner, or senior school administrator - is above the law or immune from judicial scrutiny.

In classical Islamic administration, the judiciary (**Qada**) was recognized as an independent branch of governance. A judge (*Qadi*) derived their authority from divine law, empowering them to issue binding verdicts against the reigning Khalifah. 

A traditional account from the caliphate of Ali ibn Abi Talib illustrates this independence. The report says that the Caliph found his coat of mail in the possession of a Christian citizen in Kufah and took the matter to court before Qadi Shurayh rather than using his executive power to seize it. When the Caliph produced his son and his servant as witnesses, the judge ruled that a son's testimony on behalf of his father was inadmissible, and because no independent witnesses remained, he dismissed the case. The account adds that the citizen, astonished that the supreme ruler was required to appear in court on equal terms with an ordinary minority citizen, embraced Islam and returned the armor. Classical historians report the story; the unit uses it as an illustration of the principle of judicial independence, not as a verified court record.

#### Institutional accountability (Muhasabah)

A legitimate Islamic administration is characterized by institutional transparency and **Muhasabah** (public accountability). Public office is a public trust, and administrators are answerable to both God and the citizenry for their conduct and stewardship of public funds (*Bayt al-Mal*).

Traditional histories report that Umar ibn al-Khattab set high standards of administrative oversight. Provincial governors were required to submit an inventory of their private wealth before taking office and upon leaving office, and any unexplained increase was confiscated for the public treasury (*Bayt al-Mal*, the public treasury). In public assemblies in Madinah, citizens are reported to have questioned the Caliph directly. In one well-known report, a citizen interrupted the Caliph's sermon to ask where he had obtained enough Yemeni cloth for a long garment, since the standard public distribution was insufficient for a tall man; the assembly allowed the sermon to continue only after the Caliph's son confirmed that he had given his own share to his father. These reports are recorded in classical histories; the unit uses them as illustrations of public accountability, not as verified administrative records.

In contemporary educational institutions, this principle of accountability requires school administrators to maintain transparent accounts, open examination marking records to objective review, provide clear justifications for staff assignments, and establish dignified avenues for students and teachers to question administrative decisions without fear of victimization.

### Rights of minorities

A foundational test of any political and moral system is how it treats religious and cultural minorities who hold different beliefs from the governing majority. In Islamic jurisprudence, the protection of religious minorities (**huquq al-aqalliyyat**) is a sacred, inviolable legal covenant.

#### The jurisprudence of Dhimmah and civic equality

Non-Muslim citizens residing in an Islamic state are designated in classical jurisprudence as **Ahl al-Dhimmah** (people of the covenant of protection) or **Mu'ahidun** (people of treaty). Far from being second-class residents, their life, honor, property, and freedom of worship are protected by the full legal power of the state.

The primary legal principle governing minority status is absolute freedom of religious conscience, explicitly established in the Qur'an:

> "There is no compulsion in religion." (Qur'an 2:256, Saheeh International)

Islamic law strictly forbids forcing any non-Muslim to convert, compelling them to participate in Islamic worship rituals, or restricting their internal communal affairs. Non-Muslim communities enjoy internal legal autonomy: their personal family laws (marriage, divorce, inheritance) and internal religious practices are governed by their own religious authorities and courts.

Furthermore, the Holy Qur'an teaches that without the mutual restraint of people, monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques would have been demolished (22:40). The verse names monasteries, churches, and synagogues *before* mosques, showing that the protection of religious sanctuaries is a divine duty incumbent upon legitimate political authority.

The Prophet Muhammad delivered an uncompromising warning against violating the rights of non-Muslim citizens: whoever is cruel to a non-Muslim subject, burdens them beyond their capacity, or takes their property without consent will face his adversary on the Day of Resurrection (*Sunan Abi Dawud* 3052).

#### Historical precedent: The Charter of Madinah (Mithaq al-Madinah)

The foundational constitutional precedent for minority rights in Islam is the **Charter of Madinah** (*Mithaq al-Madinah*), promulgated by Prophet Muhammad in 622 CE upon establishing the first Islamic state. Classical historians describe it as an early written constitutional document, and some legal historians have called it the world's first written constitution; the unit presents it as a reported historical precedent rather than a fully verified text. The charter is reported to have established a multi-religious civic commonwealth:
- It declared that the Muslim believers and the Jewish tribes of Madinah formed one civic community (*ummah wahidah*).
- It guaranteed freedom of religion for the Jewish tribes.
- It established equal civil rights, shared defense responsibilities against external aggression, and mutual support in times of hardship.
- It guaranteed equal access to justice, prohibiting tribal favoritism or legal discrimination based on religious identity.

#### Constitutional protections in Pakistan (1973 Constitution)

In Pakistan, these classical Islamic principles are reflected in the **1973 Constitution of the Islamic Republic of Pakistan**:
1. **Article 20 (Freedom to Profess and Practice Religion):** Subject to law, public order and morality, guarantees that every citizen has the right to profess, practise and propagate their religion, and every religious denomination has the right to establish, maintain and manage its religious institutions.
2. **Article 22 (Educational Safeguards):** Protects students in educational institutions from being required to receive religious instruction or take part in the ceremonies or worship of a religion other than their own. This clause forbids religious coercion in instruction and worship. It does not by itself settle examination scheduling, which is an administrative inclusion policy question.
3. **Article 25 (Equality of Citizens):** Declares that all citizens are equal before the law and entitled to equal protection of law (25(1)).
4. **Article 36 (Protection of Minorities):** A Principle of Policy rather than an individually enforceable fundamental right, it directs the State to safeguard the legitimate rights and interests of minorities, including their due representation in the Federal and Provincial services.

#### Correcting the misconception about minorities in an Islamic state

**Misconception: "Religious minorities have no rights in an Islamic state and must accept secondary status."**
A widespread and dangerous misconception suggests that an Islamic state is an intolerant theocracy where non-Muslims are deprived of civil liberties, barred from public employment, and forced to submit to majoritarian cultural domination.

As shown by scriptural evidence, classical jurisprudence, and Pakistani constitutional law, this misconception is misleading. Islamic jurisprudence has long recognized the autonomous legal standing and religious freedom of minority communities. In educational institutions across Pakistan, respecting minority rights is both a constitutional duty and an Islamic moral obligation. Non-Muslim students must never face discrimination in admissions, grading, seating, cafeteria access, or extracurricular participation. Educators must model the Quranic principle of justice (*Adl*), ensuring that schools are sanctuaries of safety, dignity, and mutual respect for every child.

## Activity: Establishing a consultative school policy on inclusive scheduling

A trainee teacher discovers that the proposed sports week falls during the Hindu festival of Holi, while the parent-teacher conference is scheduled on a Christian holy day. When the trainee raises the issue, a committee member states: "Our school calendar is crowded. We cannot change administrative dates for a handful of non-Muslim students." The committee's authority is limited to the internal school calendar and co-curricular schedule; it cannot change public examination dates set by the board, and pupils' religious and family information must be kept confidential.

1. In your pair, critique the committee member's statement using **Article 20** (freedom to profess and practice religion) and **Article 25** (equality before the law) of the Constitution of Pakistan and the Islamic principle of **Adl (impartial justice)**.

3. Propose two concrete administrative guidelines that the school should adopt to guarantee that examination and co-curricular schedules respect the religious holidays of all enrolled students.

## Summary

The political and administrative system of Islam establishes governance as a sacred trust (*Amanah*) rooted in the supreme sovereignty of Allah (*Hakimiyyah*) and human vicegerency (*Khilafat*). Political authorities and institutional leaders do not hold arbitrary monarchical power; they are executive servants bound by higher moral law to administer public affairs through collective consultation (**Shura**), impartial justice (**Adl**), and rigorous transparency (**Muhasabah**).

The Islamic framework rejects authoritarian dictatorship and majoritarian tyranny, insisting that the rule of law binds rulers and citizens equally without executive immunity. Furthermore, Islamic jurisprudence establishes an enduring charter of minority rights (**huquq al-aqalliyyat**), guaranteeing absolute freedom of conscience (Qur'an 2:256), communal legal autonomy, and the protection of places of worship (22:40). In Pakistan, these principles are reinforced by Articles 20, 22, 25, and 36 of the 1973 Constitution. For professional educators, these governance principles provide an indispensable foundation for democratic school leadership, consultative problem-solving, and the creation of inclusive, equitable classrooms.
