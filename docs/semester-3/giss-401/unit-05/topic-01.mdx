# Topic 5.1 - The economic system of Islam

## A real classroom situation

During an eight-week teaching practicum at a government higher secondary school in Khairpur, Sindh, a trainee teacher notices an ongoing ethical issue involving the school canteen. The canteen contractor has a monopoly on campus and sells expired biscuits, unhygienic fried snacks, and watered-down fruit drinks at prices twenty percent higher than the local bazaar. When several Grade 10 students complain that they have no choice but to spend their daily bus fare on sub-standard food, the contractor replies dismissively: "I paid a high lease to the school committee. Business is business, and whatever profit I make is halal because you hand over the money voluntarily." 

Later that week, the trainee learns that two junior lab assistants and an office peon have taken informal cash advances from a local money lender to pay for urgent medical treatment. The lender deducts fifteen percent interest each month directly from their salaries, trapping the employees in perpetual debt and forcing them to seek irregular side work during school hours.

When the trainee discusses these observations with the senior Islamiat teacher, the senior educator remarks that many people mistakenly believe Islam regulates only personal prayers and dietary rules, leaving market transactions to unrestrained self-interest. The trainee decides to design a learning cycle on the Islamic economic system. She wants her prospective secondary school learners to understand that Islamic economics is grounded in divine stewardship (*amanah*), where lawful (*halal*) earning excludes monopolistic exploitation, where wealth must circulate through *Zakat* to support the vulnerable, and where the prohibition of interest (*riba*) protects human dignity from predatory debt.

## Explanation

In secular economics, economic behaviour is frequently analyzed through the lens of scarcity and individual utility maximization: human beings are viewed as self-interested actors seeking to maximize material consumption with minimal regard for spiritual outcomes. In contrast, the Islamic economic system integrates material enterprise within an overarching moral framework. Economic activity is an integral branch of human vicegerency (*khilafat*) upon earth.

The foundational principle of Islamic economics is that absolute ownership of all natural resources, capital, and wealth belongs to Allah Almighty alone:

> "To Allāh belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth." (Qur'an 2:284)

Human beings hold property not as sovereign absolute owners who may squander or hoard resources at will, but as appointed trustees (*mustakhlafin*). Private property and legitimate enterprise are recognized and vigorously protected in Islamic jurisprudence, but they are bounded by social responsibility, ethical production, and the imperative of communal equity. Rahman and Siradjuddin (2020) emphasize that Islamic economics balances individual initiative with collective social welfare, ensuring that markets serve human flourishing rather than capital concentration.

### Concept of halal and haram

The boundaries of economic activity in Islam are defined by the twin concepts of **halal** (lawful, permissible, wholesome) and **haram** (prohibited, corrupt, harmful). While these terms apply across all aspects of human conduct, their application to commerce and livelihood (*al-kasb al-halal*) forms the bedrock of Islamic commercial law (*Fiqh al-Muamalat*).

#### The scope of lawful and prohibited transactions

Earning a livelihood through lawful productive work is praised in Islamic tradition as an honorable form of worship. The Holy Qur'an commands believers to enjoy the good and wholesome provisions of the earth while remaining mindful of ethical boundaries:

> "O mankind, eat from whatever is on earth [that is] lawful and good and do not follow the footsteps of Satan. Indeed, he is to you a clear enemy." (Qur'an 2:168)

In Islamic commercial ethics, an economic activity is lawful only when both the object of trade and the method of transaction conform to divine standards. Halal earnings require honest effort, transparency, equitable bargaining, and the production of genuine social utility. Conversely, transactions are categorized as haram when they involve deceit, injustice, coercion, or the exploitation of human vulnerability. 

The Holy Qur'an explicitly forbids the unjust appropriation of wealth:

> "And do not consume one another's wealth unjustly or send it [in bribery] to the rulers in order that [they might aid] you [to] consume a portion of the wealth of the people in sin, while you know [it is unlawful]." (Qur'an 2:188)

Islamic jurisprudence identifies several major categories of prohibited economic practices:
1. **Ghash and Tadlis (Fraud and Deceit):** Concealing defects in merchandise, misrepresenting weight or quality, tampering with measurement scales, or providing misleading descriptions of goods and services.
2. **Ihtikar (Monopolistic Hoarding):** Withholding essential commodities (such as grain, fuel, or medicines) from the open market to create artificial shortages and drive up prices at the expense of struggling citizens.
3. **Gharar (Excessive Ambiguity and Speculation):** Contracts characterized by hazardous uncertainty, where essential terms (such as delivery, quantity, or price) are indeterminate, leading to gambling-like disputes.
4. **Maysir and Qimar (Gambling):** Gaining wealth through chance rather than productive labor or legitimate commercial value creation.
5. **Bribery (Rishwah):** Offering or accepting illicit payments to subvert justice, obtain undue commercial advantages, or misappropriate public property.

#### Worked example: Evaluating institutional commercial ethics

To help prospective teachers identify these principles in educational administration, consider this practical institutional scenario:

*Scenario:* A district education office allocates a government grant of 500,000 Pakistani Rupees to a high school for science laboratory supplies. The procurement committee faces three supplier proposals:
- *Supplier A:* Offers certified equipment at market rates with a transparent, itemized warranty.
- *Supplier B:* Offers low-grade uncertified glassware at full price and quietly offers the headteacher a ten percent private "consultancy fee" in cash.
- *Supplier C:* Bids fifty percent below cost to eliminate local competitors, with an undisclosed contract clause requiring the school to buy all future consumable chemicals exclusively from Supplier C at triple the market price.

*Islamic analysis:*
1. *Supplier B's proposal* violates the prohibition against bribery (*rishwah*) and unjust consumption of public wealth (Qur'an 2:188). Accepting a private kickback betrays the public trust (*amanah*) and diverts state education funds into private hands.
2. *Supplier C's proposal* incorporates deceptive predatory pricing and contractual ambiguity (*gharar*), using artificial manipulation to capture a monopoly (*ihtikar*) and exploit the school in future cycles.
3. *Supplier A's proposal* fulfills the conditions of mutual consent in honest trade (Qur'an 4:29): the transaction is transparent, the goods possess genuine utility, and neither party exploits structural advantages to extract unjust gains.

#### Correcting the misconception about halal and haram

**Misconception: "Halal and haram apply only to food and personal slaughtering rules."**
In popular discourse across Pakistan, many citizens treat "halal" as an adjective reserved almost exclusively for meat slaughtered according to Islamic rites (*dhabihah*) and dietary abstinence from pork or alcohol. Consequently, individuals may scrupulously inspect food labels while engaging in financial fraud, tax evasion, adulteration of goods, charging illegal commissions, or drawing government salaries for hours they never worked.

In classical Islamic jurisprudence, this division is entirely invalid. Earning through dishonest commercial transactions or corrupt administration renders the resulting income haram, regardless of how the money is subsequently spent. Early Islamic scholars noted that eating food purchased with embezzled money or interest-based profits impairs spiritual integrity as directly as eating prohibited meat. The Prophet Muhammad warned that a person who travels on pilgrimage and calls out to Allah with hands raised to the sky will find their supplication unanswered if their food, drink, clothing, and livelihood are sustained by haram wealth (*Sahih Muslim* 1015). For an educator, maintaining halal livelihood requires total professional integrity: punctual attendance, impartial assessment, transparent fee collection, and honest reporting.

### Zakat and charity

While halal enterprise governs the creation of wealth, the Islamic economic system institutes systematic mechanisms to govern the distribution of wealth. Foremost among these mechanisms is **Zakat**, the third practical pillar of Islam, reinforced by voluntary benevolence (**Sadaqah**).

#### The institutional nature of Zakat

Zakat is not an optional act of personal charity or an arbitrary gift bestowed by a wealthy patron upon a beggar. In Islamic law, Zakat is a mandatory, institutional financial obligation (*ibadah maliyyah*) levied upon surplus capital. The Holy Qur'an defines Zakat as a recognized, enforceable right (*haqq*) belonging to the destitute:

> "And in their properties was [given] the right of the petitioner and the unasked [poor]." (Qur'an 51:19)

By designating support for vulnerable groups as an inherent divine right, Islam preserves the psychological dignity of recipients. The poor do not receive Zakat as an act of humiliating patronage; rather, the wealthy fulfill an overdue legal trust that purifies their remaining wealth.

#### Calculation, thresholds, and the eight beneficiary categories

Zakat is levied at a statutory rate upon surplus assets that have been held in a person's continuous possession for one full lunar year (known as the **hawl**). To protect vulnerable low-income households from unjust taxation, Zakat applies only when net assets exceed a specified minimum threshold called the **nisab**:
- *Gold Nisab:* Equivalent to 87.48 grams (approximately 7.5 tolas) of gold.
- *Silver Nisab:* Equivalent to 612.36 grams (approximately 52.5 tolas) of silver, traditionally used as the baseline for evaluating savings, liquid cash, commercial merchandise, and financial securities.

For standard commercial inventory, gold, silver, bank savings, and liquid investments, the annual rate of Zakat is exactly **2.5%** of the net qualifying balance. Agricultural output is subject to **Ushr**, a distinct rate levied at harvest time: 10% for crops watered naturally by rainfall or river inundation, and 5% for crops irrigated by artificial mechanical systems requiring capital expenditure.

Surah At-Tawbah specifies the exact, exclusive categories of lawful Zakat recipients:

> "Zakāh expenditures are only for the poor and for the needy and for those employed to collect it and for bringing hearts together and for freeing captives and for those in debt and for the cause of Allāh and for the [stranded] traveler - an obligation [imposed] by Allāh. And Allāh is Knowing and Wise." (Qur'an 9:60)

These eight categories address distinct dimensions of socioeconomic vulnerability:
1. *Al-Fuqara (The Destitute Poor):* Individuals who possess no wealth or earning power to secure basic necessities.
2. *Al-Masakin (The Needy):* Hardworking individuals whose income falls below the minimum subsistence level required for family survival.
3. *Al-Amilina Alayha (Zakat Administrators):* Authorized personnel who collect, verify, audit, and distribute Zakat funds.
4. *Muallafat al-Qulub (Reconciling Hearts):* People supported to strengthen social solidarity, ease hostility, or assist recent converts experiencing economic isolation.
5. *Fir-Riqab (Freeing Bonded Persons):* Emancipating enslaved individuals and assisting vulnerable workers trapped in bonded labor or unjust human trafficking.
6. *Al-Gharimin (Overwhelmed Debtors):* Individuals trapped in debt contracted for basic survival, family emergencies, or communal reconciliation, who have no means of repayment.
7. *Fi Sabilillah (In the Cause of Allah):* Supporting constructive public endeavours that uphold truth, advance community knowledge, and protect the common welfare.
8. *Ibn As-Sabil (The Stranded Traveler):* A traveler who has lost access to funds while away from home, ensuring safe passage regardless of their status in their hometown.

#### Poverty alleviation and macroeconomic circulation

Rahman and Siradjuddin (2020) demonstrate that the primary macroeconomic function of Zakat is preventing the idle accumulation and concentration of capital. In conventional economies, surplus wealth often pools within capital-holding dynasties, leaving low-income populations without purchasing power. The Holy Qur'an explicitly warns against this structural inequality:

> "...so that it will not be a perpetual distribution among the rich from among you..." (Qur'an 59:7)

By placing an annual 2.5% levy on idle, stagnant wealth, Islamic law establishes a powerful economic incentive: capital owners are motivated to invest their assets in productive, employment-generating enterprises rather than hoarding cash or gold, because stagnant assets are continuously diminished by the annual Zakat levy. Simultaneously, transferring liquid funds directly to the poorest households injects purchasing power into the real economy. Because low-income families immediately spend their assistance on food, clothing, healthcare, and education, Zakat stimulates aggregate demand, revives local trade, and encourages grassroots productivity.

#### Zakat and Sadaqah compared

While Zakat functions as an institutionalized statutory levy, Islam encourages continuous voluntary charity (**Sadaqah**). As illustrated in Figure 5.2, the two forms of giving serve complementary roles:
- *Zakat* provides a guaranteed constitutional safety net with fixed percentages, strict annual timing, and legally restricted beneficiaries. It cannot be diverted to general infrastructure projects (such as constructing school boundary walls) or paid to one's own dependent parents or children.
- *Sadaqah* represents flexible, open-ended generosity. It carries no minimum nisab threshold, no fixed percentage, and no calendar restrictions. It may be given to anyone in need, including non-Muslim neighbours, extended relatives, or community development projects.

Furthermore, the Prophet Muhammad broadened the definition of sadaqah to encompass non-material social virtues: greeting a fellow citizen with a warm smile, speaking a gentle word to a struggling learner, directing a confused traveler, guiding the visually impaired, and removing a harmful obstacle from a public pathway are all recorded as acts of sadaqah (*Sahih al-Bukhari* 2989; *Sahih Muslim* 1009).

### Prohibition of interest (riba)

While Zakat redistributes surplus wealth downward, the total prohibition of **riba** (usury and interest) prevents the systematic upward extraction of wealth. In Islamic jurisprudence, the ban on riba is an absolute, uncompromising moral and legal command.

#### Theological and legal definition of Riba

Linguistically, *riba* signifies excess, increase, growth, or addition. In Islamic legal terminology, *riba* refers to any predetermined, contractual increase or excess demanded by a lender over the principal sum of a loan, without the provision of an equivalent counter-value or the assumption of commercial risk.

The prohibition of riba was revealed in progressive Quranic stages, culminating in the severe declarations of Surah Al-Baqarah:

> "Those who consume interest cannot stand [on the Day of Resurrection] except as one stands who is being beaten by Satan into insanity. That is because they say, 'Trade is [just] like interest.' But Allāh has permitted trade and has forbidden interest. So whoever has received an admonition from his Lord and desists may have what is past, and his affair rests with Allāh. But whoever returns to [dealing in interest or usury] - those are the companions of the Fire; they will abide eternally therein." (Qur'an 2:275)

> "O you who have believed, fear Allāh and give up what remains [due to you] of interest, if you should be believers. And if you do not, then be informed of a war [against you] from Allāh and His Messenger. But if you repent, you may have your principal - [thus] you do no wrong, nor are you wronged." (Qur'an 2:278-279)

The severe wording of these verses reflects the catastrophic social and moral harm caused by interest-based finance. In the Constitution of Pakistan (1973), Article 38(f) explicitly commands the state to "eliminate riba as early as possible," identifying the removal of interest as a fundamental constitutional goal for national socioeconomic well-being.

#### Economic arguments against interest

Islamic economics rejects interest-based transactions on rigorous ethical and macroeconomic grounds:
1. **Unearned Gain and Risk Transfer:** In a debt-based interest contract, the capital provider shifts the entire burden of real-world risk onto the borrower. Whether a farmer's crop is devastated by drought, a shopkeeper's store burns down, or an entrepreneur faces economic recession, the lender demands their full principal plus compounding interest. The lender guarantees risk-free profit for themselves while the borrower bears all operational loss.
2. **Perpetual Debt Servitude:** Because interest accumulates purely with the passage of time regardless of economic productivity, borrowers frequently become trapped in compounding cycles of debt. In rural and peri-urban Pakistan, low-income laborers, tenant farmers (*haris*), and school service staff often spend their entire working lives paying monthly interest charges without ever diminishing the underlying principal.
3. **Artificial Separation from Real Value:** Riba allows capital to multiply without producing tangible goods, services, or employment. Money is treated as a commodity that breeds more money, encouraging speculative bubbles, financial inflation, and financial volatility while starving the real productive economy of direct investment.
4. **Erosion of Human Sympathy:** An economy rooted in interest fosters callous calculation, where lenders profit directly from the distress and emergencies of fellow human beings rather than offering benevolent support or shared partnerships.

#### The Islamic alternative: Risk-sharing and equity partnerships

Islamic finance does not oppose profit, commerce, or investment. Rather, it insists that profit must be justified by real economic effort (*kasb*), capital investment, and the assumption of commercial risk (*ghurm*). The fundamental legal maxim states: *Al-kharaj bi al-daman* (benefit is tied to liability).

Islam replaces debt-based interest contracts with risk-sharing and asset-backed partnerships:
- **Mudarabah (Profit-Sharing Partnership):** An agreement where one party provides capital (*rabb al-mal*) and the other provides managerial expertise and labour (*mudarib*). Profits are shared according to an agreed percentage, while financial loss is borne by the capital provider, provided the manager acted without negligence or fraud.
- **Musharakah (Joint Venture / Equity Partnership):** All partners contribute both capital and managerial oversight. Profits are distributed according to agreed ratios, while financial losses are strictly shared in exact proportion to each partner's capital contribution.
- **Murabahah (Cost-Plus Trade Financing):** The financier purchases a tangible asset requested by a client and sells it to the client at an agreed cost plus an explicit, disclosed profit margin, paid in installments. Unlike an interest-bearing cash loan, Murabahah involves a genuine sale of an identifiable physical good with real commercial liability.
- **Qard al-Hasan (Benevolent Non-Interest Loan):** A compassionate loan provided to an individual in need, where the lender expects the return of the exact principal amount only, seeking divine reward rather than material gain.

#### Correcting the misconception about bank profit and interest

**Misconception: "Any bank profit or commercial return is automatically riba."**
Many learners arrive with the misconception that because interest is forbidden, any profit made by a financial institution or commercial enterprise must be sinful. Some conclude that Muslims must avoid all modern banking instruments, while others assume that Islamic banks merely disguise conventional interest under Arabic terminology.

This misconception conflates legitimate commercial profit (*ribh*) with prohibited usurious increase (*riba*). As the Qur'an itself records, skeptics in seventh-century Arabia made the identical claim: *"Trade is just like interest"* (2:275). The divine response was clear: *"Allāh has permitted trade and has forbidden interest."*

The essential distinction lies in commercial risk and real asset backing:
- *Riba* is a contractually guaranteed premium paid on a cash loan, where money is exchanged for money with an increase over time, involving zero trade risk for the creditor.
- *Trade Profit (Ribh)* is the surplus earned from buying, selling, or producing real goods and services, where the merchant assumes market risks, storage costs, quality depreciation, and price fluctuations.

When an Islamic financial institution engages in genuine risk-sharing contracts (such as *musharakah* or asset-backed *murabahah*), the earned profit reflects real economic value creation. What is prohibited is not profit itself, but the risk-free, guaranteed extraction of return through debt bondage.

## Activity: Evaluating economic justice in school welfare

**Work in small groups of 3 to 4. About 20 minutes.**

Review the following authentic scenario from a government secondary school in Larkana:

> The school welfare committee has 150,000 Pakistani Rupees available to assist disadvantaged students and support lower-grade staff. Two distinct proposals are submitted to the committee:
> 
> *Proposal 1 (Informal Loan Pool):* The school establishes a revolving loan fund where non-teaching staff can borrow up to 20,000 Rupees for emergency expenses. To "grow" the fund, the committee proposes charging a modest administration fee of 5% per month, with all proceeds returned to the fund.
> 
> *Proposal 2 (Targeted Dignity Fund):* The committee separates the resources into two transparent accounts:
>   - Account A (Compulsory Student Relief): Provides direct, unconditional grants for textbooks, uniform shoes, and examination fees to the poorest twenty students identified through an anonymous faculty assessment, applying the principles of Zakat and social rights (*haqq*).
>   - Account B (Benevolent Qard al-Hasan): Provides emergency cash advances to staff with zero interest or surcharges, repaid through manageable, interest-free payroll deductions over ten months.

1. In your group, analyze Proposal 1: Why does charging a 5% monthly fee on emergency staff loans constitute *riba*, even though the revenue is designated for a school welfare pool?
2. Evaluate Proposal 2 using the Islamic economic principles of *Zakat*, *Amanah*, and *Qard al-Hasan*: How does this approach protect the dignity of vulnerable individuals while avoiding predatory practices?
3. Appoint one group member to present a three-point summary of your group's ethical recommendations to the class.

## Summary

The Islamic economic system establishes a moral, humane alternative to unrestrained capitalist exploitation and state-controlled collectivism. Rooted in the supreme sovereignty of Allah and the human obligation of divine stewardship (*amanah*), Islam recognizes private property and honest commerce while establishing rigorous ethical boundaries between lawful (*halal*) enterprise and prohibited (*haram*) exploitation.

By instituting *Zakat* as an enforceable right of the destitute (Qur'an 51:19; 9:60), the economic framework prevents the dangerous concentration of wealth among capital-holding elites (59:7) and systematically circulates surplus resources to the most vulnerable citizens. Simultaneously, the absolute prohibition of interest (*riba*) eliminates the predatory extraction of unearned gain, replacing debt servitude with productive, risk-sharing partnerships (*Musharakah* and *Mudarabah*). In educational institutions, these economic values guide teachers and administrators toward transparent resource management, compassionate staff welfare, and the modeling of economic justice in classroom life.
