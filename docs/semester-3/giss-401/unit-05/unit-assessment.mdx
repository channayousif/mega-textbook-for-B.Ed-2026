# Unit 5 Assessment: The Economic and Political Systems of Islam

## Unit summary

This unit examined the ethical and structural foundations of the Islamic economic and political systems, demonstrating how Islamic principles govern collective institutional life. Rather than treating commerce and governance as secular domains detached from moral values, Islam integrates both within the comprehensive framework of divine accountability.

In Topic 5.1, we investigated the Islamic economic system, grounded in the belief that absolute ownership of all resources belongs to Allah Almighty alone (Qur'an 2:284), while human beings act as appointed trustees (*mustakhlafin*). We established the clear legal boundary between lawful (*halal*) productive enterprise and prohibited (*haram*) exploitation, showing that halal earnings require honesty, transparent mutual consent (4:29), and social utility, while barring deceit (*ghash*), hoarding (*ihtikar*), bribery (*rishwah*), and excessive speculation (*gharar*). We analyzed *Zakat* as an obligatory financial pillar (*ibadah maliyyah*) and a recognized divine right of the destitute (*haqq*, 51:19), calculating its fixed rates across gold, silver, and commercial assets exceeding the *nisab* threshold, and detailing its distribution among the eight designated categories in Surah At-Tawbah (9:60). Drawing upon Rahman and Siradjuddin (2020), we evaluated how Zakat is designed to prevent the continuous concentration of capital among wealthy elites (59:7) and to increase the purchasing power of the poor. Finally, we explored the absolute prohibition of interest (*riba*, 2:275-279; Article 38(f) of the Constitution of Pakistan), evaluating how debt-based extraction can trap vulnerable borrowers in debt servitude and contrasting it with equitable, risk-sharing partnerships (*Mudarabah* and *Musharakah*).

In Topic 5.2, we explored the political and administrative systems of Islam, founded upon the supreme sovereignty of Allah (*Hakimiyyah*) and human leadership as a sacred executive trust (*Amanah*). We analyzed consultative decision-making (**Shura**) as an obligatory constitutional command (Qur'an 42:38; 3:159), highlighting how institutional councils (*Ahl al-Hall wal-Aqd*) prevent authoritarian despotism (*istibdad*) while utilizing specialized competence for public welfare (*maslahah*). We examined the indispensable pillars of Islamic administration: impartial justice (**Adl**, 4:58; 5:8), the rule of law that binds rulers and citizens without executive immunity, judicial independence (*Qada*), and transparent public accountability (**Muhasabah**). Lastly, we evaluated the comprehensive charter of minority rights (**huquq al-aqalliyyat**) in Islamic jurisprudence, grounded in freedom of conscience (2:256), the sanctity of life and property, the protection of churches and synagogues (22:40), and the reported precedent of the Charter of Madinah (*Mithaq al-Madinah*). We connected these principles directly to the 1973 Constitution of Pakistan, examining the guarantees of religious freedom (Article 20), educational safeguards against religious coercion (Article 22), legal equality (Article 25), and minority protection (Article 36) in educational leadership.

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. **Economic Justice: Zakat versus Debt-Based Extraction** *(Analyze)*
   Analyze how the combination of institutional *Zakat* and the absolute prohibition of *riba* (interest) aims at economic justice in an Islamic society. In your essay:
   - Detail the ways in which Zakat mobilizes idle capital, discourages hoarding, and transfers purchasing power to vulnerable households (citing Qur'an 9:60 and 59:7).
   - Critically analyze how debt-based interest moves wealth from borrowers to lenders, which can increase inequality and lead to debt servitude.
   - Contrast this arrangement with Islamic risk-sharing commercial models (*Mudarabah* and *Musharakah*), explaining why tying profit to real productive risk (*ghurm*) is held to foster more equitable economic growth.

2. **Comparative Evaluation of Institutional Zakat and Discretionary Charity** *(Evaluate)*
   Evaluate the institutional design of *Zakat* compared to voluntary charity (*Sadaqah*) in alleviating poverty and preserving human dignity. In your essay:
   - Compare Zakat and Sadaqah across legal status, rate/threshold determination, recipient eligibility, and administrative structure.
   - Critically evaluate why establishing financial support as an enforceable divine right (*haqq*, Qur'an 51:19) protects the recipient's sense of dignity, contrasting this with the potential for discretionary charity to humiliate recipients or treat them as dependents.
   - Assess how a public secondary school in Sindh can apply these principles to organize an anonymous, dignified student welfare fund that provides textbooks and uniforms without exposing impoverished children to social stigma.

3. **Consultative Governance (Shura) and Administrative Accountability in School Leadership** *(Analyze)*
   Analyze how the Islamic governance principles of *Shura* (consultation), *Adl* (impartial justice), and *Muhasabah* (accountability) can reform authoritarian administrative cultures in Pakistani schools. In your essay:
   - Formulate the scriptural and theological basis of Shura (Qur'an 42:38; 3:159), explaining why consultation binds educational leaders.
   - Examine how the rule of law and the independence of justice in Islamic history (such as the traditional account of Caliph Ali before Qadi Shurayh) apply to internal school dispute resolution and disciplinary hearings.
   - Construct a concrete consultative governance framework for a high school, detailing the composition, mandate, and deliberative procedures of a School Consultative Council comprising teachers, parents, and student representatives.

4. **Constitutional and Islamic Legal Protections of Minority Rights in Pakistani Education** *(Evaluate)*
   Evaluate the legal protections guaranteed to religious minorities in Islamic jurisprudence and under the 1973 Constitution of Pakistan, focusing on educational environments. In your essay:
   - Analyze the scriptural foundations of minority protection in Islam (Qur'an 2:256; 22:40; 60:8) and the severe prophetic warnings against violating the covenants of non-Muslim citizens.
   - Evaluate the constitutional guarantees in Pakistan's Constitution (Articles 20, 22, 25, and 36), examining how Article 22 specifically protects minority pupils from religious coercion in schools.
   - Formulate a code of conduct for Pakistani school administrators that guarantees non-Muslim students equal dignity, equitable co-curricular inclusion, and reasonable accommodation during religious festivals.

5. **Integrated Institutional Reform: Designing an Equitable, Consultative School Ecosystem** *(Create)*
   Synthesize the economic and political teachings of Islam examined in this unit into a comprehensive operational policy proposal titled **"The Ethical School: Operationalizing Islamic Economic Justice, Consultative Management, and Minority Safeguards in Secondary Education."** In your proposal:
   - Design a Transparent Procurement and Welfare System that eliminates kickbacks, ensures halal contracting, and operates an anonymous Student Dignity Fund and an interest-free Staff Qard al-Hasan facility.
   - Establish a Consultative Administrative Charter that mandates regular Shura deliberations for institutional decisions and institutes transparent accountability (*Muhasabah*) for school management committees.

## Answers and marking guidance

### MCQ answer key

1. B - Islam establishes that absolute sovereignty over wealth belongs to Allah alone (Qur'an 2:284); humans hold property as accountable trustees (*mustakhlafin*).
2. B - *Ihtikar* is the monopolistic withholding of essential goods from the open market to artificially inflate prices and exploit struggling consumers.
3. B - Surah At-Tawbah (9:60) explicitly designates *al-gharimin* (individuals overwhelmed by unpayable debt incurred for basic necessities) as an authorized recipient category.
4. A - Under Islamic law, the statutory Zakat rate on commercial goods and liquid cash exceeding the silver nisab threshold held for a full lunar year is exactly 2.5 percent.
5. B - Demanding a contractually guaranteed percentage surcharge on cash loans without risk or counter-value is the precise legal definition of prohibited *riba*.
6. B - *Shura* is the obligatory, institutional practice of mutual consultation between leadership, qualified specialists, and the public in collective affairs.
7. A - Surah Ali 'Imran (3:159) directly commands the Prophet Muhammad to consult his companions, demonstrating that no leader possesses autocratic authority to bypass consultation.
8. B - The traditional account of Caliph Ali's appearance before Qadi Shurayh illustrates that the judiciary is independent and the rule of law binds the head of state and an ordinary non-Muslim citizen equally.
9. A - Article 20 of Pakistan's Constitution guarantees the fundamental right of every citizen to profess and practice their religion, which most directly protects students who wish to observe their religious festival.
10. A - Article 22(1) explicitly guarantees that no student attending an educational institution shall be required to receive religious instruction or take part in worship relating to a religion other than their own.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. **Model answer:** Lawful earning (*al-kasb al-halal*) refers to income generated through productive, honest labor, trade, or services characterized by mutual consent (Qur'an 4:29) and genuine utility. Prohibited gain (*haram*) involves wealth extracted through deceit (*ghash*), monopolistic hoarding (*ihtikar*), usury (*riba*), bribery (*rishwah*), or coercion, which the Qur'an explicitly forbids: "And do not consume one another's wealth unjustly" (2:188).
   - **Marks (3):** 1 mark for defining halal earning based on productive effort and mutual consent; 1 mark for defining haram gain through deceit, exploitation, or usury; 1 mark for citing a relevant Quranic verse (4:29 or 2:188).

2. **Model answer:** Some secular property theories treat ownership as a strong individual right to accumulate, use, or dispose of property. Modern constitutional systems, however, commonly limit that right through taxation, zoning, anti-hoarding laws, and public-interest regulation. In Islam, absolute ownership belongs to Allah alone (2:284); humans hold wealth as appointed trustees (*mustakhlafin*). Consequently, private property is conditioned upon social responsibility: wealth must be earned lawfully, purified through Zakat, and utilized to serve both family sustenance and broader community welfare.
   - **Marks (3):** 1 mark for describing a secular position without caricaturing it; 1 mark for explaining the Islamic doctrine of wealth as an *Amanah* from Allah; 1 mark for identifying the resulting ethical restrictions on accumulation and use. Supported alternative descriptions of secular property theory that note constitutional limits are acceptable.

3. **Model answer:** The merchant's claim is false because in Islamic commercial law, subjective agreement does not validate an inherently prohibited transaction. Selling adulterated cooking oil involves fraud (*ghash*) and concealment of defects (*tadlis*), which the Prophet Muhammad explicitly condemned: "Whoever cheats us is not of us" (*Sahih Muslim* 101). Genuine mutual consent (*an taradin minkum*, 4:29) requires full transparency regarding quality; exploiting consumer ignorance or lack of alternatives constitutes unjust enrichment (*akl al-mal bi al-batil*).
   - **Marks (3):** 1 mark for refuting the claim that consent validates fraud; 1 mark for explaining the legal violation of *ghash* / *tadlis*; 1 mark for explaining that Islamic mutual consent requires full product transparency.

4. **Model answer:** The annual 2.5% Zakat levy on surplus wealth penalizes idle capital hoarding: if an owner leaves wealth stagnant in safes or bank vaults, the annual deduction gradually diminishes it, creating a powerful economic incentive to invest in productive, employment-generating enterprises. Furthermore, transferring these funds to the eight vulnerable categories injects liquid purchasing power directly into low-income households, who spend it immediately on basic necessities, thereby stimulating local commerce and fulfilling the Quranic mandate that wealth must not circulate solely among the rich (59:7).
   - **Marks (3):** 1 mark for explaining the disincentive against capital hoarding; 1 mark for explaining how wealth transfer increases purchasing power; 1 mark for linking this mechanism to preventing wealth concentration under Surah 59:7.

5. **Model answer:** First, regarding capital liability: in a conventional interest contract, the borrower is contractually obliged to repay the principal plus interest whatever happens to the enterprise, so the lender does not share the operational risk and the borrower bears the business loss alone (the lender separately faces the risk of default). In *Musharakah*, financial losses are shared in exact proportion to each partner's capital contribution. In *Mudarabah*, the capital provider bears the financial loss, while the manager loses only their labour, unless the manager is negligent or fraudulent. Second, regarding return on investment: conventional interest guarantees a fixed, predetermined return based on the passage of time, whereas Islamic partnerships tie returns to actual operational profits generated through real economic enterprise.
   - **Marks (3):** 1 mark for contrasting the conventional allocation of operational risk; 1 mark for distinguishing *Musharakah* loss-sharing from *Mudarabah* loss-bearing, including the manager misconduct exception; 1 mark for contrasting fixed predetermined interest with actual profit-sharing.

6. **Model answer:** *Hakimiyyah* denotes that supreme, ultimate sovereignty over the universe belongs to Allah alone. In political theory, this principle prevents autocratic tyranny by establishing that no human monarch, parliament, or headteacher possesses absolute, unchecked authority. Rulers are not sovereigns who can dictate arbitrary laws; they are executive trustees (*khulafa*) bound by objective divine standards of justice, human dignity, and consultation, which cannot be overturned by majoritarian whim.
   - **Marks (3):** 1 mark for defining *Hakimiyyah* as divine sovereignty; 1 mark for explaining how it strips human rulers of absolute authority; 1 mark for explaining that executive power is bound by higher moral and legal limits.

7. **Model answer:** In Islamic jurisprudence, *Shura* is an obligatory constitutional command because the Holy Qur'an explicitly pairs it with fundamental religious duties: "and whose affair is [determined by] consultation among themselves" (42:38). Furthermore, in Surah Ali 'Imran (3:159), Allah explicitly commanded Prophet Muhammad to consult his followers in public affairs. Because this command was given to the Messenger himself, no subsequent human ruler has the legal right to govern autocratically without consulting the community.
   - **Marks (3):** 1 mark for noting that consultation is paired with foundational duties in Qur'an 42:38; 1 mark for citing the direct divine command in 3:159; 1 mark for concluding that consultation is an obligatory check against autocratic governance.

8. **Model answer:** Under the principle of *Shura*, the headteacher should have convened a consultative scheduling committee comprising the department heads and affected science teachers before finalizing the timetable. The committee would review lab constraints collaboratively, identify clashing periods, and adjust timings through mutual consensus, thereby preventing operational paralysis and respecting the professional expertise of the faculty.
   - **Marks (3):** 1 mark for proposing a consultative scheduling committee; 1 mark for reviewing lab constraints collaboratively with staff; 1 mark for reaching an operational solution through consensus before implementation.

9. **Model answer:** The *Charter of Madinah* (622 CE) is reported to have established a multi-religious civic commonwealth by declaring that Muslim believers and Jewish tribes formed one community (*ummah wahidah*). It is reported to have guaranteed freedom of religion for the Jewish tribes, protected personal property, mandated mutual defense against external aggression, and established equal access to justice. The charter is presented as a reported historical precedent for civic equality and minority rights, not as a fully verified text.
   - **Marks (3):** 1 mark for citing the concept of one community (*ummah wahidah*); 1 mark for explaining the reported guarantee of religious freedom; 1 mark for detailing mutual defense and equal civic rights. Answers that note the reported nature of the charter are acceptable.

10. **Model answer:** Article 20 of Pakistan's Constitution guarantees the fundamental right of every citizen to profess, practice, and propagate their religion, establishing general religious freedom. Article 22 operationalizes this protection within educational institutions by explicitly barring schools from forcing any student to receive instruction or participate in worship relating to a religion other than their own. School administrators must uphold both articles by accommodating minority religious holidays and exempting non-Muslim pupils from compulsory Islamic worship rituals.
    - **Marks (3):** 1 mark for explaining Article 20's general religious freedom; 1 mark for explaining Article 22's specific educational safeguards against coercion; 1 mark for applying both to school administrative accommodation.

### ERQ rubrics

#### ERQ 1: Macroeconomic Justice: Zakat versus Debt-Based Extraction

- **Analytic rubric (10 marks total):**
  - *Doctrinal accuracy and Quranic grounding (0-2 marks):* Accurately details Zakat rules (2.5% rate, nisab baseline, annual assessment) and cites Quranic verses on poverty alleviation (9:60) and capital circulation (59:7).
  - *Macroeconomic analysis of Zakat (0-3 marks, **Analyze**):* Rigorously analyzes how an annual wealth tax penalizes stagnant capital hoarding, motivates real investment, and transfers purchasing power directly to vulnerable households.
  - *Critique of debt-based interest (0-3 marks, **Analyze**):* Analyzes how interest (*riba*) separates the lender's return from the borrower's operational risk, guaranteeing the lender a fixed return while the borrower bears the enterprise's losses, and explains how this can concentrate capital and lead to debt servitude.
  - *Risk-sharing alternatives and economic growth (0-2 marks):* Accurately explains *Mudarabah* and *Musharakah*, justifying why tying profit to real productive risk (*ghurm*) fosters equitable, non-inflationary economic growth.
