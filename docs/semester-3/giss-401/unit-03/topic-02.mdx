# Topic 3.2 - The moral and ethical system of Islam (Akhlaq)

## A real classroom situation

At an elementary college in Larkana, Sindh, a trainee teacher invigilates an end-of-term examination. Halfway through the test, he notices two pupils sitting at the back whispering and exchanging sheets of paper. When the trainee confiscates the notes and questions the boys outside the hall, one pupil offers an unexpected defense: "Sir, my family has sacrificed everything so I can pass; helping my cousin pass is an act of family loyalty and kindness." Later that afternoon, the trainee overhears an influential local community leader speaking to a senior teacher, politely suggesting that certain pupils from notable families should receive grace marks to protect their family standing in the district. The trainee realizes that both the cheating pupils and the adult leaders know the abstract slogans of Islamic morality, yet both bend ethical principles when confronted with personal interest, family prestige, or social pressure. To build genuine moral character in schools, future educators must grasp that Islamic ethics (*Akhlaq*) is not an abstract catalogue of slogans, but an unyielding, lived standard of truthfulness, justice, and accountability before God.

## Explanation

### Moral values in Islam

In Islamic terminology, **Akhlaq** is the plural of *khuluq*, which denotes an internal state of the soul or deeply ingrained disposition from which actions proceed naturally and effortlessly, without requiring artificial strain or forced calculation. Classical Muslim scholars, including Imam Al-Ghazali, distinguished between an occasional good deed and a genuine virtue: a person who occasionally tells the truth under pressure does not possess the virtue of truthfulness (*sidq*); only when truthfulness becomes their habitual, spontaneous reaction in all circumstances does it constitute authentic *khuluq*.

Islamic ethics is distinguished by its holistic and transcendent foundation. While secular ethical philosophies often ground morality in utilitarian outcomes (the greatest happiness for the greatest number), social convention, or legal contract, Islamic morality is rooted directly in divine revelation (*Wahi*), reverent consciousness of God (*Taqwa*), and inescapable accountability in the Hereafter (*Akhirat*). An action is fundamentally good (*ma'ruf*) because Allah has commanded it and placed love for it within the primordial human conscience (*fitrah*); an action is fundamentally evil (*munkar*) because Allah has forbidden it due to its inherent harm to the soul and society.

This grounding establishes an objective, absolute moral standard that cannot be compromised for political expediency, tribal loyalties, or personal financial gain. A Muslim does not tell the truth merely to maintain a profitable business reputation; a Muslim tells the truth because Allah is all-Hearing and all-Seeing, and because every word will be weighed on the Day of Judgement.

Furthermore, Islamic ethics represents the ultimate purpose of prophetic guidance. Prophet Muhammad explicitly declared his central mission:

> "I was sent only to perfect noble character [makarim al-akhlaq]." (Reported in Muwatta Malik and Musnad Ahmad)

The Holy Qur'an identifies moral excellence as the defining characteristic of the Prophet himself:

> "And indeed, you are of a great moral character." (Qur'an 68:4)

In Islamic thought, faith (*Iman*), worship (*Ibadat*), and morality (*Akhlaq*) form an indivisible organic whole. Iman is the root planted in the heart; Ibadat provides regular spiritual nourishment through prayer, fasting, and charity; Akhlaq is the visible, fragrant fruit borne in daily human relationships. If a tree bears poisoned fruit, its root is diseased; similarly, if a believer's daily life is characterized by fraud, deceit, cruelty, and injustice, their profession of faith and observance of ritual prayer remain spiritually hollow.

### Truthfulness, justice, honesty and patience

Islamic ethical tradition identifies four cardinal moral values that are foundational for individual character and essential for professional integrity in education:

#### 1. Truthfulness (Sidq)

**Sidq** implies total conformity between reality, the convictions of the heart, the words of the tongue, and outward actions. It is the bedrock of all virtue, as falsehood (*kidhb*) is the root of all hypocrisy. The Holy Qur'an commands:

> "O you who have believed, fear Allah and be with those who are true [in word and deed]." (Qur'an 9:119)

In school environments, *sidq* demands rigorous intellectual and operational honesty:
- Teachers must report accurate attendance and authentic examination grades without falsification or inflation.
- Educators must demonstrate the intellectual humility to say "I do not know, but let us investigate together" when a student asks a question outside their mastery, rather than fabricating inaccurate explanations.
- In peer relationships, truthfulness requires constructive feedback and refusing to participate in gossip, flattery, or administrative cover-ups.

#### 2. Justice (Adl)

**Adl** means placing everything in its rightful place and rendering every person their exact due without partiality, prejudice, or personal bias. Islam insists upon absolute, uncompromising justice that transcends tribal kinship, social class, and even religious differences. Surah An-Nisa proclaims:

> "O you who have believed, be persistently standing firm in justice, witnesses for Allah, even if it be against yourselves or parents and relatives. Whether one is rich or poor, Allah is more worthy of both. So follow not personal inclination, lest you not be just." (Qur'an 4:135)

Surah Al-Ma'idah reinforces this principle even in situations of deep interpersonal or communal animosity:

> "And do not let the hatred of a people prevent you from being just. Be just; that is nearer to righteousness." (Qur'an 5:8)

In the classroom, *adl* is the cornerstone of effective teaching:
- A teacher must distribute instructional time, encouragement, and feedback equitably across all students, completely rejecting favouritism based on family wealth, political connections, or ethnic background.
- Assessment must follow transparent, objective criteria where identical performance receives identical marks regardless of who produced the work.
- In classroom disputes, a teacher must listen impartially to all parties before reaching a decision, avoiding predetermined assumptions about who is at fault.

#### 3. Honesty and Trustworthiness (Amanah)

**Amanah** encompasses the faithful safeguarding of every duty, right, resource, or confidence entrusted to a person by Allah or by fellow human beings. Its opposite is betrayal (*khiyanah*), which the Prophet described as a definitive sign of hypocrisy. The Qur'an commands:

> "Indeed, Allah commands you to render trusts to whom they are due and when you judge between people to judge with justice." (Qur'an 4:58)

For a teacher, *amanah* is the defining professional concept:
- The pupils sitting in a classroom are not mere consumers; they are a sacred trust (*amanah*) placed by parents and society into the teacher's care. To neglect their learning, waste class time, or subject them to physical or psychological abuse is a grave breach of trust.
- Examination papers, answer keys, and grading records are trusts; leaking questions or altering results violates both institutional ethics and divine law.
- School resources, laboratory equipment, and library books must be preserved and utilized with scrupulous care.

#### 4. Patience and Perseverance (Sabr)

**Sabr** is frequently mistranslated as passive resignation or fatalistic helplessness. In classical Islamic scholarship, *sabr* is an active, dynamic virtue signifying steadfast endurance, emotional self-regulation, and determined perseverance in doing good while restraining the soul from panic, despair, and wrongdoing. The Qur'an states:

> "O you who have believed, seek help through patience and prayer. Indeed, Allah is with the patient." (Qur'an 2:153)

Scholars classify *sabr* into three interconnected dimensions:
1. *Patience in obedience*: Steadfastly performing righteous duties (such as meticulous lesson preparation and fair marking) even when exhausted or unappreciated.
2. *Patience in refraining from sin*: Actively restraining oneself from anger, sarcasm, harsh punishment, or accepting illicit favors.
3. *Patience during adversity*: Maintaining composure, hope, and professional dignity when facing overcrowded classrooms, inadequate teaching resources, or administrative challenges.

In the classroom, *sabr* prevents burnout and protects children. When a struggling student fails to understand a mathematical concept after three explanations, the teacher exercising *sabr* does not lash out in anger or humiliate the child. Instead, they pause, regulate their emotions, and patiently devise an alternative instructional approach.

### Character building

How does an educational institution transform abstract moral virtues into enduring pupil character?

**Worked example: Resolving a grading dilemma with Islamic virtues.**
Consider a trainee teacher facing an ethical dilemma in a high school in Mirpurkhas:
1. *The dilemma*: An influential community member visits the school and asks the trainee to increase his son's science grade from 58% to 80% so the boy qualifies for a regional scholarship, promising that the family will donate ceiling fans to the school in return.
2. *Analysis through virtues*:
   - *Sidq (Truthfulness)*: The student earned 58%; certifying 80% is a direct falsehood (*kidhb*).
   - *Adl (Justice)*: Inflating marks gives an unearned advantage to one student while depriving hardworking, deserving students of their rightful scholarship opportunity.
   - *Amanah (Trust)*: The grading ledger is an official and divine trust; altering it for institutional gifts is bribery (*rishwah*), which is strictly forbidden.
   - *Sabr (Patience/Courage)*: The trainee must calmly endure potential social friction or administrator displeasure without capitulating.
3. *Action*: The trainee respectfully but firmly declines: "Sir, your son's test reflects his current mastery. It would be unjust to him and other children to falsify this record. However, I will happily offer him extra tutorial time after school to help him genuinely master the difficult topics."
4. *Pedagogical lesson*: The teacher's principled refusal models integrity for the entire school community, demonstrating that Islamic values are non-negotiable standards of professional practice.

#### Sutomo's framework: from character education to Akhlaq education

Sutomo (2014) investigates how modern character education can be modified into comprehensive *akhlaq* education for contemporary society. Drawing upon both contemporary developmental psychology and classical Islamic scholarship, Sutomo demonstrates that authentic character building requires an integrated developmental cycle:

1. **Moral Knowledge (*Ilm*)**: The cognitive stage where learners acquire knowledge of ethical principles, understand the reasons why certain actions are good and others evil, and learn to identify moral dilemmas in real life.
2. **Moral Feeling (*Hal* / *Niyyah*)**: The affective and spiritual stage where knowledge is transformed into emotional conviction. Learners develop empathy, a genuine love for goodness, a healthy hatred for injustice, and *taqwa* (reverent awe before God). The intention (*niyyah*) is purified so that good deeds are performed for the sake of Allah, not to show off (*riya*).
3. **Moral Practice (*Amal*)**: The behavioural stage where values are enacted through deliberate, repeated choices in authentic social contexts. Knowledge and feeling remain dormant unless translated into action.
4. **Formed Habit and Steadfastness (*Istiqamah*)**: Through continuous practice and reinforcement across diverse school routines, virtuous conduct becomes habituated. As actions are repeated, cognitive resistance diminishes until virtue becomes second nature (*khuluq*).

Throughout this cycle, Sutomo emphasizes the paramount importance of **role modelling (*qudwah hasanah*)**. Children do not form moral habits primarily by listening to lectures or memorizing textbooks; they form character by observing how trusted adults behave when stakes are high. If a teacher preaches honesty but routinely signs attendance registers for absent colleagues, pupils internalize the hypocrisy rather than the sermon. Conversely, when a teacher consistently displays fairness, patience, and kindness in the face of provocation, learners internalize those virtues as realistic, desirable norms.

#### Addressing the misconception about character building

**Misconception 3: "Good character comes only from knowing ethical rules."**
A widespread flaw in moral instruction is the assumption that teaching rules intellectually is sufficient to produce good human beings. Schools frequently require pupils to memorize definitions of virtues, reproduce hadith texts on examination papers, and recite moral pledges during morning assemblies, yet administrators are dismayed when academic cheating, bullying, and vandalism persist.

This approach fails because cognitive moral knowledge (*ilm*) addresses only the intellect. Without cultivating moral empathy (*hal*), establishing repeated practice (*amal*), and providing living adult role models (*qudwah hasanah*), intellectual rules remain abstract theories. A pupil can score 100% on an Islamic Studies written test while actively bullying a classmate during recess if the school culture does not scaffold the full character-building cycle. Sutomo (2014) demonstrates that *akhlaq* education succeeds only when educational institutions deliberately integrate cognitive instruction with reflective dialogue, practical community service, consistent school-wide norms, and uncompromised teacher role-modelling.

> During the annual sports day selection, the school sports teacher selects the son of a prominent district education official for the cricket team over a significantly more talented student from an underprivileged rural family. The sports teacher privately confides to his colleague: "If I select the official's son, our school will easily receive the new computer lab funds we have requested for three years. It is for the greater good of the whole school."

1. Working with your partner, analyze the sports teacher's justification using the Islamic virtues of **Adl (justice)**, **Sidq (truthfulness)**, and **Amanah (trustworthiness)**.
2. Why does Islamic ethics reject the utilitarian claim that "committing an injustice against one student is acceptable if it benefits the majority"?
3. Draft a three-sentence script showing how the colleague can respectfully challenge the sports teacher and propose a principled course of action.

## Summary

The Islamic moral system (*Akhlaq*) provides an objective, divinely grounded framework of virtue ethics designed to purify the soul and establish social harmony. Inextricably linked to faith (*Iman*) and nourished by worship (*Ibadat*), Islamic morality emphasizes cardinal virtues: truthfulness (*sidq*), impartial justice (*adl*), sacred trust (*amanah*), and steadfast perseverance (*sabr*). Character building in schools cannot rely upon cognitive rule-memorization alone; it requires an integrated educational cycle combining moral knowledge, emotional commitment (*taqwa*), deliberate practice, consistent habituation, and authentic teacher role-modelling (*qudwah hasanah*).

## Summative task

Write a policy brief (approximately 400 - 500 words) for a Pakistani school headteacher titled: **"Institutionalizing Akhlaq: A School-Wide Strategy for Integrity and Fairness."**

Your policy brief must:
1. Identify two critical areas of school life where ethical failures commonly occur (such as biased examination grading, academic dishonesty, or favouritism in student leadership appointments).
