# Topic 2.2 - The Holy Qur'an

## A real classroom situation

At a primary school in Sukkur, a pupil says that being fair means giving every child the same reading exercise. Another pupil is still learning the script and cannot begin the exercise. During practicum, Bilal wants to connect the discussion with Qur'anic teaching on justice. He can recall a phrase but cannot remember its reference. His cooperating teacher asks him to find the passage, read its meaning, and explain how his proposed response follows from it.

## Explanation

### Introduction to the Qur'an

Muslims believe the Qur'an is Allah's revelation to the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him. The Arabic text is arranged into **surahs**, or chapters, made up of **ayat**, or verses. The standard chapter sequence has 114 surahs, from Al-Fatihah to An-Nas. The chapter number is an address for finding a passage, not a mark of the chapter's importance. A short chapter and a long chapter both belong to the Qur'an.

A reference such as 96:1–5 identifies surah 96, ayat 1 through 5. It does not mean page 96 of every printed copy. Printed page numbers vary with the edition and layout. A learner who gives a page number alone may make the passage hard for another person to find. Giving the surah name as well as the numerical reference helps a class check its work across different copies.

**Worked example: locating a passage.** Start with "Al-'Alaq 96:1–5". Find Al-'Alaq in the contents or enter the reference on Quran.com. Confirm that the chapter is 96, then read ayat 1 to 5 together. Check the translator's name before reading the English meaning. Finally, write a reference in the notebook rather than copying the website's whole page. The five ayat form the selected passage; the whole surah includes more than those opening lines.

The Qur'an was revealed over the Prophet's mission, rather than as a modern book composed chapter by chapter in its printed order. The present chapter order should therefore not be used as a simple timeline of revelation. Quran.com's [information page for Al-'Alaq](https://quran.com/al-alaq/info) distinguishes its opening revelation from later passages of that surah. This is a useful warning against assuming that every verse within a chapter was revealed on the same occasion.

Two terms often used in introductions are **Makki**, meaning connected with revelation before the Hijrah, and **Madani**, meaning connected with revelation after it. **Hijrah** is the Prophet's migration from Makkah to Madinah. The common time-based distinction does not simply ask whether every verse was physically revealed inside one city. A chapter classification is useful background, but details may need checking at passage level. Do not decide a verse's meaning by a label alone.

Makki passages often emphasise belief, accountability and moral warning; Madani passages often address the life of an established community as well as belief and morals. These are broad teaching patterns, not a rule that ethics belongs only to one period and law only to another. For an introductory account of the distinction, see Ahmad von Denffer's *Ulum al-Qur'an*, chapter 5, "Makkan and Madinan Revelations", linked below. The selected passages in this topic need their own reading, whatever their classification.

A **translation** gives a translator's account of the Arabic meaning in another language. **Tafsir** is explanation or commentary that helps readers consider meaning, context and connections. A short English paraphrase in a lesson has a more limited job: it restates the point being discussed. These are related tools, but they should not be given the same label. If Bilal writes his own sentence about fair reading groups, he must not present that sentence as the literal words of the Qur'an.

This topic uses Dr Mustafa Khattab's *The Clear Quran* on Quran.com for checking English meanings. The explanations below paraphrase; they are not quotations. A teacher using another recognised translation may find different English wording. The next step is to compare the meaning and consult commentary where needed, not assume that a difference in wording creates a different Qur'an. An unfamiliar word in a translation is a reason to ask, not a reason to invent a meaning.

### The Qur'an as a source of guidance

**Guidance** means direction for understanding and action. [Qur'an 2:2](https://quran.com/2/2) introduces the Book as guidance. Reading guidance asks more than "Can I repeat the words?" It also asks "What does this teach, and how should it shape my response?" Recitation, understanding and conduct have related roles. A lesson about honesty is incomplete if pupils learn its reference but the teacher then alters their marks to favour a relative.

A useful routine has four moves: locate, understand, connect and check. Locate the passage with a precise reference. Understand its teaching through a reliable translation and, where needed, tafsir. Connect that teaching with the situation, stating which part of the situation matters. Check whether your application respects the passage's meaning and the people involved. This is a classroom reading routine, not an independent method for issuing legal rulings.

**Worked example: Bilal's reading groups.** He locates [4:135](https://quran.com/4/135), which requires standing firmly for justice even where personal interest or close relationships are involved. He states the principle: a decision should not be distorted by favouritism. He then examines the reading task. One pupil needs support to access it; another is ready for a harder passage. Bilal proposes different levels of support with a shared learning goal. The verse does not specify a reading-group method; it supports a commitment to justice that he must carry into his teaching judgment.

He also checks the possible harm in his application. If he calls one group "the weak children" in front of everyone, he may make access harder by humiliating pupils. If he quietly prepares suitable materials and lets pupils move as they progress, the support may be more respectful. He should assess whether it works rather than assume that naming a Qur'anic principle makes his method successful. Religious grounding does not remove the need for good teaching and evidence about learning.

**Common misconception: a theme word lets us use any verse as proof of any school rule.** A theme is a starting point for study, not permission to detach a phrase from its passage. A verse about duties to parents should first be understood in that relationship. A teacher may then draw a careful connection to respectful speech, while keeping the original meaning visible. A general lesson and a specific rule are different levels of claim.

For example, a school might ban a particular notebook colour and call it "Qur'anic discipline" without giving a relevant reference. The rule may have an administrative reason, but attaching a religious label does not demonstrate that reason. Ask what the source actually says and how the proposed rule follows. A sound explanation can acknowledge a human choice: "We chose this system to organise books." That is more accurate than claiming revelation fixed the colour.

### Selected Qur'anic verses: a theme-based study

The four passages below connect knowledge with moral conduct. Read each whole selected passage before making a school connection. In the notebook, keep a column for the theme, one for the reference and paraphrased principle, and one for your own application. The last column is open to revision. It records your reasoning, not additional scripture.

**Knowledge: Al-'Alaq 96:1–5.** These opening ayat connect reading or reciting with the Creator and with teaching through the pen. They draw attention to human learning as a gift, not a reason for pride. Quran.com's Al-'Alaq information page identifies the opening passage with the beginning of revelation. In school, a possible connection is a patient approach to learning to read: the child who cannot yet form a letter is a learner to support, not someone to mock.

Work through that connection carefully. The passage is not a timetable prescribing how many minutes a child must practise handwriting. It gives a religious frame for valuing learning. A teacher chooses the practice length from the children's needs and the lesson goal. For example, Bilal might demonstrate one letter, invite a pupil to trace it and offer another attempt. His particular sequence is a teaching choice; his respect for learning is the connection he is explaining.

**Justice: An-Nisa 4:135.** This ayah requires truthful witness and justice even when they go against oneself, parents or relatives, and without favour based on wealth. The emphasis challenges a common temptation: adjusting the facts for someone close to us. A school example is marking a cousin's test with the same stated criteria as everyone else's. The teacher must use the actual work, not family expectations, as the evidence.

Here is a worked judgment. Two scripts make the same mistake. One belongs to the head teacher's relative. Bilal records the same feedback on both, checks the marking criteria and keeps the scripts available for review. He explains the decision through evidence, rather than accusing the family of wrongdoing. The proposed school practice follows the principle of resisting favouritism; the ayah does not supply the numerical marking grid. That grid remains a human tool that should itself be fair.

**Kindness: Al-Isra 17:23–24.** This passage joins worship of Allah with good treatment of parents, including respectful speech and care in old age. The relationship named in the passage matters. A learner should first be able to state that meaning. A possible wider classroom connection is learning to express frustration without contempt, while the teacher makes clear that the direct passage concerns duties to parents.

In a Sindh practicum class, a child may live with a grandparent or another carer, or may have lost a parent. A teacher can explain the passage faithfully without asking children to disclose painful details. Pupils can discuss an invented conversation about speaking respectfully to an older family member. The teacher should not demand a public statement about each child's home or use the lesson to silence a child reporting harm. Careful application considers who is in the room as well as the words on the board.

**Honesty: Al-Ahzab 33:70.** This ayah calls believers to mindfulness of Allah and upright speech. A possible school connection is the truthful attendance record. If a pupil arrives late, the teacher should record what actually happened under the school's procedure rather than change it to please someone. If the teacher is unsure of a fact, honest speech includes saying that it needs checking.

Suppose a class representative reports that ten books are missing. The teacher has counted only eight. A truthful response is to separate the known count from the unconfirmed report: "I found eight so far; we will check the rest." It would be inaccurate to write ten as an established fact just because the larger number draws attention. It would also be premature to accuse the representative of lying. The principle supports accurate speech, while the investigation must still establish the facts.

The themes strengthen one another. Knowledge helps a teacher check a reference and count the books. Justice guards against accusing only an unpopular pupil. Kindness shapes how questions are asked. Honesty shapes what is finally recorded. Studying these connections is more demanding than memorising four words, because the learner has to explain what evidence supports each step of a decision.

One useful final check is to change a detail of the example. If the late pupil is the teacher's niece, should the attendance record change? If the child who struggles to read is very confident, does support stop being needed? If a family situation is sensitive, can the lesson use an invented case? These changes test whether the proposed application rests on a principle rather than on sympathy for one particular person.

## Activity: Build and challenge a theme card

Work in pairs for about 25 minutes. Each pair takes one of the four selected passages. One partner locates and reads it; the other writes a three-sentence explanation from memory: its direct teaching, a possible school application and a limit on that application. Swap roles and check the reference and meaning together.

Exchange cards with another pair. They must identify where the card moves from meaning to application and ask one question about the connection. Revise the card to answer that question. Use fictional cases, and do not ask classmates to report their family relationships. At the end, combine the four themes into one class display with references and a clear "possible applications" label.

## Check your understanding

1. Explain what surah and ayah mean, and decode the reference 96:1–5 without using a page number. *(Remember)*
2. Explain the difference between an Arabic verse, a translation, tafsir and your own school application. *(Understand)*
3. A teacher changes a relative's mark without checking the script. Use 4:135 to explain a better response, and identify which part of your response is a school procedure rather than the verse's wording. *(Apply)*

## Summary

A precise reference allows another learner to check a passage. Translation and tafsir support understanding, while a classroom application requires a further, explicit connection. Knowledge, justice, kindness and honesty give related directions for conduct; naming a theme does not make every proposed school rule a Qur'anic requirement.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can locate a passage by surah and ayah and explain why chapter order is not a simple timeline.
- [ ] I can distinguish text, translation, explanation and application.
- [ ] I can explain all four selected themes with accurate references.
- [ ] I can justify a school application while stating what the passage does not directly specify.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask the cooperating teacher to choose one selected passage suitable for the class. Prepare a short explanation separating the direct meaning from one proposed application. Invite the teacher to check the connection before using it. Afterwards, record one pupil question and how you would improve your explanation, without recording the pupil's name. Use an invented case if the discussion could expose a child's family circumstances.

## Summative task

Prepare a 350–450 word explanation of a fair response to Bilal's reading-group problem. Use two selected passages, locate them accurately and distinguish their meanings from your teaching choices. Analyze one possible unfair or insensitive application and improve it. *(Analyze)*

Mini-rubric, 10 marks: reference accuracy, 0–2 (2: both precise and relevant; 1: one usable; 0: neither); meanings, 0–3 (3: both faithful with context; 2: broadly accurate; 1: theme names alone; 0: unsupported); analysis, 0–3 (3: identifies a problem and explains a better connection; 2: explains one part; 1: asserts a preference; 0: no reasoning); suitable school response and limits, 0–2 (2: practical and distinguishes human choices; 1: only one; 0: neither).
