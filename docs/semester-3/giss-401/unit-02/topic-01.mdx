# Topic 2.1 - The sources of Islamic knowledge

## A real classroom situation

At a government school in Hyderabad, trainee teacher Amina helps prepare a lesson about honesty. One pupil brings a Qur'anic reference, another repeats a saying attributed to the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, and a third says that a rule is true because everyone in their group agrees. Amina wants to welcome their contributions without treating all three as the same kind of evidence. She writes one question on the board: how do we know what each claim rests on?

## Explanation

### The Holy Qur'an as a source

The **Qur'an** is, in Islamic belief, Allah's revelation to the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him. It is the foundational source of Islamic guidance. A **source** is where an account or claim comes from. Asking for a source is therefore different from asking who spoke most confidently. A pupil may repeat a true teaching without knowing its reference. Another may give a precise reference but misunderstand it. A teacher helps both pupils connect a claim with evidence and meaning.

The Qur'an addresses belief, worship, moral conduct and human relations. It gives guidance with which Muslims approach their lives. For example, [Qur'an 4:58](https://quran.com/4/58) connects returning trusts with judging fairly. A trust is something placed in a person's care. When a school gives Amina a box of library books, the books are in her care; they do not become her property. She can explain responsibility through this familiar situation without claiming that the verse specifically describes a school library.

Notice the two steps in that explanation. The source gives the principle about trusts and justice. Amina then proposes a classroom application. Her proposal needs thought about the actual situation: which books were received, who may borrow them, and how losses will be handled fairly. The verse is the source of the principle; a borrowing register is a practical way to act on it. The register itself is not a revealed requirement.

A Qur'anic reference normally gives the surah number followed by the ayah number. A **surah** is a chapter; an **ayah** is a verse. Thus 4:58 means surah 4, ayah 58. A reference lets another reader find the passage and check what surrounds it. It also helps the teacher correct an error without making the issue personal. Amina can say, "Let us open the passage together," instead of asking the class to trust her memory alone.

**Worked example: moving from a claim to its basis.** A notice says, "A teacher should record borrowed books honestly because the Qur'an teaches trustworthiness." First, find the relevant reference, here 4:58. Second, read the passage rather than an isolated slogan. Third, state the principle in ordinary words: return what has been entrusted to you and judge fairly. Fourth, identify the school decision: keep an accurate record and return books. Finally, state the limit: the passage supports the responsibility, while the particular record system is a human choice.

This is a reading exercise, not a method for producing legal rulings. A **religious ruling** is a judgment about what Islamic law requires, permits or forbids in a case. Such judgments may need Arabic knowledge, several texts, and knowledge of scholarly interpretation. A trainee can accurately explain a clear teaching on honesty and still need a qualified scholar's help with a difficult legal question. Recognising that limit is part of using sources responsibly.

### Hadith and Sunnah as a source

**Hadith** refers to reports of the Prophet's sayings, actions and approvals. **Sunnah** refers here to his authoritative example or way of life. The words overlap in everyday use, but their teaching jobs differ. A hadith is a report through which we learn about the Sunnah; the Sunnah is the Prophetic example that reports and transmitted practice help preserve. Saying they are exactly the same can hide the need to examine the reliability and meaning of a particular report.

The Qur'an itself directs believers to obey Allah and the Messenger and to refer disputes back to them ([4:59](https://quran.com/4/59)). The Sunnah therefore does not compete with the Qur'an as though Muslims must choose between them. It explains and demonstrates guidance. In a familiar example, the Qur'an commands prayer, while the Prophetic example teaches how prayer is performed. [Sahih al-Bukhari 631](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:631) records the Prophet directing learners to pray as they had seen him pray.

The word **Sahih** in the collection title means sound or authentic. Topic 2.3 will distinguish a collection title, a report's reference and the grading of a report. For now, keep the collection name and hadith number together. A number alone is not enough, because different collections contain different reports at the same number, and older numbering systems can differ from the online numbering used here.

Consider Amina's second pupil. He offers a familiar saying about treating learners gently. Amina does not reject it because it came from a child. Nor does she call it a hadith simply because it sounds wise. She asks where it is recorded. The class can examine [Sahih Muslim 2593](https://sunnah.com/muslim:2593), a report on gentleness, and then discuss what gentleness might mean when correcting a mistake. The hadith is the cited report; patient correction is one proposed application of its teaching.

**Worked comparison.** Statement A is "Sahih Muslim 2593 reports a teaching about gentleness." Statement B is "A teacher lets a nervous child try reading again without ridicule." Statement A identifies a report. Statement B describes conduct that may put its teaching into practice. The conduct is not itself a newly discovered hadith. The teacher should not put quotation marks around her classroom rule and attribute those exact words to the Prophet.

There is another limit. A practice is not automatically Sunnah because it is old, local or popular. A routine of greeting pupils may reflect Prophetic values, but a particular school bell, seating plan or uniform needs its own description. Teachers can value useful customs while distinguishing them from claims about the Prophet. This protects both accurate religious teaching and respect for families whose harmless customs differ.

### Ijma and Qiyas

<Figure id="fig-U2-2" src="/img/figures/giss-401/unit-02/fig-U2-2.svg" alt="Four sources are compared by definition and use, separating revelation and the Prophetic example from dependent scholarly consensus and analogy." kind="table" caption="Original schematic by textbook.com.pk." />

**Ijma** means scholarly consensus. In the introductory Sunni legal framework used here, it refers to the agreement of qualified Muslim jurists of an era after the Prophet on a religious legal matter. **Jurists** are scholars trained to understand and reason about law. Ijma does not mean that a class, village or social-media group has voted for an idea. Identifying a valid consensus is itself a scholarly matter; a trainee should not announce that one exists merely because they have heard no disagreement.

**Consensus when revelation does not state a direct ruling.** An established historical instance of Ijma on an issue not directly ruled by a specific verse or hadith is the compilation of the Qur'an into a single bound volume (*mushaf*) during the caliphate of Abu Bakr. Revelation contained no direct explicit command to bind all written portions between two covers. Following the Battle of Yamama, in which many memorisers died, Umar proposed gathering the manuscript portions to prevent loss. Abu Bakr and other Companions initially asked how they could initiate a task the Prophet had not directly commanded, but through deliberation they recognized the shared necessity of preserving the revealed text. The Companions reached unanimous consensus (*Ijma*) to assemble the written records into a single volume under Zaid ibn Thabit. In this case, consensus did not innovate or contradict scripture; it addressed an unprecedented situation to safeguard revealed guidance.

**Qiyas** means reasoned analogy in Islamic law. It relates a new case to an established case through a relevant shared cause. It is more careful than noticing that two things look alike. A useful introductory analysis separates four parts: the original case, its established ruling, the new case and the relevant cause shared by both. If the shared cause cannot be justified, the analogy has not been established.

Mudawam (2021) distinguishes the Qur'an and Sunnah from dependent sources and methods, including Ijma and Qiyas. His article stresses understanding legal reasoning in relation to its sources and purposes. The distinction matters here: reasoning does not become a permission to ignore revealed guidance. This four-source outline is a common Sunni introductory framework. Muslim legal traditions differ over the definition and authority of some methods; this unit does not claim that every tradition describes them identically.

**Common misconception: Ijma and Qiyas can override the Qur'an.** In this framework, they depend on the Qur'an and Sunnah. They help scholars understand and apply guidance to questions that are not directly settled by a simple reading of one text. They do not create a new revelation or license a group to reverse a clear teaching because another outcome is convenient. Likewise, an unsettled question is not empty space in which any personal preference counts as law.

**Worked legal analogy, simplified for learning.** The Qur'an requires avoidance of intoxicants ([5:90](https://quran.com/5/90)). [Sahih Muslim 2003a](https://sunnah.com/muslim:2003a) explicitly extends prohibition to intoxicants generally. An introductory illustration of Qiyas can compare an established intoxicating drink with a newly named intoxicating substance: original case, established prohibition; new case, the new substance; relevant cause, intoxication. The point is the cause, not the substance's colour, country or brand. Because general textual evidence also covers intoxicants, this example illustrates an analogy's structure; it is not a claim that analogy alone is needed to establish the prohibition.

**Analogy when revelation does not state a direct ruling.** To see technical Qiyas operating where revelation does not state a direct ruling on the new case, classical jurists examined commercial contracts during the Friday prayer call. The Qur'an directly prohibits buying and selling (*bay'*) once the call is made: "leave off business" ([62:9](https://quran.com/62/9)). The text does not mention other transactions, such as leasing property (*ijarah*), hiring, or concluding employment contracts at that hour. Jurists applied the four-part framework of Qiyas:
1. Original case (*asl*): Buying and selling (*bay'*) during the Friday prayer call.
2. Established ruling (*hukm*): Prohibition (*tahrim*).
3. New case (*far'*): Leasing, renting or concluding other contracts during that same call.
4. Relevant shared cause (*'illah*): Distraction from attending the mandatory Friday prayer (*shughl 'an as-salah*).

Because concluding a lease or employment contract at that hour causes the exact same distraction from prayer as buying and selling, jurists extended the prohibition through analogy. Revelation did not mention leasing directly; the shared cause explains why the new case receives the established ruling.

Now consider a new school question: can a digital borrowing record serve the purpose of a paper record? This is an educational comparison, not a declaration that the school is practising technical Qiyas. The relevant need is an accurate, usable record of a trust. A digital system that loses entries may serve that need badly; a paper register that teachers cannot access may also serve it badly. Comparing cases through their relevant features helps a learner understand disciplined reasoning, while the religious legal method remains the work of qualified jurists.

To see the difference between a strong and a weak comparison, work through two proposals. Proposal one says a new drink has the same legal status as another because both come in glass bottles. The container does not explain the prohibition, so the comparison is weak. Proposal two asks whether the new drink intoxicates. That question concerns a relevant cause. Even then, the factual question about what a substance does and the legal question about its ruling must be established, not guessed by a class.

Ijma and consultation also need separating. Amina and three teachers agree to number library books. This is a school agreement. It may be useful and fair, but it is not Ijma in the technical legal sense. If one teacher disagrees about the best numbering system, that disagreement is not a religious offence. Naming the level of a claim keeps school authority from borrowing a religious status it has not earned.

Return to the three pupils. Amina can now put their contributions in different columns. A Qur'anic reference is checked as a passage of revelation. A saying attributed to the Prophet is checked as a report, with its source and reliability. The group's agreement is described as their agreement, unless there is genuine evidence for a scholarly consensus. This approach neither silences pupils nor treats every statement as equally grounded.

An evidence note can therefore contain four short lines: "My claim is...", "My source is...", "The source supports...", and "My application is...". Add "I still need to check..." when something is uncertain. Amina's note about the library would distinguish the Qur'anic principle from the chosen register. The uncertainty line might ask how to handle books that were already missing before she arrived. Good sourcing makes the remaining question more precise.

## Activity: Sort claims and explain the difference

Work in groups of three for about 20 minutes. One learner reads a claim, another classifies it, and the third asks for the evidence. Rotate after each claim. Start with these statements: "Qur'an 4:58 teaches returning trusts"; "Bukhari 631 records an instruction about learning prayer"; "our group agreed to a new library rule"; "two cases share a relevant cause". Without reopening the explanation, sort them into a Qur'anic reference, a hadith reference, an ordinary agreement and an outline of analogical reasoning.

Then reopen the sources and check the first two. For each, write the principle and one possible school application in separate columns. Improve one sentence that confuses an ordinary agreement with Ijma. Each group gives another group its classification and reason; the second group must identify one claim that needs more evidence. Finish by recording what changed after checking, rather than simply announcing that the group was right.

2. In your own words, explain the difference between a hadith report and the Sunnah it helps us know. Give one example from the explanation. *(Understand)*

## Summary

The Qur'an is the foundational source of Islamic guidance, and the Sunnah is the Prophetic example that explains and demonstrates guidance. Hadith reports require accurate attribution. Ijma and Qiyas are disciplined forms of scholarly authority and reasoning in the framework studied here; they cannot be replaced by a classroom vote or a comparison based on appearance.

## Try this at your practicum school

With the cooperating teacher's permission, examine one displayed moral reminder. Record whether it gives a Qur'anic reference, a hadith reference or no attribution. Check a reference together with the teacher and propose a clearer label, such as "classroom application of..." where appropriate. Do not photograph pupils or collect names. If an attribution is uncertain, record the question privately for the teacher rather than declaring the display false in front of children.

## Summative task

Write 300–400 words responding to Amina's three pupils. Distinguish their kinds of evidence, model one source-based explanation about returning trusts, and analyze why a group vote cannot override revealed guidance. Show how scholars use Ijma or Qiyas when revelation does not state a ruling directly - analyzing either the Companions' consensus on compiling the Qur'an or the four-part analogy extending the Friday prayer trade prohibition to other contracts - and contrast this with cases covered by general texts. Conclude with one reason a classroom exercise cannot establish a new religious ruling. *(Analyze)*

Mini-rubric, 10 marks: source distinctions, 0–3 (3: all distinctions accurate; 2: one minor confusion; 1: names sources without distinguishing; 0: absent); use of a checked reference, 0–2 (2: reference and supported principle; 1: reference without clear connection; 0: no usable reference); analysis of non-direct-ruling reasoning, 0–3 (3: explains how Ijma or four-part Qiyas applies when revelation states no direct ruling, identifying the shared cause or consensus basis; 2: explains the method without full cause; 1: merely names methods; 0: unsupported); classroom explanation and limits, 0–2 (2: suitable example and clear limit; 1: only one; 0: neither).
