# Topic 6.2 - Islamic culture and civilization

## A real classroom situation

In an invented Sukkur classroom, pupils prepare a display about Muslim scholarship. One group lists names without explaining any contribution. Another says that a Muslim scholar invented every modern scientific method, while a third thinks scholarship stopped after the medieval period. Their trainee teacher, Bilal, wants a display that builds informed respect rather than unsupported pride. He asks each group for evidence of what a scholar did and how learning was supported.

## Explanation

### Contributions of Muslims to knowledge

A contribution is a specific addition to knowledge or practice. It might be a new explanation, an improved calculation, a careful observation, a useful instrument, or a translation that makes a text available to new readers. Saying that someone was a great scholar does not yet explain their contribution. A history lesson should help pupils connect a person or group with a field, an action and evidence. This is a more demanding task than memorising names.

Sardar's (2011) Metropolitan Museum essay explains that scholars in the medieval Islamic world drew on earlier Greek, Iranian and Indian work and improved astronomical calculation and models. It places translation activity in eighth- and ninth-century Baghdad and describes al-Sufi's work in the tenth century. This shows both inheritance and development. Receiving knowledge from another society does not cancel a contribution. Nor does improving a method justify claiming that the earlier knowledge never existed.

For a second example, the UNESCO Portal to the Heritage of Astronomy describes Ulugh Beg's school at Samarkand in 1420 and his observatory four years later. It describes new calculations, measurements and scholarly exchange. These are fifteenth-century examples, well after the early Baghdad translation activity. The record gives us a named setting and specific learning work. It supports a selective account of scholarship, rather than a claim that every place enjoyed the same conditions.

| Scholar or scholarly setting | Field | Selected contribution | Evidence used in this unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Al-Sufi in the Isfahan setting | Astronomy | New observations recorded in the Book of the Images of the Fixed Stars | Sardar (2011) |
| Ulugh Beg and the Samarkand team | Astronomy | Observations and astronomical tables | UNESCO astronomy heritage case study |
| Al-Kashi in the Samarkand setting | Mathematics | Refined calculation supporting astronomical work | UNESCO astronomy heritage case study |

This small selection is deliberate. It is not a complete history or a ranking of civilizations. Bilal should prefer two well-supported examples to ten names linked to doubtful inventions. His pupils can investigate further examples later, using the same evidence test. Do not infer that a short table includes every contributor or every field of knowledge.

Here is Bilal's worked correction of a display card. The original says, 'Our scholars invented science.' First he asks which field and which action the pupils mean. They choose astronomy and the production of tables at Samarkand. Next he asks for the evidence. They use the linked heritage account. Finally, they rewrite the card: 'At Samarkand, Ulugh Beg and other scholars developed astronomical observations and tables in the fifteenth century.' The revised statement is narrower, but it teaches more because another learner can check it.

The same move works with the phrase 'first ever'. Such a claim needs a comparison with earlier evidence across relevant places. A single biography often cannot establish it. If a source supports an improvement, call it an improvement. If it supports a translation, name that work. If attribution is uncertain, say that it is uncertain. Respect for scholarship includes resisting attractive exaggerations about it.

In a Sindh classroom, this distinction can be taught without a library of rare books. Give pupils two short teacher-written cards based on a checked source. One card makes a broad claim; the other identifies a contribution and reference. Ask which claim the evidence can support. Pupils practise historical reasoning with the materials available. The skill is not searching for a favourable sentence; it is matching a conclusion to the strength of its evidence.

Do not turn this into a competition between communities. The teaching question is what was learned, how it was developed and how people shared it. A claim that another community contributed nothing is as weak as a claim that one's own community contributed everything. A learner can take interest in Muslim history while also recognising the work of others. Giving credit accurately is itself a scholarly responsibility.

### Islamic civilization

**Culture** refers here to learned ways of living, expressing meaning and relating to others. **Civilization** refers to wider patterns of social life, including institutions, cities, education, arts and systems for exchanging knowledge. **Islamic civilization** names the varied societies and traditions shaped by Islam across places and periods. The phrase does not mean that all inhabitants were Muslim or that everyone followed the same customs. Nor does it mean that every historical action represented an Islamic ideal.

The Met's educator resource, *Science and the Art of the Islamic World*, describes a scholarly community using Arabic that included different religions, ethnic groups and regions. It also connects learning to texts, instruments and practical purposes. This evidence helps separate three questions: the religious principles people held, the cultures in which they lived, and the actual practices for which historical evidence survives. These questions overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

Consider an invented classroom display about a learning centre. A building photograph alone tells little about the relationships inside it. A more useful display asks who taught, what learners studied, which languages carried knowledge, who provided resources and how work travelled. The building is one part of the explanation. People, texts, skills and support make it a place of learning. This is why a history of education cannot consist only of impressive architecture.

Use this simple model of a learning setting:

| Part of the setting | Question to investigate | Possible evidence |
|---|---|---|
| People | Who taught, learned or assisted? | Named accounts or records |
| Materials | What could learners read or use? | Manuscripts, instruments or descriptions |
| Support | How did work have time and resources? | Accounts of a school or patron |
| Exchange | How did knowledge move to others? | Copies, translations or travel accounts |

These are questions, not claims that every institution had equal access or generous support. When evidence is absent, leave the conclusion open. A modern teacher should not invent a complete social picture from one surviving instrument. Equally, incomplete evidence does not mean no learning took place. It means the account must state its limits.

The Samarkand case illustrates several parts together: a school, an observatory, scholars and calculations. The heritage account also describes later transmission of its work. That permits a connection between institutions and exchange. It does not prove that every child in the surrounding society attended advanced mathematical classes. A good history lesson notices the difference between evidence of a specialist centre and evidence of universal educational access.

For a Pakistani example of the reasoning, imagine a class studying an old local school building. A pupil says that because the building is large, every child in the town must have attended. Bilal asks what would support that wider claim. Enrolment records, accounts from the period or other checked evidence would help. The building establishes a site, not the attendance of every child. This invented local comparison makes the historical evidence problem visible without adding an unsupported claim about a real Sindh institution.

**Misconception: Muslim scholarship ended centuries ago.** A selective medieval timeline cannot establish that learning ceased after its final date. Its endpoint tells us what the figure covers, not what the world stopped doing. A contemporary student solving a research problem is doing work that must be judged on present evidence. Historical achievement can inspire that work, but it cannot replace it. We should reject both the idea of permanent decline proved by one timeline and the idea that past achievement guarantees present quality.

When pupils ask about Muslim scientists today, turn the question into a bounded research task using a current institution's own publication record. Identify a particular contribution, its date and the evidence. Do not decide scientific quality from the scholar's name or assumed religion. If you cannot verify the identity or work, do not add the person to the display. The same standard applies to historical and contemporary examples.

### Science and education in Islam

Science is a way of building and testing explanations about the natural world using evidence. Education helps people learn the concepts and methods needed for that work. Religious commitments can shape purposes and ethical responsibilities. A scientific explanation still needs observation, reasoning and appropriate testing. A historical account of a Muslim scientist does not by itself validate every claim attributed to that scientist.

Sardar (2011) notes practical connections between astronomy and needs such as prayer times, direction and calendars. This is a concrete example of religious practice encouraging technical questions. It does not mean that scripture contains a ready-made answer to every measurement problem. People needed methods, calculation and observation. Respect for the religious purpose and care with the technical work could therefore operate together.

The Met educator resource shows scientific instruments as both practical objects and objects with crafted form. An instrument is evidence of skilled making as well as an aid to investigation. But no instrument removes the need to interpret a result. A beautiful ruler with a badly placed zero still gives a poor measurement. A learner should be able to explain how a tool is being used and where an error might enter.

Bilal models this in an invented lesson about shadows. Two groups measure a stick's shadow at different times, but one measures from the wrong starting point. Their different results cannot yet establish a difference in the underlying process. He asks both groups to state the method, mark the same starting point and repeat the observation. They keep the original results and explain the correction. This practises honest inquiry: an unexpected result is a reason to check the method, not a reason to hide the record.

The analogy with historical scholarship is limited but useful. Pupils are not recreating an observatory or proving what a medieval scholar believed. They are practising a habit that makes knowledge dependable: show the procedure, preserve evidence and revise when there is a reason. Bilal should say exactly what the classroom activity demonstrates. Calling it a full reconstruction of medieval science would again make the claim larger than the evidence.

A second worked case concerns a viral message. It claims that a verse proves a newly advertised device works. The teacher separates the questions. What does the verse say in context? What exactly does the device claim to do? What evidence would test that claim? A religious reference should not be used as a substitute for a product test. Conversely, finding a weak product claim does not refute the religious teaching. Separating the types of question protects both careful science and careful religious interpretation.

In a low-resource school, pupils can still practise inquiry through diagrams, safe observations and transparent comparisons. Use a board sketch of two measurement methods if equipment is unavailable. Ask learners to predict which step would introduce an error, then explain their reason. Do not arrange observations that risk eye injury, heat exposure or unsafe materials. A historical science lesson should never require pupils to look at the sun.

Linking education and science also changes assessment. Asking pupils to memorise a list of Muslim scholars samples recall. Asking them to compare two evidence-based accounts samples understanding. Asking them to revise an unsupported display claim samples judgement. A balanced lesson may use all three. The deeper tasks should remain in plain language, with enough evidence supplied for learners to reason fairly.

Finally, acknowledge the limits of the unit. We have examined selected mathematical and astronomical examples because the linked sources allow careful teaching. Islamic civilization includes many other fields and complex debates. The point is not that this selection exhausts the history. It is that the same disciplined questions can guide a wider study: what is claimed, what was contributed, what supported it, and what evidence justifies the account?

## Activity: Repair the scholarship display

**Work in pairs, then join another pair. Allow about 25 minutes.** Use three invented display statements: 'Muslims invented every science'; 'A learning centre is only a building'; 'A timeline ending in the fifteenth century proves scholarship stopped then.' Start by recalling one evidence rule from memory.

Each pair chooses a statement and produces a revised card of 50-70 words. It must name the problem in the original, make a narrower defensible claim, cite a source from this topic and state one limit. Use the text above or printed source extracts supplied by your teacher; internet access is optional. One partner drafts and the other tests whether the source really supports the wording. Swap roles before finalising it.

Join another pair. They must challenge one word that may overstate the evidence and ask one question about the learning setting. Revise your card after the challenge. Keep a short record of the reason for the change. Judge the claim and evidence, not the pride or loyalty of the person who wrote the original. A careful correction should make the display more informative and more trustworthy.

## Check your understanding

1. Name two selected scholars or scholarly teams from this topic and state a contribution for each. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why a translation can contribute to learning even when the translator did not originate the knowledge. *(Understand)*
3. A display says a large school building proves that every child received advanced science education. Write one evidence question and a safer version of the claim. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Muslim contributions to knowledge involved particular people, methods and settings across time. Islamic civilization was varied, and scholarship included exchange with other traditions. Historical respect becomes stronger when claims are specific and evidence is clear. A selected timeline shows a scope of study, not the end of learning.

## Try this at your practicum school

Choose one historical statement in a classroom display or textbook already used in the school. Copy the wording into your private notebook with its source, without photographing pupils. Ask what evidence would justify the statement and whether its scope is clear. Write a more precise version if needed, then discuss it respectfully with your cooperating teacher. Do not alter school materials yourself. If no statement is available, use one invented display statement from this topic.

## Summative task

Prepare a 300-400 word commentary for a corrected classroom display. Include two supported contributions, a comparison of the learning settings that made them possible, and an explanation of why neither a single-inventor story nor an end-of-scholarship story follows from the evidence. Distinguish a historical claim from a classroom analogy. *(Analyze)*

| Criterion | 2 marks | 1 mark | 0 marks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contributions | Explains two specific supported contributions | Explains one specific contribution | Lists names without contributions |
| Institutional analysis | Links two setting features to learning with evidence | Names features without explaining their effect | Ignores learning settings |
| Scope and limits | Corrects both overclaims with reasons | Corrects one with a reason | Repeats an overclaim |
