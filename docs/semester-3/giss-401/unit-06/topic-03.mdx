# Topic 6.3 - Islam and contemporary issues

## A real classroom situation

In an invented Hyderabad school, a message in a parents' group accuses a pupil from a minority community of insulting a class activity. No one has checked the account, but some pupils refuse to work with the child. Trainee teacher Sana hears a colleague say that keeping peace means avoiding the issue. Another proposes making the child apologise publicly before asking what happened. Sana needs a response that protects dignity, checks evidence and preserves respectful difference.

## Explanation

### Islam and human rights

Human rights are claims to basic dignity and protection that belong to people as human beings. A right also raises questions about responsibility: who must respect it, what protection is needed and how a breach should be addressed. In school, speaking about dignity has practical consequences. A child must not lose fair treatment because the teacher dislikes the family, language or community associated with that child.

Qur'an [5:8](https://quran.com/5/8) directs believers to act justly even towards people they dislike. This is a demanding standard because unfairness often feels easier when we are angry. Its classroom application is to examine evidence before blaming a pupil, hear the pupil's account and apply a fair process. These are proposed educational actions drawn from a principle, not a claim that the verse sets out a modern school complaints procedure.

The Farewell Pilgrimage report in *Sahih Muslim* [1218a](https://sunnah.com/muslim:1218a) records the Prophet Muhammad's teaching on the protection of life and property, rejection of blood revenge, and responsibilities towards women, including maintenance. It is a specific report within a long account of pilgrimage. Popular versions of the 'Farewell Sermon' sometimes combine material from different reports. We should not attach every familiar sentence to this one hadith without checking it.

The following comparison uses selected themes. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted by the United Nations in 1948, uses a modern rights framework. Articles 1-2 address equal dignity and non-discrimination, Article 3 life, liberty and security, and Article 17 property. A thematic parallel is not proof that two texts have identical wording, scope or legal operation. This unit makes no claim that one document is a verbatim copy of the other.

| Selected teaching in the pilgrimage report | Modern counterpart for comparison | Boundary of the comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Protection of life | UDHR Article 3 | The UDHR also explicitly addresses liberty and security |
| Protection of property | UDHR Article 17 | The modern article states a particular rights formulation |
| Responsibilities towards women, including maintenance | UDHR Articles 1-2 on equal dignity and non-discrimination | These are related concerns, not equivalent detailed provisions |
| Rejection of inherited blood revenge | A concern for safety rather than private retaliation | This row is a teaching comparison, not a direct one-article match |

A pupil may ask whether the report proves a specific legal entitlement today. Answer that this table does not settle a current case or replace applicable law. Historical context, Islamic legal interpretation and contemporary legal rules need qualified study. The educational task here is to recognise protections and reason about responsible conduct. It does not authorise trainees to give legal advice to families.

For a school example, suppose two pupils accuse each other of taking a pencil case. One family is unpopular with some parents. The teacher should not treat that unpopularity as evidence. Separate the allegation from identity, hear both accounts privately and check what can be established. The object may be inexpensive, but fairness is not measured by its price. Publicly labelling a child a thief before checking can cause harm that remains even if the object is later found elsewhere.

Work through the reasoning. The relevant principle is justice despite dislike. The evidence is currently incomplete. The people affected include both pupils, because one may have lost property and the other may face an unsupported accusation. The immediate action is to protect safety and hear accounts without public humiliation. The next step is a proportionate response based on what is established, with a correction if a false accusation spread. Each step can be explained without making the teacher a judge of the child's family or faith.

Rights and duties belong together, but a child does not earn dignity by perfect behaviour. A pupil who behaved badly still needs safety and fair treatment. The teacher can address the harmful action while preserving those protections. Conversely, dignity does not mean that no one may be corrected. The question is whether correction is justified, proportionate and directed at behaviour rather than identity.

### Peace, tolerance and coexistence

**Peace** includes safety and conditions in which conflict can be addressed without violence or intimidation. **Tolerance** means respecting others' dignity and allowing difference while holding one's own beliefs. **Coexistence** means living and working together across those differences. In school, these ideas become visible in who can participate, who can disagree safely and what happens when someone is harmed.

**Misconception: tolerance means giving up one's own beliefs.** A person can hold a clear religious commitment while refusing mockery or coercion. Respecting a classmate's right to a different belief does not require agreement with that belief. Qur'an [2:256](https://quran.com/2/256) rejects compulsion in religion. Qur'an [49:11-13](https://quran.com/49/11-13) addresses ridicule and degrading labels and presents human diversity in relation to knowing one another. These references support a discussion of respectful difference; they do not erase the need for careful interpretation.

Sana's school can therefore reject the demand that a child surrender or defend an identity to join ordinary group work. The group can study a shared educational task while acknowledging that pupils differ. No learner should be made spokesperson for an entire community. Asking one child to explain what all members of a religion believe places an unfair burden on that child and encourages simplistic accounts.

Here is a worked response to a quieter conflict. Two pupils disagree about a custom described in a lesson. One says the other must be foolish. Sana first stops the insult and restates the rule: question a claim without attacking a person. She lets each pupil state the point in neutral language. She checks whether the disagreement concerns a factual claim, a family custom or a belief difference. Those need different responses. A factual error can be checked; a family custom should not be treated as universal doctrine; a belief difference can be acknowledged without a forced declaration of agreement.

Sana then returns to the shared learning goal. If the class is learning to compare accounts, each pupil can identify the claim and evidence. They need not decide which family is better. If the matter requires specialist religious interpretation, she records the question for a qualified subject teacher. She does not improvise a ruling to end discomfort. Keeping a respectful boundary is part of competent teaching.

Silence is not always peace. A classroom may be quiet because a child fears speaking. One way to examine coexistence is to ask whether pupils can access the task, take a meaningful role and seek help when harmed. These are observable questions. They are stronger evidence than the teacher's claim that everyone gets along. At the same time, one lively disagreement is not proof that coexistence has failed; the way it is handled matters.

Distinguish disagreement, bullying and danger. Disagreement concerns claims or choices and can be discussed within respectful limits. Bullying involves harmful behaviour and unequal power; it needs protection and intervention, not a debate about whether the target deserves help. An immediate threat requires the school's safety procedure and responsible adults. A trainee should not investigate a dangerous allegation alone or promise secrecy they cannot keep.

In an invented mixed-language group near Tando Allahyar, one pupil is repeatedly denied the reporting role because of an accent. The group is peaceful in the narrow sense that nobody shouts. Yet participation is unequal. A teacher can rotate roles, give preparation time and judge the report's meaning rather than accent. This is a proposed application of dignity to a classroom arrangement. It shows that coexistence involves everyday routines as well as responses to dramatic conflict.

A repair should address the actual harm. If a rumour spread publicly, a private apology alone may not correct what classmates now believe. The teacher can correct the false information to the same audience without repeating humiliating details. If someone was excluded from a task, access to a meaningful role should be restored. Repair is more than getting pupils to say they are friends. It asks what needs to change for learning to resume fairly.

### Islam and modern challenges

A modern challenge is a problem shaped by current conditions that still calls for ethical judgement. Digital messages can travel rapidly; copying from automated writing tools can hide what a learner understands; unequal device access can exclude some pupils from homework. The tools may be new, while concerns about truthfulness, responsibility and fairness remain familiar. We need to connect the principle to the actual features of the problem.

Qur'an [49:6](https://quran.com/49/6) warns about acting on unchecked news and harming people through ignorance. Applying that concern to a forwarded school message is an educational inference. The verse does not name social media. The connection lies in the risk: an unverified claim can lead people to act against others. Calling a message 'religious' or 'urgent' does not remove the need for evidence.

Return to the opening case. Sana separates three things: the message's claim, the evidence available and the actions already harming the child. She does not need to prove every detail of the rumour false before stopping exclusion. Pupils can be required to work respectfully while facts are checked. Protecting a child is an immediate duty; establishing the truth of an allegation requires a fair process. Confusing the two can leave a pupil isolated while adults argue about the message.

A practical response can follow five steps. First, stop further sharing through the classroom and protect the pupil's participation. Second, inform the responsible school adult and use the school's safeguarding or complaints route. Third, preserve only what is necessary for that route, without circulating screenshots or pupil identities. Fourth, hear relevant accounts privately and distinguish confirmed facts from assumptions. Fifth, correct misinformation and review whether exclusion has stopped. These steps are a proposed school procedure, not a quotation from scripture.

For a worked comparison, consider two options. Option A asks the accused child to apologise publicly at once. It may quiet the group but treats an unchecked claim as established and puts the burden on the child. Option B stops exclusion, refers the allegation appropriately and checks the facts before any blame is assigned. It protects safety and preserves a route to truth. Under the principles studied here, B is better justified. If evidence later confirms harmful behaviour, the school still addresses it through a fair and proportionate response.

The word 'verify' also needs care. It does not mean asking the loudest adult, counting how often a message was forwarded or treating an edited clip as the whole event. Ask who made the claim, what direct evidence exists, what context is missing and who is authorised to handle it. A trainee should not conduct a public investigation through a parents' chat group. If safety is at risk, act through responsible adults immediately while keeping uncertainty explicit.

A second case involves automated writing. In an invented Karachi class, a pupil submits a fluent paragraph produced by a tool but cannot explain its main idea. The teacher should not assume that every polished paragraph is dishonest. Ask the pupil to explain the reasoning and attempt a short related task in class. The assessment evidence concerns understanding and authorship, not whether the teacher likes the technology. Set clear rules about permitted help and acknowledgement before the next task.

The connection with Topic 6.1 is direct: information access is not the same as understanding, and wise use involves limits. The connection with Topic 6.2 is also direct: a plausible attribution needs evidence. A tool may invent a reference or combine different hadith reports. Check the named source before repeating the claim. No automated output becomes reliable merely because it uses respectful religious language.

A third case concerns access. If a teacher assigns an online task but some pupils share a single phone at home, failure to submit may reflect access rather than unwillingness. Offer the same learning goal through a printed extract, board notes or an in-class response. Do not infer a family's commitment to education from device ownership. A fair alternative should preserve the thinking demand. Giving excluded pupils a much simpler task would change what they have a chance to learn.

These cases show why good intentions need evaluation. Ask what principle the response serves, which evidence supports it, who benefits, who may be harmed and how success will be checked. A teacher may discover that the original plan did not restore participation. Revising it is part of responsibility. Religious values should shape careful conduct, not become labels used to silence a pupil who questions an unfair practice.

## Activity: Test a response, then repair it

**Work in groups of four. Allow about 25 minutes.** Use the opening case without adding real names or forwarding any actual message. Assign four rotating roles: principle finder, evidence checker, participation observer and plan writer. Begin by recalling the difference between tolerance and agreement without looking at the text.

Compare a forced public apology with a protected fair hearing. For each, identify the evidence it assumes, the possible harm and one effect on learning. Write a five-step plan that a trainee can carry out with the responsible school adult. Mark one step as an immediate protection and another as a later fact check. Use one scriptural reference accurately and identify your practical inference.

Swap plans with another group. They introduce a complication: a parent demands that the accused pupil be removed from class while the allegation is checked. Explain how your plan protects the child and follows school procedures without dismissing the parent's concern as proof. Revise the plan where needed. Finish by naming one observation that would show whether participation improved the following week.

## Check your understanding

1. Define tolerance and coexistence in your own words. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why a thematic comparison between the pilgrimage report and the UDHR does not establish that they are identical legal texts. *(Understand)*
3. A forwarded claim is causing a pupil's exclusion. State an immediate protective action and a separate evidence-checking step, with a reason for each. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Dignity and justice require fair treatment even in disagreement. Tolerance allows firm belief alongside respect for others' rights and participation. Peaceful coexistence includes addressing exclusion and repairing harm. New tools and messages need evidence checks, wise judgement and practical protection rather than quick blame.

## Try this at your practicum school

Observe one routine group task and record anonymously who receives meaningful roles. Suggest one small change, such as rotating a reporting role or allowing preparation in another language. Discuss the suggestion with your cooperating teacher before applying it. Record how you would check participation after the change. Use an invented example if observation is not possible; do not ask pupils about private beliefs or collect messages from their phones.
