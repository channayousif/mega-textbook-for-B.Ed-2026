# Topic 1.1 - What Islamic Studies is

## A real classroom situation

Ayesha is a trainee teacher at a government girls' high school in Hyderabad. On her first day of practicum she sits in a Class 9 Islamiat lesson. The teacher asks the class to write the steps of *wudu* (ablution) from memory. The pupils write quickly; they have done this since primary school.

At the end of the lesson a pupil named Hina asks Ayesha a question. "Miss, we already know how to pray and fast. Islamiat is just these rules. Why do we have to study it again in Class 9?" At lunch, the science teacher adds his own view: "Islamiat belongs to the Islamiat period. In my lesson we do science." Later that week Ayesha notices something else. The same pupils who wrote perfect notes on *wudu* copy each other's homework in the next period and see nothing wrong in it.

Ayesha wonders what Islamic Studies really is. Is it a list of rituals to memorise, one subject among many? Or does it have something to say about honesty in homework, the way a science lesson is taught, and the way a school treats its pupils? That is the question of this topic.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What does the subject called Islamic Studies cover? Why does Islam give such weight to knowledge and education? And what does it mean to say that Islam is a complete code of life?

### Meaning and scope of Islamic Studies

#### The meaning of Islam

The Arabic word *Islam* comes from the root letters *s-l-m*, which carry the ideas of peace, safety and submission. In religion, Islam means **submission to Allah**: accepting His guidance and living by it freely. A **Muslim** is a person who submits in this way. The Qur'an names Islam as the religion approved by Allah: "the religion in the sight of Allāh is Islām" (3:19).

Maududi, in *Towards Understanding Islam*, explains the meaning with an observation about the natural world. The sun, the moon, the rivers and the cells of the human body all follow the laws that Allah has set for them. In that sense the whole created world "submits". Human beings are different in one way: in their moral choices they are free to obey or to refuse. A person who chooses to obey Allah in these choices as well brings their whole life into line with the rest of creation. That free choice is what makes a person a Muslim in the full sense (Maududi, *Towards Understanding Islam*, ch. 1).

#### The meaning of Islamic Studies

**Islamic Studies** is the organised study of Islam: its beliefs, its sources, its worship, its moral teaching, its law, its history, and its guidance for family, society, the economy and the state. It is studied from the Qur'an and the Sunnah (the example of the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him) and from the long tradition of scholarship built on them.

Two points follow from this definition.

1. Islamic Studies is wider than *Islamiat* as many of us met it at school. School Islamiat often focuses on what to recite and how to pray. Islamic Studies includes these, but it also asks why, and what follows for the rest of life.
2. Islamic Studies is a field of knowledge with its own branches and methods, just as science has physics, chemistry and biology.

#### The branches of Islamic Studies

Over many centuries Muslim scholars developed separate branches of study, each with its own questions. The table below sets out the main ones and what each studies.

The **scope** of a subject is the range of questions it covers. The table shows that the scope of Islamic Studies is very wide. It reaches from the inner life of the heart (belief) to the most public questions of law and government. This course follows the same range: belief in Unit 1, the sources in Unit 2, worship and morals in Unit 3, society and the state in Units 4 and 5, education and civilization in Unit 6, and professional ethics in Unit 7.

#### Worked example: which branch answers the question?

A good way to understand the scope is to sort real questions by branch. Here are four questions a Pakistani pupil might ask, with the branch each belongs to and why.

| Question | Branch | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Which verses were revealed first?" | Ulum al-Qur'an | It is about how the Qur'an was revealed |
| "Is a hadith I saw on WhatsApp really from the Prophet?" | Ulum al-Hadith | It is about checking whether a report is authentic |
| "Is zakat due on my mother's gold jewellery?" | Fiqh | It asks for a ruling on an act of worship |
| "Why should I be honest if no one will find out?" | Aqidah and Akhlaq | It links belief in Allah and the Hereafter to moral conduct |

Notice the last question. It is not about a ritual at all. Hina's classmates who copied homework needed an answer to exactly that question. You will use this sorting method in the activity.

#### Correcting a common misconception

A common belief is that **Islamic Studies is only about rituals**: how to make *wudu*, how many *rak'ahs* to pray, when to fast. Rituals are an important part of Islam, and Unit 3 studies them. But the table above shows that they are one branch of a much larger field. The Qur'an itself makes this point. It says that righteousness is not just turning one's face towards east or west in prayer. True righteousness includes belief in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Book and the prophets, and also giving wealth to relatives, orphans and the needy, keeping promises and being patient in hardship (2:177). One verse joins belief, worship, generosity, honesty and patience. Hina's view, "Islamiat is just these rules", leaves out most of what the subject teaches.

### Importance of Islamic education

**Islamic education** here means two things: education in Islam (learning its beliefs and teachings) and education in the light of Islam (an Islamic view of why and how all learning should take place). Both matter to a teacher.

#### Knowledge in the Qur'an

The first words revealed to the Prophet (peace be upon him) were a command to read: "Recite in the name of your Lord who created" (96:1). The Qur'an teaches the Prophet to pray, "My Lord, increase me in knowledge" (20:114). It asks, "Are those who know equal to those who do not know?" (39:9). And it warns people not to follow "that of which you have no knowledge", because hearing, sight and the heart will all be questioned (17:36).

Bhat (2019), reviewing a book on the Islamic philosophy of education, notes how often the Qur'an uses words for knowledge and thinking. Words for knowledge (*ilm*) and for reasoning (*aql*), and words that call people to reflect, appear again and again. The book also presents the Prophet (peace be upon him) as the best teacher, and the teacher as an heir of the prophets and a role model for society (Bhat, 2019).

#### The aims of Islamic education

Why does Islam value knowledge so highly? The Qur'an describes the human being as a *khalifah*, a successor or trustee placed on the earth (2:30). A trustee must know what has been entrusted and how to care for it. Bhat (2019) reports that the Islamic view of education aims at the whole person: body, mind, spirit, character and skills together. Its goal is a balanced and integrated personality, useful to society, rather than a person who is clever but dishonest, or pious but unable to earn a living (Bhat, 2019).

This gives Islamic education three aims a teacher can keep in mind.

- **To know Allah and His guidance.** This is the root of all other learning.
- **To build character.** Knowledge that does not change conduct is incomplete.
- **To serve others.** Learning is a trust to be used for the good of family and society.

#### Islamic education in Pakistan

In Pakistan, Islamic education also has a constitutional basis. Article 31 of the Constitution of the Islamic Republic of Pakistan asks the state to take steps so that Muslims can order their lives by the basic principles of Islam and understand the meaning of life according to the Qur'an and Sunnah. Article 31(2)(a) asks the state to make the teaching of the Holy Qur'an and Islamiat compulsory (Government of Pakistan, 2012). This constitutional aim is one reason why Islamiat appears in the school timetable and why this course is part of your B.Ed.

#### Worked example: what Islamic education adds to a lesson

Return to the science teacher in Ayesha's school. He says Islamiat belongs only in the Islamiat period. Here is how an Islamic view of education changes his Class 9 lesson on the human eye, without turning it into an Islamiat lesson.

| Part of the lesson | Without an Islamic view of education | With an Islamic view of education |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Learn the parts of the eye for the examination | Learn the parts of the eye, and see the eye as one of the signs that invite reflection |
| Honesty | Copying the diagram from a friend is a minor matter | Copying is dishonest; the pupil's own work is a trust |
| Use of knowledge | Knowledge is for marks | Knowledge is also for service, such as helping a grandparent understand an eye test |

The science is the same in both columns. What changes is the purpose, the honesty and the use of knowledge. That is what Islamic education adds.

### Islam as a complete code of life

#### What "complete code of life" means

The Arabic word often translated as "religion" is ***din***. Its meaning is wider than the English word "religion", which many people today use for private worship only. *Din* means a whole way of life: what a person believes, how they worship, how they behave, and how they deal with others. A **complete code of life** is guidance that covers all of these areas, not only worship.

The Qur'an supports this meaning in several places.

- It tells believers to "enter into Islām completely" (2:208), not to keep it for one part of life.
- It teaches the Prophet (peace be upon him) to say that "my prayer, my rites of sacrifice, my living and my dying are for Allāh" (6:162). Prayer and the whole of life are placed side by side.
- It says, "This day I have perfected for you your religion" (5:3). The guidance is complete.

The concept map below shows the main areas of life this guidance covers.

<Figure id="fig-U1-1" src="/img/figures/giss-401/unit-01/fig-U1-1.svg" alt="Concept map with Islam as a complete code of life at the centre, linked to six connected areas of life: belief, worship, morals, society, economy and the state." />

The six areas are not separate boxes. **Belief** is the root. It gives meaning to **worship**. Worship trains **morals**. Morals shape how a person behaves in the **family and society**, how they earn and spend in the **economy**, and how rulers and citizens act in the **state**. Units 3, 4 and 5 of this course study the later areas in detail.

#### What "complete" does and does not mean

"Complete" does not mean that the Qur'an and Sunnah list a ruling for every new machine or situation. It means that they give fixed principles for every area of life, such as justice, honesty, mercy and the avoidance of harm, and that Muslims apply these principles to new situations. Scholars have methods for doing this, which Unit 2 explains. A teacher in 2026 will not find a verse about mobile phones in class, but will find clear principles about honesty, about wasting time and about the rights of others.

#### Worked example: one school day through the six areas

Here is how the six areas appear in one ordinary day of a Muslim teacher in a Pakistani school.

| Time | What happens | Area of life |
|---|---|---|
| Before school | She prays Fajr and asks Allah for help with a difficult class | Belief and worship |
| Zuhr prayer | In the school prayer room the principal and the peon stand side by side in one row | Society: equality before Allah |
| Second period | A pupil from a poor family has no copy; she treats him with the same respect as others | Morals: justice and mercy |
| Lunch | She refuses a gift from a parent who wants higher marks for his son | Economy: halal and haram earning |
| Staff meeting | The principal asks teachers for their views before deciding the exam timetable | State and public life: consultation |
| Evening | She marks papers carefully, knowing she will answer for her work | Belief: accountability in the Hereafter |

Notice that only one row is about a ritual. The others are about fairness, honesty, equality and consultation. The same teacher who prays at Fajr is guided by Islam when she marks papers at night. That is what "a complete code of life" means in practice.

#### Correcting the misconception again

We can now answer Hina. Islamiat is not "just these rules". The rules of *wudu* and prayer are part of a larger whole that also covers honesty in homework, fair treatment of classmates and the purpose of learning. The science teacher's view also needs correcting. His subject is not Islamiat, but under a complete code of life his lesson is still guided by Islamic values of honesty, reflection and service.

## Activity: Sort the questions

Work in groups of four. About 30 minutes.

1. Each member writes three questions about Islam that pupils or family members have really asked them. Examples: "Can I pray in my school uniform?", "Why did the Prophet migrate to Madinah?", "Is it wrong to sell a phone with a hidden fault?", "Why do we believe in angels?"
2. As a group, sort all twelve questions into the branches of Islamic Studies in the table of branches. Some questions may fit two branches; say why.
3. Now place each question on one of the six areas of life in the concept map.
4. Count how many of your twelve questions are about rituals and how many are about other areas. Prepare a two-minute report for the class: what does your count show about the scope of Islamic Studies?

## Check your understanding

1. Give the meaning of the word *Islam* and say what makes a person a Muslim. *(Remember)*
2. Define Islamic Studies in one sentence, then name four of its branches. *(Remember)*
3. Explain, using two Qur'anic references, why knowledge holds a high place in Islam. *(Understand)*
4. A pupil asks, "Is it allowed to pay a bribe to get a job?" Name the branch of Islamic Studies and the area of life this question belongs to, and give a reason for each. *(Apply)*
5. A colleague says, "Islam is a private matter between me and Allah; it has nothing to do with how I run my classroom." Write two sentences, each using a Qur'anic reference, to explain what this view leaves out. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Islam means submission to Allah, freely chosen. Islamic Studies is the organised study of Islam, with branches that run from the Qur'an and Hadith through belief, law, history and morals to social, economic and political thought. Its scope is far wider than the rituals many pupils associate with Islamiat.

Islam gives knowledge a very high place. The first revelation was a command to read, and the Qur'an asks whether those who know are equal to those who do not. Islamic education aims at the whole person: knowing Allah, building character and serving others. In Pakistan, Article 31 of the Constitution asks the state to make the teaching of the Qur'an and Islamiat compulsory.

Islam is a *din*, a complete code of life. Belief is its root, and its guidance reaches worship, morals, family and society, the economy and the state. "Complete" means fixed principles for every area, applied to new situations, not a separate rule for every case.

## Try this at your practicum school

During one practicum week, keep a simple log of moments when Islamic teaching appears outside the Islamiat period: in assembly, in another subject, in the way staff treat pupils, or in a rule of the school. Note also one moment where a teaching of Islam, such as honesty or fairness, was ignored. Write a short reflection in your journal: in this school, is Islam treated as one subject or as a complete code of life? Give your evidence.

1. Explain the meaning of Islam and the scope of Islamic Studies, using at least three branches.
