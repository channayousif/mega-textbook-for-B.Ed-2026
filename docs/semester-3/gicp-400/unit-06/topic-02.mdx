## A real classroom situation

Bilal is a trainee at a boys' secondary school in Hyderabad. He runs a mock Parliament with Class 10. The class proposes lowering the voting age to sixteen, and most hands go up. "It has passed, Sir. We have a majority!" one boy says. Bilal is pleased, until he notices that the voting age is written in Article 51(2) of the Constitution.

In the staff room later, he mentions it to a senior colleague. "Amendments?" the colleague says. "The Eighteenth one only changed the name of the NWFP. Nothing else happened." Bilal now has two problems. His class thinks a simple majority can change the Constitution, and his colleague thinks a major amendment changed almost nothing. What should he say to each?

A Bill altering the limits of a Province also needs two-thirds of the total membership of that Provincial Assembly. It then goes to the President for assent, and the Constitution is amended." />

## Explanation

A <strong>constitutional amendment</strong> is a formal change to the words of the Constitution. It adds, removes or replaces a provision. This topic asks how that happens, what three major amendments did, and whether they changed the balance of power in the federation. Unit 5 explained <Glossary term="Federalism" />. Here you test it against evidence.

### Procedure of amendments

Article 238 says the Constitution may be amended by Act of the Majlis-e-Shoora. Article 239 sets a much harder route than an ordinary Bill.

| Question | Ordinary Bill (Article 70) | Constitution Amendment Bill (Article 239) |
|---|---|---|
| Votes needed | A majority of those voting | Two-thirds of the **total membership** of each House |
| If the Houses disagree | A joint sitting can decide, by a majority of those present and voting | No joint sitting route. Each House must reach two-thirds by itself |
| Provincial consent | Not needed | Needed from a Province if its limits would change (Article 239(4)) |
| After passage | President's assent (Article 75) | President's assent (Article 239(2)-(3)) |

**Correcting a common misconception: "Any law can change the Constitution."** An ordinary Act cannot. If an ordinary law conflicts with the Constitution, the Constitution prevails. This is why Bilal's class has not changed the voting age. Article 51(2)(b) fixes it at eighteen, so only an Amendment Bill can lower it. A class vote is a good start. It is one House with a simple majority.

**A worked example: is the vote enough?** "Total membership" is the phrase that catches people. It means all the seats, and not only the members in the room. Using the full seat numbers, the National Assembly needs 224 votes (two-thirds of 336), and the Senate needs 64 (two-thirds of 96).

*Case:* An Amendment Bill gets 230 votes in the National Assembly. In the Senate, 80 members are present, and 62 vote for it.

1. **National Assembly:** 230 is at least 224. It passes.
2. **Senate:** 62 out of 80 is more than three-quarters of those present, which looks strong. But the test is 64 of the total membership, and 62 is below that. It fails.
3. **Can a joint sitting rescue it?** No. Article 239 gives no such route.

**The special rule for provincial limits.** Under Article 239(4), a Bill that would alter a Province's limits cannot go to the President until that Province's own Assembly has passed it by two-thirds of its total membership. The Twenty-fifth Amendment shows this rule at work. It joined the Federally Administered Tribal Areas to Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, so the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Assembly had to agree. It voted on 27 May 2018, with 92 votes for and seven against (FAFEN, 2018). This protects a Province against having its territory changed without its own consent.

**Can a court strike down an amendment?** Article 239(5) says no court may call in question any amendment on any ground, and Article 239(6) says there is no limit on Parliament's power to amend. Courts have not always agreed. A case note from LUMS records that a majority of the Supreme Court held in 2015 that it could review amendments (Ashfaq, n.d.). The Twenty-seventh Amendment of 2025 rewrote clause (5) to add the words "any judgment of any court". For now, you should teach what the text says and tell learners that this question has been contested.

### Major amendments: 18th, 19th and 25th

The most recent amendment in the text used here is the Twenty-seventh, of 2025. The course guide names three for special study.

<Figure id="fig-U6-4" src="/img/figures/gicp-400/unit-06/fig-U6-4.svg" alt="Table comparing the 18th, 19th and 25th Amendments by year, main change and effect on federalism. The 18th, 2010, removed the Concurrent List, limited the President's powers, added rights and created the Judicial Commission; its effect was large, with law-making and resources moving toward the Provinces. The 19th, in force 2011, changed how judges are appointed under Article 175A; its effect was small and indirect. The 25th, 2018, merged the tribal areas into the Provinces and reduced seats in both Houses; its effect was large for the merged areas, where federal rule ended and provincial government began." />

**The Eighteenth Amendment (assent 19 April 2010).** Its preamble says the people struggled for a State where citizens' rights are secured and the Provinces have an equitable share in the Federation. Hussain and Kokab (2012) report that a committee representing all parties in Parliament prepared it. It is very long, but its changes fall into five groups.

| Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Undoing past military changes | Repealed the Legal Framework Order 2002 and the Seventeenth Amendment; widened the high treason article (Article 6) |
| Parliamentary balance | President acts on advice within time limits (Article 48); wide dissolution power removed (Article 58(2)(b)); Prime Minister elected by the Assembly (Article 91); Cabinet size capped (Article 92) |
| Rights | Added the right to education (Article 25A), and rights to fair trial and information (Hussain & Kokab, 2012) |
| Provinces | Removed the Concurrent List (Article 142); gave provinces more say in shared bodies and resources (see below); renamed the North-West Frontier Province as Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and corrected the spelling of Balochistan and Sindh (Article 1) |
| Judges | Created the Judicial Commission (Article 175A), with a Parliamentary Committee to confirm its nominations |

**Correcting a common misconception: "The Eighteenth Amendment only renamed a province."** The renaming was section 3 of the Act, and it was only one change among many. It was also the most visible one, and the debate around it became heated (Hussain & Kokab, 2012, section 14). That may be why people remember it. A teacher should not repeat this story, because the rest of the Act changed how Pakistan is governed. It also touches your own work: the old Concurrent List included "curriculum, syllabus, planning, policy" (entry 38), and that entry no longer exists.

**The Nineteenth Amendment (in force 1 January 2011).** This amendment was about judges. Soon after the Eighteenth Amendment, its new appointment system was challenged in *Nadeem Ahmed v. Federation of Pakistan*. The Supreme Court did not strike it down. Instead, on 30 September 2010, it referred the matter back to Parliament with suggestions: more judges on the Commission, and reasons from the Committee if it refused a nominee (Ashfaq, n.d.). Parliament then acted (Constitution (Nineteenth Amendment) Act, 2010).

- It raised the number of Supreme Court judges on the Commission from two to four.
- It required the Parliamentary Committee to record its reasons when it refused a nomination by a three-fourths majority, and to send those reasons to the Commission. The Commission would then send another name.
- It made provisions for the Islamabad High Court, and it updated the list of tribal areas in Article 246.

People disagree about this change. Those who challenged the Eighteenth Amendment's system argued that it harmed judicial independence. The same case note takes another view: it argues that the Nineteenth gave up much of the role that the Eighteenth had given to elected members. The system has since changed again. The Twenty-sixth Amendment (2024) removed that Committee from ordinary judicial appointments, and the Twenty-seventh (2025) changed the Commission's membership. So teach the Nineteenth as an important step in a continuing argument, and not as today's rule.

**The Twenty-fifth Amendment (in force 31 May 2018).** This amendment changed the map. Its Statement of Objects and Reasons says it aimed to bring the people of the tribal areas into the mainstream. Before it, the Federally Administered Tribal Areas (FATA) were outside any Province, and the federal executive authority extended to them under Article 247. The courts' jurisdiction there was also limited unless Parliament provided otherwise. The amendment did the following.

- It merged FATA into Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and merged the provincially administered tribal areas into their Provinces (Article 246(d)).
- It omitted Article 247.
- It reduced the National Assembly from 342 seats to 336, and the Senate from 104 to 96 (Articles 51 and 59).
- It set Khyber Pakhtunkhwa's Assembly at 145 seats, to include the merged areas (Article 106).

### Impact on federalism

Do not just say that an amendment "helped the provinces". Test it. Ask four questions: who makes the law, who holds the money, who decides together, and whose territory is it?

| Question | 18th Amendment | 19th Amendment | 25th Amendment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who makes the law? | Concurrent List removed. Subjects not on the Federal List go to the Provinces, except criminal law, procedure and evidence, which are shared (Article 142) | No change | Provincial law can now reach the merged areas |
| Who holds the money? | A Province's share in each National Finance Commission Award cannot fall below the previous Award (Article 160(3A)). Oil and gas in a Province are owned jointly and equally with the Federation (Article 172(3)) | No change | Merged areas join a Province's budget and duties |
| Who decides together? | Council of Common Interests strengthened: the Prime Minister chairs it, with the Chief Ministers and three federal members; it meets at least every ninety days (Articles 153-154). A Province's Assembly must pass a resolution before an emergency for internal disturbance (Article 232) | Very little | Fewer seats in both Houses overall, but more provincial seats |
| Whose territory? | No change, apart from the renaming | A small update to the tribal areas list | Largest change: federal rule in FATA ended |

**A worked example: making a judgement.** Suppose you must judge this claim: "The Eighteenth Amendment made the Provinces more autonomous."

1. **Evidence for.** Provinces now make law on subjects such as curriculum. Article 160(3A) protects their finance share. Article 172(3) gives them joint ownership of oil and gas. The Governor must be a voter and resident of the Province (Article 101).
2. **Evidence of limits.** The Federal Legislative List remains long, and Part II, entry 12 still keeps "standards in institutions for higher education and research" at the federal level. Article 143 lets a valid federal law prevail over a conflicting provincial law. Article 232 still allows emergencies.
3. **Voices that disagree.** Hussain and Kokab (2012, section 12) report that autonomist parties wanted more, with some wanting only four subjects left to the federation. Others thought that removing the list went too far. They also warn that Provinces received new costs while already short of money.
4. **Judgement.** Provincial autonomy clearly grew in law and in shared bodies. How much it grew in practice depends on whether Provinces have the money and capacity to use it. The evidence supports "much more autonomous on paper, with real limits and uneven delivery". It does not support "fully autonomous" or "nothing changed".

Notice that the conclusion has a limit. Hussain and Kokab wrote soon after 2010, so their paper cannot tell you how things went afterwards. A careful writer says so.

**Correcting a final misconception: "Every major amendment increases provincial power."** The table above shows otherwise. The Nineteenth barely touched federalism. The Twenty-fifth extended provincial government to new areas, but it also reduced total seats. Each amendment must be judged by what it actually did.

**Returning to Bilal.** He tells his class, "You have a majority, and that is a good beginning. Now find 224 in the National Assembly and 64 in the Senate." He gives his colleague a friendly question: "Who decides our curriculum now, and since when?" He does not need to argue. The answer to that question makes the point.

## Activity: Amendment tribunal

Work in groups of six, divided into three pairs. About 30 minutes. Your class is deciding which of the three amendments mattered most for federalism.

1. **Prepare (10 minutes).** Each pair takes one amendment. Use the four questions from this topic. Find at least two provisions as evidence, and one limit of your own amendment.
2. **Present (9 minutes).** Each pair has three minutes. The other pairs note the strongest point and the weakest point.
3. **Vote test (5 minutes).** Together, invent one Amendment Bill with vote numbers. Work out whether it passes under Article 239.
4. **Decide (6 minutes).** Agree on a ranking. If you cannot agree, write down exactly what you disagree about, and what evidence would settle it.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State the vote required in each House for a Constitution Amendment Bill under Article 239.
2. *(Remember)* Name the year of the Eighteenth Amendment and two things it changed besides a province's name.
3. *(Understand)* Explain why the phrase "total membership" makes an amendment harder to pass.
4. *(Apply)* A Bill gets 220 votes in the National Assembly and 70 in the Senate. Using the full seat numbers, work out whether it passes, and explain why.
5. *(Apply)* A Bill would move a village from Sindh into Balochistan. Which extra approval does Article 239(4) require, and by what vote?

## Summary

The Constitution can be changed, but it takes more than an ordinary vote: two-thirds of the total membership of each House, and a Province's own consent if its limits would change. The Eighteenth Amendment changed law-making, money, shared institutions, rights and judicial appointments, so it was much more than a renaming. The Nineteenth was mainly about judges. The Twenty-fifth ended separate federal rule in the tribal areas. Their effects on federalism differ, so each one must be judged by evidence, and "more autonomy on paper" must be checked against what happens in practice.

## Summative task

Write 350-400 words evaluating this claim: "The Eighteenth Amendment gave the Provinces real autonomy." Your answer should:

1. Show at least three changes, using the articles.
2. Weigh at least two limits or criticisms, using Hussain and Kokab (2012) and the Constitution.
3. Reach a clear judgement, and state what evidence you would still need.
