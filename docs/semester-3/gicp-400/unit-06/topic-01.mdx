## A real classroom situation

Sana is a trainee teaching Pakistan Studies to Class 9 at a government girls' high school in Larkana. A pupil, Mehak, brings a newspaper with a government notice that says "the President is pleased to appoint". She says, "See, Miss. The President runs the government." Another pupil objects: "No, the Prime Minister does. My uncle says so." A third adds, "But judges can stop both of them."

All three pupils have noticed something real, and they cannot all be fully right. Sana wants to do more than give them a name. She wants to show them how to find the answer in the Constitution. How would you do it?

## Explanation

Unit 5 introduced <Glossary term="Separation of Powers" />: the work of the State is divided so that no single body holds all of it. This topic looks inside each part at the federal level. Each Province has its own smaller version: a Provincial Assembly, a Chief Minister with a Cabinet, and a High Court.

### Legislature

The <Glossary term="Legislature" /> makes laws. Pakistan's federal legislature is the **Majlis-e-Shoora (Parliament)**. Under Article 50 it consists of the President and two Houses: the National Assembly and the Senate. So the President is formally part of Parliament, although the President does not sit or vote in either House.

The two Houses are built on different ideas.

| | National Assembly | Senate |
|---|---|---|
| Size | 336 seats (Article 51(1)): 266 general, 60 for women, 10 for non-Muslims | 96 members (Article 59(1)) |
| Who elects them | Voters aged 18 or over choose general members directly. Women and non-Muslim members come through party lists, in proportion to general seats won | Provincial Assembly members elect each Province's senators. The Federal Capital's four are chosen as the President prescribes by Order |
| Basis of seats | Population, using the last published census (Article 51(5)) | Equal: each Province has 23 |
| Term | Five years, unless dissolved sooner (Article 52) | Cannot be dissolved. Members serve six years, and about half retire every three years (Article 59(3)) |

**Why two Houses?** Look at the numbers. In the National Assembly, Punjab has 173 of the seats allocated to the Provinces and the Federal Capital, Sindh has 75 and Balochistan has 20 (Article 51(3)). In the Senate, each of them has 23. The National Assembly gives weight to people. The Senate gives weight to the federating units, so a Province with a small population still has an equal voice in one House.

**What the Majlis-e-Shoora does.** It has four main jobs.

1. **It makes federal laws.** Article 70 sets the route for a Bill on the Federal Legislative List.
2. **It controls money.** No federal tax can be levied except by or under an Act (Article 77). A Money Bill, including the budget, starts in the National Assembly. The Senate has fourteen days to make recommendations, but the Assembly decides (Article 73).
3. **It holds the government to account.** The Cabinet is collectively responsible to both Houses (Article 91(6)). The National Assembly chooses the Prime Minister and can remove the Prime Minister.
4. **It can amend the Constitution.** Topic 6.2 covers this.

**A worked example: following an ordinary Bill.** Suppose a Bill on the national currency starts in the National Assembly.

1. The Assembly passes it, and it goes to the Senate (Article 70(1)).
2. Suppose the Senate rejects it. The Bill is not dead. At the request of the National Assembly, a joint sitting of both Houses can consider it. A majority of those present and voting is enough to pass it (Article 70(3)).
3. It goes to the President, who has ten days to assent or to send it back for reconsideration (Article 75(1)).
4. Suppose the President sends it back. A joint sitting passes it again. If the President does not assent within ten more days, assent is deemed to have been given (Article 75(2)).

So the President can delay an ordinary Bill, but cannot finally stop it. Notice who has the last word.

### Executive

The <Glossary term="Executive" /> carries out the law and runs the government. Two offices cause most of the confusion.

**The President** is the Head of State and "shall represent the unity of the Republic" (Article 41(1)). An electoral college of both Houses and all the Provincial Assemblies elects the President (Article 41(3)) for five years, and no one may serve more than two consecutive terms (Article 44).

**The Prime Minister** is elected by the National Assembly from among its Muslim members, by a majority of its total membership (Article 91(3)-(4)). Article 90(1) says the Federal Government consists of the Prime Minister and the Federal Ministers. It acts through the Prime Minister, "who shall be the chief executive of the Federation". The Cabinet is drawn from Parliament (Article 92(1)) and cannot be larger than eleven per cent of Parliament's total membership.

**Correcting a common misconception: "The President runs the government."** Read three articles together.

| Article | What it says | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 48(1) | The President shall act on and in accordance with the advice of the Cabinet or the Prime Minister | The President may ask once for advice to be reconsidered, within fifteen days, but must then act on the advice given |
| 90(1) | Executive authority is exercised in the President's name by the Federal Government | The name is the President's. The decision is the government's |
| 99(1) | All executive actions are expressed to be taken in the name of the President | This explains the notice that Mehak found |

The words "in the name of" are the key. A notice can say "the President is pleased to appoint" when the real decision was made elsewhere. Article 92(1) is a clear case: the President appoints ministers, but on the advice of the Prime Minister.

The President is not only a figurehead, however. The President acts in discretion where the Constitution gives that power (Article 48(2)). One example is dissolving the National Assembly when a no-confidence vote has passed and no other member can command a majority (Article 58(2)). These are narrow cases. Before 2010, the President could also dissolve the Assembly under a wider power in Article 58(2)(b). The Eighteenth Amendment removed it, and Hussain and Kokab (2012) describe this as part of a wider curtailing of presidential powers. Many older books and older people still remember that time, which is one reason this misconception survives.

**How the executive is held to account.** At least twenty per cent of the National Assembly's total membership can move a no-confidence resolution. If a majority of the total membership passes it, the Prime Minister ceases to hold office (Article 95). The President can be removed too, but only by a two-thirds vote of Parliament's total membership at a joint sitting (Article 47(8)).

**A worked example: who really decided?** Ask three questions of any government action.

*Case:* A notification says the President has appointed a new Federal Minister.

1. **In whose name was it done?** The President's (Article 99(1)).
2. **Who actually decided?** The Prime Minister, whose advice the President must follow (Articles 48(1) and 92(1)).
3. **Who answers for it?** The Cabinet answers to the Senate and the National Assembly (Article 91(6)).

**A local point for teachers.** The federal executive reaches only as far as matters on which Parliament can legislate (Article 97). School education is a provincial matter, as Unit 5 showed. So the Sindh education department belongs to the provincial executive, and not to the Federal Government.

### Judiciary

The <Glossary term="Judiciary" /> interprets the law and applies it to cases. Article 175(1) lists the courts: a Federal Constitutional Court, a Supreme Court, a High Court for each Province and for Islamabad, and other courts set up by law. A court has only the powers given by the Constitution or by law (Article 175(2)).

<Figure id="fig-U6-2" src="/img/figures/gicp-400/unit-06/fig-U6-2.svg" alt="Table comparing the legislature, the executive and the judiciary. The legislature is the President, the National Assembly and the Senate; voters and party lists fill the Assembly and Provincial Assemblies elect the Senate; it makes federal laws, controls federal money, holds the government to account and amends the Constitution. The executive is the President, the Prime Minister and the Federal Ministers; an electoral college elects the President, the National Assembly elects the Prime Minister, and ministers are appointed on the Prime Minister's advice; it carries out the law and runs the government. The judiciary is the judges of the courts; for the superior courts the Judicial Commission nominates and the President appoints; it interprets the law, decides disputes and enforces rights." />

| Court | Main work | Articles |
|---|---|---|
| Subordinate courts | Everyday civil and criminal cases in the districts, under High Court control | 175(1), 203 |
| High Courts | Orders against public authorities and for Fundamental Rights, where no other adequate remedy exists; supervise lower courts | 192, 199, 203 |
| Federal Shariat Court | Decides whether a law is repugnant to the Injunctions of Islam | 203A-203J |
| Supreme Court | Hears appeals from High Courts, except those that now go to the Federal Constitutional Court. Its decisions bind other courts, except that court | 185, 189(2) |
| Federal Constitutional Court | Disputes between governments; Fundamental Rights questions of public importance; appeals on constitutional questions; opinions asked for by the President. Its decisions bind all other courts | 175E, 175F, 175H, 189(1) |

**Correcting a misconception: "The Supreme Court is the final court on every constitutional question."** That was the position in most books you will meet, and it is now out of date. The Twenty-seventh Amendment (2025) created the Federal Constitutional Court and omitted Articles 184 and 186. Their powers now appear in Articles 175E and 175H. The Supreme Court still exists and is still the final court of appeal in many other cases. But Article 189(1) makes the Federal Constitutional Court's decisions binding on all other courts, including the Supreme Court. The new court also reflects federalism: Article 175B says it shall have an equal number of judges from each Province.

**A worked example: which court?** Ask what kind of dispute it is.

1. *A man in Sukkur is charged with theft.* This is an everyday criminal case, so it starts in a subordinate court.
2. *A district officer refuses to act on a girl's school admission and gives no lawful reason, and the law offers no other adequate remedy.* The family can apply to the Sindh High Court under Article 199.
3. *The Federal Government and the Sindh Government disagree about which of them controls a public body.* This is a dispute between governments, so it goes to the Federal Constitutional Court under Article 175E(1). That court gives a declaratory judgment only: it states the legal position (Article 175E(2)).

**How judges are kept apart from the other two organs.** Three rules matter.

- **Appointment.** The Judicial Commission under Article 175A nominates judges. It includes senior judges, the Federal Law Minister, the Attorney-General, a senior advocate, and members of both Houses. High Court appointments add the Provincial Law Minister and a provincial advocate. The President appoints the person nominated, through the Prime Minister (Article 175A(8)).
- **Security in office.** Judges retire at a fixed age, not when a government wishes: sixty-five in the Supreme Court (Article 179) and sixty-eight in the Federal Constitutional Court (Article 175I). They can be removed only through the Supreme Judicial Council, a body of senior judges (Article 209).
- **Limits on criticism.** Parliament may not discuss the conduct of these judges in the discharge of their duties (Article 68).

These rules protect the courts, but independence is not simple. The way judges are appointed has changed several times, and people disagree about whether each change helped. Topic 6.2 returns to this.

**Returning to Sana's class.** Sana puts Articles 48, 90 and 99 on the board. She tells Mehak, "You read the notice correctly. It says the President, and that is the name the law requires. Now read Article 92(1). Who gave the advice?" She tells the second pupil that the uncle is right about the chief executive, and she asks the third to find Article 199. All three pupils found one part of the system. Together they have described three organs that check each other.

## Activity: Who holds this power?

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. **Make cards (5 minutes).** Write each action below on a card: pass a federal budget; elect the Prime Minister; choose a Province's senators; appoint a minister on advice; remove a Prime Minister by vote; order an official to act lawfully; decide a dispute between the Federal and Sindh Governments; send a Bill back once for reconsideration; decide a theft case; nominate a new High Court judge; set policy as chief executive; check whether a law conflicts with the Injunctions of Islam.
2. **Sort (10 minutes).** Place each card under Legislature, Executive or Judiciary, and write the body and the article on it. Some cards may need a body from one organ acting on another. Do not look back until you have tried.
3. **Challenge (5 minutes).** Swap with another group, and mark any card you disagree with. Use the article to settle it.
4. **Connect (5 minutes).** Choose one card. Explain how another organ could check that power.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the three parts of the Majlis-e-Shoora under Article 50.
2. *(Understand)* In two or three sentences, explain why each Province has 23 members in the Senate but a different number of seats in the National Assembly.
3. *(Apply)* A notice reads, "The President has been pleased to appoint Mr X as a Federal Minister." A pupil says this proves the President chose him. Using two articles, explain what you would say.
4. *(Apply)* A family's dispute is about a public authority that refuses to do what the law requires, and no other adequate remedy exists. Name the court and the article.

## Summary

The federal government has three organs. The Majlis-e-Shoora makes laws, controls money and holds the government to account, and its two Houses balance people against Provinces. The Prime Minister and Cabinet hold real executive power and answer to Parliament. The President is Head of State and acts mostly on advice, even though orders carry the President's name. The courts interpret the law, and since 2025 the Federal Constitutional Court stands at the top on constitutional questions. To know who really holds a power, read the article and do not stop at the name on the notice.
