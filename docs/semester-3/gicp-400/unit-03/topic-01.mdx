# Topic 3.1 - From independence to the 1956 Constitution

## A real classroom situation

During a social studies lesson at a Government Boys High School in Larkana, Sindh, a trainee teacher introduces a unit on the political history of Pakistan. To gauge prior knowledge, the trainee asks the Grade 10 students a straightforward question: "What constitution governed Pakistan when the country achieved independence on 14 August 1947?"

A student in the front row raises his hand and confidently answers: "Pakistan had no constitution at all until 1973. For the first twenty-five years, our government simply operated by executive orders without constitutional law." Another student immediately objects: "That cannot be true. Quaid-e-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah presented the Objectives Resolution on the very first day of independence, and that served as our first complete constitution."

The trainee teacher quickly notes the confusion. The class holds two widespread misconceptions: first, that Pakistan operated in a legal vacuum prior to 1973, and second, that the Objectives Resolution was a complete, enacted constitution. To build historical and constitutional clarity, the teacher sketches a timeline on the board. The trainee explains that Pakistan inherited an established interim legal framework under the adapted Government of India Act 1935, adopted the Objectives Resolution in 1949 as an overarching philosophical guide, and enacted its first written constitution in 1956 after nine years of rigorous debate.

## Explanation

Pakistan needed rules for collecting revenue, running courts and deciding who could make laws. An interim constitution supplied those rules while the Constituent Assembly prepared a permanent one.

### The interim constitution: the Government of India Act 1935, as adapted

The legal foundation of Pakistan from 14 August 1947 until 23 March 1956 was provided by Section 8 of the Indian Independence Act 1947. This statutory provision stipulated that each new dominion would be governed as nearly as possible in accordance with the Government of India Act 1935, subject to any omissions, additions, and adaptations made by the Governor-General under the Pakistan (Provisional Constitution) Order 1947 (`goi-act-1935`).

The Government of India Act 1935 was originally enacted by the British Parliament to establish a federal structure in colonial India. To adapt this imperial statute for an independent, sovereign dominion, significant alterations were made:

1. **Elimination of British Imperial Control**: British parliamentary control ended and references to colonial supervision required adaptation. Pakistan remained a dominion with the British monarch as head of state until 1956. The Governor-General represented the Crown, while Pakistani institutions gained legislative authority independent of the British Parliament.
2. **Removal of Discretionary Powers**: Under the colonial regime, provincial governors and the governor-general exercised vast discretionary powers to bypass elected ministries. In the adapted framework, these discretionary vetoes were removed to establish a system of cabinet responsibility.
3. **Dual Role of the Constituent Assembly**: The Constituent Assembly of Pakistan, established in July 1947 and addressed by Quaid-e-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah on 11 August 1947, performed two separate constitutional functions. First, it acted as a constitution-making body to draft a permanent constitution. Second, it acted as the Federal <Glossary term="Legislature" /> to debate and pass ordinary laws, approve budgets, and oversee the federal executive.

#### Worked pedagogical example: Federal distribution under the interim constitution

To help school students grasp how the interim constitution operated in daily life, teachers can analyze how legislative powers were divided. The adapted 1935 Act maintained three comprehensive legislative lists:

- **Federal Legislative List**: Subjects of national importance reserved exclusively for the central government in Karachi, including defence, foreign affairs, currency, foreign trade, and national communications.
- **Provincial Legislative List**: Subjects assigned exclusively to provincial assemblies in Sindh, Punjab, the North-West Frontier Province, and East Bengal, including public order, education, agriculture, public health, and local government.
- **Concurrent Legislative List**: Subjects upon which both the federal legislature and provincial assemblies could legislate, such as civil procedure, criminal law, and commercial contracts, with federal statutes prevailing in the event of direct conflict.

When a teacher in Larkana received their provincial salary in 1950, or when a civil judge in Sukkur heard a property lawsuit, they acted under statutory powers derived directly from the adapted Government of India Act 1935. Addressing the common misconception directly, Pakistan never experienced a legal vacuum: it maintained continuous, uninterrupted statutory governance from its first hours of independence.

### The Objectives Resolution

While the adapted 1935 Act provided administrative and statutory machinery, it was an inherited colonial statute lacking ideological identity. In March 1949, Pakistan's First Constituent Assembly undertook the historic task of formulating the ideological foundations of the future constitution.

On 7 March 1949, Prime Minister Liaquat Ali Khan moved a formal resolution before the Constituent Assembly setting out the aims and objects of the constitution. After five days of vigorous debate between the Muslim League leadership and minority representatives, the Constituent Assembly adopted the <Glossary term="Objectives Resolution" /> on 12 March 1949 (`shah-2026-objectives-resolution`).

#### Core principles of the Objectives Resolution

The Objectives Resolution articulated main principles that defined the constitutional character of the Pakistani state (`shah-2026-objectives-resolution`):

1. **Divine Sovereignty and Sacred Trust**: Sovereignty over the entire universe belongs to Allah Almighty alone. The authority delegated by Him to the State of Pakistan through its people, within the limits prescribed by Him, is a sacred trust. This formulation rejected absolute human sovereignty while rejecting theocracy: authority was delegated not to a religious leadership or clergy, but to the collective people.
2. **Popular Democratic Representation**: The state shall exercise its powers and authority through the chosen representatives of the people. This clause firmly anchored Pakistan within modern representative democracy.
3. **Islamic Principles of Social Order**: The principles of democracy, freedom, equality, tolerance, and social justice as enunciated by Islam shall be fully observed.
4. **Enabling the Islamic Way of Life**: Muslims shall be enabled to order their lives in the individual and collective spheres in accordance with the teachings and requirements of Islam as set out in the Holy Qur'an and Sunnah.
5. **Safeguards for Religious Minorities**: Adequate provision shall be made for minorities to freely profess and practise their religions and develop their cultures. Their legitimate interests and civil rights shall be safeguarded.
6. **Guaranteed Fundamental Rights**: Fundamental rights shall be guaranteed, including equality of status, equality of opportunity, equal protection before the law, social, economic, and political justice, and freedom of thought, expression, belief, faith, worship, and association, subject to law and public morality.
7. **Independence of the Judiciary**: The independence of the judiciary shall be fully secured, ensuring that courts can protect citizens against government interference and enforce legal rights without political interference.
8. **Federal Integrity and Provincial Autonomy**: The territories included in or in accession with Pakistan shall form a federation wherein the constituent units shall be autonomous with such boundaries and limitations on their powers as may be prescribed.

#### Addressing the misconception: Resolution versus constitution

A frequent error among learners is treating the Objectives Resolution as if it were a functional, enforceable constitution. The Objectives Resolution contained no administrative articles, established no tax collection rates, created no executive offices, and provided no judicial writ mechanisms.

Instead, as Shah (2026) explains, it served as a constitutional compass: a statement of philosophical values and foundational objectives intended to guide the framing of articles. Its historical importance is reflected in its permanence: it served as the preamble to the 1956, 1962, and 1973 constitutions. In 1985, through Presidential Order No. 14 (the Revival of the Constitution of 1973 Order) and the Eighth Amendment, it was inserted into the legally effective text of the 1973 Constitution as Article 2A, transforming its philosophical declarations into substantive law.

### The Constitution of 1956

Despite the consensus achieved in the Objectives Resolution, the First Constituent Assembly struggled for five years to agree upon a final constitutional draft. Three major constitutional dilemmas delayed progress:

- **The Federal Dilemma and Representation**: East Bengal (later East Pakistan) comprised over 55 percent of the national population but occupied only one-seventh of the country's land area. The western wing comprised Punjab, Sindh, NWFP, the Chief Commissioner’s Province of British Balochistan, and princely states, containing a minority of the population across six-sevenths of the territory. East Bengal demanded representation based strictly on population, which West Pakistani leaders resisted out of fear of permanent Bengali majoritarian dominance.
- **The State Language Controversy**: The declaration by central leaders that Urdu alone would be the national language ignited profound protest across East Bengal, sparking the historic Language Movement of 1952.
- **The Islamic Character of the State**: Intense debate emerged between modernist leaders, who envisioned a progressive parliamentary democracy inspired by Islamic social ethics, and religious scholars, who demanded formal institutional mechanisms to vet all state laws for theological conformity.

#### The constitutional crisis of 1954

In 1953, Prime Minister Khawaja Nazimuddin was dismissed by Governor-General Ghulam Muhammad, despite Nazimuddin commanding the confidence of the Constituent Assembly. When the Assembly subsequently passed legislation in September 1954 curbing the Governor-General's arbitrary dismissal powers, Ghulam Muhammad dissolved the First Constituent Assembly on 24 October 1954 and declared a state of emergency (`abbas-2023-constitutional-crisis`).

Moulvi Tamizuddin Khan, President of the Assembly, challenged its dissolution in the Chief Court of Sindh and initially won. In April 1955, the Federal Court reversed that decision because the law giving the Chief Court power to issue the requested writs had not received the Governor-General's assent. A writ is a court order directing a public authority to act or stop acting. The Court therefore held that the Chief Court lacked jurisdiction, meaning legal authority to hear the claim. Do not confuse this ruling with the later Governor-General's Reference in May 1955, which relied on necessity to address the wider constitutional crisis. Together these events weakened the Assembly, but their legal grounds differed ([Newberg, 1995, chapter 2](https://lib.zu.edu.pk/ebookdata/Law/Judging%20the%20State%20Courts%20and%20Constitutional%20Politics%20in%20Pakistan-by%20Paula%20R.%20Newberg.pdf)).

A Second Constituent Assembly was convened in 1955. To resolve the representation deadlock, the central leadership merged the western provinces and other territories into a single administrative unit called the "One Unit" in October 1955. This administrative fusion created two provincial units without making their populations equal. Equal legislative representation, called parity, was a political compromise between East and West Pakistan, paving the way for the passage of the Constitution of 1956 on 29 February 1956. The constitution received the Governor-General's assent and came into force on 23 March 1956, marking the birth of the Islamic Republic of Pakistan.

#### Salient features of the 1956 Constitution

The 1956 Constitution established the following main arrangements (Khan, 2017; National Assembly, *Parliamentary history*):

1. **Title and State Character**: Pakistan was formally designated the "Islamic Republic of Pakistan" (Article 1).
2. **Form of Government**: It instituted a federal, parliamentary democracy modeled on the Westminster system. The President served as head of state, while executive power was wielded by the Prime Minister and a Cabinet collectively responsible to the National Assembly.
3. **Unicameral Legislature**: Unlike most federal nations that employ bicameral parliaments (with a lower house for population and an upper house for provincial equality), the 1956 Constitution created a single chamber: the National Assembly of Pakistan. It comprised 300 members, divided equally (150 seats each) between East Pakistan and West Pakistan under the principle of parity, with 10 additional seats reserved for women (5 per province) for a period of ten years.
4. **Federation and Legislative Lists**: The federation comprised two provinces: East Pakistan and West Pakistan. Federal and provincial governments shared power through Federal, Provincial and Concurrent legislative lists. Concurrent subjects were areas where both levels could make laws.
5. **Fundamental Rights**: Part II guaranteed enforceable fundamental rights, including freedom of speech, assembly, association, movement, equality before the law, and protection of property. The High Courts and Supreme Court were granted constitutional writ jurisdiction to strike down any legislation violating these rights.
6. **Islamic Provisions**: The Objectives Resolution was incorporated as the Preamble. The President was required to be a Muslim aged forty or older. Article 198 enacted a rule against laws conflicting with Islam stating that no law could be enacted contrary to the injunctions of Islam as laid down in the Holy Qur'an and Sunnah, and an advisory commission was established to recommend measures bringing existing laws into conformity with Islamic principles.
7. **Official Languages**: Resolving the language controversy, Urdu and Bengali were recognized as state languages. English could continue for official purposes during a twenty-year transition.

The 1956 Constitution represented an intricate political compromise. However, it suffered from severe structural flaws: the One Unit scheme alienated smaller provinces like Sindh, Balochistan, and the NWFP, while the parity formula denied East Bengal its population-based majority. Plagued by executive intrigue, palace politics, and delayed general elections, the constitution survived for only two and a half years before being abrogated by President Iskander Mirza under martial law on 7 October 1958.

## Summary

The period from 1947 to 1956 established the foundational precedents of Pakistan's constitutional identity. Rather than operating in an anarchic legal vacuum, the newborn state maintained essential administrative continuity through the Government of India Act 1935 as adapted by the Indian Independence Act 1947.

In March 1949, the First Constituent Assembly adopted the historic Objectives Resolution, establishing the enduring doctrine that sovereignty over the universe belongs to Allah Almighty alone, with governing authority delegated as a sacred trust to the people through elected representatives.

After years of political deadlock over federalism, minority rights, language, and population representation, the Second Constituent Assembly promulgated the Constitution of 1956. This charter established a parliamentary Islamic Republic characterized by a single-chamber legislature based on East-West parity, provincial integration under the One Unit scheme, enforceable fundamental rights, and bilingual parity between Urdu and Bengali.
