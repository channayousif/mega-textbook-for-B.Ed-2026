# Topic 3.2 - The 1962 Constitution

## A real classroom situation

During a secondary school civics practicum at a government higher secondary school in Sukkur, Sindh, a student teacher prepares a lesson on Pakistan's second constitution. To activate discussion, the trainee asks: "How did the Constitution of 1962 distribute executive power between the prime minister and the president?"

A student immediately answers: "Just like the 1956 Constitution and today's government, the prime minister ran the cabinet, while the president was a ceremonial figurehead who signed bills into law." Another student counters: "There was no prime minister or parliament at all under General Ayub Khan. Pakistan was governed solely by military martial law regulations without any written constitution."

The trainee teacher recognizes that the class confuses military martial law (1958–1962) with the civilian constitutional regime that followed. Ayub Khan did not govern without a constitution after 1962; he enacted a formal, written constitution on 1 March 1962 that established an executive-dominated presidential system. The trainee brings out two comparative charts: one illustrating the centralized power structure under the 1962 document, and another comparing its authoritarian features directly against the parliamentary framework of 1956.

## Explanation

The promulgation of the Constitution of 1962 was the culmination of a dramatic shift in Pakistan's political order. On 7 October 1958, President Iskander Mirza abrogated the 1956 Constitution, dissolved the national and provincial assemblies, dismissed the central and provincial cabinets, banned political parties, and declared nationwide <Glossary term="Martial law" /> with General Muhammad Ayub Khan, Commander-in-Chief of the Pakistan Army, appointed as Chief Martial Law Administrator.

Twenty days later, on 27 October 1958, Ayub Khan forced Mirza into exile and assumed the presidency, inaugurating three and a half years of direct military rule.

### Salient features of the 1962 Constitution

Determined to replace the parliamentary system - which he claimed was inherently unstable and unsuited to the genius of the Pakistani people - Ayub Khan appointed a Constitution Commission in February 1960 headed by Justice Muhammad Shahabuddin.

The Commission reported in 1961. The resulting constitution was issued under Ayub Khan rather than adopted by an elected constituent assembly. It was announced on 1 March 1962 and came into force on 8 June, ending martial law. A written constitution now existed, but this did not make its system parliamentary (National Assembly, *Parliamentary history*).

#### Institutional architecture of the 1962 Constitution

Its main features included:

1. **Presidential Supremacy**: The President was head of state and head of government. The Constitution set eligibility rules for the presidency. The President appointed all federal ministers, provincial governors, superior court judges, and senior civil and military officers. Ministers were personal advisors responsible solely to the President; they were barred from holding seats in the National Assembly.
2. **Unicameral Legislature with Curtailed Authority**: The National Assembly was single-chamber, consisting of 156 members divided equally between East Pakistan (78 seats) and West Pakistan (78 seats), including 6 seats reserved for women (3 per province). The Assembly's legislative powers were severely restricted: the President could return bills and had strong powers over the Assembly. These powers had constitutional procedures and limits; they were not an unlimited personal licence to rule. Furthermore, when the Assembly was not in session, the President could issue Ordinances having the immediate force of law.
3. **Severe Budgetary Restrictions**: The 1962 Constitution stripped the National Assembly of sovereign financial control. State expenditure was divided into "recurring expenditure" (charged upon the central consolidated fund, including executive salaries, debt servicing, and ongoing projects) and "new expenditure". The Assembly could vote only on new taxation and new expenditure; it had no constitutional power to reject or reduce recurring expenditure, ensuring the President remained financially autonomous from legislative oversight.
4. **The Basic Democracies Electoral System**: Direct universal adult suffrage was abolished for national and provincial elections. Under the Basic Democracies Order 1959, the country was divided into 80,000 local electoral wards (40,000 in East Pakistan and 40,000 in West Pakistan). The adult public voted directly only to elect local ward members, termed "Basic Democrats". These 80,000 local councillors then served as an exclusive indirect Electoral College to elect the President of Pakistan, the National Assembly, and the two Provincial Assemblies.
5. **Initial Omission of Fundamental Rights**: In stark contrast to the 1956 Constitution, the original 1962 text contained no court-enforceable chapter on fundamental rights. Instead, it provided twelve non-enforceable "Principles of Law-Making" (Article 6), which explicitly stated that the validity of a law could not be challenged in any court on the ground that it violated basic human liberties. This sparked massive protests across the country, led by bar associations, university students, and opposition leaders. Forced to compromise, the regime enacted the Constitution (First Amendment) Act 1963 (Act I of 1964) on 10 January 1964, which restored Part II fundamental rights, revived judicial writ jurisdiction, and officially changed the state title from the "Republic of Pakistan" back to the "Islamic Republic of Pakistan".
6. **Islamic Provisions and Institutions**: While the Objectives Resolution was retained as the Preamble, the 1962 Constitution created an Advisory Council of Islamic Ideology to examine whether laws conformed to the injunctions of Islam. However, its recommendations were strictly advisory: courts had no power to strike down legislation based on Islamic non-conformity.

### Causes of its abrogation

Although Field Marshal Ayub Khan heralded the 1962 Constitution as an instrument of political stability and economic modernization, its authoritarian architecture holded internal contradictions that led to its collapse within seven years (`abbas-2023-constitutional-crisis`):

#### 1. Political disvoting rights and bureaucratic corruption

The Basic Democracies system alienated the vast majority of Pakistani citizens. By restricting the national franchise to 80,000 individuals, the system became highly susceptible to administrative coercion, police intimidation, and state patronage. Deputy commissioners and civil administrators routinely pressured Basic Democrats to vote for government candidates, destroying public confidence in the integrity of the ballot.

#### 2. The disputed 1965 presidential election

In the January 1965 presidential election, the Combined Opposition Parties (COP) rallied behind Mohtarma Fatima Jinnah, the sister of Quaid-e-Azam. Despite immense popular enthusiasm and massive street rallies supporting Fatima Jinnah, Ayub Khan secured 63 percent of the Basic Democrats' votes. Widespread state manipulation, state media monopolies, and administrative harassment of opposition electors discredited the regime and demonstrated that the constitutional machinery was engineered to prevent democratic transfer of power.

#### 3. Deepening regional and economic disparity

The 1962 Constitution maintained the controversial One Unit scheme in West Pakistan, suppressing the linguistic and administrative identity of Sindh, Balochistan, and the NWFP. More fatally, it exacerbated alienation in East Pakistan. Economic planning favored large industrial business groups in West Pakistan, while foreign exchange generated by East Pakistan's jute exports was channeled into western industrialization. In 1966, Sheikh Mujibur Rahman launched the Awami League's Six Points movement, demanding complete regional autonomy, separate currencies or accounting systems, and independent provincial militia, directly challenging the centralist 1962 order.

#### 4. The 1968–1969 mass uprising and constitutional collapse

Following the September 1965 war with India and the 1966 Tashkent Declaration, public discontent erupted into open rebellion. In the autumn of 1968, massive student demonstrations in Rawalpindi and Peshawar ignited a nationwide popular uprising. In West Pakistan, Zulfikar Ali Bhutto mobilized industrial workers, students, and rural peasants through the Pakistan Peoples Party, demanding democratic socialism and parliamentary rule. In East Pakistan, students and workers paralyzed the provincial administration through general strikes.

Besieged by mass unrest, declining health, and the withdrawal of military backing, Ayub Khan announced in February 1969 that he would not seek re-election. However, rather than adhering to the succession rule in his own 1962 Constitution - which explicitly mandated that if the President resigned, the Speaker of the National Assembly (Abdul Jabbar Khan, a Bengali) must assume acting presidency - Ayub Khan wrote a letter on 25 March 1969 handing power to Army Commander-in-Chief General Agha Muhammad Yahya Khan.

Yahya Khan promptly declared the country's second martial law, abrogated the Constitution of 1962, and dissolved the national and provincial assemblies, bringing the 1962 constitutional experiment to an end (`abbas-2023-constitutional-crisis`, `hamid-khan-const`).

### Comparative overview of the 1956 and 1962 constitutions

Analyzing the structural differences between the 1956 and 1962 constitutions clarifies the enduring constitutional debates of Pakistan:

#### Parliamentary versus presidential governance

The 1956 Constitution embraced the classical British parliamentary model, in which the executive emerges from and remains continually accountable to the legislature. The prime minister governed only so long as they retained the confidence of elected representatives.

In sharp contrast, the 1962 Constitution established an powerful presidency: the President stood outside the legislature, selected an appointed cabinet of specialists and supporters, and exercised strong veto and dissolution powers over the National Assembly.

#### Legislative power and fiscal sovereignty

Under the 1956 Constitution, the National Assembly had a stronger role in holding the cabinet to account for public spending. Some expenditure was still charged on public funds rather than submitted to an annual vote. Under the 1962 Constitution, the National Assembly was weakened: the President controlled recurring financial expenditure, leaving parliament powerless to withhold funding for executive administration.

#### Franchise and popular legitimacy

The 1956 Constitution drew its legal authority from a Constituent Assembly representing major political currents and guaranteed direct adult franchise for general elections. The 1962 Constitution was authored by an executive committee, promulgated by a single military general, and rested on the narrow, indirect electorate of 80,000 Basic Democrats. This absence of popular legitimacy made the 1962 Constitution brittle: having eliminated legitimate institutional channels for democratic dissent, political opposition increasingly spilled into the streets as revolutionary unrest.

#### Worked example: comparing accountability

A trainee in Sukkur compares two hypothetical cabinets. In the first, ministers must retain the confidence of the elected Assembly. In the second, the President chooses ministers who answer to him. The trainee first identifies who can require ministers to leave office. She then concludes that the first arrangement is parliamentary and the second presidential. The second is the pattern used in 1962. This comparison concerns cabinet accountability, not whether every presidential system is undemocratic. Elections, courts and limits on power must also be examined.

For a second comparison, consider a Sindh voter who directly chooses a local Basic Democrat, who then votes in a national election. The voter participates, but indirectly. Calling this a complete absence of voting would be wrong. The problem discussed here is the narrow route from public votes to national office. A Larkana teacher can demonstrate that route with three labelled cards: citizens, local electors and national representatives.

When teaching the 1969 collapse in a Sindh school, separate two questions: why did public support weaken, and who formally ended the constitution? Unequal regional influence and election disputes help explain opposition. Yahya Khan's imposition of martial law explains the legal ending. A cause of discontent and an act of abrogation are related, but they are not the same event.

## Summary

The Constitution of 1962 represented Pakistan's first experiment with a centralized presidential form of government. Promulgated by presidential decree following three and a half years of direct military rule, it discarded the 1956 parliamentary model in favor of concentrated executive supremacy centered on the President.

Under its provisions, the President exercised extensive appointment, ordinance, and veto powers, while the National Assembly was deprived of control over recurring financial expenditure. Direct popular elections were replaced by an indirect electoral college of 80,000 Basic Democrats divided equally between East and West Pakistan.

Fundamental rights, omitted from the original text, were restored only after intense popular protest through the First Amendment in 1964. The disvoting rights of the general populace, combined with deep regional alienation in East Pakistan and economic disparity, provoked a massive nationwide uprising between 1968 and 1969. On 25 March 1969, Ayub Khan resigned and unconstitutionally transferred power to the army chief, leading to the abrogation of the constitution and the re-imposition of martial law.
