# Topic 7.1 - Education, citizenship and the teacher

## A real classroom situation

Sajid is a trainee teacher at a government boys high school in Khairpur, Sindh. His Class 9 class has forty-two pupils. Some speak Sindhi at home, some Urdu, and a few speak Seraiki or Balochi with their families. One afternoon, a pupil named Imran asks, "Sir, why do we study Pakistan Studies? It is only about elections and the Prime Minister. That is not our life." Another pupil, Bashir, says, "My father says the country is held together by the army, not by schools." A third pupil, Zainab, who is visiting the class with her teacher-education college, says nothing. Later she tells Sajid that she feels her village dialect is treated as "less than" Urdu in the school.

Sajid is not sure how to answer. He knows the Constitution promises something better, but he has not yet connected its words to his classroom. By the end of this topic, you will be able to do that.

## Explanation

This topic asks a simple question: what does education have to do with the ideology and the Constitution of Pakistan? The answer has three parts. Civic education tells pupils what citizenship means. National integration tells a diverse country how to stay one. Teachers carry the daily responsibility for both.

### Civic education

<Glossary term="Civic education" /> is education that prepares students to understand their rights and responsibilities, participate actively in democratic life, and contribute constructively to their community and country. It is not only about elections. Elections are one event in a citizen's life. Civic education prepares a person for the everyday work of citizenship: understanding the Constitution, respecting the rights of others, resolving disputes peacefully, and taking part in community life.

Kennedy (2012) reviewed civic and citizenship education across many countries. He found that it is a component of the school curriculum in every nation state, but its form and purposes differ from place to place. Globalisation, migration and supranational groupings have made citizenship look more global, yet the key responsibility for it still rests with nation states. His lesson for a country like Pakistan is that schools must deliberately create an active and engaged citizenship, not leave it to chance. A pupil who only memorises facts is not yet an active citizen.

Dean (2005), writing about Pakistani schools, found a serious gap. Civics instruction had long been dominated by a transmission model: pupils memorised the names of government departments, historical dates and constitutional bodies. This produced what she called passive citizenship. Pupils could repeat facts for an examination, but they had not learned the skills and values needed for democratic life. She argued that meaningful civic education has three interlinked dimensions:

1. **Civic knowledge**: understanding the Constitution, the Fundamental Rights, the separation of powers and the federal structure you studied in Units 5 and 6.
2. **Civic skills**: deliberation, peaceful conflict resolution, critical media literacy and collaborative problem-solving.
3. **Civic values**: a commitment to social justice, equal dignity for every person, pluralism and human rights.

The three dimensions need one another. Knowledge without skills produces a pupil who can name the courts but cannot use them. Skills without values produce a clever speaker who can win an argument without caring who is hurt. Values without knowledge produce good intentions that do not know which article protects a child. A complete civic education joins all three.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Civic education is only about elections."** Voting is an important civic duty, but it happens once every few years. Real civic life happens every day. Civic education teaches a pupil how to treat a neighbour, how to resolve a dispute without violence, how to demand accountability from a local official, and how to defend the constitutional rights of a vulnerable fellow citizen. A school that teaches only about elections has taught only the smallest part of citizenship.

**A worked example: from memorising local government to using it.** Follow the difference between a passive lesson and an active one.

| The passive lesson | The active lesson |
|---|---|
| The teacher writes the three tiers of local government on the board. Pupils copy the notes and memorise them for a weekly test. | The teacher presents a real local problem: standing drainage water outside the school gate that breeds mosquitoes and stops children walking to school safely. |
| Pupils can name the tiers but cannot say who is responsible for the drain. | In groups, pupils work out which local body is legally responsible for sanitation. They draft a respectful letter describing the health hazard and asking for action. |
| The lesson ends with a test. | The lesson ends with pupils using local government, not just naming it. |

The active lesson teaches the same facts, but it also builds civic skills and civic agency. That is the difference Dean (2005) asks for. In a Sindh school, the same shift can happen with a lesson on Article 25A: instead of only copying the article, pupils find out which children in their own class or village are out of school, and write down one reason and one possible remedy.

### National integration

<Glossary term="National integration" /> is the process of bringing diverse regional, linguistic, ethnic and religious groups together into a cohesive national community based on equal citizenship and mutual respect. It is not the same as making everyone identical.

The distinction matters. **Assimilation** demands that every group give up its language, customs and heritage to adopt one uniform identity. **Integration** keeps the diversity and unites the country through equal rights and shared values. Forced assimilation breeds resentment. Democratic integration builds a durable cohesion. Pakistan's own history carries the lesson: the attempt to treat the two wings of the country as one, without respecting their different languages and political weight, helped lead to the separation of 1971.

Pakistan is a multi-ethnic, multilingual federation. The 1973 Constitution speaks directly to this. Article 33 says: "The State shall discourage parochial, racial, tribal, sectarian and provincial prejudices among the citizens." Article 36 safeguards the legitimate rights and interests of minorities, including their due representation in the federal and provincial services. Article 251(1) declares Urdu the national language, and Article 251(3) lets a Provincial Assembly prescribe measures for teaching, promoting and using a provincial language alongside Urdu. The Constitution does not ask a Sindhi or Pashto speaker to stop being one. It asks every citizen to stand on equal ground.

The school is one of the main places where this happens. A school builds integration when it teaches Urdu as a shared medium and also respects the mother tongues pupils bring. It builds integration when its history and social studies lessons present the contributions of every province with dignity. It builds integration when pupils from different backgrounds work together on a shared task and learn that a classmate's language is not a threat.

**Correcting a common misconception: "National integration means everyone must be the same."** That is assimilation, and it is the opposite of what the Constitution asks. Article 33 fights prejudice, not diversity. Article 251 protects provincial languages. National integration in Pakistan means unity in diversity: one citizenship, many cultures.

**A worked example: a school assembly that builds integration.** Imagine a government school in Sukkur that holds a "Many Cultures, One Nation" week. Each class prepares a short presentation on one province's poetry, music, dress or food. The presentations are in Urdu and in the relevant regional language, with a pupil explaining the meaning in Urdu for the whole school. At the end, the head teacher reads Article 33 and asks the pupils to name one way their class already practises it. The week does not erase differences. It turns them into shared knowledge, and it makes the constitutional promise visible.

A second example shows the same idea in an ordinary classroom. In a school in Karachi, a teacher asks a mixed group of pupils to prepare a short talk on "the river that feeds my province". The Sindhi pupil speaks about the Indus, the Punjabi pupil about the Ravi, and the Pashtun pupil about the Kabul river. The class then builds one map of Pakistan's rivers and one list of what the rivers share: water, irrigation, and a reason to cooperate. The lesson teaches geography and integration at the same time.

### Responsibilities of teachers

If civic education and national integration are to reach pupils, teachers must carry them. The teacher is not a neutral technician who only delivers a syllabus. In a constitutional democracy, the teacher is a civic actor.

Dean (2005) put it clearly: teachers are the primary agents of civic formation in schools. To foster active, democratic citizens, teachers must change their classroom pedagogy from rote learning to collaborative inquiry. When a classroom is an open and fair space where every pupil's voice is heard, regardless of gender, background or ethnicity, pupils experience democratic values in practice. Nation building in education is not indoctrination. It is the deliberate cultivation of responsible, reflective and empathetic citizens.

The Constitution gives this responsibility a legal frame. Article 37(b) tells the State to "remove illiteracy and provide free and compulsory secondary education within minimum possible period". Article 25A gives every child aged five to sixteen the right to free and compulsory education. Article 5(1) says loyalty to the State is the basic duty of every citizen, and Article 5(2) says obedience to the Constitution and law is an inviolable obligation. A teacher who helps a pupil understand these provisions is not adding politics to the classroom. The teacher is explaining the basic terms of citizenship.

The teacher's responsibility has limits, and they matter. A teacher should be fair and non-partisan. She should present the Constitution as it is, not as one party wishes it to be. She should welcome a pupil's honest question, even when it is awkward, and answer it with evidence. She should not use the classroom to promote a party or a personal view. The responsibility is to teach citizens, not to make supporters.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Teachers should stay out of citizenship education."** Some people fear that teaching citizenship is a kind of political indoctrination. That fear confuses education with propaganda. Civic education does not tell pupils what to think. It teaches them how the Constitution works, what rights they hold, and how to reason together about public life. A teacher who stays out of it leaves pupils to learn citizenship from whatever they hear outside school, which is often less fair and less accurate. The teacher's responsibility is to make the classroom a small model of the constitutional order: fair, reasoned and respectful.

**A worked example: a teacher handles a classroom dispute.** A pupil accuses a classmate of stealing a notebook. The teacher does not shout or punish on the spot. Instead, she asks both pupils to state what happened, lets the class discuss what fairness requires, and reaches a decision that follows a clear rule the class agreed earlier. The teacher then connects the event to Article 5(2): obedience to law is an obligation, and law applies to everyone, including the accused until proven otherwise. The dispute becomes a lesson in the rule of law.

A second example shows the responsibility in a harder setting. In a rural school, a girl is kept home to work, and the class begins to treat her absence as normal. The teacher visits the family, explains Article 25A, and arranges for the girl to return with a timetable that respects the family's need for help at harvest time. The teacher does not lecture the family about rights. She shows them a practical way to keep their daughter in school. That is civic responsibility in practice.

**Returning to Sajid's class.** He can now answer Imran: "Pakistan Studies is not only about elections. It is about how a citizen takes part in public life every day." He can answer Bashir: "Schools are one of the places where the country is held together, because they teach pupils to live together under one Constitution." And he can tell Zainab: "Your dialect is not less than Urdu. Article 251 protects provincial languages, and our class will respect them too."

## Activity: Design a value into a lesson

Work in pairs. About 25 minutes. Choose one constitutional value from this topic, such as equal citizenship, mutual respect, the rule of law or the right to education. Design a short classroom activity (about 20 minutes of class time) that would help a Class 8 or Class 9 pupil understand that value through experience, not just through a definition.

Your plan should name:

1. The value and the article that carries it.
2. The concrete situation or problem the pupils will work on.
3. The steps of the activity and what the pupils will produce.
4. One question you will ask at the end to help pupils reflect on what they learned.

Then exchange your plan with another pair. Check whether the activity really makes the pupil use the value, or only repeat its name. Give one piece of feedback.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Define civic education in your own words, and name its three dimensions.
2. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between assimilation and integration, and give one constitutional reason why Pakistan chooses integration.
3. *(Apply)* A colleague says, "Civic education is only about elections, and teachers should stay out of it." Using one article and one example from this topic, explain what is wrong with this view.

## Summary

Civic education prepares pupils for the everyday work of citizenship, not just for elections. It joins knowledge, skills and values. National integration in Pakistan is unity in diversity: Article 33 fights prejudice and Article 251 protects provincial languages, so the country is one without being uniform. Teachers carry this responsibility in the daily life of the classroom, where fair rules, respectful talk and reasoned inquiry model the constitutional order.
