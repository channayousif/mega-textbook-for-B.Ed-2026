# Topic 7.1 - Education, citizenship and the teacher

## A real classroom situation

Sajid is a trainee teacher at a government high school in Khairpur, Sindh. His Class 9 class has forty-two pupils. Some speak Sindhi at home, some Urdu, and a few speak Seraiki or Balochi with their families. One afternoon, a pupil named Imran asks, "Sir, why do we study Pakistan Studies? It is only about elections and the Prime Minister. That is not our life." Another pupil, Bashir, says, "My father says the country is held together by the army, not by schools." A third pupil, Zainab, sits quietly. Later she tells Sajid that she feels her regional mother tongue is treated as "less than" Urdu in the school.

Sajid is not sure how to answer. He knows the Constitution promises something better, but he has not yet connected its words to his classroom. By the end of this topic, you will be able to do that.

## Explanation

This topic asks a simple question: what does education have to do with the ideology and the Constitution of Pakistan? The answer has three parts. Civic education tells pupils what citizenship means. National integration tells a diverse country how to stay one. Teachers carry the daily responsibility for both.

### Civic education

<Glossary term="Civic education" /> is education that prepares students to understand their rights and responsibilities, participate actively in democratic life, and contribute constructively to their community and country. It is not only about elections. Elections are one event in a citizen's life. Civic education prepares a person for the everyday work of citizenship: understanding the Constitution, respecting the rights of others, resolving disputes peacefully, and taking part in community life.

Kennedy (2012) reviewed civic and citizenship education across many countries. He found that it is an established component of the school curriculum in every nation state, though its forms and purposes differ across national contexts. While globalisation, migration and international connections make civic life more complex, Kennedy argues that the fundamental responsibility for preparing active and engaged citizens continues to rest with nation states. Drawing on this international perspective, educators in a diverse federation like Pakistan recognise that building an active and participatory citizenry cannot be left to chance; schools must deliberately nurture civic understanding and public engagement. A pupil who only memorises facts without learning civic agency is not yet an active citizen.

Dean (2005), examining Pakistani schools, observed that civics instruction had long been dominated by a transmission model: teachers transmit textbook content and ensure its rote memorisation for examinations. This produced what she identified as exclusionary and passive citizenship: pupils could repeat information on paper, but they did not develop the skills (such as problem-solving and decision-making) or values and dispositions (such as civic mindedness and critical consciousness) needed for active participation in a democracy. She argued that meaningful citizenship education requires a body of knowledge, relevant skills, values and dispositions, and participatory experience:

1. **Civic knowledge**: understanding the Constitution, Fundamental Rights, the rule of law and the institutions of government studied in Units 5 and 6.
2. **Civic skills**: collaborative inquiry, decision-making, critical problem-solving and peaceful dialogue across differences.
3. **Civic values and dispositions**: civic mindedness, mutual respect, critical consciousness and equal dignity for all persons.

These components need one another. Knowledge without skills produces a pupil who can recite constitutional provisions but cannot participate in public life. Skills without democratic values produce a clever speaker who can win an argument without caring for justice. Values without knowledge produce goodwill that does not know which constitutional protections exist. A complete civic education connects all of them with active experience.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Civic education is only about elections."** Voting is an important civic duty, but it happens once every few years. Real civic life happens every day. Civic education teaches a pupil how to treat a neighbour, how to resolve a dispute without violence, how to demand accountability from a local official, and how to defend the constitutional rights of a vulnerable fellow citizen. A school that teaches only about elections has taught only the smallest part of citizenship.

**A worked example: from memorising local government to using it.** Follow the difference between a passive lesson and an active one.

| The passive lesson | The active lesson |
|---|---|
| The teacher writes the three tiers of local government on the board. Pupils copy the notes and memorise them for a weekly test. | The teacher presents a real local problem: standing drainage water outside the school gate that breeds mosquitoes and stops children walking to school safely. |
| Pupils can name the tiers but cannot say who is responsible for the drain. | In groups, pupils work out which local body is legally responsible for sanitation. They draft a respectful letter describing the health hazard and asking for action. |
| The lesson ends with a test. | The lesson ends with pupils using local government, not just naming it. |

The active lesson teaches the same facts, but it also builds civic skills and civic agency. That is the shift from rote transmission that Dean (2005) calls for. In a Sindh school, the same shift can happen with a lesson on Article 25A: instead of only copying the article, pupils find out which children in their own village or neighbourhood are out of school, and write down one reason and one possible remedy.

### National integration

<Glossary term="National integration" /> is the process of bringing diverse regional, linguistic, ethnic and religious groups together into a cohesive national community based on equal citizenship and mutual respect. It is not the same as making everyone identical.

The distinction matters. **Assimilation** demands that every group give up its language, customs and heritage to adopt one uniform identity. **Integration** keeps the diversity and unites the country through equal rights and shared values. In a diverse federation, forced assimilation breeds resentment and division, whereas democratic integration builds durable cohesion around shared constitutional rights and mutual respect.

Pakistan is a multi-ethnic, multilingual federation. The 1973 Constitution speaks directly to this. Under Fundamental Rights, Article 28 provides: "any section of citizens having a distinct language, script or culture shall have the right to preserve and promote the same and subject to law, establish institutions for that purpose." Under the Principles of Policy, Article 33 directs: "The State shall discourage parochial, racial, tribal, sectarian and provincial prejudices among the citizens," and Article 36 safeguards the legitimate rights and interests of minorities. In Part XII, Article 251(1) declares Urdu the national language to provide a shared medium across the federation, while Article 251(3) is permissive: a Provincial Assembly may by law prescribe measures for the teaching, promotion and use of a provincial language alongside the national language.

As studied in Unit 5, there is an important constitutional distinction here. Fundamental Rights such as Article 28 are justiciable and enforceable through the courts. Principles of Policy such as Article 33 are non-justiciable under Article 30(2), meaning an action cannot be challenged in court simply on the ground that it does not accord with them; nonetheless, Article 29 directs that every organ and authority of the State must act in accordance with them. The Constitution does not demand that a Sindhi, Balochi, Punjabi or Pashto speaker give up their linguistic identity. It balances a shared national language with the constitutional right to preserve regional cultures.

The school is one of the main places where this happens. A school builds integration when it teaches Urdu as a shared national medium and simultaneously respects the mother tongues and regional languages pupils bring. It builds integration when its history and social studies lessons present the heritage and contributions of every province with dignity. It builds integration when pupils from different backgrounds collaborate on shared tasks and learn that cultural differences are a national strength.

**Correcting a common misconception: "National integration means everyone must be the same."** That is assimilation, and it contradicts the constitutional framework. Article 33 discourages prejudice, not diversity. Article 28 guarantees the right to preserve distinct languages and cultures, while Article 251(3) permits provinces to legislate for provincial languages. National integration in Pakistan means unity in diversity: one citizenship, many cultures.

**A worked example: a school assembly that builds integration.** Imagine a government school in Sukkur that holds a "Many Cultures, One Nation" week. Each class prepares a short presentation on one province's poetry, music, dress or food. The presentations are in Urdu and in the relevant regional language, with a pupil explaining the meaning in Urdu for the whole school. At the end, the head teacher reads Article 33 and asks the pupils to name one way their class already practises it. The week does not erase differences. It turns them into shared knowledge, and it makes the constitutional promise visible.

A second example shows the same idea in an ordinary classroom. In a school in Karachi, a teacher asks a mixed group of pupils to prepare a short talk on "the waters that connect our country". Pupils research the Indus in Sindh, the Ravi in Punjab, the Kabul river in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and the Hingol river and karez irrigation systems in Balochistan. The class then builds one map of Pakistan's shared waters and discusses their interdependence: irrigation, agriculture and the shared necessity of cooperation across provinces. The lesson teaches geography and national integration at the same time.

### Responsibilities of teachers

If civic education and national integration are to reach pupils, teachers must carry them. The teacher is not a neutral technician who only delivers a syllabus. In a constitutional democracy, the teacher is a civic actor.

Dean (2005) emphasized that the teacher is essential to the reconceptualization of education for democratic citizenship. To foster active and responsible citizens, teachers must shift pedagogical practice away from textbook transmission toward collaborative inquiry and cooperative learning where students work with and for each other. When a classroom is an open and fair environment where every pupil's voice is heard and respected, pupils experience democratic values in practice. Civic education in schools is not political indoctrination; it is the deliberate cultivation of informed, reflective and responsible citizens who can think critically and act collaboratively.

The Constitution provides the legal framework for this civic role. Article 4 establishes the fundamental principle of the rule of law: the inalienable right of every individual to enjoy the protection of law and to be treated in accordance with law. Article 5(1) states that loyalty to the State is the basic duty of every citizen, and Article 5(2) makes obedience to the Constitution and law an inviolable obligation. Article 10A secures the right to a fair trial and due process. In education, Article 25A guarantees free and compulsory education to all children aged five to sixteen in such manner as may be determined by law (enacted in Sindh through the Sindh Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2013), and Article 37(b) establishes the Principle of Policy to remove illiteracy and provide free secondary education. A teacher who introduces pupils to these provisions is not introducing partisan politics; the teacher is teaching the foundational rules of Pakistani citizenship.

The teacher's responsibility has limits, and they matter. A teacher should be fair and non-partisan. She should present the Constitution as it is, not as one party wishes it to be. She should welcome a pupil's honest question, even when it is awkward, and answer it with evidence. She should not use the classroom to promote a party or a personal view. The responsibility is to teach citizens, not to make supporters.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Teachers should stay out of citizenship education."** Some people fear that teaching citizenship is a kind of political indoctrination. That fear confuses education with propaganda. Civic education does not tell pupils what to think. It teaches them how the Constitution works, what rights they hold, and how to reason together about public life. A teacher who stays out of it leaves pupils to learn citizenship from whatever they hear outside school, which is often less fair and less accurate. The teacher's responsibility is to make the classroom a small model of the constitutional order: fair, reasoned and respectful.

**A worked example: a teacher handles a classroom dispute.** Two pupils disagree over the rightful ownership of shared learning materials during group work. Rather than shouting, taking sides or imposing an arbitrary penalty, the teacher listens to both accounts calmly. She guides the pupils to consult the classroom agreement they formulated at the start of term, where rules about borrowing and sharing were collectively adopted. The teacher connects the resolution to the Constitution: Article 5(2) establishes that rules apply to everyone equally, while Article 4 and Article 10A embody the principle of due process, ensuring that every person is heard fairly before any decision is made. The dispute becomes a practical lesson in procedural fairness and the rule of law.

A second example shows this civic responsibility in a demanding community setting. In an agricultural area, a female pupil is kept home during harvest season, and classmates start to consider her prolonged absence normal. The teacher visits the home, listens respectfully to the family's economic difficulties, and explains that Article 25A and the Sindh Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2013 safeguard the child's right to continue schooling. Working with the head teacher, the teacher provides structured review packets and flexible catch-up sessions so the pupil can return to class without penalty. The teacher demonstrates civic responsibility not by lecturing down to parents, but by helping them find practical paths to protect their child's education.

**Returning to Sajid's class.** He can now answer Imran: "Pakistan Studies is not only about elections. It is about how citizens participate in community life and public decisions every day." He can answer Bashir: "Schools are one of the key places where the country is held together, because they prepare diverse citizens to live together under shared constitutional principles." And he can tell Zainab: "Your mother tongue and cultural heritage are respected. Article 28 gives citizens the right to preserve their distinct language and culture, while Article 251 allows provinces to promote regional languages alongside Urdu. In our classroom, every pupil's background is treated with dignity."

## Activity: Design a value into a lesson

**Constitutional values** are the core democratic and ethical principles established by the Constitution that guide national life and public institutions. Key constitutional values include:
- **Equal citizenship and non-discrimination**: embodied in Article 25 (equality of all citizens before law and equal protection of law).
- **The rule of law and due process**: embodied in Article 4 (the inalienable right of every individual to be dealt with in accordance with law) and Article 10A (fair trial).
- **Mutual respect and linguistic preservation**: embodied in Article 28 (right to preserve distinct languages, scripts and cultures) and Article 33 (discouraging parochial and provincial prejudices).
- **The right to education**: embodied in Article 25A (free and compulsory education for all children aged five to sixteen) and Article 37(b).

Work in pairs. About 25 minutes. Choose one constitutional value from the list above, noting its supporting article. Design a short classroom activity (about 20 minutes of class time) that helps a Class 8 or Class 9 pupil understand that value through experience, not just through a definition.

Your plan should name:

1. The value and the article that carries it.
2. The concrete situation or problem the pupils will work on.
3. The steps of the activity and what the pupils will produce.
4. One question you will ask at the end to help pupils reflect on what they learned.

Then exchange your plan with another pair. Check whether the activity really makes the pupil use the value, or only repeat its name. Give one piece of feedback.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Define civic education in your own words, and identify its core components of knowledge, skills, values and dispositions, and participatory experience.

3. *(Apply)* A colleague says, "Civic education is only about elections, and teachers should stay out of it." Using one article and one classroom example from this topic, explain what is wrong with this view.

## Summary

Civic education prepares pupils for the everyday work of citizenship, not just for elections. It connects knowledge, skills, values and dispositions with active experience. National integration in Pakistan is unity in diversity: Article 33 discourages prejudice, Article 28 protects distinct languages and cultures, and Article 251 balances the national language with provincial language legislation, so the federation is united without being forced into uniformity. Teachers carry this civic responsibility in the daily life of the classroom, where procedural fairness, mutual respect and reasoned inquiry model the constitutional order.
