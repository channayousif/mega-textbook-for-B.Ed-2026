## A real classroom situation

Imran is a trainee at a government high school in Sukkur. Two things happen in one week. First, a pupil named Bilal posts insults online about another pupil's religious sect. When he is warned, he says, "Article 19. I have freedom of speech. Nobody can stop me."

Then a father, Mr Kumar, comes to the school. His daughter Pooja, a Hindu pupil in Class 8, has been told by a teacher that she must sit the Islamiat lesson and join in. Mr Kumar quietly asks Imran, "Sir, is this allowed? And if it is not, where does a person like me go?"

One person claims too much for a right. Another does not know that he holds one. A teacher should be able to help both. This topic shows you how.

## Explanation

### Definition and importance of fundamental rights

<Glossary term="Fundamental Rights" /> are basic rights that the Constitution guarantees and that ordinary law cannot simply take away. In the 1973 Constitution, they are in Part II, Chapter 1: Articles 8 to 28.

**What makes them different from ordinary rights.** A right given by an ordinary law can be changed by another ordinary law. A Fundamental Right stands above ordinary law. Article 8 says this in two steps.

- Article 8(1): an existing law or custom with the force of law is void to the extent that it conflicts with these rights.
- Article 8(2): the State shall not make a law that takes away or abridges these rights, and a law made in breach of this is void to that extent.

To **abridge** a right means to cut it down. **Void** means having no legal effect.

**Who must respect them.** The rights mainly bind "the State". Article 7 defines this as the Federal Government, Parliament, a Provincial Government, a Provincial Assembly, and local or other authorities with legal power to impose a tax or cess. This is a key point that pupils often miss. Fundamental Rights protect people mainly from the misuse of public power. Bilal's classmate may have a complaint against Bilal, but that complaint would normally be under school rules or an ordinary law.

**Who holds them.** Read the first words of each article carefully, because they differ.

| Words used | Who is covered | Example |
|---|---|---|
| "No person" | Anyone in Pakistan | Article 9: no person shall be deprived of life or liberty save in accordance with law |
| "Every citizen" | Citizens | Article 19: freedom of speech |
| "All children" of a given age | Children of that age | Article 25A: education at ages five to sixteen |

**Why they matter.** There are three reasons, and each one connects to a school.

1. **They protect the weaker person against power.** A majority, a government or an official can be wrong. A right gives a person something to stand on, even when they are alone. Pooja's father is one parent, and he still has a right to rely on.
2. **They set the terms of citizenship.** Unit 1 showed that the Preamble and Article 25 promise equal citizenship. These articles are where that promise becomes law.
3. **They come with duties.** Article 5 says loyalty to the State is the basic duty of every citizen, and obedience to the Constitution and law is an inviolable obligation. A pupil who learns only rights, and no duties, has half the lesson.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Fundamental Rights can never be limited."** Most of them can. The limits are often written inside the article itself. The list below shows some.

- Article 15, freedom of movement: subject to reasonable restriction imposed by law in the public interest.
- Article 16, freedom of assembly: peaceful and without arms, subject to reasonable restrictions imposed by law in the interest of public order.
- Article 19, freedom of speech: subject to reasonable restrictions imposed by law on grounds such as the glory of Islam, the integrity or security of Pakistan, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, or incitement to an offence.
- Article 20, religion: subject to law, public order and morality.

Some laws are also outside Article 8, for example certain laws about discipline in the armed forces and the police (Article 8(3)). During an emergency, some rights can also be restricted or their enforcement suspended (Article 233).

The opposite mistake is just as serious. A right is not whatever a government says it is. A limit must come from law, and where the article says so, it must be reasonable. Article 8(5) says the rights shall not be suspended except as the Constitution expressly provides.

**A worked example: Bilal's claim.** Ask three questions whenever someone claims a right.

1. **Does the article cover this?** Yes. Article 19 covers speech, including speech online.
2. **Does the article name a limit?** Yes. It allows reasonable restrictions by law, including for public order, morality and incitement to an offence. Article 33 also tells the State to discourage sectarian prejudice, though that is a Principle of Policy rather than a limit in itself.
3. **Is the limit in this case lawful and reasonable?** A school warning a pupil under its rules about insults against a classmate's sect is a mild step that protects order in the school. Bilal's claim that "nobody can stop me" is too wide.

So Imran can say: "Bilal, you have a real right. You may disagree, argue and criticise. But the same article that gives you this right also says it has limits."

### Rights guaranteed by the Constitution

Articles 8 to 28 can be read as four groups.

| Group | Articles | Rights in brief |
|---|---|---|
| Life, liberty and fair treatment | 9, 9A, 10, 10A, 11, 12, 13, 14 | Life and liberty; clean and healthy environment; safeguards on arrest; fair trial; no slavery or forced labour; no retrospective punishment; no double punishment or forced self-incrimination; dignity and privacy |
| Freedoms | 15-19A, 20, 23, 24 | Movement; assembly; association; trade or profession; speech; information; religion; property |
| Equality | 25, 26, 27 | Equality before law; no discrimination in access to public places or in public service |
| Education, religion and culture | 21, 22, 25A, 28 | No tax for another religion; safeguards in educational institutions; right to education; language, script and culture |

Article 11(3) keeps children under 14 out of factories, mines and hazardous work. Article 14(1) protects dignity. Article 19 gives freedom of speech with reasonable limits. Article 19A gives access to public information. Article 20 protects religion. Article 22 bars forced religious instruction and unfair admission. Article 25 gives equality before law. Article 25A gives free and compulsory education at ages five to sixteen. Article 28 protects language and culture." />

**The list has grown.** The Eighteenth Amendment of 2010 added the rights to fair trial (10A), information (19A) and education (25A) (Hussain & Kokab, 2012). The Twenty-sixth Amendment of 2024 added Article 9A, the right to a clean, healthy and sustainable environment.

**Courts can read a right widely.** In *Shehla Zia v. WAPDA* (PLD 1994 SC 693), the Supreme Court read "life" in Article 9 to include quality of life, and linked it with dignity in Article 14. That let it treat a clean environment as protected, about thirty years before Article 9A was added (Mansoor, 2025). So the words of an article are where you begin, and the courts' reading matters too.

**Rights a teacher meets every week.** Four articles matter most in schools.

- **Article 22(1).** A person attending any educational institution shall not be required to receive religious instruction, or to take part in a religious ceremony or worship, if it relates to a religion other than their own.
- **Article 22(3)(b).** No citizen shall be denied admission to an educational institution receiving aid from public revenues on the ground only of race, religion, caste or place of birth.
- **Article 25.** All citizens are equal before law and there shall be no discrimination on the basis of sex. Article 25(3) still allows special provision for women and children, so a girls' school or a scholarship for girls does not break it.
- **Article 25A.** The State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children aged five to sixteen, in the manner determined by law. In Sindh, that law is the Sindh Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act 2013. Section 3 gives the right, and section 13(3) says no child shall be subjected to corporal punishment or mental harassment.

**A worked example: match the situation to the article.** Use this routine: name who acted, name what was affected, and find the article.

*Situation (invented for practice):* An aided school in Mirpur Khas refuses to admit a boy from a Kolhi family, and the clerk says the reason is his caste.

1. **Who acted?** A school receiving public funds.
2. **What was affected?** Admission, on the ground of caste.
3. **Which article?** Article 22(3)(b) fits exactly. Article 25 supports it, and Article 25A applies if the child is aged five to sixteen.

*Now Pooja:* A teacher in a government school requires a Hindu pupil to receive Islamiat instruction. The article is 22(1). Article 31(2)(a), which you will study in Topic 5.3, concerns "the Muslims of Pakistan", so it does not reach Pooja.

### Protection and enforcement of rights

A right on paper needs a way to make it real. **Enforcement** means getting an authority, usually a court, to make the State respect the right. The Constitution provides several layers.

**Layer 1: Laws that break rights are void.** Article 8 does this, as you saw.

**Layer 2: The High Court.** This is the main route for most people. Under Article 199(1)(c), a High Court can, on the application of any aggrieved person, give directions to any person or authority, including a government, to enforce a Fundamental Right. An **aggrieved person** is someone directly harmed by the action. This application is a <Glossary term="Writ petition" />. In Sindh, it goes to the Sindh High Court. Three conditions matter.

- The High Court must be satisfied that the law provides no other adequate remedy (Article 199(1)). So if a departmental appeal or an ordinary court can properly deal with the problem, that usually comes first.
- The right to go to the High Court for enforcement shall not be abridged, subject to the Constitution (Article 199(2)).
- The High Court cannot go beyond the contents of the application by acting on its own (Article 199(1A)).

**Layer 3: The Federal Constitutional Court.** This court was created by the Twenty-seventh Amendment of 13 November 2025. Under Article 175E(3), if it considers that a question of public importance about enforcing a Fundamental Right is involved, it can make an order of the kind a High Court can make. It acts on an application filed by a person. It also hears appeals from Article 199 decisions, if it grants leave (permission) to appeal (Article 175F(1)(c)).

**Watch out for older books.** Most books, past papers and many teachers will tell you that Article 184(3) gives the Supreme Court this power. That was correct for decades, and *Shehla Zia* was decided that way. But the Twenty-seventh Amendment omitted Article 184 (Constitution (Twenty-seventh Amendment) Act, 2025, section 29). When you read about past cases, "Article 184(3)" is right for its time. When you explain the Constitution today, use Article 175E(3). Check whether your examination board has updated its syllabus, and tell your pupils about both.

**Layer 4: The limits in an emergency.** While a Proclamation of Emergency is in force, Articles 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 and 24 do not restrict the State's power to make laws or take executive action (Article 233(1)). The President may also, by Order, suspend the right to move a court to enforce the rights named in the Order (Article 233(2)). Each Order must go before both Houses for approval (Article 233(3)). Unit 4 studied the constitutional breakdowns in Pakistan's history, so you can see why these limits matter.

**A worked example: where does Mr Kumar go?** A teacher does not give legal advice, but a teacher can explain the route.

1. **Begin with the school.** Show the head teacher Article 22(1). Most problems of this kind end here, and that is the best outcome for Pooja.
2. **Try the education department.** Mr Kumar can complain in writing to the district education office. This may also be the "other adequate remedy".
3. **Go to the High Court if needed.** If nothing else works, he can file a writ petition in the Sindh High Court under Article 199(1)(c), with a lawyer's help. Pooja is the aggrieved person, and a lawyer can advise how her father can act for her.
4. **What about the Federal Constitutional Court?** Only if a court decision is appealed with leave, or if the matter raises a question of public importance. One pupil's case will usually be decided earlier.

**Returning to Imran.** He can now tell Mr Kumar, "This is not allowed, and Article 22(1) says so. Let us speak to the head teacher today. If that fails, I will show you the next step." And he can say to Bilal, "You do have a right, and it has limits." The two answers come from the same habit: reading the article.

## Activity: Rights clinic

Work in groups of four. About 30 minutes. Your group is a "rights clinic" at a school in Sindh. Two members are advisers, one is the person with the problem and one is the recorder. Choose two of these cases. All are invented for practice.

- A. A private school with no public funding will not let a Christian pupil skip a Muslim prayer assembly.
- B. A head teacher bans all pupils from speaking Sindhi at break.
- C. A local official refuses to tell parents how much public money was spent on repairing their school.
- D. A government school sends home a girl because her family cannot pay for books.

For each case, the advisers must use the three-question test or the matching routine, name the article, say whether the right has any limit, and describe the next step along the route. The person with the problem should push back: "But what if the head teacher says no?" The recorder notes one thing your group was unsure of. Share that with the class. Being unsure of a hard case is honest, and a lawyer might be needed.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State where Fundamental Rights are found in the Constitution, and what Article 8(2) says about a law that takes away such a right.
2. *(Understand)* Explain, using Article 19 or Article 20, why "Fundamental Rights can never be limited" is wrong. Then explain why a government still cannot limit a right however it likes.
3. *(Apply)* A government school in Khairpur refuses a Bheel child, aged nine, because of the family's caste. Name two articles that apply, and describe the first two steps that the family could take.

## Summary

Fundamental Rights stand above ordinary law, and a law that takes them away is void. They mainly protect people against public power, and they come with citizens' duties. Most can be limited, but only as the Constitution allows. A High Court can enforce them when no other adequate remedy exists, and the Federal Constitutional Court now holds the power once held under Article 184(3). A right is useful only when people know it and know where to take it, and a teacher is often the first person who can tell them.

- [ ] I can show from the text of an article that a right has limits, and explain why those limits are themselves limited.

- [ ] I can describe the route for enforcing a right, and I know why Article 184(3) is out of date.

## Try this at your practicum school

Look for one practice at your school that touches Article 22(1), 25 or 25A. It could be how non-Muslim pupils are treated during Islamiat, whether boys and girls have the same access to a facility, or what happens when a child cannot pay for something. Observe quietly and do not accuse anyone. Write a short private note: what you saw, which article it touches, and what you would do as the teacher. Discuss it with your cooperating teacher only if it feels appropriate.

## Summative task

Write 300-350 words as advice to a new teacher who has heard this from a pupil: "I know my rights. I can do what I like, and if the school stops me I will go straight to the Federal Constitutional Court." Your advice should:

1. Explain what is right and wrong in the pupil's idea of rights, using one article that contains a limit.
2. Analyse the pupil's idea of enforcement, using Articles 199 and 175E(3).
3. Suggest how the teacher should reply, and justify the reply.
