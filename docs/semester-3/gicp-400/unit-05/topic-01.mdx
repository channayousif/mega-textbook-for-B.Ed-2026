## A real classroom situation

Sanam is a trainee at a government girls high school in Larkana. Her head teacher has just read out a notice about free schooling under a Sindh law. In Class 9, a pupil named Marvi asks, "Miss, if Sindh makes the law for our school, is Sindh like its own country? Could it also print its own money?" Another pupil, Sadia, laughs. "No, the Prime Minister is the boss of everything. He can tell the Chief Minister and the judges what to do."

Both pupils have noticed something real, and both have drawn the wrong conclusion. Sanam wants to answer with the Constitution, not with her opinion. By the end of this topic you will be able to do that too.

## Explanation

The 1973 Constitution has many features. The course guide asks you to study three of them. Each one answers a question about power.

| Feature | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| Federalism | How is power shared between the federation and the provinces? |
| Parliamentary system | How is the government chosen and removed? |
| Separation of powers | How do the organs of the State keep each other in check? |

### Federalism

<Glossary term="Federalism" /> means that the constitution divides power between a central government and regional governments. Each level has its own powers, given by the constitution. Article 1(1) says Pakistan "shall be a Federal Republic". Article 1(2) names the four Provinces: Balochistan, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, the Punjab and Sindh.

**Who makes which laws.** Articles 141 and 142 divide law-making. The idea is simple, and it has four parts.

1. Parliament alone makes laws on the matters in the Federal Legislative List, which is in the Fourth Schedule (Article 142(a)).
2. Parliament and a Provincial Assembly can both make laws on criminal law, criminal procedure and evidence (Article 142(b)).
3. A Provincial Assembly makes laws on every other matter (Article 142(c)). This is the large remaining area.
4. Parliament makes laws for areas that are not in any Province, such as Islamabad (Article 142(d)).

The Federal Legislative List has two parts. Part I includes defence (entry 1), external affairs (entry 3) and currency (entry 8). Part II includes standards in institutions for higher education and research (entry 12). Part II matters are also supervised by the Council of Common Interests, which we meet below.

<Figure id="fig-U5-1" src="/img/figures/gicp-400/unit-05/fig-U5-1.svg" alt="Diagram of the federation. The Constitution at the top divides law-making power between two levels. Parliament alone makes laws on the Federal Legislative List, such as defence, external affairs and currency. The four Provincial Assemblies of Balochistan, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh make laws on every matter not on that list, such as school education. Both levels may legislate on criminal law, criminal procedure and evidence, and a valid Act of Parliament prevails over a conflicting provincial Act." />

**What the Eighteenth Amendment changed.** Until 2010, a third list existed, called the Concurrent Legislative List. Both levels could make laws on its matters, and it included curriculum, syllabus and standards of education (entry 38). The Eighteenth Amendment of 2010 removed this list. Its subjects went to the provinces, apart from those placed elsewhere in the Schedule. Hussain and Kokab (2012) describe this as a major step for provincial autonomy. They also report a real debate. Some autonomist parties said it gave too little. Some centrist voices said that removing the whole list went too far and would leave the provinces with heavy new costs. Pupils can be told that fair-minded people still disagree.

**A worked example: which legislature makes this law?** Follow three steps.

1. **Is the matter in the Federal Legislative List?** If yes, Parliament makes the law.
2. **Is it criminal law, criminal procedure or evidence?** If yes, both levels can make law.
3. **Neither?** Then the Provincial Assembly makes the law.

| Matter | Step that decides it | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Printing banknotes | Step 1: currency, entry 8 | Parliament only. This answers Marvi's question: Sindh cannot print its own money. |
| Free schooling for children in Sindh | Step 3: not in the list | Sindh Assembly. It passed the Sindh Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act 2013. |
| Standards for universities | Step 1: Part II, entry 12 | Parliament. Policy goes through the Council of Common Interests. |

**Correcting a common misconception: "Federalism means the provinces are independent."** They are not. A province has its own law-making area, but it is part of one country and it works under one Constitution. Here is the evidence.

- Article 143 says that if a provincial Act conflicts with an Act that Parliament is competent to make, the Act of Parliament prevails. The provincial Act is void to that extent.
- The provinces have no powers over defence, foreign affairs or currency.
- A province cannot change the Constitution on its own.

The two levels also meet. The National Assembly gives seats by population (Article 51(5)), so Punjab has the most. The Senate gives each Province the same number of seats: 14 general seats, four for women, four for technocrats and one for a non-Muslim (Article 59(1)). That is how a small province keeps a voice. The Council of Common Interests brings the Prime Minister, the four Chief Ministers and three federal members together to set policy on Part II matters (Articles 153 and 154).

So Marvi was partly right. Sindh does make the law for her school. But that power comes from the Constitution, and it has limits.

### Parliamentary system

A **parliamentary system** is one in which the head of government comes from the elected assembly, and stays in office only while that assembly supports them. Compare it with a presidential system, in which voters or an electoral body choose a president who does not depend on the assembly's support in this way. You met one such system in Unit 3, in the 1962 Constitution.

**How the 1973 Constitution does this.** Follow the chain.

1. Voters who are citizens aged 18 or over elect members of the National Assembly (Article 51(2)).
2. The National Assembly elects the Prime Minister from among its Muslim members, normally by a majority of its total membership (Article 91(3) and (4)).
3. The Prime Minister is "the chief executive of the Federation" (Article 90(1)).
4. The Cabinet is collectively responsible to the Senate and the National Assembly (Article 91(6)). This means the ministers stand together and answer together.
5. The National Assembly can remove the Prime Minister. A no-confidence resolution passed by a majority of its total membership ends the Prime Minister's term (Article 95).

The President is Head of State, and executive authority is exercised in the President's name (Article 90(1)). But the President acts on the advice of the Cabinet or the Prime Minister (Article 48(1)), except where the Constitution gives the President a discretion (Article 48(2)). The President can ask for advice to be reconsidered, and must then act on the reconsidered advice. A Province follows the same pattern. Its Provincial Assembly elects the Chief Minister, and the provincial Cabinet answers to that Assembly (Article 130).

**The system has been damaged and repaired.** Under Generals Zia-ul-Haq and Musharraf, amendments moved power towards the President. One of them, Article 58(2)(b), let the President dissolve the National Assembly at his discretion. The Eighteenth Amendment deleted that power and returned the Constitution towards its parliamentary form (Hussain & Kokab, 2012). Hamid Khan's *Constitutional and Political History of Pakistan* tells this longer story.

**A worked example: answering Sadia.** Sadia said, "The Prime Minister is the boss of everything." Test the claim.

1. **Who put the Prime Minister there?** The National Assembly, by a vote (Article 91).
2. **Who can remove the Prime Minister?** The National Assembly, by a no-confidence vote (Article 95).
3. **Can the Prime Minister make any law alone?** No. Laws go through Parliament or, for provincial matters, a Provincial Assembly.
4. **Conclusion.** The Prime Minister leads the government, but holds that place only with the support of the elected Assembly. "Boss of everything" is wrong. "Leader who must keep the Assembly's support" is right.

**Correcting a misconception: "The President runs the government."** People may think so because the President is the highest office and many orders are issued in the President's name. In the parliamentary system, the Prime Minister and the Cabinet decide, and the President acts on their advice.

### Separation of powers

<Glossary term="Separation of Powers" /> means that the work of the State is divided among three organs, so that no one person or body holds all its power.

- The <Glossary term="Legislature" /> makes laws. At federal level, this is Parliament: the President, the National Assembly and the Senate (Article 50).
- The <Glossary term="Executive" /> runs government and applies the laws. At federal level, this is the Prime Minister and the Federal Ministers (Article 90).
- The <Glossary term="Judiciary" /> decides disputes and interprets the law. Article 175 now lists a Federal Constitutional Court, a Supreme Court, a High Court for each Province and for Islamabad, and other courts set up by law.

**Separate does not mean sealed off.** Each organ also has a check on the others. A **check** is a power that one organ has to stop or correct another.

<Figure id="fig-U5-2" src="/img/figures/gicp-400/unit-05/fig-U5-2.svg" alt="Concept map of separation of powers. The legislature, the executive and the judiciary each have their own main job, and arrows show the checks between them. The National Assembly can remove the Prime Minister by a vote of no-confidence, and the Prime Minister can advise dissolving the National Assembly. Courts can treat a law that takes away Fundamental Rights as void, and members of both Houses sit on the Judicial Commission that nominates judges. High Courts can give directions to officials, and the Federal Law Minister sits on the Judicial Commission." />

| Who checks whom | The check | Article |
|---|---|---|
| Legislature checks executive | No-confidence vote; the Cabinet answers to both Houses | 95, 91(6) |
| Executive checks legislature | The Prime Minister can advise the President to dissolve the National Assembly | 58(1) |
| Judiciary checks legislature | A law that takes away Fundamental Rights is void | 8(2) |
| Judiciary checks executive | High Courts can direct officials and governments | 199 |
| Legislature and executive take part in appointing judges | Members of both Houses and the Federal Law Minister sit on the Judicial Commission | 175A |

Each organ is also limited. Parliament may not discuss how a judge conducts their duties (Article 68). Courts may not question Parliament's proceedings because of an irregular procedure (Article 69). A judge can be removed only after an inquiry by the Supreme Judicial Council and its report to the President (Article 209).

**Correcting a misconception: "Separation of powers means the three organs never touch."** In a parliamentary system, two of them overlap by design. The Prime Minister and the ministers come from Parliament, so the executive sits inside the legislature. The clear separation is that of the courts. Article 175(3) required the judiciary to be separated progressively from the executive. So a precise statement is this: the legislature and the executive work closely and check each other, while the courts stand apart from both.

**A worked example: a law meets all three organs.** Imagine that a Provincial Assembly passes a law saying girls over twelve may not enrol in government schools. This case is invented for practice.

1. **Legislature.** The Assembly has power over schools, so the subject is within its area. But that is not enough.
2. **Executive.** The education department starts to apply the law, and a school in Dadu refuses a girl.
3. **Judiciary.** Her father petitions the High Court. Article 25(2) bars discrimination on the basis of sex, and Article 25A gives every child aged five to sixteen a right to education. Article 8(2) says a law that takes away such rights is void to that extent. The court can decide that the law cannot stand.

Notice what happened. Federalism gave the province the subject. Separation of powers let a court stop the misuse of that power. The same case shows how the features work together, and Topic 5.2 follows that court route more closely.

**Returning to Sanam's class.** She can now tell Marvi: "Sindh makes the law for our school because the Constitution gives it that power. It cannot print money, because currency is a federal matter." And she can tell Sadia: "The Prime Minister leads the government but must keep the support of the National Assembly, and judges are not under the Prime Minister's orders. Let us read the articles together."

## Activity: Whose job is it?

Work in groups of three. About 25 minutes. Each person takes one role: Federalism expert, Parliamentary system expert or Separation of powers expert. Spend five minutes re-reading your section. Without looking at the text, then work through these five cases together:

- A. A teacher in Hyderabad asks who can change the Class 5 syllabus in Sindh.
- B. A rumour says the President has dismissed the Prime Minister because they disagreed about a budget.
- C. Pakistan wants to sign an education agreement with another country.
- D. Parents believe a provincial rule breaks their daughters' right to education.
- E. A pupil says a provincial law on theft is impossible because crime is a federal matter.

For each case, the expert whose feature best fits leads. Name the feature and the article, and give the answer in two sentences. The other two must try to find a mistake. Then share your hardest case with another group.

## Summary

The 1973 Constitution divides power in two directions. Federalism divides it between the federation and the provinces, with the Federal Legislative List for Parliament and all remaining matters for the provinces, apart from a shared area of criminal law. The provinces have real law-making power, but they are not independent. The parliamentary system makes the Prime Minister and Cabinet answerable to the elected Assembly. Separation of powers divides the State's work among three organs that check one another, with the courts standing most clearly apart.
