## A real classroom situation

Rukhsana is a trainee at a government primary school in a village near Badin. The nearest secondary school is a long way off, and many pupils stop after Class 5. At a school management committee meeting, a parent, Mr Soomro, holds up a copy of the Constitution. "Article 37 says the State shall remove illiteracy and provide free and compulsory secondary education. It says 'shall'! Let us take the government to court and make it build a school."

Another parent shakes his head. "Those words mean nothing. It is all paper." Rukhsana can see that both are partly wrong and partly right. But the two of them are looking at Article 37 alone, and there is an article that could change the whole discussion. Which one is it, and how would a teacher explain the difference? This topic answers that.

## Explanation

The <Glossary term="Principles of Policy" /> are in Part II, Chapter 2 of the Constitution: Articles 29 to 40. They set goals for the State. They sit next to the Fundamental Rights in the same Part, but they work very differently. Articles 29 and 30 tell you how.

| Article | What it says | In plain words |
|---|---|---|
| 29(1) | Each organ and authority of the State, and each person acting for it, is responsible for acting in accordance with these Principles | They are real duties, and not only advice |
| 29(2) | A Principle may be subject to the availability of resources | The State can say it cannot yet afford this |
| 29(3) | A yearly report on their observance goes to Parliament and the Provincial Assemblies, with provision for discussion | Elected members can question the government |
| 30(1) | Each organ or authority itself decides whether its action follows the Principles | The organ judges itself first |
| 30(2) | A law or action cannot be challenged on the ground that it does not follow the Principles, and no action lies on that ground | You cannot sue on this ground |

**Correcting a common misconception: "Principles of Policy can be enforced in court like rights."** They cannot, and Article 30(2) says so directly. This is what Mr Soomro has missed. He is right that the word "shall" appears, but a court cannot strike down a law or order the State to act on the ground that it has failed a Principle.

**Correcting the opposite misconception: "Then the Principles mean nothing."** This is also wrong. Article 29(1) makes them a responsibility of every organ of the State. The yearly report (Article 29(3)) gives elected members a chance to ask questions. Voters can judge a government by them. A person performing functions for the State may also fall under Article 29(1), so a government teacher can reasonably see their own work as part of this duty. The Principles work through politics and public duty, not through a judge's order.

### Islamic way of life

Article 31 is headed "Islamic way of life". It has two parts.

- **Article 31(1):** steps shall be taken to enable the Muslims of Pakistan, individually and collectively, to order their lives in accordance with the fundamental principles and basic concepts of Islam. Facilities shall also be provided so that they can understand the meaning of life according to the Holy Quran and Sunnah.
- **Article 31(2):** the State shall endeavour, as respects the Muslims of Pakistan, to make the teaching of the Holy Quran and Islamiat compulsory, to encourage the learning of Arabic, to secure correct printing of the Holy Quran, to promote unity and Islamic moral standards, and to organise zakat, ushr, auqaf and mosques properly.

Here is a local example. When a Sindh government school teaches Islamiat to Muslim pupils, it works in the direction that Article 31(2)(a) sets.

**A worked example: reading a Principle closely.** Ask three questions of any Principle.

1. **Who is it about?** "The Muslims of Pakistan". So it does not require non-Muslim pupils to study Islamiat. Article 22(1) protects them, and Article 36 tells the State to safeguard the rights of minorities.
2. **What is the verb?** "Steps shall be taken", and "shall endeavour", which means shall try hard. This describes a direction of effort, and not a result due by a fixed date.
3. **What is its force?** It is a Principle, so Articles 29 and 30 apply. If a province does not make Islamiat compulsory, this article gives no ground for a court case.

**Do not confuse this with other Islamic provisions.** Article 2 makes Islam the State religion. Article 227 says no law shall be enacted that is repugnant to the Injunctions of Islam. Unit 1 and Unit 4 discussed these. They are outside Part II and have their own machinery. Article 31 is different: it is a goal for the State, with the force of a Principle.

**Correcting a misconception: "Article 31 means every child in every school must be taught Islamiat."** The text does not say that. A teacher in a mixed school in Tharparkar can hold both duties: teach Islamiat well to Muslim pupils, and respect the rights of the others.

### Social justice and equality

**Social justice** means that the benefits and burdens of society are shared fairly, with special care for those who start with the least. The Constitution has no single article with that definition. Its meaning comes from several Principles read together.

| Article | Duty of the State | A Sindh example |
|---|---|---|
| 33 | Discourage parochial, racial, tribal, sectarian and provincial prejudices | Jokes in class about a pupil's province or sect work against this goal |
| 34 | Ensure full participation of women in all spheres of national life | Women teachers and head teachers in rural schools |
| 36 | Safeguard the rights and interests of minorities, including representation in services | Hindu and Christian candidates in the public service |
| 37(d), (e) | Inexpensive and quick justice; just and humane conditions of work; children and women not in unsuitable work | Children working in brick kilns or at home instead of attending school |
| 38(a) | Raise living standards; prevent wealth gathering in a few hands; fair adjustment between employers and employees, landlords and tenants | Haris (tenant farmers) and landlords in rural Sindh |
| 38(d) | Basic necessities for those who cannot earn because of infirmity, sickness or unemployment | Support for a family after the earner falls ill |

**Equality appears twice, and the difference matters.** Article 25 says all citizens are equal before law. That is a Fundamental Right. Article 38(a) tells the State to secure well-being irrespective of sex, caste, creed or race, and to narrow the gap between rich and poor. That is a Principle. You can see the difference in one example.

- A clerk refuses a girl a government job form because she is a girl. That is direct discrimination, and Article 25(2) applies. The matter can go to court.
- Two villages have very different incomes, so one has better schools because of private support. That is an inequality that Article 38(a) asks the State to reduce. No one can sue on that ground alone.

Legal equality says the law must treat people alike. Social justice asks whether real life gives them a fair chance. A society may have the first without the second. That is why the Constitution has both.

**A worked example: which kind of inequality is this?** Use two questions.

*Case:* A school in Umerkot has no toilet for girls, so older girls stop coming.

1. **Did a law or official treat someone differently because of who they are?** Not directly. Nobody told the girls to leave.
2. **Is it a gap in resources or opportunity?** Yes. So Article 37(a) on backward areas and Article 34 on women's participation both apply as Principles.

There is one more step. If these girls are aged five to sixteen, Article 25A may also apply, because it is a right. That is why a careful teacher looks in both chapters. The same problem can touch a Principle and a right.

### Promotion of education

Education has its own place in Article 37. Note the verbs.

- **37(a):** promote with special care the educational and economic interests of backward classes or areas.
- **37(b):** remove illiteracy and provide free and compulsory secondary education within minimum possible period.
- **37(c):** make technical and professional education generally available, and higher education equally accessible to all on the basis of merit.
- **37(f):** enable people of different areas, through education and training, to take full part in national activities, including the service of Pakistan.

Article 38(d) also lists education among the basic necessities to be provided for those unable to earn.

**From Principle to right.** For almost forty years, the Constitution dealt with this mainly as a Principle. Then in 2010, the Eighteenth Amendment added Article 25A, placing the State's duty on education to children aged five to sixteen among the Fundamental Rights (Hussain & Kokab, 2012). The move matters. It shifted part of the promise from a goal that could wait to a right that can be claimed.

**A worked example: Article 25A and Article 37(b) compared.** This is the central skill of the unit. Compare them on five points.

| Question | Article 25A | Article 37(b) |
|---|---|---|
| Which chapter? | Chapter 1, Fundamental Rights | Chapter 2, Principles of Policy |
| Who is covered? | All children aged five to sixteen | Everyone affected by illiteracy; those at secondary level |
| What is promised? | Free and compulsory education, in the manner set by law | Remove illiteracy; secondary education within minimum possible period |
| Can resources excuse it? | Article 29(2) does not apply, since this is not a Principle | Yes, Article 29(2) applies |
| Can you go to court? | Yes, through Article 199(1)(c), since it is a Fundamental Right | No, Article 30(2) bars it |

**Now look at the limits honestly.** A right is stronger, but it has limits too. Article 25A says "in such manner as may be determined by law". The provinces decide the details, as Sindh did in its 2013 Act. A court can direct the government to act, but a court order does not build a school overnight. A right can also go unused if families do not know about it or cannot reach a lawyer. So the better conclusion is this: Article 25A gives a stronger legal claim than Article 37(b), but the claim still has to be made and carried out.

**Returning to Rukhsana's meeting.** She can now explain the difference.

"Mr Soomro, you are right that the Constitution says 'shall'. But Article 30(2) says we cannot go to court on Article 37 alone. Our friend is also mistaken, because those words are still a real duty on the government, and it must report on them every year. There is a stronger article: Article 25A. Many of our children are aged five to sixteen, and for them free education is a Fundamental Right. So let us start by writing to the education department, using Article 25A and the Sindh law of 2013. Let us also ask our elected members about Article 37. If nothing changes, we can ask a lawyer about the High Court."

Her answer does not promise a school. It gives the parents a true map of what they can do.

## Activity: Structured controversy

Work in groups of four. About 30 minutes. Discuss this statement: "Pakistan should turn every Principle of Policy into a Fundamental Right."

1. **Prepare (5 minutes).** Two members prepare the case for. Two prepare the case against. Each side must use at least two articles from this unit, and one example from a school or village in Sindh.
2. **Present (5 minutes).** Each pair speaks in turn. The listeners take notes and do not interrupt.
3. **Switch (10 minutes).** Each pair now argues the other side, using the best points it heard.
4. **Agree (10 minutes).** Drop your roles. As a group, write three sentences that all four of you can honestly accept. They might name one Principle that should become a right, one that should not, and the reason.

Use what you learned in Article 29(2) and in the comparison of Articles 25A and 37(b). Read your three sentences to another group.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State what Article 30(2) says about a law or action that does not follow the Principles of Policy.
2. *(Understand)* Explain in three sentences how Article 25 and Article 38(a) deal with equality differently.
3. *(Apply)* A parent says, "The government has not made higher education accessible in our district, so I will file a case under Article 37(c)." Explain what you would tell the parent, and name one thing they could do instead.

## Summary

The Principles of Policy set goals for the State on matters such as the Islamic way of life, social justice and education. They are real duties on every organ of the State, and they are checked through a yearly report, elected members and voters. They cannot be enforced in court, and they may depend on available resources. A Fundamental Right can be enforced in court, which is why moving education for children aged five to sixteen into Article 25A mattered. But a right must still be claimed and carried out. A careful teacher asks of any constitutional promise: is this a right or a Principle, and what can people actually do with it?

## Try this at your practicum school

Find out how many pupils at your school stopped attending in the last year, and if possible why. Ask your cooperating teacher. Do not record any names. Take one reason, such as distance, cost, child work or the lack of a facility. Decide which Principle of Policy it touches, and whether Article 25A also applies. Write your findings in about six lines, and suggest one small step that the school or the school management committee could take.

## Summative task

Write 300-350 words as a note for a school management committee. Your note should explain to parents the difference between a Fundamental Right and a Principle of Policy, using the right to education in Article 25A as the case. It should:

1. Explain what Article 25A and Article 37(b) each promise.
2. Analyse how they differ in their force, using Articles 8, 29(2), 30(2) and 199.
3. Advise the committee on what to do about a child aged twelve who cannot reach school, and justify your advice.
