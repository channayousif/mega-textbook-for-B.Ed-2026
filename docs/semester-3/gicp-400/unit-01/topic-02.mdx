## A real classroom situation

Zainab is a trainee at a government high school in Mithi, Tharparkar, where Hindu and Muslim families have long lived as neighbours. Her Class 9 lesson is on the Two-Nation Theory. She writes the definition on the board. A pupil, Deepak, quietly asks, "Miss, does this mean my family should not be here?" His friend Ahmed looks embarrassed. Another pupil says, "Pakistan is an Islamic state, so it is for Muslims."

Zainab knows the exam definition. She does not know how to answer Deepak honestly. She wants to be faithful to the history, and she wants every child in the room to know that this is their country too. Is that possible? By the end of this topic you will be able to answer that question yourself.

## Explanation

Pakistan's founding ideas can be pictured as a tree. Two roots, the Two-Nation Theory and the Islamic concept of state, feed one trunk, the Ideology of Pakistan. We take them one at a time, and then see how they join.

### Two-Nation Theory

**What it claims.** The <Glossary term="Two-Nation Theory" /> holds that the Muslims and Hindus of South Asia were two separate nations, not one, and that the Muslims therefore had a right to govern themselves in a homeland of their own. It is a claim about **nationhood and political power**. A nation here means a people with a shared sense of who they are, and a claim to decide their own future. This is a political claim, not a claim that every Muslim held one political view.

**How it grew.** Hassan (2020) traces it through three leaders. The ideas developed over time, and the leaders did not all begin by wanting a separate country.

| Leader | Time | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| Sir Syed Ahmad Khan | After 1857 | Hassan calls him the pioneer of the idea. He argued it after the Hindi-Urdu controversy. |
| Allama Muhammad Iqbal | 1930 | At Allahabad, he asked for "a consolidated North-West Indian Muslim State" (Iqbal, 1930). He gave the idea a philosophical basis. |
| Quaid-e-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah | 1940 | At Lahore, he argued that Muslims formed a nation and should have their own state (Jinnah, 1940). He turned the idea into a political demand, which led to Pakistan. |

Jinnah had worked for Hindu-Muslim unity for many years before this (Hassan, 2020). Hassan also points to the experience of Congress rule in 1937-1939 as one event that pushed many Muslims towards the League. Unit 2 studies this history in detail.

**Jinnah's reasons.** At Lahore he argued that Hindus and Muslims have different religious philosophies, social customs and literature, different heroes and different histories (Jinnah, 1940). So they should not be forced into one state in which one permanent majority would rule the other.

**Correcting a common misconception: "The Two-Nation Theory means Muslims and Hindus cannot live side by side."** You may even read this in books. Hassan (2020) reports a common narrative that the two "could not be united peacefully". Read that sentence with care, because the argument was about *being united in one political nation under one government*. It was not a rule that neighbours of different faiths must live apart. Here is the evidence.

1. In the same 1940 speech, Jinnah said, "We wish to live in peace and harmony with our neighbours" (Jinnah, 1940).
2. On 11 August 1947 he told the Constituent Assembly of Pakistan that "you are free to go to your temples", and that religion "has nothing to do with the business of the State" (Jinnah, 1947).
3. The Constitution protects every citizen's right to profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to law, public order and morality (Article 20), and tells the State to safeguard the rights of minorities (Article 36) (Government of Pakistan, 1973/2012).
4. Hindu, Christian, Sikh and Parsi Pakistanis have lived here as citizens since 1947. The people of Mithi are a living example.

**A worked example: answering Deepak.** Follow these three steps whenever a founding idea is misread.

1. **Say what the idea claimed.** "In the 1940s, Muslim leaders said that Muslims were a nation with a right to their own state."
2. **Say what it did not claim.** "They did not say that Hindus and Muslims cannot be neighbours or that non-Muslims could not live in Pakistan."
3. **Say what the law says today.** "You are a citizen of Pakistan. Article 25 says all citizens are equal before law. Your family belongs here."

**Historians disagree, and pupils may be told so.** Hassan (2020) describes two groups. Historians such as Ishtiaq Hussain Qureshi see the Two-Nation Theory as the reason for Pakistan's creation. Others, such as Ayesha Jalal, argue that religion was one reason but not the only one. Some critics also say that the separation of East Pakistan in 1971 challenged the theory. Hassan's own conclusion is that the theory played an important role, but that Pakistan came from a series of events and decisions and cannot be linked to one cause. In class, you can teach the main idea clearly and also tell pupils that serious historians weigh it differently.

### Islamic concept of state

The second root is older than South Asian politics. It is a set of ideas about what a state is for and where its authority comes from. Scholars have explained these ideas in different ways. Here we study this concept through Iqbal's account and the Preamble. Other Muslim thinkers have offered different accounts. The table links these ideas to Qur'anic passages and constitutional wording; it is not a claim that one verse specifies a complete modern system of government.

| Feature | Meaning | Support |
|---|---|---|
| Sovereignty of Allah | Final authority belongs to Allah alone | Preamble: "sovereignty over the entire Universe belongs to Almighty Allah alone" |
| Authority as a trust | Those who govern hold power as a trust and must answer for it | Preamble: "a sacred trust"; Qur'an 4:58 |
| Justice | Rulers must judge fairly between people | Qur'an 4:58 |
| Consultation (*shura*) | Public matters are decided by consulting people | Qur'an 42:38; 3:159 |
| Equality | People differ in piety, not in birth or tribe | Qur'an 49:13 |
| Freedom of faith | Nobody may be compelled in religion | Qur'an 2:256 |

<Glossary term="Sovereignty" /> is the highest authority in a state. In most modern theories it belongs to the people. The Preamble states it differently: sovereignty belongs to Allah, and the people exercise authority "within the limits prescribed by Him". The people still choose their government. The Preamble says that the State shall act "through the chosen representatives of the people".

**Iqbal's explanation.** In the sixth lecture of *The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam*, Iqbal says that the essence of *Tauhid* (the oneness of Allah), as a working idea, is "equality, solidarity, and freedom". The state, in his view, is an attempt to make these ideals real in a human organisation (Iqbal, 1934, Lecture VI). Solidarity means people accepting responsibility for one another. For example, a Sindh school committee that consults parents, accounts for its spending and treats families fairly can illustrate consultation, trust and justice. It does not itself exercise the powers of a state. He also calls the republican form of government "thoroughly consistent with the spirit of Islam". And he suggests that the work of interpreting law for modern conditions could pass to an elected Muslim legislative assembly.

**Correcting a misconception: "An Islamic state is a theocracy."** A <Glossary term="Theocracy" /> is a state ruled by a class of religious leaders who claim to govern in God's name, without the people's consent. Iqbal directly rejects this picture. He writes that the Islamic state is not headed by a representative of God on earth who can hide his will behind a claim of infallibility (Iqbal, 1934, Lecture VI). He uses the word "theocracy" only in the special sense that a state serves ideal principles. The pupil in Zainab's class who said "Islamic state, so it is for Muslims" is also mistaken, as the worked example below and the last row of the table above both show.

**A worked example: test one claim against the text.**

*Claim:* "In Pakistan, religious scholars rule and people have no say."

1. **What does the Preamble say?** Power is exercised "through the chosen representatives of the people".
2. **Where do laws come from?** Through Parliament: the President, National Assembly and Senate (Article 50). The assemblies and Senate give elected representatives a law-making role; Parliament is not a council of religious rulers.
3. **Where does Islam come in?** Article 227 says no law shall be enacted that is repugnant to the Injunctions of Islam. So Islam sets a limit on what the elected Parliament may do.
4. **What about non-Muslims?** Article 227(3) says this Part shall not affect the personal laws of non-Muslim citizens "or their status as citizens".
5. **Conclusion.** The claim is false. The Constitution combines elected government with an Islamic limit on law.

### Ideology of Pakistan

The <Glossary term="Ideology of Pakistan" /> is the set of ideas on which Pakistan was founded. It joins the two roots into one trunk. It can be stated as three commitments.

1. **Self-government for the Muslims of South Asia in a homeland of their own.** This comes from the Two-Nation Theory.
2. **Islamic principles in public life.** Muslims should be able to order their lives by the teachings of Islam, and the State should observe justice, equality and consultation. This comes from the Islamic concept of state.
3. **Democracy and equal citizenship.** Government is chosen by the people, and every citizen, of any faith, has equal rights. This comes from both roots and from the speeches of the founders.

<Figure id="fig-U1-4" src="/img/figures/gicp-400/unit-01/fig-U1-4.svg" alt="Table of three ideas with their meaning, a key statement and a common misreading: the Two-Nation Theory (Muslims as a nation with a right to self-government; misread as saying Muslims and Hindus cannot live side by side), the Islamic concept of state (authority as a trust under Allah's sovereignty; misread as rule by religious leaders), and the Ideology of Pakistan (self-government, Islamic principles and equal citizenship; misread as meaning only Islam or only a slogan)." />

**Where it appears in law.** The clearest bridge is the Objectives Resolution, which Pakistan's first Constituent Assembly passed on 12 March 1949. Its ideas now form the Preamble, and Article 2A makes the Resolution a substantive part of the Constitution. Topic 1.1 showed how such a chain works. Here is the chain for this ideology.

| Commitment | Principle in the Preamble | Articles |
|---|---|---|
| Self-government | Authority exercised "through the chosen representatives of the people" | Article 50 (Parliament) |
| Islamic principles | Muslims enabled to order their lives by "the teachings and requirements of Islam" | Articles 2, 31, 227 |
| Equal citizenship | "equality of status, of opportunity and before law"; minorities free "to profess and practise their religions" | Articles 20, 25, 36 |

**There is a real tension here, and it should be named.** Pakistanis have long debated how the commitments fit together. Some read the speech of 11 August 1947 as promising a state where religion is a private matter. Others stress the Objectives Resolution and Article 227. The Constitution holds both. Article 2 makes Islam the State religion, and Article 25 makes all citizens equal before law. A teacher does not have to settle this debate. A teacher does have to present it fairly, and must not use it to make any pupil feel less of a citizen.

**Correcting a common misreading: "The Ideology of Pakistan just means Islam."** Islam is central to it, but this statement leaves out self-government, democracy and equal citizenship. A student who says this has seen one branch of the tree and missed the others.

**Returning to Deepak.** Zainab can now say: "The Two-Nation Theory was a claim, in the 1940s, that the Muslims of South Asia were a nation with a right to self-government. It was never a claim that you do not belong. The same founders promised equal citizenship, and the Constitution makes that promise law. Deepak, you and Ahmed are equal citizens of this country. Now, let us see where the Constitution says so."

## Activity: Claim and evidence

Work in groups of four. About 30 minutes. Each group gets one of these claims:

- A. "The Two-Nation Theory means Muslims and Hindus cannot live side by side."
- B. "Pakistan is an Islamic state, so non-Muslims are not full citizens."
- C. "An Islamic state means rule by religious leaders."
- D. "The Ideology of Pakistan is only a slogan from old textbooks."

Two members argue that the claim is wrong. The other two look for what is true or understandable in it, and why a person might believe it. Use only the evidence in this topic: the speeches, the Preamble and the articles. After 10 minutes, each pair presents its best point. Then the four of you write one fair reply to the claim, using the three steps from the worked example. Say what the idea claimed, what it did not claim, and what the law says.

Read your reply aloud to another group. They should ask: "Would every pupil in a mixed class in Tharparkar feel respected by this reply?" Change it if the answer is no.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State the Two-Nation Theory in one sentence, and name the three leaders linked to it, with one contribution of each.
2. *(Remember)* From memory, list four features of the Islamic concept of state.
3. *(Understand)* Explain, in three sentences, why the Islamic concept of state as described by Iqbal is different from a theocracy.
4. *(Apply)* A pupil in your class says, "My uncle says Christians should go to another country because of the Two-Nation Theory." Write what you would say, using the three steps.

## Summary

The Two-Nation Theory is a claim about nationhood and self-government. It is not a claim that people of different faiths cannot be neighbours. The Islamic concept of state holds that sovereignty belongs to Allah, and that the people hold authority as a trust, to be used with justice, consultation and equality. It is not rule by a religious class. These two roots feed the Ideology of Pakistan, which joins self-government, Islamic principles and equal citizenship. The Preamble and the articles of the Constitution carry these commitments into law. Serious people debate how they fit together, and a good teacher presents that debate fairly and in a way that respects every pupil as a citizen.
