## A real classroom situation

Sadia is a B.Ed trainee on practicum at a government girls' high school near Khairpur. A Class 8 pupil, Rukhsana, stays behind after a lesson. Her father wants her to leave school at the end of the year, because the family needs help at home. Sadia tells her that Article 25A of the Constitution promises free and compulsory education up to the age of sixteen.

Rukhsana looks doubtful. "Miss, last week the Pakistan Studies teacher told us that education is part of our national ideals. Now you say it is in the Constitution. Are those the same thing? Is it a nice idea, or is it a law?"

Sadia starts to answer and stops. She has memorised both words for exams. She has never had to say how an ideal differs from a law, or how one becomes the other. By the end of this topic you will be able to answer Rukhsana.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What is an ideology? What is a constitution, and why does a country need one? And how are the two connected?

### Meaning of ideology

An <Glossary term="Ideology" /> is a set of shared ideas and beliefs about how a group of people should live together and be governed. Three parts of this definition matter.

1. **It is shared.** One person's opinion is not an ideology. An ideology belongs to a group: a party, a movement or a nation.
2. **It is about how things should be.** It does not only describe society. It says what a good society would look like.
3. **It guides action.** People use it to decide what to demand, what to build and what to oppose.

**A national ideology** answers questions like these. Why does this nation exist? What does it stand for? What kind of society does it want to build?

**The word has more than one use.** Roberts (2025) shows that scholars use "ideology" in many different ways. Sometimes it is a neutral word for a group's worldview. Sometimes it is an insult, meaning beliefs that hide the truth or protect the powerful. In daily talk in Pakistan you may hear this second use: "That is just his ideology." In this course we use a neutral working definition. Calling something an ideology here does not praise it or attack it. It only says that a group holds these ideas together and acts on them.

**A worked example: is it an ideology?** Use the three parts as a test.

| Statement | Shared? | About how things should be? | Guides action? | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| "I prefer tea to coffee." | No | No | No | A personal taste |
| "Sukkur is on the River Indus." | Yes | No, it describes a fact | No | A fact |
| "Every child in the country deserves schooling, so the State must provide it." | Yes, many people hold it | Yes | Yes, people campaign and pass laws because of it | An idea that can be part of an ideology |

The third statement is one idea, not a whole ideology. An ideology is a connected set of such ideas. Still, this is what Rukhsana's teacher meant by "national ideals".

### Concept and importance of a constitution

A **constitution** is the highest law of a state. It sets up the main institutions of government, says what powers each one has, limits those powers, and protects the rights of the people. Waluchow and Kyritsis (2023) describe a constitution as a set of rules, principles or values that create and structure government power, and that may also set limits on it. Most constitutions, like Pakistan's, are written in one main document. The United Kingdom is the well-known exception, because its constitution is spread across many laws and practices.

**What a constitution does.** The table below groups three jobs: setting up institutions, limiting power and protecting rights (Government of Pakistan, 1973/2012). The following examples explain each job.

**Why a constitution matters.** Look at what each job does for an ordinary person.

- **Institutions.** Everyone knows who makes laws, who carries them out and who settles disputes. Without this, power goes to whoever is strongest. Unit 4 shows what happened in Pakistan when these rules broke down.
- **Limits on power.** Article 8(2) says the State "shall not make any law" that takes away the <Glossary term="Fundamental Rights" />. A law that conflicts with a protected right is void to that extent, subject to the Constitution's own exceptions. Even Parliament is under the Constitution. This is the central idea of constitutional government: power is legally limited (Waluchow & Kyritsis, 2023).
- **Rights.** Article 25(1) says "All citizens are equal before law". Because this is in the highest law, a person treated unequally has something firm to point to.
- **Stability.** Ordinary laws are passed by a simple majority. Under Article 239, a bill to amend the Constitution needs at least two-thirds of the total membership of each House. So basic rules cannot change whenever a government finds them inconvenient.

**How a constitution differs from an ordinary law.** Compare a law on school fees with Article 25A. A provincial assembly can change the fee law through its normal process. It cannot pass a law that cancels Article 25A, because other laws must agree with the Constitution. The Constitution sits above them. In a Hyderabad school, a fee rule must therefore be checked against the constitutional duty to provide free education, rather than treated as the highest rule.

**Correcting a common misconception: "A constitution is only a legal document with no values in it."** Many trainees think of the Constitution as a long book for lawyers. Open the first page. The Preamble speaks of "democracy, freedom, equality, tolerance and social justice", and of the place of Islam in national life. Part II lists rights such as equality and freedom of religion. These are values. A constitution is a legal document *and* a statement of what a nation believes. The law gives the values force. The values give the law its purpose.

### Relationship between ideology and constitution

Now we can answer Rukhsana. **Ideology and constitution are not the same thing, but they are closely linked.**

| | Ideology | Constitution |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A set of shared ideas | The highest law of a state |
| Where it lives | In speeches, books, movements and people's minds | In a document with numbered articles |
| What it says | What society should be like | What institutions exist, what they may do, what rights people hold |
| Who it binds | Creates no legal duties by itself | People and State institutions within its scope |
| How it changes | Slowly, through debate | Through the formal amendment process |

**Correcting a common misconception: "Ideology and constitution are the same thing."** This mistake is easy to make, because the two share so many words. But an idea in a speech does not bind anybody. A political promise by itself does not have the legal force of a constitutional right. An article can bind, because the State has adopted it as law.

**How one becomes the other: a three-step chain.** The concept map at the top of this topic follows this chain.

1. **Values.** A people or a movement holds shared values. These are the ideology.
2. **Principles.** When the people's representatives write a constitution, they state those values as general principles. In Pakistan this is done in the Preamble, which comes from the <Glossary term="Objectives Resolution" /> of 1949.
3. **Articles.** The principles are then turned into exact rules that say who must do what.

**A worked example: tracing equality.** Here is how to follow one value through the chain.

- **Value.** Three days before independence, Quaid-e-Azam told the Constituent Assembly that "we are all citizens, and equal citizens, of one State" (Jinnah, 1947).
- **Principle.** The Preamble says that the fundamental rights guaranteed shall include "equality of status, of opportunity and before law" (Government of Pakistan, 1973/2012).
- **Articles.** Article 25(1) makes equality before law a Fundamental Right. Article 36 tells the State to safeguard the rights and interests of minorities.

Now read the chain in the other direction. If you want to understand why Article 25 is there, go back to the principle and the value. If you want to know what the value means in practice, go forward to the article.

**The chain can weaken at any step.** This is the analytical part, and it matters for a teacher. The articles do not all have the same legal force.

- A **Fundamental Right** (Articles 8 to 28) is enforceable within its constitutional scope. Article 8 makes conflicting laws void to that extent, but also contains exceptions. Read the whole provision before applying it.
- A <Glossary term="Principles of Policy">Principle of Policy</Glossary> (Articles 29 to 40) guides the State, but Article 30(2) says that a law or action cannot be challenged in court only because it does not follow one.

So the same value can reach the Constitution with different strength. A second problem is the gap between law and practice. An article can be strong on paper and still not be carried out in every village.

**A worked example: analysing Article 25A.**

| Question | Analysis |
|---|---|
| What value lies behind it? | Every child deserves schooling, and the State is responsible for providing it. |
| What does the article say? | The State "shall provide free and compulsory education" to children aged five to sixteen, "in such manner as may be determined by law". |
| How strong is it? | It is a Fundamental Right, so it is stronger than a Principle of Policy. But the words "as may be determined by law" mean it also depends on other laws. |
| What is the gap? | Some children, like Rukhsana, still leave school early. The promise is real, but it needs families, schools and government to carry it out. |

**Returning to Rukhsana.** Sadia can now say: "Both of your teachers are right. That every child should be educated is an ideal. When it was written into Article 25A, it also became law. The law does not make it happen by itself, but it gives you and your father something firmer than a hope. Let us talk to your father, and to the headteacher, about what it means for you."

## Activity: Follow one value

Work in groups of three. About 25 minutes.

Each group takes one value: **religious freedom**, **removing illiteracy**, or **ending prejudice between groups**. Each member takes one job.

1. **The value finder** explains in two sentences why this value matters in your district. Use a real example, such as a local school, shrine, temple or church.
2. **The principle finder** looks through the Preamble phrases below and picks the words that state this value as a principle. If no words fit exactly, say so. That is a finding too.
3. **The article finder** matches the value to an article from this list and reads out what it says, in plain words: Article 20 (freedom to profess religion), Article 33 (discouraging parochial, racial, tribal, sectarian and provincial prejudices), Article 37(b) (removing illiteracy).

Preamble phrases to search: "freely to profess and practise their religions"; "tolerance and social justice"; "freedom of thought, expression, belief, faith, worship".

As a group, decide:

- Is your article a Fundamental Right or a Principle of Policy? How can you tell from its number?
- What can a citizen do if the State does not follow it?

Then pair with another group and explain your chain from memory, without notes.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Without looking back, give the three parts of the definition of an ideology.
2. *(Remember)* State three jobs that a constitution does, and give one article of the Constitution of Pakistan for each.
3. *(Understand)* A classmate says, "Ideology and constitution are the same thing." In three sentences, explain what is wrong with this statement and how the two are connected.
4. *(Apply)* A pupil asks why Article 33 cannot be used in court to cancel a law, while Article 25 can. Explain the difference to the pupil in simple words.

## Summary

An ideology is a shared set of ideas about how people should live and be governed. It guides people, but it creates no legal duties by itself. A constitution is the highest law of a state. It sets up institutions, limits power and protects rights, and it also states values. The two are linked by a chain: values become principles, and principles become articles. The chain can weaken, because articles differ in legal force and because law on paper is not always carried out in practice.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find one rule at your school that pupils know well, such as a rule about uniform, fees, attendance or the language of the assembly. Ask your cooperating teacher what value lies behind it. Then look through Articles 20, 22, 25, 25A and 37 to see whether any of them speaks to the same value. Write three lines: the rule, the value, and the article, or a note that no article speaks to it.

## Summative task

Write 250-300 words. Choose **one** of these articles: Article 20, Article 25, Article 25A, Article 33 or Article 36.

1. Name the value behind the article, and the words of the Preamble that state it as a principle.
2. Explain what the article requires, in your own words.
3. Analyse how far the article carries the value. Consider its legal force and one gap you know of between the article and life in your district.

*(Analyze)*

**Mini-rubric (10 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Value and principle (2 marks) | Names the value and quotes fitting Preamble words (2) | Names the value only (1) | Absent or unrelated |
| Meaning of the article (3 marks) | Explains accurately in own words (3) | Mostly accurate, or copied from the text (1-2) | Wrong or absent |
| Analysis of how far the article carries the value (5 marks) | Uses legal force and a specific local gap to reach a reasoned conclusion (4-5) | Mentions force or a gap, without reaching a reasoned conclusion (2-3); only states an opinion (1) | No analysis |

The analysis criterion asks you to show how your evidence leads to your conclusion. Praising the article or criticising the government does not meet it.
