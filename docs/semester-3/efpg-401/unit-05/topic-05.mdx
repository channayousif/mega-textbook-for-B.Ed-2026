## A real classroom situation

Saima teaches Grade 6 in a school in Mirpurkhas. She has a prose lesson on a story about a boy who helps a lost goat, and a poetry lesson on a short poem about rain. For the prose lesson, she reads the story aloud twice and asks the class five questions from the textbook. The pupils answer well, and she moves on. For the poetry lesson, she reads the poem once, asks the pupils to memorise it, and gives them the meanings of four difficult words to copy.

At the end of the week, the pupils can answer questions about the story but cannot say what the story is really about. They can recite the poem but cannot say what it makes them feel. Saima's colleague asks, "Did the pupils meet the story, or did they only answer questions about it? Did they hear the poem, or did they only memorise it?"

## Explanation

Prose and poetry need different lessons because they do different things. Prose tells a story or gives information, so the lesson moves towards understanding and using the language. Poetry gives an experience, so the lesson moves from hearing and feeling the poem towards talking about it.

### Planning a prose lesson

A prose lesson has four main stages:

1. **Pre-reading.** The teacher prepares the pupils for the text. A picture, a short question or a discussion about the topic activates what the pupils already know. The aim is to give them a reason to read.
2. **Reading.** The pupils read the text silently or listen to the teacher read it. A first reading is for the main idea; a second reading is for detail. The teacher should not explain every word before the pupils read, or the reading becomes a translation exercise.
3. **Comprehension and language work.** The teacher checks understanding with questions that move from literal to inferential: "What did the boy do?" becomes "Why did he do it?". New words are taught in the context of the story.
4. **Extension activity.** The pupils use the language of the story in a new task, such as role-playing the ending, writing a different ending, or interviewing a character.

### Planning a poetry lesson

A poetry lesson has a different flow, because a poem is meant to be heard:

1. **Listening for enjoyment.** The teacher reads the poem aloud well, twice, while the pupils listen with their books closed. The first aim is not understanding but feeling the rhythm and the mood.
2. **Recitation and reading.** The pupils read the poem aloud, individually and in chorus. The teacher corrects pronunciation and models the rhythm.
3. **Discussing meaning.** The teacher talks about the poem through simple questions: "What picture do you see?", "How does the poem make you feel?", "Which line do you like best?". The pupils are allowed to give different answers.
4. **Appreciation and response.** The pupils copy the poem, draw the scene it describes, or write two lines about their own experience of rain. The aim is a personal response, not a single correct meaning.

### Worked example: prose and poetry side by side

Study the two plans for the same class. Notice how the questions change.

| Stage | Prose lesson: "The Lost Goat" | Poetry lesson: "Rain" |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Show a picture of a goat; ask "Have you seen a goat?" | Read the poem aloud twice with books closed |
| First encounter | Pupils read the story silently for the main idea | Pupils read the poem aloud in chorus |
| Deeper work | Ask "Why did the boy help the goat?" | Ask "What picture do you see in your mind?" |
| Language work | Teach three new words from the story in context | Talk about one or two difficult words through the poem |
| Response | In pairs, pupils role-play the boy meeting the goat's owner | Pupils draw the scene and write two lines of their own |

### Common misconceptions

One wrong idea is that poetry is just memorisation. Memorising a poem is a useful exercise for rhythm and vocabulary, but it is not the aim. The aim is that the pupils hear the poem, feel something and talk about it. Another wrong idea is that a prose lesson is only comprehension questions. Questions check understanding, but the lesson also needs an extension activity that lets the pupils use the language. Celce-Murcia (1991) emphasises that reading lessons should move from global understanding to detailed work and then to personal response.

## Activity: Compare two lesson openings

**Work in pairs.** Time allocation: approximately 20 minutes.

Read the two openings below. The first is a prose opening, the second a poetry opening.

> **A.** "Today we will read a story about a boy and a goat. Open your books to page 30. I will read the story. Then answer questions 1 to 5."
> **B.** "Close your books. I am going to read you a poem about rain. Listen to the words and the sound. After I finish, tell me one word that describes how the poem makes you feel."

1. For each opening, identify the stage it belongs to and say what the pupils are expected to do.
2. Explain which opening gives the pupils a better reason to read or listen, and why.
3. Rewrite opening A so that it gives the pupils a reason to read before they open their books.
4. Exchange your rewrite with another pair. Does it activate the pupils' own experience? If not, suggest one change.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* List the four stages of a prose lesson and the four stages of a poetry lesson.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why a poetry lesson begins with listening rather than reading, and what that first listening achieves.
3. *(Apply)* A teacher reads a story aloud and then explains every new word before the pupils read. Explain why this makes the silent reading harder, and describe a better order for the vocabulary work.

## Summary

A prose lesson moves from pre-reading through reading and comprehension to an extension activity. A poetry lesson moves from listening for enjoyment through recitation and discussion to a personal response. The two lessons share the same plan shape, but the questions and activities are different. The aim of both is that the pupils meet the text, not just answer questions about it.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher whether they will let you observe a prose or poetry lesson. Note the stages the teacher uses and the questions they ask. After the lesson, ask the teacher why they chose those questions, and compare their answer with the stages in this topic.

## Summative task

Choose a short prose text or a short poem that you could teach to Grade 6. Write a lesson plan that shows the stages, the teacher's questions, the pupil activities and the aim of each stage. Then write a short note explaining how the lesson would be different if you swapped the text type.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1 mark) | Proficient (2 marks) | Exemplary (3-4 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stages** | The stages are missing or confused between prose and poetry. | The stages suit the chosen text type and are in order. | The stages are well paced and each has a clear aim. |
