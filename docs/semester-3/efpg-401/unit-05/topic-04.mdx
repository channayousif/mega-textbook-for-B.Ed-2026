## A real classroom situation

Bilal teaches Grade 4 in a school in Larkana. He wants to teach the pattern "There is a ... / There are ..." for describing a room. He begins by writing the two sentences on the board and explaining the difference between "is" and "are". The pupils copy the sentences. Then he points at the classroom and says, "Tell me what is in the room." The pupils look at him blankly.

He tries again. This time he draws a simple picture of a room on the board and labels four things: a table, two chairs, a window and a door. He models the sentences slowly, pointing at each thing. The pupils repeat after him. Then he asks pairs to make sentences about the picture, and finally to describe their own classroom. By the end of the lesson, most pupils can say three true sentences.

Bilal tells his colleague, "The difference was not my explanation. It was the picture and the practice. The pupils needed to see the pattern before they could say it."

## Explanation

A structural lesson teaches a sentence pattern. The pattern might be a tense, a question form or a phrase such as "There is a ...". The structural approach, which you met in Unit 3, treats language as a set of patterns that pupils learn through graded practice. A structural lesson is not a grammar lecture. It is a sequence that moves the pattern from the teacher's mouth to the pupil's own speech.

### What a structural lesson is

In a structural lesson, the teacher chooses one pattern and makes it the centre of the lesson. The pattern is presented in a clear context, practised under control, and then used freely. The lesson is successful when the pupils can use the pattern correctly in a new situation, not when they can repeat the rule. For example, pupils who can say "There are two chairs in my room" about their own room have learned the pattern, even if they cannot explain why "are" is used.

### The three stages: presentation, controlled practice, free practice

1. **Presentation.** The teacher introduces the pattern in a context the pupils can see or hear. A picture, a real object or a short dialogue can carry the meaning. The teacher models the pattern two or three times and the pupils repeat. The aim is for the pupils to understand what the pattern means and how it sounds.
2. **Controlled practice.** The pupils use the pattern in a task where the language is held steady. Substitution drills, gap-filling and pair question-and-answer are common. The teacher corrects errors immediately, because the aim at this stage is accuracy. Every pupil should produce the pattern several times.
3. **Free practice.** The pupils use the pattern to talk or write about something real, with less support. For example, they describe their own classroom or their own family. The teacher does not correct every error here; the aim is fluency and ownership.

### Worked example: a filled plan for "There is / There are"

Study this plan for Grade 4. Notice that the pattern is presented through a picture, not a rule.

| Stage | Time | Teacher action | Pupil action | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Warm-up | 4 min | Show a picture of a market and ask what pupils can see | Name things in English | Activate vocabulary |
| Presentation | 8 min | Draw a room on the board; label a table, two chairs, a window; model "There is a table", "There are two chairs" | Repeat the sentences and point at the things | Introduce the pattern in context |
| Controlled practice | 10 min | Hold up flashcards one by one and ask "What is there?" | Answer "There is a ..." / "There are ..." | Build accuracy with support |
| Free practice | 10 min | Ask pairs to describe their own classroom | Describe the classroom to a partner | Use the pattern freely |
| Assessment | 4 min | Ask each pupil to write two true sentences about the classroom | Write two sentences | Check the pattern is used correctly |
| Homework | - | Describe one room at home in three sentences | Write three sentences | Extend the practice |

### Common misconceptions

One wrong idea is that drills are meaningless. A substitution drill gives pupils many repetitions of the pattern in a short time, and repetition is how a new pattern becomes automatic. The drill becomes meaningless only when it has no context at all. Another wrong idea is that the structural lesson is only for grammar. Any pattern, including questions, requests and greetings, can be taught through the same three stages. Celce-Murcia (1991) notes that the structural approach works best when the pattern is presented in a meaningful situation and then practised in a graded sequence.

## Activity: Build a structural lesson

**Work in groups of four.** Time allocation: approximately 25 minutes.

Choose one of these patterns for Grade 5: "Can you ...?", "I am going to ...", or "This is the ... that ...". Build a structural lesson for it.

1. Choose the context you will use to present the pattern (a picture, a real object or a short dialogue). Describe it in two sentences.
2. Write the presentation stage, including the two model sentences the teacher will say.
3. Design a controlled-practice task that gives every pupil at least three repetitions of the pattern.
4. Design a free-practice task where pupils use the pattern to talk about their own lives.
5. Exchange your plan with another group. Check that the practice task is truly controlled and the free task is truly free, and suggest one improvement.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the three stages of a structural lesson in order, and state the teacher's aim at each stage.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why the presentation stage should use a picture or a context rather than a rule.
3. *(Apply)* A teacher presents the pattern "There is / There are", then asks pupils to write a paragraph about their favourite place. Explain why the free-practice task is too large a jump from the presentation, and design a controlled-practice task that would bridge the gap.

## Summary

A structural lesson teaches one sentence pattern through three stages: presentation, controlled practice and free practice. The pattern is introduced in a context, practised under control for accuracy, and then used freely for fluency. The pupils have learned the pattern when they can use it in a new situation, not when they can repeat the rule.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher whether they have taught a structural lesson recently. If so, ask which sentence pattern they chose and how they presented it. If not, offer to plan one using the three stages and ask for feedback after you teach it.

## Summative task

Write a complete structural lesson plan for Grade 5 on the pattern "There is a ... / There are ...". The plan must show the three stages with a time box, the teacher action, the pupil action and the purpose of each, and it must include the picture or object you will use to present the pattern. Then write a short note explaining how the controlled-practice task differs from the free-practice task.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1 mark) | Proficient (2 marks) | Exemplary (3-4 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Three stages** | The stages are missing or in the wrong order. | All three stages are present with a clear aim. | Each stage has a clear aim and the sequence is logical and well paced. |
| **Context and tasks** | The pattern is presented as a rule with no context. | A context is used and both practice tasks are present. | The context makes the meaning clear and the tasks are graded from control to freedom. |
| **Justification** *(Analyze)* | Says the tasks are "practice" without explaining the difference. | Distinguishes accuracy practice from fluency practice. | **Analyze:** Analyses how the amount of support changes across the three stages and why that sequence helps pupils own the pattern. |
