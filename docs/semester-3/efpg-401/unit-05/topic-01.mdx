## A real classroom situation

Nasreen teaches English in a government primary school in Shahdadkot. Her Grade 5 class is large, the textbook is the only resource, and she has thirty-five minutes. For a lesson on the past tense, she explains the rule on the board, reads two example sentences, and asks the class to copy ten sentences from the textbook. The pupils are quiet and the room is orderly.

At the end of the lesson she asks, "What is the rule for the past tense?" The class recites the rule perfectly. Then she asks, "What did you eat for breakfast this morning?" and the room goes silent. The pupils know the rule, but they cannot use it to say one true sentence about their own lives.

During the break her colleague asks what went wrong. Nasreen says, "I taught the whole lesson. They listened." Her colleague replies, "They listened to English, but they did not do anything with it. A lesson is not a lecture with exercises at the end. The pupils need to use the language in every part of the lesson, not only when the bell is about to ring."

## Explanation

Nasreen's lesson failed because the pupils spent almost all of it receiving language and almost none of it using language. In a language lesson, the pupil is the one who must practise the skill. The teacher can talk about English for thirty-five minutes and the pupils will still be unable to speak it.

### What an activity is

An activity is any planned task in which the pupils use language to do something. The something can be small: ordering five picture cards, asking a partner about their weekend, or filling a gap in a dialogue. What makes it an activity is that the pupil has to produce or process language, not just hear it. A good activity has a clear outcome the teacher can see, such as a completed card order, a short spoken answer, or a written sentence.

### Why every lesson needs activities

Activities matter for three reasons. First, language is a skill, and a skill improves through use. A pupil who only hears the past tense is like a swimmer who only watches the coach. Second, activities give the teacher evidence. When pupils do something with language, the teacher can walk around, listen and see who has understood. Third, activities hold attention. A class of forty ten-year-olds cannot sit through thirty-five minutes of explanation, and a lesson built around short tasks keeps them working.

Paulsrud and Gheitasi (2024) studied how pre-service English teachers plan lessons for linguistically diverse classes in Sweden. They found that planning is a situated activity: teachers make decisions about activities by reading the class in front of them, the materials they have, and the outcomes they must reach, not by following a fixed recipe. In other words, choosing an activity is a professional decision, not a trick.

### The balance of teacher talk and pupil work

A useful rule of thumb is that the teacher's talk should shrink as the lesson moves on. In the opening, the teacher explains and demonstrates. In the middle, the pupils try the language with support. In the closing, the pupils use it with little or no support. Sheikh (1998) describes the lesson as a movement from controlled work, where the teacher holds the language steady, to free work, where the pupils take it over. If the teacher talks for most of the lesson, the pupils never cross that line.

### Worked example: a 35-minute lesson with an activity at every stage

Study this plan for Grade 5, topic "My Village". Notice that every stage has something for the pupils to do.

| Stage | Time | Pupil activity | What it achieves |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm-up | 4 min | Name five things they can see from the classroom window | Settles the class and activates English words they already know |
| Presentation | 7 min | Look at three pictures of a village and guess two new words | Introduces new vocabulary through context, not translation |
| Practice | 10 min | In pairs, ask and answer "Is there a ... in your village?" | Controls the new structure so every pupil says it several times |
| Production | 10 min | In groups of four, describe their own village to the group | Uses the language freely for a real communicative purpose |
| Review | 4 min | Write three true sentences about their village and read one aloud | Checks learning and gives the teacher evidence to act on |

Now compare it with Nasreen's lesson. Her lesson had one activity, copying sentences, and it came at the end. The revised plan has five activities and the hardest work happens while the teacher is still nearby to help.

### Common misconceptions

Two wrong ideas spoil many lessons. The first is that activities waste time that should be used for the syllabus. In fact, an activity is how the syllabus gets taught; a lesson that covers the textbook without giving pupils practice leaves nothing behind. The second is that a lesson plan is paperwork for the head teacher. A plan is a thinking tool for the teacher. It forces the teacher to decide what the pupils will do, not only what the teacher will say. Celce-Murcia (1991) reminds teachers that a language classroom should be organized around learner participation, because the learner does the learning.

## Activity: The activity audit

**Work in pairs.** Time allocation: approximately 20 minutes.

Read the following short lesson description for Grade 4:

> The teacher reads a short story about a fisherman twice. She asks the class three questions about the story. Then she explains five new words and asks pupils to copy them into their notebooks. Finally she reads the story a third time and asks pupils to answer five questions in writing at the end of the book.

1. Identify every place where the pupils could be doing something more active. Say what the activity would be.
2. Rewrite the lesson as five stages, each with a pupil activity and a time box.
3. Exchange your plan with another pair. Check whether the pupil activity grows from controlled to free, and whether the teacher talk shrinks.
4. Give one piece of feedback that would help the other pair improve their plan.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* In one sentence, define what makes a classroom task an activity rather than an exercise the teacher performs.
2. *(Understand)* Explain two reasons why a language lesson built only around teacher explanation leaves pupils unable to use the language.
3. *(Apply)* A Grade 6 teacher explains the present continuous for twelve minutes and then gives pupils a worksheet to complete individually. Describe two changes that would move the lesson towards pupil activity, and say what each change achieves.

## Summary

Every English lesson needs activities because language is a skill that improves through use. An activity is a planned task in which pupils produce or process language towards a visible outcome. The teacher's talk should shrink as the lesson moves on, while pupil work grows from controlled practice to free production. Choosing the right activity for the class and the aim is a professional decision, not a trick.

## Try this at your practicum school

During your next visit, ask your cooperating teacher for a recent English lesson plan, or observe one English lesson. Note down how many minutes the teacher talked and how many minutes the pupils spent on an activity. Then write one short suggestion for an activity that would give the pupils more practice, and share it with the teacher.

## Summative task

Choose a Grade 5 or 6 English topic you could teach in thirty-five minutes. Write a one-page activity plan that shows five stages, and for each stage gives the time, the pupil activity and what the activity achieves. Then write a short justification explaining how the activities move the pupils from controlled practice to free production.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1 mark) | Proficient (2 marks) | Exemplary (3-4 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stage design** | Lists activities without a clear sequence or time boxes. | Five stages are present with activities and approximate times. | Each stage has a clear purpose and the sequence moves logically from controlled to free work. |
| **Activity quality** | Activities are mostly copying or listening to the teacher. | At least two activities require pupils to produce or process language. | Every stage has a visible pupil outcome the teacher can check. |
| **Justification** *(Analyze)* | Says activities are "good" without explaining why. | Explains how the activities help pupils learn. | **Analyze:** Justifies each activity by linking it to the balance of teacher talk and pupil work, and to the evidence the teacher gains from it. |
