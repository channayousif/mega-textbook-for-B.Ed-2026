## A real classroom situation

Farah teaches Grade 7 in a school in Sukkur. Her class has fifty-two pupils and the desks are bolted in rows. She has been teaching the present perfect by writing rules on the board and asking pupils to make sentences one by one. Only a handful of pupils ever speak, and the rest spend the lesson waiting for their turn.

One day she tries something different. She puts the pupils in pairs and gives each pair a set of eight picture cards showing daily actions. The task is simple: "Ask your partner what they have done today. Find three things you have both done." The noise level rises, but so does the language. Every pupil is speaking at once, and the pupils who never raised their hands are asking and answering.

At the end of the lesson Farah is tired but pleased. The class did not finish the textbook page, yet every pupil had said the new structure several times. She realises that activity-based learning is not a reward for finishing the syllabus. It is how the syllabus gets learned.

## Explanation

Activity-based learning means organising the lesson so that pupils learn by doing. The teacher still teaches, but the teaching is broken into tasks that the pupils carry out. The approach rests on a simple idea: a pupil who uses language remembers it better than a pupil who only hears it.

### What activity-based learning is

In an activity-based lesson, the teacher plans tasks with a clear outcome, gives short instructions, and then lets the pupils work. The teacher moves around the room, listens and helps. The approach does not mean the teacher stops explaining. It means explanation is short and purposeful, and it is followed by a task that makes the pupils use what was explained. Paulsrud and Gheitasi (2024) found that pre-service teachers who plan with a language-as-resource stance choose activities that let pupils bring their own languages and experiences into the English lesson, rather than treating the pupils' home languages as a problem to remove.

### Techniques: pair work, group work, games and role play

- **Pair work.** Two pupils work together on a short task. It is the quickest way to double the amount of speaking in a lesson, and it suits almost any class size. The task must be short and clear, such as asking and answering five questions, or checking a partner's sentences.
- **Group work.** Four to six pupils work together on a longer task, such as making a poster, solving a problem or preparing a short role play. Group work builds teamwork and lets stronger pupils support weaker ones. The teacher must give each pupil a role, or the strongest pupil will do everything.
- **Games.** A game is a task with a rule and a winner or a goal. Games are useful for revising vocabulary and structures because they make repetition enjoyable. A simple card game or a guessing game can fill a five-minute practice slot without any special materials.
- **Role play.** Pupils act out a situation, such as buying fruit in a market or asking for directions. Role play is powerful for speaking because it gives pupils a reason to use the language. It needs a clear situation and a short time box, or it becomes noise.

### Managing group work: four teacher moves

Group work fails when the teacher gives the task and then sits at the desk. Four moves keep it on track:

1. **Set up.** State the task, the time and the outcome in one or two sentences. Give each pupil a role, such as reader, writer, timekeeper or reporter. Check that every group knows what to do before you let them start.
2. **Monitor.** Walk around and listen. Note which groups are stuck and which are finished early. Do not stand over one group for too long.
3. **Support.** Help a stuck group with a question rather than an answer: "What is the first thing your poster needs?" Keep the support brief so the group still does the work.
4. **Report back.** Bring the class together and ask one or two groups to share. This gives the work an audience and lets you correct errors for everyone.

### Worked example: a group-work lesson on "Our School"

Study this plan for Grade 6. Notice how the teacher moves are built into the group-work stage.

- **Outcome:** pupils will describe one place in their school to the class in three sentences.
- **Warm-up (4 min):** teacher asks "What places are in our school?"; pupils call out words.
- **Presentation (6 min):** teacher models a description of the library and writes three sentences on the board.
- **Practice (8 min):** pupils copy the model and substitute their own place in pairs.
- **Production (14 min):** groups of four design a poster of one school place and prepare a three-sentence description. The teacher sets up, monitors, supports and then calls the class back.
- **Report back (4 min):** two groups read their descriptions aloud.
- **Assessment (4 min):** each pupil writes three sentences about their own favourite place.

### Common misconceptions

One wrong idea is that activities waste time that should be used for the syllabus. In a large class, activity-based learning is often the only way to give every pupil practice, so it saves time in the long run. Another wrong idea is that activity-based learning means no teacher explanation. The teacher still explains, but briefly and before the task. Sheikh (1998) warns that an activity without a clear language aim is just noise, so every activity must have a purpose the teacher can name.

## Activity: Design a pair task

**Work in pairs.** Time allocation: approximately 20 minutes.

Choose one of these Grade 5 topics: the days of the week, the names of fruits, or the parts of the school. Design a ten-minute pair-work activity that practises the topic.

1. Write the outcome of the activity in one sentence.
2. Write the instructions you would give the class, in one or two sentences.
3. Say what materials you need, and what you will do while the pupils work.
4. Exchange your activity with another pair. Check whether the instructions are short enough to hold in memory, and whether the task has a clear outcome.
5. Revise your activity using the feedback, and read the final version aloud.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the four activity-based techniques described in this topic, and give one example of each.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why pair work is often the best technique in a class of fifty pupils.
3. *(Apply)* A teacher sets a group-work task but does not give the pupils roles, and two pupils do all the work while the others watch. Describe two changes to the set-up move that would make the group work fairer.

## Summary

Activity-based learning organises the lesson around tasks that pupils carry out. Pair work, group work, games and role play each suit different aims and class sizes. Group work needs four teacher moves: set up, monitor, support and report back. Every activity must have a clear language aim, or it is only noise.

## Try this at your practicum school

Observe one lesson and count how many pupils speak during the lesson. Then ask your cooperating teacher whether they would be willing to try a five-minute pair task in their next English lesson. Offer to help design the task, and note what happens to the number of pupils speaking.

## Summative task

Plan a fifteen-minute activity-based segment for a Grade 7 class on the topic "Asking for directions". Choose one technique (pair work, group work, a game or role play), and write the outcome, the instructions, the materials and the four teacher moves. Then explain why you chose that technique for a class of fifty pupils.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1 mark) | Proficient (2 marks) | Exemplary (3-4 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Task design** | The activity has no clear outcome or the instructions are unclear. | The activity has an outcome, short instructions and a clear time box. | The outcome, instructions and materials are all aligned and realistic for the class size. |
