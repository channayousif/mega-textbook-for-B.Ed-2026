## A real classroom situation

Imran is in his first month of teaching at a private school in Hyderabad. He writes a lesson plan every evening. It is a careful list: the topic of the lesson, the pages of the textbook, and a note to "explain the grammar". On Tuesday he walks into Grade 6 with a plan that says "Chapter 4, pages 22 to 24, explain the present perfect".

The lesson begins badly. Imran explains the present perfect for fifteen minutes, but the pupils are confused because he never showed them what the structure looks like in a sentence. He then asks them to do the textbook exercise, but half the class has no idea what to write. He finishes the chapter by the bell, feeling he has covered the syllabus. The next day, almost nobody can use the present perfect.

His senior colleague looks at the plan and says, "This is a list of what you will teach, not a plan for what the pupils will do. A lesson plan needs steps, and each step needs a purpose."

## Explanation

A lesson plan is a sequence of steps, and each step has a job. If one step is missing or in the wrong place, the whole lesson leans. The seven steps below are the ones used in this unit.

### The seven steps of a lesson plan

1. **Learning outcomes.** What the pupils will be able to do by the end of the lesson. An outcome is written from the pupil's point of view: "By the end of the lesson, pupils will be able to describe their morning routine in four sentences."
2. **Warm-up.** A short task that settles the class and brings English into the room. It can be a song, a quick question, or a one-minute pair chat.
3. **Presentation.** The teacher introduces the new language in a clear context. The pupils see or hear the language before they are asked to produce it.
4. **Practice.** Controlled tasks where the pupils use the new language with support. The teacher holds the language steady and corrects errors.
5. **Production.** Freer tasks where the pupils use the language for a real purpose, with less support. This is where the language becomes theirs.
6. **Assessment.** A check on what the pupils have learned, such as a short quiz, a pair performance, or a written sentence. The teacher uses it to decide what to reteach.
7. **Homework.** A small task that continues the learning outside the lesson, linked to what was taught.

### The purpose of each step

The steps are not a ritual. The warm-up protects the lesson from a noisy start and gives every pupil a first success. The presentation gives the pupils something to hold onto, so practice is not guesswork. Practice builds accuracy, and production builds fluency. Assessment tells the teacher whether the outcomes were reached, and homework extends the practice beyond the bell. If a step is skipped, the steps around it weaken. A lesson with no warm-up starts cold. A lesson with no production gives pupils no chance to own the language.

### A worked example: a complete plan skeleton

Here is a skeleton for a Grade 5 lesson on "My Daily Routine". Study how each step has a pupil activity and a purpose.

| Step | Time | Teacher and pupil action | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outcome | - | Pupils will describe their morning routine in four sentences | Gives the lesson a target |
| Warm-up | 4 min | Teacher asks "What did you do first this morning?"; pupils answer in pairs | Activates vocabulary and settles the class |
| Presentation | 8 min | Teacher shows a picture of a child getting ready for school and models four sentences; pupils repeat | Introduces the structure in context |
| Practice | 10 min | In pairs, pupils ask and answer "What do you do at ...?" using a clock card | Controls the new structure |
| Production | 10 min | Each pupil tells the group about their own morning routine | Uses the language freely |
| Assessment | 3 min | Pupils write four sentences about their routine; teacher collects two for marking | Checks the outcome |
| Homework | - | Write four sentences about your evening routine | Extends the practice |

### Common misconceptions

One wrong idea is that the plan must be followed exactly, minute by minute. A plan is a guide, not a contract. If the assessment shows that half the class is lost, the teacher should slow down and reteach, even if that means the homework changes. Another wrong idea is that one plan template fits every lesson type. The seven steps stay the same, but the activities inside them change completely. A poetry lesson and a grammar lesson both have a presentation and a practice stage, but what happens inside those stages is very different. The next topics in this unit show how.

## Activity: The missing step

**Work in groups of four.** Time allocation: approximately 25 minutes.

Read this plan for a Grade 4 reading lesson. One step is missing.

> Outcome: pupils will answer four questions about a short paragraph. Warm-up: teacher shows a picture of a market and asks what pupils can see. Presentation: teacher reads the paragraph aloud twice. Practice: pupils read the paragraph silently. Production: pupils write answers to four questions. Assessment: teacher marks the answers. Homework: read the paragraph again.

1. Decide which step is missing, and explain what the pupils lose by not having it.
2. Write the missing step into the plan with a time box and a pupil activity.
3. Compare your answer with another group. Do you agree on which step was missing? If not, defend your choice using the purpose of the steps.
4. As a group, write one sentence explaining why a plan with a missing step is harder to teach than a plan with an extra step.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* List the seven steps of a lesson plan in order, starting with the learning outcomes.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why the presentation step must come before the practice step, using the idea of support.
3. *(Apply)* A teacher's plan has a detailed presentation and a homework task but no assessment step. Describe two consequences for the teacher and the pupils, and write a replacement assessment activity.

## Summary

A lesson plan has seven steps: learning outcomes, warm-up, presentation, practice, production, assessment and homework. Each step has a purpose, and the steps work together. The plan is a guide that the teacher can adjust when the class needs it. The steps stay the same across lesson types, but the activities inside them change.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can list the seven steps of a lesson plan and state the purpose of each.
- [ ] I can explain why the presentation must come before the practice.
- [ ] I can spot a missing or weak step in a described plan and replace it with a purposeful activity.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher for a lesson plan they used recently. Copy the steps into your notebook, then write one sentence about the purpose of each step as the teacher described it. If any step is missing, ask the teacher how they handled that part of the lesson.

## Summative task

Write a complete lesson plan for a Grade 6 English lesson on a topic of your choice. The plan must show all seven steps with a time box and a pupil activity for each, and it must state the learning outcome in one sentence. Then write a short note explaining how the practice and production stages differ in the support they give pupils.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1 mark) | Proficient (2 marks) | Exemplary (3-4 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Completeness** | Fewer than seven steps, or steps with no activities. | All seven steps present with an activity and a time box. | Every step has a clear pupil activity and the times add up to the lesson length. |
| **Sequence and purpose** | Steps are out of order or the purpose is unclear. | Steps are in order and each has a stated purpose. | The purpose of each step is linked to the outcome and to the next step. |
| **Analysis of support** *(Analyze)* | Describes practice and production as the same kind of task. | Says production gives less support than practice. | **Analyze:** Explains how the amount of support changes across the lesson and why that matters for pupil learning. |
