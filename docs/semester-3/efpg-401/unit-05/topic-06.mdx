## A real classroom situation

Rashid teaches Grade 5 in a school in Dadu. For a writing lesson, he writes the topic "My Best Friend" on the board and says, "Write a paragraph. Twenty minutes." The pupils stare at their pages. One boy writes two sentences and stops. Another copies the topic and waits. After twenty minutes, Rashid collects eleven paragraphs, and most of them are three lines long with the same opening: "My best friend is very good."

Rashid asks his colleague what went wrong. She says, "They had a topic but no model, no plan and no support. Writing is not a test of what they already know. It is a skill you teach step by step."

## Explanation

Writing lessons fail when the teacher gives a topic and expects a finished piece. Pupils need a model to read, a plan to follow and support that is slowly taken away. The three writing lessons below share this shape, but the model and the output change.

### Planning a paragraph lesson

A paragraph lesson teaches pupils to write one organised paragraph. The teacher shows a model paragraph on the board, reads it aloud and talks about its parts: a topic sentence, two or three supporting sentences and a closing sentence. The pupils analyse the model together, then plan their own paragraph using a simple frame, and finally write it. The teacher's role is to keep the frame visible until the pupils can do without it.

### Planning a story lesson

A story lesson teaches pupils to write a short narrative with a beginning, a middle and an end. The teacher reads a short story aloud and helps the pupils map its parts on the board: the setting, the characters, the problem and the solution. The pupils then plan their own story using the same map, tell it to a partner, and write it. The partner's questions help the writer notice what is missing.

### Planning an essay lesson

An essay lesson for older primary pupils teaches them to write a short composition with an introduction, two or three body paragraphs and a conclusion. The teacher shows a model essay and highlights how each paragraph does one job. The pupils brainstorm ideas together, group them into paragraphs, and then write. The teacher's support moves from a full frame to a simple checklist as the pupils gain confidence.

### The guided writing sequence

All three lessons follow the same five-step sequence, often called guided writing:

1. **Model.** The teacher shows a good example of the kind of writing the pupils will produce. The model should be short and should be at the pupils' level, not far above it.
2. **Analyse.** The class looks at the model together and names its parts. "What does the first sentence do? How many supporting sentences are there?"
3. **Plan together.** The teacher and pupils plan a new piece on the board. The pupils suggest ideas and the teacher organises them, so everyone sees how a plan is made.
4. **Write.** The pupils write their own piece using the plan and the model. The teacher moves around and helps individuals.
5. **Share.** The pupils read their work to a partner or the class. Sharing gives the writing an audience and shows the class different ways to do the same task.

### Common misconceptions

One wrong idea is that writing is just grammar. Grammar matters in writing, but a writing lesson also teaches organisation, ideas and audience. A pupil who can fill a grammar worksheet may still not be able to write a paragraph. Another wrong idea is that the teacher should not show a model because it stops pupils from being original. A model does not copy; it shows what a good piece looks like, and the pupils still choose their own ideas. Celce-Murcia (1991) notes that process writing works best when the teacher supports the pupil through drafting and feedback rather than only marking the final product.

## Activity: Build a paragraph lesson

**Work in groups of four.** Time allocation: approximately 25 minutes.

Build a paragraph lesson for Grade 5 on the topic "My School".

1. Write a short model paragraph of four sentences, with a topic sentence, two supporting sentences and a closing sentence.
2. Write two analysis questions the class could answer about the model.
3. Design a planning frame the pupils could fill in before they write.
4. Write one instruction you would give for the writing stage.
5. Exchange your plan with another group. Check that the model is short and that the planning frame matches the model, and suggest one improvement.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the five steps of the guided writing sequence in order.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why a model paragraph helps pupils more than a list of instructions does.
3. *(Apply)* A teacher asks pupils to write an essay on "The Season I Like Best" with no model and no planning time. Describe two things the pupils will find hard, and explain how the guided writing sequence would fix each one.

## Summary

Paragraph, story and essay lessons all follow the guided writing sequence: model, analyse, plan together, write and share. The model shows what good writing looks like, the analysis makes its parts visible, the shared plan shows how to organise ideas, and the writing and sharing give the pupils practice and an audience. Writing is taught, not tested.

- [ ] I can design a planning frame for a paragraph, a story or an essay.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher whether you can teach a short guided writing lesson. Prepare a model text and a planning frame, and ask the teacher to watch the pupils during the writing stage. Afterwards, ask which pupils needed more support and what the teacher would change.

## Summative task

Plan a complete writing lesson for Grade 6 on the topic "A Day at the Market". Choose a paragraph, a story or an essay. The plan must show the five steps of guided writing, with a short model text, two analysis questions, a planning frame, the writing task and the sharing activity. Then write a short note explaining how the support changes across the five steps.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1 mark) | Proficient (2 marks) | Exemplary (3-4 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Five steps** | The sequence is incomplete or out of order. | All five steps are present and in order. | Each step has a clear purpose and the sequence is paced for the class. |
| **Model and frame** | The model is missing or the frame does not match it. | A short model and a planning frame are present. | The model is at the pupils' level and the frame supports the writing without doing it for them. |
| **Justification** *(Analyze)* | Says the steps are "helpful" without explaining how. | Describes how the model and plan help pupils. | **Analyze:** Analyses how the support is gradually removed across the five steps and why that helps pupils become independent writers. |
