## A real classroom situation

Naeem is a trainee teacher completing his practicum at a government primary school in Badin. During an English reading comprehension lesson in Grade 4, he asks his pupils to read a short passage about a helpful village doctor. In the second paragraph, the pupils encounter the word *unkind*.

Several children immediately stop reading, look distressed, and raise their hands. "Sir, we have not memorised the word *unkind* in our vocabulary list," one pupil announces. "Our textbook only had the word *kind*." Naeem realizes that the pupils perceive every unfamiliar English word as a completely new, isolated puzzle that must be looked up in a bilingual dictionary or translated by the teacher.

Naeem approaches the chalkboard and writes the word *kind* in large, clear letters. "Do you know this word?" he asks. The entire class choruses: "Yes, sir! *Kind* means meharban (مہربان)!" Naeem smiles, steps back, and uses blue chalk to write the prefix *un-* in front of the root word, separating it with a hyphen: *un-kind*. He explains: "The small word-part *un-* means 'not' or the opposite. If *kind* means meharban, what does *unkind* mean?" A wave of realization sweeps across the classroom. A girl in the front row shouts with delight: "Sir, na-meharban (نامهربان)! It means not kind!" Naeem writes three more words on the board: *unhappy, unfair, unwell*. Without any coaching, the pupils deduce the meanings instantly. By demonstrating how word building works, Naeem transforms passive memorizers into active language decoders.

## Explanation

Word building, scientifically known as morphology, is the study of how words are formed from smaller meaningful linguistic units called **morphemes**. In second-language acquisition, vocabulary size is one of the strongest predictors of reading comprehension and overall academic success. However, memorizing English vocabulary word-by-word through rote bilingual lists is an excruciatingly slow and inefficient strategy that quickly overwhelms a primary school pupil's working memory.

A prevalent misconception among elementary teachers is that "word building and morphology are advanced linguistic topics that belong in university linguistics departments, far too difficult for young children in primary school." Celce-Murcia (1991) refutes this assumption, emphasizing that morphological awareness is one of the most natural, empowering, and generative tools available to young language learners. A child does not need to memorize fifteen thousand English words individually. If a learner understands fifty common base root words, along with ten high-frequency prefixes and ten common suffixes, they possess the generative key to decode, understand, and produce hundreds of related English words independently.

### Word building

Word building in elementary English instruction is organized around three primary morphological mechanisms:

1. **Base Roots (Free Morphemes):** A root or base word is the core semantic building block that can stand alone as an independent word carrying dictionary meaning (e.g. *play, help, farm, light, kind*). All word families sprout outwards from a central root.

2. **Affixation (Bound Morphemes):** Affixes are meaningful linguistic units that cannot stand alone as independent words; they must attach to a root word. Affixation consists of two major types:
   - **Prefixes (Added to the front of a base word):** Prefixes primarily alter the *meaning* or semantic polarity of the word, but usually do not change its grammatical part of speech. Common primary prefixes include:
     - *un-* (not / opposite): *unhappy, unkind, untie, unlock*
     - *re-* (again / back): *rewrite, retell, reopen, replay*
     - *dis-* (not / reverse): *dislike, disagree, disappear, distrust*
     - *pre-* (before): *preheat, prepay, preview*
   - **Suffixes (Added to the end of a base word):** Suffixes frequently change the *grammatical category* (part of speech) of the word, such as transforming a verb into a noun, or a noun into an adjective:
     - *-er / -or* (Agentive noun: one who does): *teach -> teacher, play -> player, farm -> farmer*
     - *-ful* (Adjective: full of): *care -> careful, help -> helpful, joy -> joyful*
     - *-less* (Adjective: without): *care -> careless, help -> helpless, fear -> fearless*
     - *-ly* (Adverb: in a manner): *quick -> quickly, slow -> slowly, loud -> loudly*
     - *-ness* (Abstract noun: state of being): *kind -> kindness, dark -> darkness, happy -> happiness*

3. **Compounding:** Compounding is the process of joining two or more independent base words together to create a brand-new compound word with a specialized meaning. Compounding is exceptionally accessible and enjoyable for primary pupils because they can see two familiar words combine into a new concept:
   - *sun + flower = sunflower*
   - *black + board = blackboard*
   - *rain + bow = rainbow*
   - *class + room = classroom*
   - *foot + ball = football*

#### Worked Example: A Primary Lesson on Word Families and Suffixes

To see how a teacher introduces morphological word building to a Grade 4 class in interior Sindh, examine the following worked classroom routine:

- **Target Root:** *HELP*
- **Instructional Aids:** Chalkboard, colored chalk, four cardboard word-cards (*-er, -ful, -less, un-*).

- **Step 1: Identifying the Base Root (4 minutes):**
  - Teacher writes *HELP* in the center of the chalkboard inside a drawn green circle.
  - Teacher asks: "What does *help* mean?" Pupils respond: "Madad karna (مدد کرنا)!"
  - Teacher: "Yes! *Help* is our mother root. Let us watch how this root grows new branches."

- **Step 2: Attaching Agentive Suffix -er (6 minutes):**
  - Teacher draws a branch extending to the right and writes: *help + er = helper*.
  - Teacher explains: "When we add *-er* to an action word, it means the *person* who does that action."
  - Teacher gives examples: "A person who teaches is a *teacher*. A person who helps is a..." Class shouts: "A *helper*!"
  - Teacher reinforces spelling: points out that *helper* is a noun.

- **Step 3: Attaching Suffixes -ful and -less (8 minutes):**
  - Teacher draws two opposing branches above and below the root circle:
    - *help + ful = helpful* (full of help, willing to assist others).
    - *help + less = helpless* (without help, unable to defend oneself).
  - Teacher presents concrete classroom context: "When Aslam picks up his classmate's fallen pencils, Aslam is being *helpful*. When a tiny bird falls from a tree and cannot fly yet, the baby bird is *helpless*."
  - Pupils contrast the meaning of *-ful* (abundance) with *-less* (absence).

- **Step 4: Compounding and Prefix Extension (7 minutes):**
  - Teacher writes on board: *un- + helpful = unhelpful* (not willing to help).
  - Teacher shows compound connection: *help + desk = helpdesk*.
  - Class summarizes the word family tree: from the single word *help*, pupils have constructed five distinct, expressive English words (*helper, helpful, helpless, unhelpful, helpfully*).

#### Pedagogical Impact of Morphological Instruction

Teaching word building provides distinct advantages in second-language classrooms:
- **Exponential Vocabulary Growth:** Rather than learning words linearly one at a time, pupils acquire exponential vocabulary networks, understanding how words morph across parts of speech.
- **Independent Reading Decoding:** When encountering an unfamiliar long word in a reading comprehension passage, pupils can analyze and segment it into prefix + root + suffix, inferring its approximate meaning independently.
- **Improved Spelling Accuracy:** Understanding morphological roots prevents common spelling errors (such as knowing that *careful* has only one 'l' because *-ful* is spelled with a single 'l').

However, teachers must remain mindful of common morphological pitfalls:
- **Overgeneralization:** Young learners often overgeneralize affixation rules, producing non-words such as *"unpossible"* (instead of *impossible*), *"cooker"* (for a chef), or *"beautifullyness"*. Teachers must provide gentle, corrective feedback, noting that while affixes follow general rules, English vocabulary contains historical irregularities that require confirmation.

## Activity: The root-word expansion challenge

**Work in pairs.** Time allocation: approximately 20 minutes.

Review the following five common English root words suitable for primary school:
1. *Farm*
2. *Act*
3. *Friend*
4. *Paint*
5. *Clean*

For each root word:
- Add a suffix that turns the word into an **agentive noun** (the person who does the action).
- Add a suffix that turns the word into an **adjective**.
- Add a prefix (if applicable) that reverses or negates the meaning.
- Combine the root with another noun to form a common **compound word**.
- Create a visual "Word Sprout Diagram" on paper for one of the roots (similar to fig-U3-11), showing the root at the center with four branches radiating outwards.
- Exchange your diagram with another pair and verify that all generated words are standard English terms.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Define the difference between a free morpheme (base root) and a bound morpheme (affix).
2. *(Understand)* Explain why adding a prefix usually alters word meaning without changing the part of speech, whereas adding a suffix frequently changes the grammatical part of speech.
3. *(Apply)* A Grade 5 pupil in Nawabshah writes the following sentence in her essay: *"The street was very unlight and we were fearness."* Identify the morphological errors the pupil made, identify the correct target words, and explain how you would teach the correct affixes using a visual contrast table.

## Summary

Word building equips second-language learners with morphological awareness, enabling them to expand their English vocabulary exponentially through the systematic analysis of base roots, affixes, and compounds. Prefixes alter meaning and polarity, suffixes transform grammatical categories across parts of speech, and compounding fuses independent base words into new conceptual units. In Pakistani elementary education, explicit instruction in word building transforms pupils from passive memorizers of static word lists into active, autonomous language decoders who can deduce the meanings of unfamiliar words and construct expressive, grammatically appropriate English sentences.

## Try this at your practicum school

During your next practicum lesson, conduct a five-minute "Word Hunt" activity using your pupils' English textbook:
- Write two common prefixes (*un-, re-*) or suffixes (*-ful, -er*) on the chalkboard.
- Instruct pupils to look through the story they are reading and find three words that carry those affixes.
- Have pupils circle the root word and underline the affix.
- Invite three pupils to write their discovered words on the chalkboard and explain the root meaning.
Observe how this morphological awareness improves their confidence while reading aloud.
