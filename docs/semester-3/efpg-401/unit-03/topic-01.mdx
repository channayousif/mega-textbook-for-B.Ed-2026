## A real classroom situation

Farhan is a prospective teacher completing his third-semester practicum at a government boys high school in Nawabshah. He is assigned to observe Sir Jameel, a senior English teacher who has taught Grade 6 for over twenty-five years. When Sir Jameel enters the crowded classroom of fifty-five pupils, he instructs everyone to open their textbooks to Unit 4, titled "A Journey by Train."

Sir Jameel writes a grammatical heading on the chalkboard: "Past Continuous Tense: Subject + was/were + Verb(-ing)." He turns to the class and explains the grammatical formula entirely in Sindhi, pointing out that "was" is used for singular subjects and "were" for plural subjects. Next, he writes a column of five English words on the board alongside their Sindhi and Urdu translations: *platform* (پلیٹ فارم), *passengers* (مسافر), *whistle* (سیٹی), *carriage* (ڈبہ), and *departed* (روانہ ہوا). He instructs the boys to copy the list into their notebooks and memorise the spellings.

Sir Jameel then reads the opening English paragraph aloud line by line, pausing after every sentence to provide an exact word-for-word translation in Sindhi. Finally, he assigns five English sentences on the board for the pupils to translate into Sindhi in silence. During the thirty-five-minute lesson, no pupil speaks an English sentence aloud, and Sir Jameel does not ask any communicative questions. At the end of the period, Farhan asks Sir Jameel why the children did not practice speaking. Sir Jameel smiles and answers: "Our matriculation board examinations test translation, grammar formulas, and written comprehension. If they understand the rules and can translate accurately, they will pass the examination."

## Explanation

The Grammar-Translation Method (GTM), historically known in Europe as the Classical Method or Prussian Method, was originally developed to teach classical Greek and Latin. Its primary goal was not to prepare learners for oral communication, but rather to enable them to read classical literature, develop intellectual discipline, and master formal grammar rules. When English education was introduced across the subcontinent during the nineteenth century, GTM became the default methodology in government schools, and it continues to exert a powerful influence across Pakistan today.

A widespread misconception among many educators is that "older methods like Grammar-Translation are completely useless today and should be entirely discarded." Conversely, many traditional teachers believe that "Grammar-Translation is the only method that works in crowded Pakistani classrooms." Both extremes represent flawed thinking. As Kiran (2021) shows in an empirical investigation of Pakistani secondary schools, GTM provides structured clarity for reading comprehension and formal grammatical parsing, especially in large classes with limited instructional technology. However, when used exclusively, GTM fails to develop spoken fluency, listening comprehension, or spontaneous communicative ability. Understanding its mechanisms allows teachers to recognize both its utility for text analysis and its critical shortcomings for oral competence.

### Grammar-translation method

The Grammar-Translation Method is characterized by several fundamental principles and classroom procedures:

1. **Primacy of Written Language:** The primary focus is placed on the written skills of reading and writing. Oral skills (listening and speaking) receive little or no systematic instruction. Pronunciation is addressed only incidentally when pupils read text aloud.

2. **Deductive Grammar Instruction:** Grammar is taught deductively. The teacher explicitly presents a grammatical rule or formula first, explains its exceptions in the mother tongue, and then requires pupils to apply the rule to practice sentences. For example, a teacher explains the rule for forming the passive voice in Sindhi or Urdu before asking pupils to convert active sentences into passive constructions.

3. **Mother Tongue as Medium of Instruction:** The learner's native language (L1) serves as the medium of instruction. The teacher explains new ideas, clarifies vocabulary, and manages the classroom in Urdu, Sindhi, Pashto, or Punjabi. English is treated as an academic object of study rather than a living vehicle for communication.

4. **Bilingual Vocabulary Lists:** Words are taught through isolated bilingual word lists. Learners memorize English words alongside their direct L1 equivalents. Abstract words are defined through translation rather than contextual demonstration or visual realia.

5. **Sentence-Level Translation:** The core learning activity consists of translating isolated sentences from the target language into the mother tongue, and vice versa. Textual coherence and communicative context are secondary; grammatical accuracy at the sentence boundary is paramount.

#### Worked Example: A Traditional Grammar-Translation Lesson Sequence

To understand how a GTM lesson is constructed, examine the following worked sequence planned for a Grade 6 lesson on the simple past tense in an interior Sindh classroom:

- **Phase 1: Explicit Rule Statement (6 minutes):**
  - Teacher writes on the board: *"Rule: Simple Past Tense = Subject + Verb(2nd form) + Object."*
  - Teacher explains in Urdu: "We use the second form of the verb to describe an action that was completed in the past. Regular verbs add '-ed' to their base form."
  - Teacher writes rules for spelling changes: *walk -> walked, play -> played, carry -> carried*.

- **Phase 2: Bilingual Vocabulary List (5 minutes):**
  - The teacher writes vocabulary pairs on the chalkboard and pupils copy them:
    - *village* = گاؤں / ڳوٺ
    - *harvested* = فصل کاٹا / فصل لُڻيو
    - *canal* = نہر / واھ
    - *field* = کھیت / ٻني

- **Phase 3: Model Examples (8 minutes):**
  - Teacher demonstrates applying the formula to three sentences on the board:
    1. *The farmer walked to the field.* (کسان کھیت کی طرف چلا۔)
    2. *They harvested the wheat yesterday.* (انہوں نے کل گندم کاٹی۔)
    3. *Ali cleaned his bicycle.* (علی نے اپنی سائیکل صاف کی۔)
  - Teacher parses the grammatical elements: identifies Subject, Verb, and Object.

- **Phase 4: Bidirectional Translation Practice (12 minutes):**
  - Teacher writes five practice sentences for pupils to translate independently into their notebooks:
    1. *Translate into Urdu/Sindhi:* "The boys played cricket near the canal."
    2. *Translate into Urdu/Sindhi:* "Mother cooked rice in the kitchen."
    3. *Translate into English:* "احمد نے کل ایک خط لکھا۔" (Ahmad wrote a letter yesterday.)
    4. *Translate into English:* "ہم نے باغ میں پرندے دیکھے۔" (We saw birds in the garden.)
  - Teacher circulates, checks notebooks with red pen, and writes corrections on the board.

#### Instructional Strengths and Pedagogical Weaknesses

In the context of Pakistani schooling, GTM offers several distinct operational advantages:
- It requires minimal audio-visual technology or specialized materials, functioning effectively in schools without electricity or audio equipment.
- It can be managed easily with large classes of sixty or more pupils seated in traditional benches.
- It places fewer demands on the teacher's oral English fluency, allowing teachers with limited spoken confidence to conduct lessons based on textbook keys.
- It develops high competence in reading literary texts, formal grammar analysis, and translation accuracy required by traditional public examinations.

However, GTM carries severe pedagogical limitations:
- **Absence of Spoken English:** Learners spend years studying English without ever producing spontaneous spoken sentences or understanding natural conversational speech.
- **Cognitive Translation Habit:** Pupils develop an ingrained habit of mentally translating every phrase into Urdu or Sindhi before responding, creating severe hesitation and cognitive bottlenecking during real-world communication.
- **Decontextualised Language:** Words are learned in isolated pairs rather than natural collocations, leading to awkward, unnatural English phrasing (such as translating idioms literally).
- **Passive Learner Role:** The lesson is predominantly teacher-centered, leaving pupils in a passive role of copying and memorising formulas.

## Activity: Evaluating grammar-translation exercises

**Work in pairs.** Time allocation: approximately 20 minutes.

Review the following exercise extracted from a traditional Grade 5 supplementary grammar booklet:
*"Directions: Memorise the following three rules for comparative adjectives. Then translate the five sentences below into Urdu, and translate the three Urdu sentences into English."*

1. Identify which language skills (listening, speaking, reading, or writing) are actively developed by this exercise, and which are completely omitted.
2. Discuss why a pupil who scores 10/10 on this written exercise might struggle to say "Your bag is heavier than my bag" during an informal classroom conversation.
3. Rewrite one of the translation items as a contextualized communicative prompt that requires two pupils to compare two actual classroom objects (such as their school bags or water bottles) orally in English.
4. Share your rewritten prompt with another pair and discuss how it balances grammatical awareness with active oral production.

## Summary

The Grammar-Translation Method is a traditional teacher-centered approach that emphasizes explicit deductive grammar rules, bilingual vocabulary memorization, and bidirectional translation of written sentences. In Pakistani schools, GTM remains widespread due to large class sizes, limited physical resources, and exam systems that prioritize written translation. While GTM develops explicit grammatical knowledge and reading comprehension, its exclusive use leaves learners unable to speak fluently or comprehend spoken English. Prospective teachers must understand GTM's structural mechanics while recognizing the necessity of integrating interactive methods that develop oral communicative competence.
