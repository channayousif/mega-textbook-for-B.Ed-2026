## A real classroom situation

Mehran is a student teacher conducting his practicum in an urban elementary school in Hyderabad. Having observed that his Grade 5 pupils could recite grammar definitions from memory but fell silent whenever asked an authentic question, he decides to organize a communicative lesson under the guidance of his cooperating teacher, Sir Saeed.

Mehran prepares an activity called "The Community Helper Interview." Instead of lecturing on modal verbs and occupational vocabulary from the textbook, he divides the class of forty-eight pupils into twenty-four pairs. Each bench partner receives a different role card. Partner A plays the role of a local police officer, a hospital doctor, or a postman in Hyderabad, holding a small card detailing their work hours, uniform, and daily duties. Partner B plays the role of a young school newspaper reporter who holds an interview questionnaire with specific missing details: "What time do you start work?", "Where do you work?", and "Why do you like your job?"

Initially, the classroom is filled with noise and hesitation. Some pupils begin asking questions in Urdu. Mehran circulates between the narrow wooden benches, encouraging the children gently: "Remember, the reporter is interviewing for the English school wall magazine. Ask your partner in English: 'Where do you work?'" Within ten minutes, the room buzzes with purposeful English interaction. One boy on the back bench, who had never raised his hand during whole-class reading, excitedly asks his partner: "Do you catch thieves at night?" His partner grins and responds: "Yes, I patrol the market at night." Watching from the back of the room, Sir Saeed nods approvingly: "For the first time this term, your pupils are using English to discover real information rather than simply reciting sentences for a test."

## Explanation

The Communicative Approach, widely known as Communicative Language Teaching (CLT), emerged in Britain and North America during the 1970s and 1980s through the scholarship of linguists such as Dell Hymes, Michael Halliday, Henry Widdowson, and Keith Morrow. It developed as a profound critique of both the Grammar-Translation Method and the Audio-Lingual Approach. Linguist Dell Hymes coined the term **communicative competence** to challenge Noam Chomsky's narrow definition of linguistic competence. Hymes argued that knowing the abstract grammatical rules of a language (linguistic competence) is worthless unless a speaker also knows *when* to speak, *what* to say to whom, *how* to say it politely, and *where* it is socially appropriate (sociolinguistic and pragmatic competence).

A persistent misconception among many Pakistani teachers is that "the Communicative Approach completely ignores grammar, resulting in chaotic, inaccurate English where pupils speak gibberish without correction." Ahmad (2013) refutes this misconception in a detailed study of Pakistani English classrooms. CLT does not abandon grammar; rather, it fundamentally re-situates grammar within authentic communicative contexts. In traditional methods, grammar is taught as an isolated academic end in itself. In CLT, grammar is taught as an essential functional tool that enables speakers to achieve real-world purposes (such as requesting assistance, apologizing, negotiating purchases, or sharing information). When grammar is anchored in communicative necessity, learners acquire both structural accuracy and spontaneous communicative fluency.

### Communicative approach

The Communicative Approach is grounded in several core theoretical principles and classroom practices:

1. **Communicative Competence as the Goal:** The primary aim of instruction is to develop the four dimensions of communicative competence:
   - *Grammatical Competence:* Knowledge of vocabulary, morphology, syntax, and phonology.
   - *Sociolinguistic Competence:* Understanding social context, politeness norms, and appropriate register (e.g. speaking politely to an elder versus informally to a classmate).
   - *Discourse Competence:* Connecting sentences logically into cohesive paragraphs, conversations, or stories.
   - *Strategic Competence:* Using coping techniques (such as rephrasing, gestures, or asking for clarification) to overcome communication breakdowns.

2. **The Information-Gap Principle:** Authentic communication occurs only when one person possesses information that the other person does not know. In traditional classrooms, a teacher points to a chalkboard and asks: "Is this a chalkboard?" The pupil replies: "Yes, it is a chalkboard." This is not communication; it is a display of known information. In CLT, activities are deliberately engineered with an **information gap** (e.g. Partner A has a bus timetable that Partner B must consult to plan a journey).

3. **Authentic Communicative Purpose and Meaningful Tasks:** Language learning activities mirror real-world tasks. Pupils complete surveys, solve logic puzzles, role-play social situations, read real maps, and follow instructions to assemble objects. Language is a vehicle for accomplishing tasks, not merely an object of study.

4. **Fluency Before Accuracy:** In the initial stages of oral practice, teachers prioritize message transmission and fluency over mechanical perfection. Constant mid-sentence interruption to correct minor grammatical slips raises the learner's anxiety (affective filter) and destroys spontaneous speech. Teachers observe pupil errors quietly, take diagnostic notes, and address common error patterns during a structured debriefing session at the conclusion of the activity.

5. **Learner-Centered Collaborative Interaction:** The teacher shifts from an authoritative lecturer to an interactive facilitator, monitor, and resource guide. Student talk time (STT) is maximized through structured pair work and small-group problem solving, allowing dozens of pupils to practice speaking simultaneously.

#### Worked Example: A Complete Information-Gap Lesson Plan (Grade 5)

To see how an information-gap task is executed in a crowded Pakistani classroom with forty to fifty pupils, study this worked example:

- **Topic:** "Finding the Lost Cow in Hala Bazaar"
- **Target Language:** Prepositions of place (*opposite, next to, behind, between*); question forms (*Where is...?*).
- **Classroom Structure:** 20 pairs seated bench-to-bench.

- **Phase 1: Pre-Task Preparation and Language Framing (6 minutes):**
  - Teacher sketches a simple street grid of Hala town on the board showing four landmarks: *Mosque, Tea Stall, School, Bus Stand*.
  - Teacher pre-teaches question structures: "Excuse me, where is the [landmark]?" and response forms: "It is next to the [landmark]."
  - Teacher models the routine with one volunteer pupil in front of the class so everyone understands the procedure.

- **Phase 2: Distributing Complementary Task Sheets (4 minutes):**
  - Teacher distributes Worksheet A to all pupils on the left side of benches, and Worksheet B to pupils on the right side.
  - *Worksheet A:* Shows a map with the *Hospital, Post Office,* and *Bakery* marked, but the *Police Station, Fruit Market,* and *Lost Cow* are blank squares.
  - *Worksheet B:* Shows the *Police Station, Fruit Market,* and the *Lost Cow* marked, but the other three locations are blank.
  - *Ground Rule:* Pupils must hold their sheets upright like a shield; they are strictly forbidden from looking at their partner's paper.

- **Phase 3: Communicative Pair Negotiation (While-Task) (12 minutes):**
  - Partner A asks: "Where is the police station?"
  - Partner B consults Worksheet B and replies: "The police station is behind the mosque."
  - Partner A writes "Police Station" in the empty square on Worksheet A.
  - Partner B asks: "Where is the hospital?"
  - Partner A consults Worksheet A: "The hospital is next to the school."
  - Pupils continue asking and answering until both partners have completely filled in all six locations and successfully located the lost cow.
  - Teacher circulates quietly with a notebook, jotting down pronunciation slips and preposition errors without interrupting the flow of conversation.

- **Phase 4: Post-Task Verification and Form Focus (8 minutes):**
  - Pupils lay both worksheets side by side on the bench to verify that their map locations match perfectly.
  - Teacher asks three pairs to report their findings aloud: "Where did you find the lost cow?"
  - Teacher conducts a five-minute language focus on the board addressing the errors noted during circulation:
    - Writes common mistake on board: *"Hospital is next the school."*
    - Prompts class: "What small word is missing here?" Pupils correct: *"next to the school."*

#### Navigating the Practical Realities of Pakistani Schools

While CLT is recognized in the National Curriculum of Pakistan as the ideal pedagogical framework, teachers often face distinct systemic challenges:
- **Large Class Sizes (50 to 80 Pupils):** Managing noisy pair work in congested classrooms requires clear visual signals (such as hand raising or tapping a bell) to regain attention instantly.
- **Fixed Physical Furniture:** In many government schools, heavy wooden desks are bolted to the floor in long rows. Teachers must organize pair work between bench partners (seated side by side) or turning-around pairs (front bench turning to face the bench behind) rather than moving furniture.
- **Examination Alignment:** When provincial board examinations test rote translation and grammar rules, teachers must explicitly reassure pupils that communicative pair tasks actually strengthen the deep grammatical understanding needed to score high marks on written exams.

## Activity: Designing an authentic classroom survey

**Work in pairs.** Time allocation: approximately 20 minutes.

Design a communicative "Classroom Hobby Survey" for Grade 6 pupils in Hyderabad:
1. Construct a simple four-row, four-column interview grid with headers: *Name, Favourite Sport, Time Spent Weekly, Why You Like It*.
2. Formulate three specific English question prompts that pupils must use during their interviews (e.g. "What sport do you play?", "How many hours do you play?", "Why do you enjoy it?").
3. Establish a clear information gap: ensure that each pupil interviews four different classmates seated across the aisle to collect genuine, previously unknown personal data.
4. Design a five-minute reporting phase where pupils analyze their collected data and state one summary finding (e.g. "In our row, seven pupils play cricket and two play football").
5. Conduct a quick five-minute peer demonstration where one pair tests the interview prompts on another pair.

## Summary

Communicative Language Teaching (CLT) shifts the objective of second-language instruction from reciting abstract grammatical rules to developing functional communicative competence in authentic social contexts. Built upon the principles of real communicative purpose, information gaps, and interactive collaboration, CLT prioritizes fluency before accuracy during meaning-making tasks. While teachers in Pakistani public schools must navigate large class sizes, fixed furniture, and traditional examination expectations, carefully scaffolded pair tasks - such as information-gap maps, marketplace role plays, and classroom surveys - empower primary pupils to transform passive grammatical knowledge into active, confident spoken English.

- Prepare two simple complementary cards with missing numbers or animal names.
- Seat two pupils back-to-back or place a notebook between them as a visual barrier.
- Instruct them to ask English questions to discover the missing items on their card.
- Observe whether the visual barrier compels them to communicate through spoken English rather than simply pointing with fingers.
