## A real classroom situation

Bilquis is a prospective teacher placed at a government girls primary school in Larkana. She is preparing to teach a Grade 4 lesson on sentence construction. Looking through her pupils' exercise books from the previous term, Bilquis notices a recurring problem. When asked to write English sentences freely, the girls produce jumbled, ungrammatical fragments such as: "Book is read by she," "School going the children," and "Ali yesterday play ball."

Bilquis consults Madam Jamila, a master trainer at the local Government Elementary College of Education (GECE). "The girls know many English words," Bilquis explains, "but they arrange them in the word order of Sindhi or Urdu, placing verbs at the end of sentences and confusing subject-verb agreement."

Madam Jamila nods in agreement and opens a classic teacher-training manual. "You are witnessing mother tongue syntactic interference," Madam Jamila explains. "Urdu and Sindhi follow a Subject-Object-Verb (SOV) sentence order: 'احمد (S) کتاب (O) پڑھتا ہے (V).' English, however, follows a strict Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) pattern: 'Ahmad (S) reads (V) a book (O).' If you ask children to build sentences by translating word by word, their L1 syntax will inevitably distort their English. You need the **Structural Approach**. By introducing selected sentence patterns through visual substitution tables, you provide clear structural slots that guide pupils to generate dozens of correct sentences without memorising abstract grammatical terminology."

## Explanation

The Structural Approach developed in the mid-twentieth century through the work of language education specialists such as Harold Palmer, A. S. Hornby, and F. G. French. It emerged from the recognition that mastering a language does not consist of accumulating an encyclopedic vocabulary or memorizing abstract grammatical rules; rather, language competence depends upon mastering the core structural patterns (syntax and morphology) that form the underlying skeleton of the language.

A widespread misconception among many educators is that "the Structural Approach is simply rote memorization of rigid sentence formulas that prevents expressive writing." Mohammad (1998) demonstrates that in Pakistani second-language contexts, the Structural Approach provides an essential scaffolding architecture that empowers young learners to generate novel, creative sentences safely. A sentence pattern is not a static string of words; it is a flexible, generative framework containing functional slots into which thousands of different lexical items can be inserted. By internalizing foundational sentence patterns through systematic practice, learners acquire intuitive grammatical confidence that prevents mother tongue syntactic interference.

### Structural approach

The Structural Approach is built upon four foundational pillars:

1. **Selection of Structures:** Not all grammatical structures in English are equally useful for elementary learners. Curriculum designers select structures based on four criteria:
   - *Frequency:* How commonly the pattern occurs in everyday English.
   - *Simplicity:* How easily young children can grasp the structural form.
   - *Teachability:* How readily the structure can be demonstrated using classroom objects or visual aids.
   - *Productivity:* How many new sentences the pattern enables learners to generate.

2. **Gradation of Structures:** Selected structures are arranged in a carefully sequenced hierarchy from simple to complex. Simple patterns are mastered first, followed by structures that expand upon the foundational base. Gradation ensures that learners never encounter complex grammatical transformations (such as relative clauses or passive voice) until they have thoroughly automated basic Subject-Verb-Object syntax.

3. **Pattern Practice Over Formal Rules:** Structures are learned through active oral and written pattern manipulation rather than abstract rule recitation. Instead of defining what a "transitive verb" is, the teacher models the Subject-Verb-Object pattern and guides pupils to produce it repeatedly.

4. **The Substitution Table:** The primary pedagogical tool of the Structural Approach is the substitution table. A substitution table is a graphic grid divided into vertical columns, where each column represents an essential syntactic slot (Subject, Auxiliary, Verb, Object, Adverbial). Learners assemble sentences by selecting one item from each column from left to right, guaranteeing grammatical accuracy.

#### Worked Example: Constructing and Using a Substitution Table

To understand how the Structural Approach operates in a Grade 4 or 5 Pakistani classroom, examine this worked substitution table designed for teaching daily routines in the simple present tense:

```
+------------------+-------------------+------------------+------------------------------+
| Column 1         | Column 2          | Column 3         | Column 4                     |
| Subject          | Frequency Adverb  | Verb             | Object / Adverbial Phrase    |
+------------------+-------------------+------------------+------------------------------+
| The pupils       | always            | clean            | their classroom desks.       |
| We               | usually           | read             | interesting English stories. |
| The children     | often             | play             | cricket in the afternoon.    |
| Good students    | never             | arrive           | late for morning assembly.   |
+------------------+-------------------+------------------+------------------------------+
```

#### Step-by-Step Classroom Delivery:

- **Step 1: Structural Demonstration (5 minutes):**
  - Teacher draws the four-column table on the chalkboard.
  - Teacher explains the column functions using simple labels: "Who" (Column 1), "How often" (Column 2), "Action" (Column 3), and "What/Where" (Column 4).
  - Teacher models reading one complete sentence horizontally across the board: "The pupils always clean their classroom desks."

- **Step 2: Controlled Oral Assembly (8 minutes):**
  - Teacher points a wooden ruler to Column 1: "We."
  - Points to Column 2: "usually."
  - Points to Column 3: "read."
  - Points to Column 4: "interesting English stories."
  - The class reads aloud in unison: "We usually read interesting English stories."
  - Teacher calls on individual pupils to construct different combinations by pointing to new items.

- **Step 3: Calculating Generative Output (5 minutes):**
  - Teacher points out that each column has 4 items ($4 \times 4 \times 4 \times 4$).
  - From this single table on the chalkboard, pupils can generate dozens of grammatically valid, meaningful English sentences.
  - The table completely prevents common syntax errors: pupils cannot place the adverb in the wrong position or invert the verb-object sequence because the visual columns maintain strict syntactic alignment.

- **Step 4: Written Independent Production (12 minutes):**
  - Pupils choose and write five unique sentences from the table in their notebooks.
  - Pupils then write two personalized sentences about their own daily routines following the exact same structural template.

#### Instructional Strengths and Methodological Limits

In the context of Pakistani government schools, the Structural Approach provides immense practical value:
- It systematically overcomes mother tongue (SOV) syntactic interference by ingraining English (SVO) word order through visual alignment.
- It builds learner confidence by enabling every pupil to produce 100% grammatically accurate sentences from their very first lesson.
- It maximizes instructional efficiency: a single chalkboard substitution table provides extensive differentiated practice for large, mixed-ability classes.
- It provides curriculum planners and textbook authors with a clear, graded roadmap for organizing language syllabi from Grade 1 through Grade 8.

However, the Structural Approach also exhibits notable limitations:
- **Structural Over-Focus:** If lessons terminate at the substitution table, language learning remains confined to mechanical assembly, neglecting the communicative purpose of why people speak or write.
- **Artificial Contexts:** Sentences generated from substitution tables can sometimes be semantically bizarre or contextually irrelevant if the teacher does not curate vocabulary carefully.
- **Limited Spontaneous Interaction:** The method excels at controlled and guided sentence production, but it does not prepare learners for the unpredictable, messy exchanges of real-world dialogue.

## Activity: Designing a graded substitution table

**Work in pairs.** Time allocation: approximately 20 minutes.

Your task is to design a substitution table for a Grade 5 class in Shikarpur that teaches the pattern: *Subject + have/has + Past Participle (3rd form) + Object* (Present Perfect Tense).

1. Draw a four-column substitution table on paper.
2. Populate Column 1 with two singular subjects (e.g. *Sana, The farmer*) and two plural subjects (e.g. *The pupils, We*).
3. Populate Column 2 with the appropriate auxiliary verbs (*has / have*), ensuring that column alignment prevents agreement errors.
4. Populate Column 3 with three irregular past participles (e.g. *finished, seen, eaten*).
5. Populate Column 4 with contextualized object phrases appropriate for rural Sindh (e.g. *their homework, the morning train, sweet mangoes*).
6. Verify that any horizontal combination across your table yields a grammatically flawless, semantically meaningful English sentence.
7. Exchange your table with another pair and identify any combination that produces an awkward or nonsensical sentence.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State the four criteria used to select language structures in the Structural Approach.
2. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between sentence syntax in English (SVO) and Urdu/Sindhi (SOV), and describe how substitution tables help resolve this contrast.
3. *(Apply)* A Grade 4 teacher in Dadu notices that her pupils frequently write "She can sings well" and "They can to swim." Design a three-column substitution table for modal auxiliary *can* that systematically eliminates these two common structural errors.

## Summary

The Structural Approach centers second-language instruction on the mastery of fundamental sentence patterns and grammatical structures rather than isolated vocabulary memorization. Through rigorous principles of selection and gradation, structures are introduced sequentially from simple intransitive patterns to complex multi-clause sentences. The substitution table serves as the primary pedagogical instrument, providing visual syntactic slots that guide learners to generate numerous error-free sentences while overcoming mother tongue word-order interference. While highly effective for structural accuracy and foundational syntax, the approach must be supplemented with communicative tasks to ensure pupils can transfer sentence patterns into authentic social communication.
