## A real classroom situation

Asif is a trainee teacher observing an English lesson in Grade 5 at a government elementary school in Shikarpur. The teacher, Master Rafiq, is known for his energetic and structured teaching style. Today, Master Rafiq is introducing a dialogue about visiting a healthcare clinic.

Master Rafiq instructs the seventy boys in his class to place their textbooks inside their desks. He stands firmly at the front of the room, claps his hands twice for complete silence, and says in a loud, expressive voice: "Listen carefully. Good morning, Doctor. I have a headache." He models the line three times, pointing to his ear to signal listening. Then, he gestures with both open palms toward the entire class: "Repeat together!" The seventy boys respond in thunderous unison: "Good morning, Doctor. I have a headache!"

Master Rafiq repeats the line bench by bench, correcting pronunciation immediately whenever a pupil mispronounces *headache*. Next, he introduces a substitution drill. Master Rafiq calls out a single cue word: "A fever!" Instantly, the pupils chant back: "Good morning, Doctor. I have a fever!" Master Rafiq calls out: "A cough!" The class roars: "Good morning, Doctor. I have a cough!" For fifteen intense minutes, Master Rafiq runs through substitution and transformation drills with rapid-fire speed. Asif is amazed by the pupils' rhythmic accuracy and 100% participation. However, during the lunch break, when Asif meets two of the boys outside and asks them in English, "How are you feeling today?", both boys freeze, look bewildered, and cannot utter a word in response.

## Explanation

The Audio-Lingual Approach (also known as the Army Method or Aural-Oral Approach) originated in the United States during the Second World War. When military personnel needed to acquire practical conversational competence in foreign languages rapidly, linguists and psychologists combined structural linguistics with behaviorist psychology. Structural linguists viewed language as a system of distinct structural patterns and phonemes, while behaviorist psychologists, notably B. F. Skinner, defined learning as habit formation through stimulus, response, and positive reinforcement.

A pervasive misconception among language teachers is that "mastering dialogues and repeating drills automatically translates into spontaneous conversational fluency in daily life." As Cook (1991) points out in second-language learning theory, mechanical habit formation develops automatic oral articulation and structural familiarity, but it does not equip learners to generate creative, unscripted language in unpredictable communicative situations. When learners are trained exclusively through rote stimulus-response conditioning, they become skilled mimics who can chant scripted dialogues flawlessly, yet remain paralyzed when confronted with an authentic conversational exchange that deviates from the practiced formula. Understanding the Audio-Lingual Approach enables teachers to harness pattern drills for muscular and acoustic habituation while recognizing the imperative of bridging drills to meaningful, open-ended communication.

### Audio-lingual approach

The Audio-Lingual Approach is structured around specific theoretical foundations and instructional procedures:

1. **Habit Formation Through Conditioning:** Language learning is conceptualized as the mechanical formation of speech habits. By hearing a stimulus, producing an immediate oral response, and receiving instant positive reinforcement, learners automate grammatical structures until they produce them without conscious deliberation.

2. **Strict Primacy of Aural-Oral Skills:** Skills are taught in a rigid sequential hierarchy: Listening first, Speaking second, Reading third, and Writing last. Spoken language is considered the primary linguistic reality; written script is treated merely as a secondary graphic representation of speech sounds.

3. **Dialogue as Core Instructional Unit:** New grammatical structures and vocabulary are presented within contextualized, memorized dialogues. Pupils memorize dialogues through choral mimicry before analyzing any grammatical elements.

4. **Pattern Drills as Main Technique:** Structural mastery is achieved through intensive, rapid pattern drills (repetition, substitution, transformation, and expansion). Drills manipulate structural "slots" while keeping the grammatical frame stable.

5. **Immediate Error Prevention and Correction:** Errors are viewed as dangerous linguistic contaminations that, if left uncorrected, will harden into permanent bad habits (fossilization). Teachers correct pronunciation and grammatical slips instantly to maintain error-free speech production.

#### Worked Example: A Complete Audio-Lingual Dialogue Drill Sequence

To see how an audio-lingual routine is executed in a Grade 5 Pakistani classroom, study the following structured lesson plan:

- **Target Dialogue (Expressing Preferences):**
  - *Person A:* "Do you like fresh mangoes?"
  - *Person B:* "Yes, I like sweet mangoes very much."

- **Phase 1: Model Presentation (Listen) (4 minutes):**
  - Teacher reads both roles of the dialogue with natural intonation, expressive pacing, and clear facial gestures three times. Pupils listen with closed books.
  - Teacher uses flashcards of mangoes, apples, and bananas to anchor meaning.

- **Phase 2: Choral and Bench Mimicry (Repeat) (6 minutes):**
  - Teacher: "Do you like fresh mangoes?"
  - Whole class repeats: "Do you like fresh mangoes?"
  - Right side of classroom repeats as Person A; left side responds as Person B: "Yes, I like sweet mangoes very much."
  - Roles reverse. Individual benches repeat to ensure accurate sentence stress and intonation contours.

- **Phase 3: Substitution Drilling (Substitute) (8 minutes):**
  - Teacher maintains the frame: "Do you like fresh [Noun]?"
  - *Teacher cue:* "apples" -> *Pupils:* "Do you like fresh apples?"
  - *Teacher cue:* "oranges" -> *Pupils:* "Do you like fresh oranges?"
  - *Teacher cue:* "guavas" -> *Pupils:* "Do you like fresh guavas?"
  - Teacher introduces adjective substitution:
  - *Teacher cue:* "sweet" -> *Pupils:* "Yes, I like sweet apples very much."
  - *Teacher cue:* "ripe" -> *Pupils:* "Yes, I like ripe apples very much."

- **Phase 4: Transformation Drilling (Transform) (7 minutes):**
  - Teacher provides an affirmative statement and cues pupils to transform it into a question:
    - *Teacher:* "She likes sweet mangoes." [Question]
    - *Pupils:* "Does she like sweet mangoes?"
    - *Teacher:* "They like sweet apples." [Question]
    - *Pupils:* "Do they like sweet apples?"
    - *Teacher:* "Ali likes fresh milk." [Negative]
    - *Pupils:* "Ali does not like fresh milk."

- **Phase 5: Guided Pair Performance (Use) (10 minutes):**
  - Pupils turn to their bench partners. Partner A asks Partner B about their actual fruit preferences using the drilled frame.
  - Partner B replies truthfully with appropriate adjectives.
  - Teacher circulates, monitoring for correct auxiliary verb use (*do/does*) and natural pronunciation.

#### Pedagogical Strengths and Inherent Limitations

In Pakistani primary education, the Audio-Lingual Approach offers several valuable benefits:
- It creates an energetic, highly disciplined learning atmosphere where every pupil speaks English aloud multiple times during a single period.
- Choral repetition lowers individual anxiety in large classes, allowing shy or hesitant pupils to practice pronunciation safely within the collective voice of the group.
- It automates difficult syntactic transformations (such as auxiliary inversion in questions or negative insertions) through rhythmic motor practice.
- It requires no costly laboratory equipment, relying purely on the teacher's voice, rhythmic clapping, and chalkboard cues.

However, audio-lingual methodology has serious limitations:
- **Mechanical Parroting:** Pupils often recite memorized dialogues mechanically without processing the deep semantic meaning of what they are chanting.
- **Inability to Transfer:** Learners who excel during controlled drills frequently fail to apply the same structures when engaging in real-world communicative conversations.
- **Demotivating Monotony:** Endless repetition without creative personal expression can become mentally exhausting and boring for primary school children.
- **Suppression of Risk-Taking:** Instant, rigid correction of every minor error can inhibit learners, making them afraid to experiment with new English phrases.

## Activity: Constructing a four-part drill package

**Work in pairs.** Time allocation: approximately 20 minutes.

Choose the grammatical pattern: *Past simple of irregular verbs with time expressions* (e.g. "I went to school yesterday").

Construct an Audio-Lingual drill sequence for a Grade 5 class consisting of:
1. A **Repetition Drill**: A two-line opening model sentence.
2. A **Simple Substitution Drill**: Three noun or location cues that pupils insert into the frame.
3. A **Transformation Drill**: Three statements that pupils must convert from present tense into past tense (e.g. "I see a bird" -> "I saw a bird").
4. A **Chain Drill**: A question-and-answer prompt that pupils pass sequentially along a bench (e.g. "Where did you go on Sunday?").
5. Conduct a three-minute simulation with another pair where one partner acts as the teacher delivering cues and the other three act as primary school pupils chanting the responses.

## Summary

The Audio-Lingual Approach conceptualizes language learning as oral habit formation through stimulus, response, and reinforcement. Rooted in structural linguistics and behaviorist psychology, it prioritizes aural-oral mastery over written analysis, introducing new patterns through dialogue memorization and rapid structural drills (repetition, substitution, and transformation). In Pakistani primary classrooms, audio-lingual drills maximize oral student talk time and instill phonological confidence in large classes. However, because mechanical repetition does not automatically generate creative communicative fluency, teachers must use audio-lingual drills as a foundational stepping stone toward authentic, meaning-driven communication rather than an end in themselves.
