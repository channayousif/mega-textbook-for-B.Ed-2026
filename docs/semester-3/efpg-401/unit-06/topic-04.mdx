# Topic 6.4 - Television, video and film

## A real classroom situation

Bilal teaches Grade 5 English in a school in Dadu. The school has one small television set and a drawer of DVDs donated by a former teacher. One day he plays a ten-minute cartoon about a rainy day in the city. The pupils watch happily, laugh at the jokes and talk about the cartoon in Sindhi when it ends. But when Bilal asks, "What did the boy forget at home?", nobody can answer.

After the lesson he speaks with Madam Roshan, the senior teacher. "They enjoyed it," he says, "but they learned nothing." She asks, "Did you give them a reason to listen before you pressed play?" Bilal shakes his head. "Then the television was a treat, not a lesson," she says. "Next time, give them a question first, pause in the middle to check, and ask them to use what they saw in a speaking task afterwards."

The next week Bilal tries again. He plays only two minutes of the cartoon, stops it at a key moment and asks, "What will the boy do next?" The pupils discuss their predictions in pairs, then watch to find out. Now the video has become a lesson.

## Explanation

Television, video and film bring moving pictures and sound into the classroom. They are powerful because pupils can see the situation - a shop, a hospital, a bus station - while they hear the English words that belong to it. The guide names television, the video cassette recorder (VCR) and movies. Today the same material arrives on a DVD, a USB drive, a mobile phone or an educational channel.

A common misconception is that "showing a video is teaching". A video on its own is entertainment. It becomes teaching only when the teacher gives the pupils a reason to watch, checks that they understood, and asks them to use what they saw. Without those steps, the pupils remember the pictures but not the English. With them, the same two-minute clip can teach vocabulary, listening and speaking.

### Television, video and film in the English lesson

A video gives the pupils **context**. When they see a boy buying a ticket at a counter, the words *ticket*, *platform* and *return* stop being board notes and become part of a real event. A video also gives them **paralinguistic support**: facial expressions, gestures and the tone of voice tell them how the speakers feel, which helps them infer meaning even when they do not know every word.

A video lesson works best in five stages.

1. **Preview task.** Before playing, show a still picture or ask a question about the topic. Activate what the pupils already know and pre-teach one or two key words.
2. **Watch.** Play a short clip, ideally under three minutes. The shorter the clip, the more times you can play it and the more pupils will take from it.
3. **Check understanding.** Pause and ask a question that tests the main idea, then a question that tests a detail. Let pupils check their answers with a partner before you confirm them.
4. **Discuss.** Ask a question that has no single right answer: "What will the boy do next?" or "Why did the girl laugh?" This turns watching into thinking.
5. **Follow-up.** Give the pupils a speaking or writing task that uses the language from the clip: a role play, a short paragraph or a new dialogue.

Here is a worked example of choosing a video clip for a lesson. Suppose the outcome is: "Pupils can ask for and give directions in English."

- Choose a short clip showing someone asking for directions in a market or on a street. Avoid a clip with songs or fast editing, because the language gets lost.
- Preview with a picture of a busy street. Ask, "Have you ever asked someone for directions?" Pre-teach *straight*, *turn left* and *turn right*.
- Play the clip twice. First time: "Does the man turn left or right?" Second time: "Write down two phrases the man uses."
- Discuss: "What other ways could he ask?" Pupils suggest phrases in pairs.
- Follow-up: In pairs, one pupil asks for directions to the school and the other gives them, using the phrases from the clip.

## Activity: Design a two-minute video lesson

**Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.**

Your group has a short video clip (or a description of one): a boy goes to a shop to buy a notebook and a pen.

1. Write one **preview** question and name two key words to pre-teach.
2. Write one **main-idea** question and one **detail** question to ask while the pupils watch.
3. Write one **discussion** question that has no single right answer.
4. Write one **follow-up** pair activity in which the pupils use the language from the clip.
5. Say how long the clip should be and why a shorter clip often works better.
6. Present your plan to another group and ask them to check that each stage has a clear job.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* List the five stages of a video lesson in order.
2. *(Understand)* Explain two reasons why a video clip helps pupils understand spoken English better than an audio recording of the same scene.
3. *(Apply)* A teacher plays a five-minute video to her Grade 5 class and then asks, "What was the video about?" The pupils give only one-word answers. Describe two changes she should make to the next lesson.

## Summary

Television, video and film bring real situations and real voices into the classroom. A video teaches only when it is planned: a preview task gives pupils a reason to watch, a check confirms understanding, a discussion turns watching into thinking, and a follow-up lets pupils use the language themselves. A two-minute clip with these stages teaches more than a ten-minute film without them.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher whether the school has a television, a DVD player or a projector, and what videos or channels are available. If possible, watch one short English clip and note: its length, the situations it shows, the key words it teaches, and one task the teacher could set before playing it. Bring your note to the next class discussion.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1 mark) | Proficient (2 marks) | Exemplary (3-4 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
