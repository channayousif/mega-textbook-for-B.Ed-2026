# Topic 6.1 - Why AV aids matter

## A real classroom situation

Rukhsana teaches Grade 4 English in a government primary school in Umerkot, Tharparkar. The classroom has no electricity and no projector. One hot afternoon she introduces the words *banyan tree*, *canal* and *mud pot* by writing them on the board and asking the class to read them after her. The pupils repeat the sounds correctly, but when she asks, "What is a banyan tree?", nobody can answer. Most of them have seen a banyan tree on the way to school every day, yet the English words carry no picture in their minds.

During the staffroom break, Rukhsana tells her colleague, Sir Munir, that the pupils "cannot understand English". Sir Munir asks her what the pupils actually saw while she taught the words. "Only the board," she says. He nods. "The words are new, but the things are not. Give them something to see and the words will stick. A drawing on a chart, a leaf from the tree, a picture cut from a old magazine. The aid does the explaining before you do."

## Explanation

An <Glossary term="Audio-visual aid" /> is any picture, object, sound, video or projection a teacher uses to help pupils understand a lesson. The word "audio" covers everything pupils hear, and "visual" covers everything they see. A chart on the wall, a clay model, a recorded sentence on a phone, a short video clip and a slide on an overhead projector are all audio-visual aids.

A common misconception is that "audio-visual aids need money and electricity". Many teachers hear the phrase and picture a smartboard, a projector and a speaker system, then decide the idea is not for their school. This is false. The most useful aids in a village classroom cost almost nothing: a chart drawn on brown paper, a flash card cut from an old envelope, a real mango or a handful of pebbles. Electricity helps with some aids, but the main idea - giving pupils something to see and hear - needs neither money nor power.

### What audio-visual aids do for learning

Audio-visual aids help learning in four ways. An aid grabs **attention** because a picture or a real object is more interesting than a line of words on a board. It builds **meaning** because a new English word becomes tied to a thing the pupil can see, not to a translation they must memorise. It supports **memory** because the picture or the object gives the pupil something to recall when the word comes up again. And it invites **participation** because pupils want to point at a picture, hold an object and answer a question about what they can see.

Mahmood, Bukhari and Bahoo (2024) tested this with secondary school pupils in district Bahawalnagar. One group learned English with visual aids and a comparison group learned by the usual talk-and-chalk method. The visual-aid group scored significantly better after the treatment, which shows that an aid is not a decoration but a teaching tool that changes what pupils learn.

Here is a worked example of choosing an aid for one lesson. Suppose the lesson outcome is: "Pupils can name five things in a market." The teacher could simply list the words: *basket, scale, crate, sack, counter*. With an aid, she holds up a small tray of real objects or a picture of a market stall, points to each item and asks, "What is this?" The pupils see the thing, hear the English word and say it themselves. The aid carries the meaning, so the teacher spends less time translating and more time practising the English.

## Activity: Match the aid to the benefit

**Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.**

Your pair has six cards. Three cards name an aid (a wall chart of fruits, a recording of a market conversation, a real apple) and three cards name a benefit (attention, meaning, participation).

1. Shuffle the aid cards and place them face down. Do the same with the benefit cards.
2. Take turns to pick one aid card and one benefit card. Say why that aid gives that benefit, and add one sentence about how you would use it in a Grade 3 or 4 English lesson.
3. If your partner disagrees, explain your reasoning and try to agree on the best match.
4. When all six cards are matched, rank the three aids from "most useful in a school with no electricity" to "least useful", and write one sentence for each ranking.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* In one sentence, define an audio-visual aid and give one example that costs nothing.
2. *(Understand)* Explain two reasons why a real object helps a pupil remember a new English word better than a translation on the board.
3. *(Apply)* A teacher in a rural school wants her Grade 4 pupils to learn the words for six types of fruit. She has no electricity and no projector. Describe two aids she could use and say what each one helps the pupils do.

## Summary

Audio-visual aids are pictures, objects, sounds, videos and projections that make English visible and audible. They help because they hold attention, build meaning, support memory and invite participation. The most effective aid is not the most expensive one; it is the one that makes the new language concrete for the pupils in front of you.

Note what the aid is, how they use it, and which of the four benefits (attention, meaning, memory or participation) it most clearly supports. Bring your note to the next class discussion.
