# Topic 6.3 - Radio, audio players and the language laboratory

## A real classroom situation

Saima teaches Grade 4 English in a city school in Sukkur. Her own English is clear, but she worries that her pupils hear only her voice all day. "They will understand me," she tells her head teacher, "but they will struggle when a visitor or a radio announcement speaks to them."

The head teacher lends her a small battery radio and a USB speaker. Saima plays a two-minute recorded conversation between a shopkeeper and a customer. The first time, the pupils smile at the different voices but catch little. The second time, she gives them a simple task: "Listen and count how many things the customer buys." The pupils lean in. By the third listening, several of them can repeat whole sentences: "I want two kilograms of sugar, please."

Saima realises that the recording has done something her own voice could not. It has shown the pupils that English belongs to many speakers, not only to their teacher.

## Explanation

An audio aid is anything the pupils hear that is not the teacher's live voice: a radio broadcast, a recorded conversation, a song, a poem read aloud, or a pronunciation drill. The guide names three: radio, the cassette player, and the language laboratory. All three teach the same thing - they let pupils hear English from other voices, at natural speed, and then practise speaking it themselves.

A common misconception is that "the more aids in a lesson the better". Some teachers play a radio, then a recording, then a video, all in one period, and wonder why the pupils remember nothing. An aid works only when it has a clear job. One recording with a task before it, a check during it and a follow-up after it teaches far more than three aids used without a plan. Choose one aid, give it one job, and let it do that job well.

### Radio, audio players and the language laboratory

The **radio** is the oldest audio aid in the Pakistani classroom. It brings news, stories, songs and language programmes into the room, and it needs only batteries. A teacher can record a short radio programme and replay it in class. The radio's strength is that it carries real, natural English spoken at normal speed. Its limitation is that the teacher cannot pause or repeat a moment, so the recording must be short and the task simple.

The **cassette player** was the standard audio aid for decades. Today the same job is done by a mobile phone, a USB speaker, a computer or a Bluetooth speaker playing a downloaded audio file. The modern player is better than the cassette because it can repeat a single sentence as many times as the pupils need, and the teacher can stop and start it at will. The principle is unchanged: pupils hear a clear model of English that is not their teacher's voice.

A <Glossary term="Language laboratory" /> is a room with a console for the teacher and a row of booths for the pupils. Each booth has a headset with a microphone. The teacher can play one recording to the whole class, listen to one pupil at a time without the class hearing, and speak to a single pupil or to the whole room. A language laboratory turns listening practice into speaking practice, because every pupil can speak at the same time without disturbing the others.

Here is a worked example of using an audio recording in a three-stage listening lesson. Suppose the recording is a sixty-second description of a family visiting a zoo.

1. **Before listening.** Show a picture of a zoo gate and ask two questions: "Have you ever visited a zoo?" and "What animals would you like to see?" Pre-teach one word the pupils cannot guess: *ticket*. Tell them the task: "Listen and find out which two animals the family sees first."
2. **While listening.** Play the recording once for the main idea. Play it a second time for a detail task: "Tick the animals you hear on this list: lion, elephant, monkey, peacock." Walk around and watch who is ticking.
3. **After listening.** Ask the class to compare their lists with a partner. Confirm the answers together. Then ask each pair to make one sentence about the family's visit: "The family saw the elephants first."

## Activity: Plan a listening lesson with one recording

**Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.**

Your pair has a short recorded announcement: "Attention passengers. The bus to Hyderabad leaves from Platform 3 at 4:15. Please buy your ticket at the counter before boarding."

1. Write one question for the **before listening** stage that activates what the pupils already know about bus travel.
2. Write one **while listening** task that asks for a specific detail (the platform number or the time).
3. Write one **after listening** pair activity in which the pupils use the information in a short role play.
4. Decide which audio aid you would use to play the announcement (radio, mobile phone with a speaker, or a language laboratory), and write one sentence explaining your choice.
5. Exchange plans with another pair and check that each stage has a clear job.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the three audio aids in this topic and one strength of each.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why hearing English from a recorded voice helps pupils more than hearing only their teacher's voice.
3. *(Apply)* A teacher plays a three-minute recording to her Grade 4 class, but the pupils answer none of her follow-up questions. Give two changes she could make to the next lesson to help them listen more successfully.

## Summary

Radio, audio players and the language laboratory let pupils hear English from voices other than the teacher's. A recording can be repeated and paused, which makes it a patient model of clear speech. The key is not to use many aids but to give one aid one clear job: a task before listening, a check during listening, and a follow-up that turns listening into speaking.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher whether the school has a radio, a speaker, or any recorded English audio. If it does, ask to hear one recording and note how long it is, how fast the speaker talks, and what task the teacher gives the pupils before playing it. If there is no audio aid, suggest one short recording that could be played on a mobile phone in the next lesson.
