# Topic 6.2 - Charts, pictures, flash cards and real objects

## A real classroom situation

Imran teaches Grade 5 English in a crowded government school in Larkana. Forty-two pupils share one room, and there is no electricity. For the lesson on "A Visit to the Post Office", he brings a large sheet of brown paper on which he has drawn a post office counter, a postman and a stack of letters. He pins the chart to the wall with two blobs of clay.

The pupils lean forward. Within a minute they are pointing at the chart and calling out words they already know: *letter*, *stamp*, *bag*. Imran builds on their excitement. "What is the postman doing?" he asks. "He is putting letters in the bag," the class says. A girl in the back row, who rarely speaks, raises her hand to answer. The chart has given her a way into the lesson that the textbook page did not.

Later, Imran's colleague asks how a piece of brown paper kept forty-two pupils so quiet. "It gave them something to look at together," he says. "When they all see the same thing, they all have something to talk about."

## Explanation

Low-cost aids are the teaching materials a teacher can make from paper, cloth, clay, string, old magazines and things found in the school or the market. They matter most in crowded classrooms and in schools with no electricity, because they do not depend on a plug or a screen. The guide names five: charts, models, pictures, flash cards, and toys and real objects.

A common misconception is that "aids are only for young children". Teachers sometimes believe that charts and flash cards belong in Grade 1 and that older pupils should work from the textbook alone. This is false. A Grade 7 pupil who meets the word *evaporation* for the first time needs a diagram of the water cycle just as much as a Grade 2 pupil needs a picture of an apple. The aid matches the difficulty of the idea, not the age of the pupil.

### Charts, models, pictures, flash cards and real objects

A <Glossary term="Chart" /> is a large sheet of paper or cloth with pictures, words or a diagram on it, pinned to the wall for the whole class to see. A chart of a fruit market, for example, lets the teacher point to each fruit while the class practises "What is this?" and "It is a ...". Make a chart from brown paper or an old flour sack, use a thick marker, and keep the words short.

A model is a three-dimensional object the pupils can look at from all sides. A clay model of a post office, a bus or a human eye makes an abstract word concrete. In a lesson on "Parts of the Body", a simple clay head with labelled eyes, ears, nose and mouth helps pupils connect the English words to a real face.

A picture is any printed or drawn image, cut from an old magazine, a calendar or a textbook, or drawn by the teacher or the pupils. Pictures are the fastest aid to prepare. Keep a small box of pictures in the classroom and sort them by theme: food, animals, transport, family, school.

<Glossary term="Flash card" />s are small cards, about the size of a hand, with a word or a picture on each side. The teacher holds them up one at a time while the class reads the word or names the picture. Flash cards are ideal for a quick revision game at the start or the end of a lesson. Make them from old envelopes or the blank side of a used paper.

<Glossary term="Realia" /> means real objects: a mango, a key, a cup, a shoe, a handful of rice, a postcard. Real objects are the strongest aid of all because the pupil can hold them, smell them and describe them. In a lesson on "Things in the Kitchen", bringing a steel cup, a spoon and a pot to class teaches more than a hundred sentences on the board.

<Figure id="fig-U6-4" src="/img/figures/efpg-401/unit-06/fig-U6-4.svg" alt="Diagram of a five-step flash-card routine for new words: show, say, repeat, use in a sentence, and review the next day, arranged as a cycle." />

Here is a worked example of the flash-card routine for new words. Suppose the target words are *umbrella*, *basket* and *lantern*.

1. **Show.** Hold up the card with the picture of an umbrella. Do not say the word yet. Ask, "What is this?" Let pupils answer in Sindhi or Urdu first, then give the English word.
2. **Say.** Say the word clearly twice: "Umbrella. Umbrella." Point to the picture as you say it.
3. **Repeat.** Ask the whole class to say it twice, then ask three or four individual pupils to say it.
4. **Use in a sentence.** Hold up the card again and ask, "What do you do with an umbrella?" Pupils answer: "I open my umbrella in the rain."
5. **Review.** At the start of the next lesson, hold up the three cards again for thirty seconds and ask the class to name them. The quick review moves the words from short-term to long-term memory.

## Activity: Build a low-cost aid kit

**Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.**

Each group chooses one topic from this list: "Things in the Kitchen", "A Market Day", "Parts of the Body", or "My School".

1. As a group, decide on two aids you could make for the topic in a school with no electricity: one chart or picture, and one set of flash cards or one real object.
2. On a sheet of paper, sketch the chart or picture and write the five English words it will teach.
3. List the materials you need and where you would get them (brown paper, old magazine, clay, a real object from home).
4. Plan a three-minute mini-lesson: how you would show the aid, what question you would ask, and how the pupils would practise the words.
5. Present your plan to another group and ask them to suggest one improvement.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* List the five types of low-cost aid named in this topic and give one example of each.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why a real object is often a stronger aid than a picture of the same object.
3. *(Apply)* A Grade 5 teacher in a school with no electricity wants to teach the words for six vegetables. Describe one aid she could make and give a step-by-step plan for using it in the lesson.

## Summary

Charts, models, pictures, flash cards and real objects make English visible and touchable. They need little money and no electricity, so they suit crowded government classrooms. The right aid is the one that makes the new word or idea concrete for the pupils, whether they are six or fourteen years old.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can name five types of low-cost aid and give an example of each.
- [ ] I can make a chart or a set of flash cards from materials found in a village school.
- [ ] I can plan a short lesson sequence that uses a real object or a flash card to teach new words.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher whether the school has a box of charts, pictures or flash cards, and if so, where it is kept. If there is no kit, collect five real objects from the school or the market (a cup, a spoon, a leaf, a stone, a postcard) and use them to teach five English words in one lesson. Note how the pupils responded.

## Summative task

Choose one of these topics: "Things in the Classroom", "A Fruit Seller", or "Parts of a Plant". Write 250 to 350 words describing two low-cost aids you would make for the topic, how you would make each, and how you would use them in the lesson. Include one sentence explaining why these aids suit a school with no electricity.
