# Topic 6.5 - Projected aids

## A real classroom situation

Farzana teaches Grade 4 English in a school in Hyderabad. The school has a multimedia projector, and she is proud of it. For a lesson on "The Seasons of Pakistan", she prepares a slide with a long paragraph about each season, copied from the textbook. She dims the lights, projects the paragraph and reads it aloud while the pupils copy it into their notebooks.

Halfway through, the pupils' pens slow down. A boy in the front row whispers to his friend, "I cannot see the board and the picture at the same time." When Farzana asks a question about the seasons, the class answers slowly. The projector is on, but the slide is doing the textbook's job, not the teacher's.

Later she watches a colleague, Master Junaid, use the same projector. His slide has one picture of a mango tree in summer and four words: *hot, sunny, mangoes, harvest*. He asks, "What season is this? How do you know?" The pupils answer at once. Farzana understands: the slide had one idea, and the picture carried it.

## Explanation

Projected aids throw a large image onto a wall or a screen so that every pupil in the room can see it. The guide names four: slides, filmstrip, the overhead projector (OHP) and the multimedia projector. They differ in cost, in what they need to run, and in what they are best used for.

A common misconception is that "a slide with more words teaches more". A crowded slide forces pupils to read instead of listen, and the teacher ends up reading the slide aloud. A clear slide carries **one idea**, a few words and one picture, and leaves the explaining to the teacher. The rule of thumb is six words and one picture per slide: if you need more, use two slides.

### Slides, filmstrip, overhead projector and multimedia projector

**Slides** are small transparent photographs mounted in cardboard frames, shown one at a time through a slide projector. A teacher can make slides from printed pictures or buy a set on a theme such as "Our Country" or "The Solar System". Slides are strong because each one holds the class's attention on a single image. They are less common now because a mobile phone and a projector can show the same pictures.

A **filmstrip** is a roll of transparent film with a sequence of pictures and captions, projected one frame at a time. It is like a slide set fixed in order, so the teacher cannot easily skip ahead. Filmstrips were once used for stories and for science processes, but they have largely been replaced by digital images.

The <Glossary term="Overhead projector" /> (OHP) projects a transparent sheet, called an acetate, onto a screen. The teacher writes or draws on the acetate with a marker and places it on the glass. The OHP is popular in schools because it needs no computer, the teacher can write on it during the lesson, and it works with the classroom lights on. An OHP slide with six words and one picture is just as effective as a digital slide.

The **multimedia projector** (often called a "projector" or "beam projector") connects to a computer, a mobile phone or a USB drive and projects digital slides, pictures and video onto a screen. It is the most versatile aid in the room, but it depends on electricity, a screen and a device to play the slides.

Here is a worked example of choosing a projected aid for a lesson. Suppose the outcome is: "Pupils can describe the main features of a rainy day."

- If the school has only an OHP, the teacher draws a simple picture of rain, an umbrella and puddles on an acetate and writes four words: *rain, umbrella, puddles, wet*. The pupils describe what they see.
- If the school has a multimedia projector, the teacher shows one photograph of a rainy street and asks the same question. The picture is richer, but the job is the same.
- If the school has neither, the teacher uses a chart with the same drawing and the same four words. The aid changes; the teaching does not.

The choice follows the resources. The best projected aid is the one the school actually has and the teacher can use well.

## Activity: Design a clear slide

**Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.**

Your pair is planning a Grade 4 lesson on "A Bus Journey". You may use an overhead projector, a multimedia projector, or a chart.

1. Decide which projected aid you would choose and write one sentence explaining why.
2. Design your slide: write the title, the six words (or fewer) you will show, and describe the one picture you will use.
3. Write one question the teacher will ask before showing the slide, and one question to ask after.
4. Explain why your slide is better than a slide that carries a full paragraph from the textbook.
5. Exchange your design with another pair and check that it carries one idea, a few words and one picture.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the four projected aids in this topic and one strength of each.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why an overhead projector works well in a classroom that has no computer.
3. *(Apply)* A teacher has a multimedia projector but uses it to show a slide with a long paragraph copied from the textbook. Describe two changes she should make to turn the slide into an effective aid.

## Summary

Slides, filmstrip, the overhead projector and the multimedia projector make one idea visible to the whole class. They differ in cost and in what they need to run, but the teaching rule is the same: one idea, a few words and one picture. A clear slide supports the teacher; a crowded slide replaces her.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher whether the school has an overhead projector, a multimedia projector or a screen, and where it is kept. If the school has an OHP, ask to see the acetate markers and try writing a six-word slide on a sheet. Note how long the slide takes to prepare and how the pupils respond to a picture on the screen.

## Summative task

Write 250 to 350 words comparing the overhead projector and the multimedia projector for a Grade 5 English lesson on "A Visit to the Farm". Your answer should compare cost, power needs and best use, and end with a recommendation for a school that has electricity but no computer.
