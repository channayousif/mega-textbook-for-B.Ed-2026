# Topic 7.2 - Constructing subjective-type tests

## A real classroom situation

Shahzad teaches Grade 8 English at a government boys' high school in Larkana. His end-of-term English test has one essay question: "Write an essay on 'My Favourite Festival'." He marks the papers and finds that the same essay gets 12 out of 20 from him in the morning and 16 out of 20 when he re-reads it after lunch. He is worried that his marking is unfair to the pupils.

He discusses it with the head of department, Madam Naseem. "Your marking is not unfair," she says, "it is just inconsistent. You are marking from a general impression. If you write a rubric that says what you are looking for, you will mark every essay by the same standard." She helps him build a simple analytic rubric with four criteria: content, organisation, vocabulary, and grammar and spelling. Shahzad re-marks the same ten essays. The difference between his two marks for the same essay falls from four marks to one.

## Explanation

A <Glossary term="Subjective-type test" /> is a test in which the pupil writes a longer answer and the marker judges its quality using a rubric or mark scheme. Subjective tests are good for measuring writing, speaking, comprehension and thinking, which objective items cannot easily capture. They are harder and slower to mark, and their reliability depends on how clear the rubric is.

A common misconception is that "essay marking cannot be fair". This is false. A well-written analytic rubric makes marking fairer, not by removing the teacher's judgement but by making the judgement explicit and consistent. A second misconception is that "a test is good if many pupils fail it". A good test matches the lesson outcomes and helps the teacher see what pupils have and have not learned; a test that most pupils fail usually means the test was too hard or the teaching was weak.

### Construction of subjective-type tests

To construct a subjective-type test, start from the same lesson outcomes as for objective items, but ask the pupil to produce language rather than choose an answer. For an outcome such as "pupils can write a short paragraph about their village", the test task is: "Write a paragraph of six to eight sentences about your village." The marker then judges the paragraph against a rubric.

A subjective test item has three parts: the **task**, the **model answer**, and the **marking rubric**. The task tells the pupil what to do and how long the answer should be. The model answer shows what a good answer looks like. The rubric says which features are marked and how many marks each feature carries.

Subjective and objective tests are not rivals; they measure different things. The table above compares them on what they measure, how they are marked and how reliable they are. An objective test gives a quick, consistent mark for a fact or a rule. A subjective test gives a richer picture of how well a pupil can produce language, but it needs a clear rubric to be marked fairly.

An analytic rubric breaks the answer into criteria and gives each criterion a mark range. For an essay on "My Favourite Festival", the criteria might be:

- **Content:** Does the essay give clear ideas about the festival? (0 to 5 marks)
- **Organisation:** Does it have a beginning, middle and end? (0 to 4 marks)
- **Vocabulary:** Does the writer use festival-related words correctly? (0 to 3 marks)
- **Grammar and spelling:** Are sentences complete and mostly right? (0 to 3 marks)

The total is 15 marks. Each criterion has a short description of what earns full marks and what earns low marks. A rubric like this lets two teachers mark the same essay and get almost the same result.

<Figure id="fig-U7-4" src="/img/figures/efpg-401/unit-07/fig-U7-4.svg" alt="Diagram of an essay question on 'My Favourite Festival' with an analytic marking rubric showing four criteria and their mark ranges." />

Here is a worked example of marking an essay with the rubric above.

- **Pupil A:** writes three paragraphs about Eid with clear details (content 4), a clear beginning and end (organisation 3), right words such as *Eid-ul-Fitr*, *prayer*, *sweets* (vocabulary 3) and two grammar errors (grammar 2). Total: 12 out of 15.
- **Pupil B:** writes one paragraph with general statements (content 2), no clear structure (organisation 1), a few festival words but not used well (vocabulary 1) and several grammar errors (grammar 1). Total: 5 out of 15.

A mark scheme for the same essay question gives the marker a short model answer and the points to look for. For example: "Model answer: a paragraph or two describing a festival, with details of preparation, food, dress and activities. Give 5 marks for three or more relevant details; 3 marks for two details; 1 mark for a general statement with no detail."

## Activity: Build a rubric

**Work in pairs. About 25 minutes.**

Take the following subjective task for Grade 6 English: "Write a short paragraph (6 to 8 sentences) about 'A Visit to a Book Fair'."

1. Write a model answer of four to five sentences.
2. Build an analytic rubric with three or four criteria and a mark range for each. The total should be 12 marks.
3. Mark the following two answers using your rubric:
   - Answer A: "I went to a book fair with my friend. There were many stalls. I bought two books. We ate snacks and came home."
   - Answer B: "The book fair was held in the school ground last Friday. My class teacher took our class. I saw story books, science books and drawing books. I bought a story book about a clever crow. It cost 80 rupees. I read it at home and told my family the story."
4. Compare your marks with another pair and explain any difference.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the three parts of a subjective test item.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why an analytic rubric makes essay marking more reliable than a general impression.
3. *(Apply)* A teacher asks pupils to write a paragraph about their village but gives no rubric. Describe two problems this creates and state how a rubric would solve them.

## Summary

A subjective-type test asks the pupil to produce a longer answer, which the marker judges against a rubric. A well-written subjective item has a task, a model answer and an analytic rubric. The rubric breaks the answer into criteria and gives each a mark range, so two markers reach similar results. Subjective marking can be fair when the rubric is clear and applied consistently.

## Try this at your practicum school

During your next practicum visit, ask your cooperating teacher for an essay question and the marking they used. Note whether they had a rubric or a mark scheme and how they kept their marking consistent. Bring your notes to the next class discussion.

## Summative task

Construct a subjective-type test for Grade 6 English with one paragraph-writing task and one short essay task. For each task, write a model answer and an analytic rubric with at least four criteria and mark ranges. Add a short note explaining how your rubric keeps marking fair.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1 mark) | Proficient (2 marks) | Exemplary (3-4 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Task quality** | Tasks are vague or do not state a length. | Tasks are clear and state a length. | Tasks are clear, realistic for the grade and state a length and time. |
| **Rubric construction** | The rubric has no criteria or the marks do not add up. | The rubric has clear criteria and marks that add up. | The rubric has clear criteria with mark ranges and descriptions for each level. |
| **Fairness analysis** *(Analyze)* | Describes the rubric without explaining why it is fair. | Explains how the rubric helps marking be consistent. | **Analyze:** Explains how the rubric makes the marker's judgement explicit and consistent, and justifies the choice of criteria against the task. |
