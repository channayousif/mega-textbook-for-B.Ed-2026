# Topic 7.1 - Constructing objective-type tests

## A real classroom situation

Imran is a prospective teacher on practicum at a government primary school in Jacobabad. For his Grade 5 English test, he writes ten multiple-choice questions. One item reads: "The past tense of 'go' is: A) go B) goes C) went D) going." Almost every pupil ticks C, but Imran notices that many pupils who tick C cannot use "went" in a sentence. When he asks a pupil why he chose C, the pupil says, "It is the only one that looks like a past tense." Imran realises the item tested recognition of a form, not the ability to use it.

At the break, his cooperating teacher, Madam Riffat, looks at his paper. "Your item has one fixed answer," she says, "which is good. But the question asks pupils to recognise the past tense, not to use it. If your outcome was 'pupils can use the past tense in speaking', your test is not measuring that outcome." Imran nods. "Objective items are fair to mark," Madam Riffat adds, "but they are only fair for the outcome they actually test. Write the item from the outcome, not from the answer you want."

## Explanation

An <Glossary term="Objective-type test" /> is a test in which each item has one fixed answer that a marker can check without using personal judgement. Objective items are quick to mark, easy to mark the same way for every pupil, and useful for checking facts, vocabulary, grammar rules and simple understanding. They are less good at checking long written answers, speaking or creative work.

A common misconception is that "objective tests only measure memory". This is false. A well-written objective item can measure understanding and application. For example, a multiple-choice item that gives a short situation and asks which sentence is correct measures grammar in use, not just memory. The item writer controls the level of thinking by choosing the situation and the distractors.

### Construction of objective-type tests

To construct an objective-type test, start from the lesson outcomes, not from the textbook page. Ask what the pupil should be able to do, then write an item that asks them to do it. If the outcome is "pupils can use the past tense of regular verbs", a fill-in-the-blank item such as "Yesterday, Bilal _____ (walk) to school" measures it directly. If the outcome is "pupils can understand a short dialogue", a multiple-choice item that asks what the speaker means measures it.

Four objective item types appear most often in primary English tests. **Multiple choice** presents a stem and four options, one of which is the key. **True/false** presents a statement the pupil marks as true or false. **Matching** presents two columns the pupil joins. **Fill in the blank** presents a sentence with a gap the pupil completes.

<Figure id="fig-U7-2" src="/img/figures/efpg-401/unit-07/fig-U7-2.svg" alt="Flowchart for writing a multiple-choice item with five steps: choose the outcome, write the stem, choose the key, write three plausible distractors, and check the item." />

Each item type has a rule for writing it well.

- **Multiple choice:** the stem carries the question and can be answered before reading the options; there is exactly one fixed key; the three distractors are plausible but clearly wrong; the options are parallel in grammar and length.
- **True/false:** the statement is either clearly true or clearly false; avoid words such as "always", "never" and "only"; write a balance of true and false statements.
- **Matching:** each column has the same number of items; give clear instructions on how to match; avoid an item that matches more than one answer.
- **Fill in the blank:** there is one fixed answer for each gap; the gap falls where the missing word is clear from the sentence; do not leave the first word of the sentence blank.

DiBattista and Kurzawa (2011) studied multiple-choice items written for classroom tests and found that many items and distractors were flawed. In their analysis of 1198 items, more than 30 per cent of items had an unsatisfactory discrimination coefficient, and 45 per cent of distractors were flawed because too few examinees chose them or because they were positively rather than negatively correlated with test scores. Indrayani and colleagues (2020) reached a similar finding for teacher-made English tests: the quality of the items, not the number of items, decides whether the test measures what it should.

Here is a worked example of writing a multiple-choice item from an outcome.

- **Outcome:** Pupils can choose the right past tense form in a short sentence.
- **Stem:** "Yesterday, Saima _____ to the market with her mother."
- **Key:** C) went
- **Distractors:** A) go B) goes D) going
- **Check:** The stem asks for a past action; "went" is the only right past form; the distractors are other forms of the same verb, so they are plausible; the options are parallel.

## Activity: Spot the faulty item

**Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.**

Read the following objective items written by a Grade 5 teacher. For each item, decide whether it is a good item or a faulty item. If it is faulty, name the fault and rewrite the item.

1. "Choose the right answer: The capital of Pakistan is: A) Karachi B) Lahore C) Islamabad D) Peshawar."
2. "True or false: The sun rises in the west."
3. "Fill in the blank: _____ is the first month of the year."
4. "Choose the right answer: Which of the following is true? A) A noun is a naming word. B) A verb is an action word. C) An adjective describes a noun. D) All of the above."
5. "Match the words with their opposites: hot - cold, big - small, happy - sad."

Exchange your answers with another pair and discuss any item where you disagree.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the four objective item types and give one example of each from an English lesson.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why a multiple-choice item with the stem "Which of the following is true?" and four unrelated facts is a poor item.
3. *(Apply)* A teacher wants to test whether Grade 5 pupils can use the past tense of irregular verbs. Write one fill-in-the-blank item and one multiple-choice item for this outcome.

## Summary

An objective-type test has one fixed answer for each item, which makes it quick and fair to mark. The four common item types are multiple choice, true/false, matching and fill in the blank. A good objective item is written from the lesson outcome, has a clear stem or statement, exactly one fixed key and plausible distractors. Objective items can measure understanding and application, not only memory.

## Summative task

Construct a short objective-type test for Grade 5 English with ten items: five multiple-choice, two true/false, two matching and one fill-in-the-blank. The items must match at least three lesson outcomes you have written yourself. Add a short note explaining how each item tests its outcome.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1 mark) | Proficient (2 marks) | Exemplary (3-4 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Outcome match** | Items test facts rather than the stated outcomes. | Most items match the stated outcomes. | Every item clearly matches an outcome and tests the level it names. |
| **Item quality** | Stems are unclear or items have more than one fixed answer. | Stems are clear and items have one fixed answer. | Stems, keys and distractors are well written and plausible. |
| **Item type control** *(Analyze)* | Uses one or two item types without control. | Uses all four item types with reasonable control. | **Analyze:** Justifies the choice of each item type against the outcome it tests, explaining why another type would measure less. |
