## A real classroom situation

Jameel is a prospective teacher on practicum at a government primary school in Peshawar. He tells his Grade 6 pupils: "Your English pronunciation is wrong. You must speak like a British person." The pupils try hard but feel embarrassed. One pupil says, "Sir, we cannot sound like that. Our mouths do not make those sounds." The next day, the pupils are silent during the speaking activity.

His cooperating teacher, Master Irfan, talks with him after the lesson. "You are aiming at the wrong target," he says. "No one expects a Grade 6 pupil in Peshawar to sound British. The target is that other people can understand them. Teach the sounds that cause real confusion, not an accent."

## Explanation

Pronunciation teaching helps learners produce sounds, stress and intonation that other people can understand. It is not about copying a British or American accent. A Pakistani pupil who speaks English clearly enough to be understood has good pronunciation, even if they keep their own accent.

A common misconception is that "pronunciation must match a British or American accent." This idea makes pupils feel that their own speech is wrong, and it sets a target they will never reach. The real target is intelligibility: a listener can understand the message without strain. Intelligibility depends on a small number of sounds that change meaning, on word stress and on sentence intonation.

### Teaching of pronunciation

Three areas need attention in Pakistani classrooms: individual sounds, word stress and sentence intonation.

**Individual sounds.** Some English sounds do not exist in Urdu, Sindhi or Pashto, or are easily confused. The table above lists six common difficulties. For each, a minimal pair gives pupils a clear contrast to hear and produce. A minimal pair is two words that differ in one sound, such as *vest* and *west*. Drilling a minimal pair helps pupils notice the difference and then produce it.

To teach a minimal pair, use three steps. First, the teacher says the two words and pupils point to the one they hear. Second, the teacher says one word and pupils say which one it is. Third, pupils say the words in pairs. Keep the drill short and lively, about five minutes.

**Word stress.** English words of more than one syllable have one stressed syllable that sounds louder and longer, such as the first syllable of *teacher* and the second syllable of *about*. If the stress is wrong, listeners may not recognise the word. Teach stress by clapping the strong syllable and tapping the weak ones.

**Sentence intonation.** Intonation is the rise and fall of the voice across a sentence. It carries meaning: a rising voice at the end of a yes/no question ("Are you coming?") and a falling voice at the end of a statement ("I am coming."). Teach intonation by modelling two sentences and asking pupils to hear the difference, then having them practise.

**Worked example: a Grade 6 pronunciation lesson for /v/ and /w/ (15 minutes).**

- Model (3 minutes): The teacher says *vest* and *west* while pointing to a picture of a vest and a compass pointing west.
- Discrimination (4 minutes): The teacher says one word from each pair. Pupils point left for *vest* and right for *west*.
- Production (4 minutes): Pupils repeat the pairs in chorus, then individually. The teacher corrects gently.
- Use (4 minutes): In pairs, pupils ask and answer: "Is this a vest?" and "Which way is west?"

## Activity: Build a minimal-pair drill

**Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.**

Choose one sound pair from the table above, such as /f/ and /p/ or /iː/ and /ɪ/. Write four minimal pairs and plan a five-minute drill using the three steps: listen and point, listen and identify, and produce in pairs. Then present your drill to another pair, who play the pupils. Ask them to say whether the drill helped them hear the difference.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name three sounds that Urdu, Sindhi or Pashto speakers often find hard, and give one minimal pair for each.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why intelligibility is a better goal for pronunciation teaching than a British or American accent.
3. *(Apply)* A pupil says "I am going to the vest" when they mean "west". Design a three-step drill to help this pupil hear and produce the difference.

## Summary

Pronunciation teaching aims at intelligibility, not a native accent. The three areas are individual sounds, word stress and sentence intonation. Minimal pairs help pupils hear and produce sounds that do not exist in their mother tongue. Stress and intonation carry meaning and should be taught with modelling and practice.
