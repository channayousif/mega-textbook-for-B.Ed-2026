## A real classroom situation

Hina is a prospective teacher on practicum at a government girls' school in Hyderabad. For her Grade 6 English lesson, she writes a short poem on the board and asks the pupils to copy it five times and learn it by heart. The pupils memorise the poem perfectly and recite it in chorus. When Hina asks, "What does the poem mean?" the class falls silent. One pupil says, "We memorised the words, madam, but we do not know what they mean."

Her cooperating teacher, Madam Shaheen, speaks to her after the lesson. "Memorisation has a place," she says, "but a poem is not a list to be learned. A poem is meant to be enjoyed and responded to. If the pupils do not know what it means, you have taught the words, not the poem."

## Explanation

A poetry lesson teaches a poem: a short, patterned text that uses rhythm, rhyme and imagery to express feeling and meaning. A poem is not a prose passage with line breaks. It is a different kind of text, and it needs a different kind of lesson.

A common misconception is that "poems are taught only to be memorised." In many Pakistani classrooms, a poem lesson means copying the poem, learning it by heart and reciting it. Memorisation can be a valuable result, but it is not the main aim. The main aim is that pupils enjoy the poem, understand what it says, and respond to it in their own words.

### Teaching of poems

Antika (2017) argues that poetry is a useful tool for language learning because it fosters learners' motivation to read and write and develops their literacy competence. The author describes approaches to teaching poetry in the EFL classroom and proposes ways of assessing literary competence in poetry. The key idea is that a poem should be experienced before it is analysed.

A poetry lesson for Grades 4 to 8 can move through three stages.

1. **Listening and enjoyment (6 to 8 minutes):** The teacher reads the poem aloud twice while pupils listen with their books closed. The first reading gives the overall feeling; the second lets pupils follow the words. Ask one simple question: "How did the poem make you feel?" This stage is about enjoyment, not explanation.
2. **Understanding the poem (10 to 12 minutes):** Read the poem again, and now work on meaning. Explain any difficult words in context. Ask questions that move from literal to inferential: "What is the poem about?" (literal); "Why does the poet call the rain a 'silver thread'?" (inferential); "Have you ever felt like the poet?" (personal response). Discuss the pictures the poem creates in the mind.
3. **Reading, responding and performing (8 to 10 minutes):** Pupils read the poem aloud, first in chorus and then individually. They mark the rhythm by tapping the beat, identify the rhyming words, and talk about one image they liked. A short follow-up task might be to draw the image or write two lines of their own.

**Worked example: a Grade 5 poetry lesson for "The Rain" (35 minutes).**

- Listening and enjoyment (7 minutes): The teacher reads the poem twice while pupils listen. Ask: "Did the poem feel happy or sad?"
- Understanding the poem (12 minutes): Read again. Explain *silver thread* and *dusty street*. Ask: "What is the poem about?" (literal); "Why does the poet call the rain a silver thread?" (inferential); "Do you like the rain? Why?" (personal response).
- Reading, responding and performing (10 minutes): Chorus reading, then individual reading. Tap the rhythm. Underline the rhyming words *street* and *feet*. Draw the silver thread image.
- Follow-up (6 minutes): Pupils write two sentences beginning "The rain makes me think of...".

## Activity: Plan a poetry lesson

**Work in small groups. About 25 minutes.**

Choose a short poem suitable for Grade 5. Plan a three-stage poetry lesson: listening and enjoyment, understanding the poem, and reading and responding. For each stage, write one question you will ask and one action the pupils will take. Then present your plan to another group and ask them to identify which stage builds enjoyment and which builds understanding.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the three stages of a poetry lesson in order.
2. *(Understand)* Explain two ways a poetry lesson differs from a prose lesson.
3. *(Apply)* A teacher asks pupils to memorise a poem before reading it aloud or discussing it. Give two reasons why this order limits the pupils' response to the poem.

## Summary

A poetry lesson teaches pupils to enjoy, understand and respond to a poem. Memorisation may be one result, but it is not the aim. The stages are listening and enjoyment, understanding the poem, and reading, responding and performing. Rhythm, rhyme and imagery help pupils experience the poem before they analyse it.
