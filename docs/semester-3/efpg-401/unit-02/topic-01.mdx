## A real classroom situation

Rashid is a prospective teacher on practicum at a government primary school in Sukkur. For his Grade 5 English lesson, he selects a short story, "The Honest Woodcutter." He reads the story aloud twice, then translates every sentence into Sindhi. He asks the pupils to copy the translation into their notebooks. When he finishes, he asks: "What happened to the woodcutter?" The pupils repeat the translation from memory, but none can answer the question in English.

At the break, his cooperating teacher, Madam Nighat, talks with him. "You read well," she says, "but you taught the translation, not the English. The pupils never had to read the English words for meaning. Prose teaching is not translation. It is teaching pupils to read English and understand it."

## Explanation

A prose lesson teaches a continuous English text: a story, an essay or an informational passage. The aim is not to translate the text but to help pupils read it and understand it in English. A good prose lesson moves through clear stages so that pupils meet new words before they read, hear the text read well, read it themselves, and then respond to it.

A common misconception is that "teaching prose means reading the lesson aloud and translating it." Many teachers were taught this way themselves, and it feels safe. But translation alone does not teach reading. Pupils may memorise a translation and still be unable to read the same words on a new page. The teacher's job is to build understanding in English, using the mother tongue only as a support, not as a substitute.

### Teaching of prose

The steps below are a reliable sequence for Grades 4 to 8. You can adjust the time for each step to fit a 35-minute period.

1. **Warm-up (3 to 4 minutes):** Connect the lesson to pupils' lives. Show a picture from the story, ask two or three simple questions, and introduce the topic in English. For "The Honest Woodcutter," show a drawing of a woodcutter and ask: "What work does a woodcutter do?" and "Have you seen an axe?"
2. **New words (5 to 6 minutes):** Pre-teach four to six words that pupils cannot guess from the text. Present each word in a simple English sentence, with a picture or action where possible. For the story, teach *axe*, *river*, *honest* and *reward* this way. Do not write a long list of twenty words; a short, well-taught set is more useful.
3. **Model reading (4 to 5 minutes):** The teacher reads the text aloud with natural expression, while pupils follow in their books. This models pronunciation, pausing and intonation. Read once without stopping to explain; the meaning work comes later.
4. **Silent reading (6 to 8 minutes):** Pupils read the text silently at their own pace. This is the heart of the lesson. Silent reading lets each pupil focus on meaning without the pressure of performing aloud. Walk around and support pupils who are stuck.
5. **Comprehension questions (8 to 10 minutes):** Ask questions that move from literal to inferential to personal response. Literal questions check facts in the text ("Who found the axe?"). Inferential questions ask pupils to read between the lines ("Why did the woodcutter refuse the gold axe?"). Personal response questions ask for the pupil's own view ("Would you have returned the axe? Why?"). A good set has all three types.
6. **Follow-up (5 to 6 minutes):** Pupils use the text for a short speaking or writing task. They might retell the story in pairs, write three sentences about the woodcutter, or discuss what happened next.

**Worked example: a Grade 6 prose lesson for "The Honest Woodcutter" (35 minutes).**

- Warm-up (4 minutes): Show a picture of a woodcutter. Ask: "What does a woodcutter do?" and "What tools does he use?" Write the word *axe* on the board.
- New words (5 minutes): Teach *axe*, *river*, *honest* and *reward* with simple sentences and pictures.
- Model reading (5 minutes): Read the story aloud once while pupils follow.
- Silent reading (7 minutes): Pupils read silently. The teacher supports two pupils who are stuck on the word *river*.
- Comprehension questions (9 minutes): Ask: "Who found the axe in the river?" (literal); "Why did the woodcutter say the gold axe was not his?" (inferential); "Would you have returned the axe? Why?" (personal response).
- Follow-up (5 minutes): In pairs, pupils retell the story in four sentences.

## Activity: Build a comprehension question set

**Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.**

Take a short Grade 5 story from your textbook or from the example above. Draft a set of six comprehension questions: two literal, two inferential and two personal response. Then exchange your set with another pair. Check whether each question can be answered from the text and whether the three types are genuinely different. Revise any question that is really the same type in disguise.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* List the six stages of a prose lesson in order.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why silent reading is given more time than model reading in a prose lesson.
3. *(Apply)* A teacher asks only literal questions after a story. Add two questions, one inferential and one personal response, that would deepen the pupils' understanding of the same story.

## Summary

A prose lesson teaches pupils to read English for meaning, not to translate it. The stages are warm-up, new words, model reading, silent reading, comprehension questions and follow-up. Comprehension questions should move from literal facts to inference and personal response. Translation may support understanding, but it is not the goal of the lesson.

During your next practicum visit, ask your cooperating teacher if you may observe an English prose lesson. Note the stages the teacher uses and the types of questions asked. After the lesson, discuss one question the teacher could add to reach the inferential or personal response level.

|---|---|---|---|
