# Unit 4 Assessment - Teaching of Grammar

This end-of-unit assessment measures your understanding of the functional approach to teaching grammar. Complete the summative assessment tasks before reviewing the model answers and marking guidance at the end of this page.

## Unit summary

Unit 4 introduced the functional approach to teaching grammar, which treats grammar as a resource for making meaning rather than a list of rules to memorise. A grammar point has three dimensions: **form** (its grammatical pattern), **meaning** (the message it encodes) and **use** (how it is applied in a real context). A pupil has not learned a grammar point until all three are in place.

The unit contrasted two ways of presenting grammar. **Deductive presentation** starts with the rule and then gives examples, which is quick and clear. **Inductive presentation** starts with examples in context and guides pupils to notice the pattern, which builds deeper understanding. Both have a place, and the choice depends on the class, the structure and the time available.

Finally, the unit showed that grammar practice should move from **controlled practice**, which builds accuracy, to **meaningful tasks**, which build the ability to use the structure in real communication. Saying a rule is not the same as using it. The functional approach keeps form, meaning and use together so that pupils learn grammar as a tool for saying what they mean.

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. *(Remember)* What is the main purpose of the functional approach to teaching grammar?
   - A) To help pupils memorise grammar rules quickly
   - B) To treat grammar as a resource for making meaning
   - C) To prepare pupils for grammar examinations
   - D) To replace all grammar teaching with communication games

2. *(Remember)* Which three dimensions make up a grammar item in Larsen-Freeman's model?
   - A) Reading, writing and speaking
   - B) Form, meaning and use
   - C) Rules, exercises and tests
   - D) Listening, vocabulary and grammar

3. *(Understand)* What does "use" mean in the form-meaning-use model?
   - A) The grammatical pattern of a structure
   - B) The message a structure encodes
   - C) How a structure is applied in a real communicative context
   - D) The number of times a structure appears in a text

4. *(Understand)* What is the main difference between deductive and inductive presentation?
   - A) Deductive presentation starts with the rule; inductive starts with examples
   - B) Deductive presentation is faster; inductive is slower
   - C) Deductive presentation is for older pupils; inductive is for younger pupils
   - D) Deductive presentation uses textbooks; inductive uses pictures

5. *(Apply)* A teacher shows a picture of a busy market and describes six people using the present continuous. Which dimension is the teacher establishing first?
   - A) Form
   - B) Meaning
   - C) Use
   - D) Rule

6. *(Apply)* A teacher writes the rule for the present continuous on the board and then gives a substitution drill. Which kind of presentation is this?
   - A) Inductive
   - B) Deductive
   - C) Functional
   - D) Task-based

7. *(Remember)* Which of the following is the correct form of the present continuous?
   - A) Subject + verb base
   - B) Subject + am/is/are + verb-ing
   - C) Subject + will + verb base
   - D) Subject + verb-ed

8. *(Understand)* Why is a rule-and-drill exercise limited in the functional approach?
   - A) It is too difficult for primary pupils
   - B) It teaches the form but not the meaning and use
   - C) It takes too much time to prepare
   - D) It does not follow the textbook

9. *(Apply)* A pupil can state the rule for the present continuous but cannot describe a picture. What does this show?
   - A) The pupil has learned the grammar point fully
   - B) The pupil has declarative knowledge but not procedural knowledge
   - C) The pupil needs more grammar-translation practice
   - D) The pupil has a hearing problem

10. *(Apply)* Which task best represents "free use" of a grammar point?
    - A) A gap-fill exercise with the target structure
    - B) A substitution drill with a table
    - C) A pair activity where pupils describe different pictures and find differences
    - D) A written test on the rule

8. *(Understand)* Explain why the functional approach is not the same as "no grammar teaching".

9. *(Analyze)* Analyse how a functional grammar lesson differs from a grammar-translation lesson, using the present continuous as an example.

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. *(Analyze)* A textbook exercise asks pupils to complete ten sentences with the correct form of the present continuous, but it gives no picture, context or situation. Rewrite the exercise as a functional, meaning-first task. Your answer must include a context, a notice step, a statement of form, meaning and use, a controlled practice step and a free-use communicative task. Analyse why your version teaches the grammar point more effectively.

2. *(Evaluate)* Read this description of a grammar lesson: "The teacher wrote the rule for the present continuous on the board, gave two examples, led a choral drill, and then asked pupils to write ten sentences using the tense. At the end of the lesson she marked the sentences and found that eight pupils had used the wrong form." Evaluate whether this lesson taught grammar as a resource for meaning. Judge its strengths and weaknesses against the form-meaning-use model, and propose two changes that would improve it.

3. *(Create)* Design a complete 30-minute functional grammar lesson for a Grade 6 class on "should / ought to for advice". Your plan must include a context that shows meaning, a notice step, a statement of form, meaning and use, a controlled practice activity, a free-use communicative task, and a short assessment check. Include a mini-rubric for the free-use task.

4. *(Analyze)* Analyse how the functional approach relates to the communicative approach studied in Unit 3. In your answer, explain why a functional grammar lesson is not a return to the grammar-translation method, and how a teacher can combine inductive and deductive presentation within a functional lesson.

5. *(Evaluate)* Evaluate the claim that "grammar is best taught implicitly, without ever stating rules". Use the form-meaning-use model and evidence from the readings to support your judgement. Say what the claim gets right and what it overlooks.

## Answers and marking guidance

### MCQ answer key

1. B - The functional approach treats grammar as a resource for making meaning, not as a list of rules to memorise.
2. B - Larsen-Freeman's model identifies form, meaning and use as the three dimensions of a grammar item.
3. C - Use is the pragmatic dimension: how a structure is applied in a real communicative context.
4. A - Deductive presentation states the rule first; inductive presentation begins with examples and guides pupils to notice the pattern.
5. B - Describing a picture establishes what the structure means before any rule is stated.
6. B - Stating the rule first and then drilling it is deductive presentation.
7. B - The present continuous is formed with am, is or are plus the -ing form of the verb.
8. B - A rule-and-drill exercise builds accuracy in the form but leaves meaning and use untouched.
9. B - Reciting a rule is declarative knowledge; using the structure in speech needs procedural knowledge.
10. C - A pair activity with a real communicative purpose represents free use, not controlled practice.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. **Model Answer:** Form is the grammatical pattern of a structure, such as subject plus am, is or are plus verb-ing. Meaning is the message the structure encodes, such as an action happening now. The present continuous has the form "She is reading" and the meaning that the action is happening at the moment of speaking.
   - **Marking Scheme (2 marks):**
     - 1 mark: Correctly defines form as the grammatical pattern.
     - 1 mark: Correctly defines meaning as the message, with a valid example.

2. **Model Answer:** Deductive presentation states the rule first and then gives examples, which is quick and clear. Inductive presentation gives examples first and guides pupils to notice the pattern, which builds deeper understanding. Deductive presentation is an advantage when time is short or the structure is simple. Inductive presentation is an advantage when the class benefits from discovering the pattern or the structure is complex.
   - **Marking Scheme (3 marks):**
     - 1 mark: Correctly describes deductive presentation.
     - 1 mark: Correctly describes inductive presentation.
     - 1 mark: Gives one valid advantage of each.

3. **Model Answer:** Reciting a rule is a memory task that uses declarative knowledge, which is knowledge about the language. Using a structure in speaking is a communication task that needs procedural knowledge, which is knowledge of how to do something. Speaking happens in real time and leaves no time to recall a rule, so a pupil who can say the rule may still fail to use the structure. The functional approach therefore gives pupils many chances to use the structure in meaningful situations.
   - **Marking Scheme (3 marks):**
     - 1 mark: Distinguishes declarative knowledge from procedural knowledge.
     - 1 mark: Explains why real-time speaking makes recall difficult.
     - 1 mark: Connects the explanation to the need for meaningful practice.

4. **Model Answer:** Controlled practice keeps the target structure fixed so that pupils can focus on accuracy. An example for "there is / there are" is a gap-fill worksheet with pictures: "There ___ a book on the table" (is) and "There ___ three boys in the yard" (are). Pupils complete the sentences and then check their answers with a partner.
   - **Marking Scheme (2 marks):**
     - 1 mark: Correctly defines controlled practice as fixed-structure accuracy work.
     - 1 mark: Gives a valid example.

5. **Model Answer:** Deductive presentation is quick and clear, which helps a mixed-ability class stay on task, but it can leave weaker pupils behind because they must grasp the rule before they see it in use. Inductive presentation builds understanding and curiosity, which helps weaker pupils connect the rule to meaning, but it takes more time and some pupils may not notice the pattern without support. For a mixed-ability Grade 5 class, a teacher might present the rule deductively for clarity and then use an inductive noticing task to deepen understanding.
   - **Marking Scheme (4 marks):**
     - 1 mark: Identifies a strength of deductive presentation.
     - 1 mark: Identifies a weakness of deductive presentation.
     - 1 mark: Identifies a strength of inductive presentation.
     - 1 mark: Identifies a weakness of inductive presentation.

6. **Model Answer:** A meaningful task asks pupils to use the grammar point for a communicative purpose, so that they must attend to meaning as well as form. An example is a pair activity where Pupil A has one picture and Pupil B has a different picture of the same scene. They describe what people are doing and find three differences. It is meaningful because the pupils need to understand each other to complete the task.
   - **Marking Scheme (3 marks):**
     - 1 mark: Defines a meaningful task as purposeful communication.
     - 1 mark: Gives a valid example.
     - 1 mark: Explains why the example is meaningful.

7. **Model Answer:** The exercise neglects meaning and use. It teaches only the form, because pupils must choose the correct verb form without any picture or situation to show what the sentence means or when it would be used. As a result, pupils may be able to fill the gaps correctly without being able to use the structure to describe a real scene or communicate a message.
   - **Marking Scheme (3 marks):**
     - 1 mark: Identifies that meaning and use are neglected.
     - 1 mark: Explains that only form is taught.
     - 1 mark: States the consequence for the pupils' ability to communicate.

8. **Model Answer:** The functional approach does not remove grammar. It changes the order and the purpose. The teacher still teaches the form clearly, but the form is taught after the meaning has been made clear and always with a use in mind. Pupils still learn rules, but they learn them as tools for communication rather than as facts to recite.
   - **Marking Scheme (2 marks):**
     - 1 mark: States that grammar is still taught.
     - 1 mark: Explains that the order and purpose change, with form taught after meaning and with use in mind.

9. **Model Answer:** A grammar-translation lesson teaches the form first and often leaves meaning and use out. It uses the mother tongue to explain rules and gives translation exercises. A functional grammar lesson begins with meaning in a context, helps pupils notice the form, states the rule as a tool, and asks pupils to use the structure in a communicative task. For the present continuous, a functional lesson would begin with a picture of a market, not with the rule on the board.
   - **Marking Scheme (3 marks):**
     - 1 mark: Describes the grammar-translation lesson accurately.
     - 1 mark: Describes the functional lesson accurately.
     - 1 mark: Uses the present continuous as a concrete example.

10. **Model Answer:** A controlled practice activity for "there is / there are" is a picture gap-fill. The teacher shows a picture of a classroom with a clock, three chairs, one teacher and many books. Pupils complete sentences: "There ___ one teacher" (is) and "There ___ three chairs" (are). To lead into free use, pupils then work in pairs to describe their own classroom to a partner, who draws it. The drawing task gives pupils a reason to use the structure.
    - **Marking Scheme (3 marks):**
      - 1 mark: Provides a valid controlled practice activity.
      - 1 mark: Provides a free-use follow-up task.
      - 1 mark: Explains how the controlled activity leads into the free-use task.

### ERQ rubrics

#### ERQ 1: Rewriting a Rule-and-Drill Exercise as a Functional Task
- **Analytic Rubric (10 marks total):**
  - *Analysis of the original exercise (0-3 marks):* Identifies that the exercise teaches form only and neglects meaning and use.
  - *Task design (0-3 marks):* Provides a context, a notice step, a statement of form, meaning and use, a controlled practice step and a free-use task.
  - *Justification and analysis (0-4 marks, **Analyze**):* **Analyze:** Explains precisely how the original exercise separates form from meaning and use, and why the rewritten version builds procedural knowledge that the original cannot.

#### ERQ 2: Evaluation of a Described Grammar Lesson
- **Analytic Rubric (10 marks total):**
  - *Diagnosis of the lesson (0-3 marks):* Identifies that the lesson teaches form through a rule and a drill but gives pupils no meaningful context or communicative use.
  - *Judgement against the model (0-3 marks):* Judges the lesson against all three dimensions of the form-meaning-use model, noting what is present and what is missing.
  - *Critical evaluation (0-4 marks, **Evaluate**):* **Evaluate:** Weighs the lesson's clarity and pace against its failure to build communicative ability, and proposes two specific, evidence-based changes that would make the lesson functional.

#### ERQ 3: A 30-Minute Functional Grammar Lesson Plan
- **Analytic Rubric (10 marks total):**
  - *Lesson architecture (0-3 marks):* Includes a context, a notice step, a statement of form, meaning and use, a controlled practice activity, a free-use task and an assessment check.
  - *Pedagogical soundness (0-3 marks):* Sequences the lesson from meaning to form to use, and the free-use task has a real communicative purpose.
  - *Rubric and creation (0-4 marks, **Create**):* **Create:** Designs a mini-rubric for the free-use task that separates communicative success from grammatical accuracy, justifying the separation.

#### ERQ 4: The Functional Approach and the Communicative Approach
- **Analytic Rubric (10 marks total):**
  - *Relationship between the approaches (0-3 marks):* Explains that the functional approach builds on the communicative approach by keeping grammar as a tool for communication.
  - *Distinction from grammar-translation (0-3 marks):* Explains why a functional lesson is not a return to grammar-translation, citing the treatment of meaning and use.
  - *Integrative analysis (0-4 marks, **Analyze**):* **Analyze:** Analyses how inductive and deductive presentation can be combined within a single functional lesson, and why that combination serves pupils better than either presentation alone.

#### ERQ 5: Evaluation of the Claim that Grammar Should Be Taught Implicitly
- **Analytic Rubric (10 marks total):**
  - *Understanding the claim (0-3 marks):* Explains the deep-end view that grammar is best learnt unconsciously through communication.
  - *Use of the model and readings (0-3 marks):* Tests the claim against the form-meaning-use model and the evidence that explicit and implicit teaching each have a place.
  - *Critical evaluation (0-4 marks, **Evaluate**):* **Evaluate:** Weighs what the claim gets right (implicit learning builds fluency) against what it overlooks (some structures need conscious noticing and clear rules), and reaches a justified judgement.
