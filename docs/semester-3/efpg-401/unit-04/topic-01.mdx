## A real classroom situation

Saima is a prospective teacher on practicum at a government girls' primary school in Khairpur. For her Grade 5 English lesson, she prepares what she believes is a clear grammar explanation. She writes on the board: "The present continuous tense is formed with is, am or are plus the -ing form of the verb." She reads the rule aloud twice, the class repeats it in chorus, and then she drills six substitution sentences. The pupils chant them perfectly.

Confident, Saima holds up a large picture of a busy Sukkur market. She points to a woman carrying a basket on her head and asks, "What is the woman doing?" The class falls silent. A few pupils whisper the rule to themselves, but nobody can answer. Saima feels frustrated. She taught the rule clearly, and the pupils repeated it without error. Why can they not use it?

At the break, she speaks with her cooperating teacher, Madam Rukhsana. "They know the rule," Saima says, "but they cannot use it." Madam Rukhsana nods. "They have memorised the form," she says, "but they have not connected it to meaning. You told them how to build the sentence, but you did not show them what it means or when to use it. Grammar is not a list of rules to memorise. It is a tool for saying things."

## Explanation

Grammar is often taught as if it were a list of rules to memorise. Pupils repeat the rules, complete exercises, and take tests. Yet many of them cannot use the grammar when they speak or write. The functional approach to teaching grammar starts from a different idea: grammar is a resource for making meaning. Its purpose is to help pupils say what they mean, in the right situation, to the right person. This topic explains what that means in practice.

### What the functional approach to teaching grammar means

A **functional approach** is a way of teaching grammar that begins with meaning and use instead of rules. The teacher asks three questions about every grammar point: What is its **form**? What is its **meaning**? What is its **use**? Larsen-Freeman (2003, 2014), cited in Koca, Stavre and Kaçani (2024), calls this the three-dimensional model of grammar: **form** (the accurate use of a grammatical feature), **meaning** (the message the feature encodes) and **pragmatic use** (how the feature is applied in a real context). A grammar item is not learned until a pupil can handle all three.

Take the **present continuous** tense as an example. Its **form** is subject plus am, is or are plus the -ing form of the verb (for example, "She is reading"). Its **meaning** is that an action is happening now, at the moment of speaking. Its **use** is to describe what people are doing in a picture, a video still or a classroom scene. A pupil who can repeat the rule but cannot describe a picture has only learned the form.

This is the central difference from the grammar-translation method you studied in Unit 3. Grammar-translation teaches the form first and often leaves meaning and use out altogether. The functional approach keeps all three together. It also builds on the communicative approach: grammar is taught so that pupils can communicate, not so that they can pass a grammar test.

A common misconception is that "grammar is learned by memorising rules". This is false. Memorising a rule gives a pupil declarative knowledge, which is knowledge about the language. Using the language in speech and writing needs procedural knowledge, which is knowledge of how to do something. The two are related but not the same. A pupil can know that a verb needs -ing in the present continuous and still fail to add -ing while speaking, because speaking happens in real time and leaves no time to recall a rule. The functional approach therefore gives pupils many chances to use the structure in meaningful situations, not just to state its rule.

A second misconception is that "a functional approach means no grammar teaching". This is also false. The functional approach does not remove grammar. It changes the order and the purpose. The teacher still teaches the form clearly, but the form is taught after the meaning has been made clear and always with a use in mind. Pupils still learn rules, but they learn them as tools for communication rather than as facts to recite.

### Presenting grammar inductively and deductively

There are two main ways to present a new grammar point. In **deductive presentation**, the teacher states the rule first and then gives examples. In **inductive presentation**, the teacher gives examples first and guides pupils to notice the pattern and work out the rule themselves. Both are useful, and a good teacher chooses between them according to the class, the structure and the time available.

Kiran (2021) compares the grammar-translation method with the communicative approach in Pakistani classrooms and reports that grammar-translation teaches grammar deductively, while the shallow-end approach of communicative language teaching teaches grammar inductively. In the shallow-end approach, pupils are given examples that are not detached from their context, and they are asked to catch the underlying structure. This links new grammar to what they already know.

Let us take the same grammar point, the present continuous, and see how each presentation would work in a Grade 5 class.

**Deductive presentation (rule-first).** The teacher writes on the board: "We use is, am or are plus verb-ing for an action happening now." The teacher reads the rule, gives two or three examples ("The boy is running", "They are playing"), and then asks pupils to make their own sentences from a substitution table. This is quick and clear. It suits a simple structure, an older class, or a short lesson. Its risk is that pupils may memorise the rule without understanding when to use it.

**Inductive presentation (examples-first).** The teacher shows the same market picture and describes it: "The woman is carrying a basket. The man is selling fruit. The children are running." The teacher asks, "What do you notice about the verbs?" Pupils look at the examples and notice that every verb ends in -ing and that is, am or are comes before it. The teacher then helps the class state the rule in their own words. This takes more time and needs careful guidance, but it builds understanding and curiosity. It suits a complex structure, a younger class, or a class that enjoys discovering patterns. Its risk is that some pupils may not notice the pattern without support, so the teacher must guide the noticing with questions.

A common misconception is that "if pupils can say the rule they can use the structure". This is false, and it is one of the most persistent beliefs in grammar teaching. Saying a rule is a memory task. Using a structure is a communication task. A pupil can say "We use is, am or are plus verb-ing" and still be unable to describe a picture, because the rule is stored as a sentence to recall, not as a tool to use. The functional approach therefore checks learning by asking pupils to use the structure, not by asking them to recite it.

### Practising grammar through meaningful tasks

Once pupils have noticed the structure and understood its form, meaning and use, they need practice. Practice in the functional approach moves along a path from control to freedom. At the controlled end, the teacher holds the language steady and pupils repeat, substitute or fill gaps. At the free end, pupils use the structure to communicate their own meaning in a real task.

**Controlled practice** keeps the target structure fixed so that pupils can focus on accuracy. Examples include substitution drills, gap-fill sentences with picture prompts, and matching verbs to pictures. Controlled practice has a place, but it is not enough on its own. It teaches the form, but it does not teach the use.

**Meaningful practice** asks pupils to use the structure for a purpose. In a meaningful task, the pupil must attend to meaning, not only to form. Koca, Stavre and Kaçani (2024) report that teachers in Albania find it hard to teach grammar communicatively because of large classes, limited resources and traditional curricula, yet most of them believe that integrated grammar teaching helps pupils use language in real situations. The study recommends combining implicit and explicit grammar teaching and integrating grammar with listening, speaking, reading and writing. Willis and Willis (2007), also cited in Koca et al. (2024), describe a task as an activity that gives primary focus to meaning, relates to real-world activities and measures success by a non-linguistic outcome.

For the present continuous, a meaningful task might be a "picture description" pair activity. Pupil A has a picture of a street scene; Pupil B has a different picture of the same street. Without showing each other the pictures, they describe what people are doing and find three differences. The task has a real purpose: the pupils need to understand each other to complete it. The grammar is used as a tool, not as an end in itself.

### Worked example: a functional grammar lesson for Grade 5

Study this worked example before you design your own task. It shows the whole path from meaning to use for the present continuous.

- **Context (meaning first):** The teacher shows a large picture of a busy railway station in Sukkur. She asks, "What can you see?" and accepts any answer. Then she says, "Look at the boy. He is running. Look at the woman. She is carrying a bag." She describes six people in the picture using the present continuous.
- **Notice:** The teacher writes her six sentences on the board and asks, "What do you notice about the verbs?" Pupils work in pairs to spot the pattern. The teacher circulates and prompts: "What comes before the verb? What letter does every verb end with?"
- **Understand:** The teacher confirms the pattern and states it clearly: "We use is, am or are plus verb-ing for an action happening now." She checks meaning with two questions: "Is the boy running now, in the picture? Is the woman carrying a bag now?" She checks use with a question: "When do we use this tense?" Pupils answer in their own words.
- **Controlled practice:** Pupils complete a gap-fill worksheet with six more pictures from the same station. Each picture has a sentence with a missing verb: "The man ___ selling tickets" (is). Pupils work alone, then check answers with a partner.
- **Free use:** In pairs, pupils describe a new picture to their partner. The partner cannot see the picture and must draw what they hear. When they finish, they compare the drawing with the picture. The task has a real outcome: an accurate drawing.

Notice the order. The meaning comes first, the rule comes after the pupils have noticed the pattern, and the free task asks them to use the grammar for a purpose. The rule is taught, but it is taught as the last step in understanding, not the first.

## Activity: Design a functional mini-lesson

**Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.**

Choose one grammar point from this list: **there is / there are**, **can / can't for ability**, or **should / ought to for advice**. Design a 15-minute functional mini-lesson for a Grade 4 or 5 class. Your plan must include:

1. A **context** that shows the meaning of the grammar point (a picture, a short text, or a classroom scene).
2. A **notice** step, where pupils look at examples and spot the pattern.
3. An **understand** step, where you state the form, meaning and use in simple words.
4. A **controlled practice** step, such as a gap-fill or a substitution table.
5. A **free-use task**, where pupils use the grammar point to communicate something real.

Exchange your plan with another pair. Check whether the free-use task genuinely needs the grammar point, or whether pupils could complete it without using the structure. If they could, suggest a change that makes the grammar necessary.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State the three dimensions of a grammar item in the functional approach, and give one example of each for a grammar point you know.
2. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between deductive and inductive presentation in your own words, and give one situation where each would be the better choice.
3. *(Apply)* A colleague teaches the present perfect by giving pupils a rule and then a gap-fill exercise. Using the form-meaning-use model, explain what is missing from the lesson and what you would add.

## Summary

The functional approach treats grammar as a resource for making meaning, not as a list of rules to memorise. Every grammar point has three dimensions: form, meaning and use, and a pupil has not learned it until all three are in place. Grammar can be presented deductively, with the rule first, or inductively, with examples first; the choice depends on the class, the structure and the time. Practice should move from controlled exercises, which build accuracy, to meaningful tasks, which build the ability to use the structure in real communication. Saying a rule is not the same as using it, and the functional approach is built on that difference.

## Summative task

Rewrite a rule-and-drill grammar exercise as a functional, meaning-first task. Start from this exercise, which is typical of a grammar-translation textbook:

> **Exercise:** Complete these sentences with the correct form of the verb in brackets.
> 1. She ______ (read) a book. 2. They ______ (play) cricket. 3. I ______ (write) a letter.

Your task has three parts:

1. **Analyse** the exercise. Identify which dimensions of the form-meaning-use model it teaches and which it neglects. Write three or four sentences.
2. **Rewrite** it as a functional task. Provide a context that shows the meaning, a notice step, a clear statement of form, meaning and use, a controlled practice step, and a free-use communicative task. Write the steps a teacher would follow.
3. **Justify** your design. In two or three sentences, explain why your version teaches the grammar point better than the original exercise.
