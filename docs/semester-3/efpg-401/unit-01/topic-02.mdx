## A real classroom situation

Hina is teaching a Grade 5 English class at a government girls' elementary school in Larkana. The topic in the textbook is "My Family and Home." Eager to promote active participation, Hina stands by the chalkboard and calls on individual pupils: "Farzana, stand up and tell the class three sentences about your mother in English."

Farzana slowly rises to her feet. She grips the edge of her wooden desk, looks down at the floor, and blushes deeply. Ten seconds pass in complete silence. Several pupils at the back of the room giggle softly. Farzana whispers a single word in Sindhi and sits down abruptly. Hina moves to another pupil, but the response is identical: anxiety, hesitation, and refusal to speak.

In the staffroom, Hina confides in her senior colleague, Madam Najma. "Our pupils refuse to speak English," Hina sighs. "They know the vocabulary from their notebooks, but when I ask them to speak, they freeze." Madam Najma nods with immediate recognition. "Think about the social situation you created," she points out. "You asked a hesitant ten-year-old child to perform alone in front of forty peers in an unfamiliar foreign language, with the fear of public embarrassment if she makes a grammatical error. Speaking is a vulnerable social act. If we want pupils to talk, we must first build a safe climate, provide clear conversational models, and let them practice in low-stakes pairs before demanding whole-class performance."

## Explanation

Speaking is an interactive, productive language skill that requires the simultaneous coordination of cognitive, linguistic, and socio-affective processes. To speak English, a learner must retrieve appropriate vocabulary from memory, organize words into grammatically coherent structures, articulate English sounds with correct stress and rhythm, and monitor the listener's reactions - all in real time.

A prevalent misconception in many schools is that "speaking practice means repeating after the teacher in chorus." While choral repetition of textbook lines can familiarize pupils with the physical articulation of new words, it does not constitute authentic speaking. Choral drilling is mechanical; it involves no personal choice of meaning, no information exchange, and no genuine communicative purpose. When children only repeat memorized sentences together, they remain incapable of asking for a glass of water, describing their feelings, or answering an unfamiliar question independently. Genuine oral proficiency develops when pupils use English to communicate real messages to real listeners.

### A favourable classroom environment for speaking

To overcome hesitation and silence, teachers must cultivate a classroom environment characterized by psychological safety. Stephen Krashen's Affective Filter Hypothesis highlights that high anxiety, lack of self-confidence, and fear of negative evaluation act as a mental barrier (a high affective filter) that blocks language acquisition and speech production. When the affective filter is lowered, learners feel relaxed, willing to take communicative risks, and receptive to practice.

Teachers can establish a favourable speaking climate through specific pedagogical actions:

1. **Destigmatizing Errors:** Language acquisition inevitably involves trial and error. If a teacher interrupts a child mid-sentence to correct every dropped third-person "-s" or missed article, the child's train of thought is shattered and their willingness to speak evaporates. Teachers must distinguish between *accuracy-focused* practice (where form is corrected gently) and *fluency-focused* activities (where communication is paramount). During communicative speaking, errors should be noted unobtrusively on a notepad and addressed collectively at the end of the lesson without identifying individual culprits.
2. **Maximizing Student Talking Time (STT) via Pair Work:** In a traditional teacher-centered classroom of 40 pupils, Teacher Talking Time (TTT) often consumes 80 percent of the period. If the remaining time is divided among 40 individual recitations, each child speaks for less than twenty seconds. By shifting to structured pair work (think-pair-share, peer interviews, collaborative problem-solving), every child in the room speaks English simultaneously for several minutes. Pair work also removes the intimidating spotlight of the whole class; pupils speak quietly to a trusted friend who shares their proficiency level.
3. **Establishing Routines of Encouragement:** Set explicit ground rules against laughing at mistakes. Praise communicative effort and successful meaning-transfer rather than penalizing imperfect grammar: "Well done, Ali! I understood exactly where you went yesterday."

### The value of pronunciation and intonation in speaking

Effective spoken communication does not require adopting an artificial foreign accent; the goal in primary English teaching is **international intelligibility**. Intelligible speech allows listeners to understand the speaker's message without undue strain or misunderstanding. Celce-Murcia (1991) categorizes pronunciation instruction into two interrelated domains: segmental features and suprasegmental features.

#### 1. Segmental Features (Vowels and Consonants)
Segmentals are the individual speech sounds (phonemes) of English. Pakistani learners often face challenges when English contains sounds that do not exist in their mother tongues (Sindhi, Urdu, Pashto, Punjabi, or Balochi), or where native language sound patterns interfere with English:
- *Consonant Distinctions:* Distinguishing between the voiceless bilabial plosive /p/ and voiced bilabial plosive /b/ (e.g. *pin* vs *bin*), or between labiodental fricative /v/ and labiovelar approximant /w/ (e.g. *vine* vs *wine*).
- *Consonant Clusters:* English frequently groups consonants together without intervening vowels (e.g. *school*, *street*, *desk*, *helped*). Learners often insert epenthetic vowels (saying *is-school* or *is-tation*). Teachers should use rhythm clapping and physical tapping to help pupils articulate initial and final consonant clusters smoothly.
- *Minimal Pairs:* A highly effective technique is minimal-pair practice, contrasting two words that differ by only a single phoneme: *ship* vs *sheep*, *cat* vs *cot*, *fan* vs *van*.

#### 2. Suprasegmental Features (Stress, Rhythm, and Intonation)
Suprasegmentals operate above the level of individual sounds and carry enormous communicative weight. English is a stress-timed language, where stressed syllables occur at roughly regular intervals regardless of how many unstressed syllables intervene.

- **Word Stress:** Every multi-syllable English word has a primary stressed syllable that is spoken louder, longer, and with higher pitch: *TEACH-er*, *com-PU-ter*, *ed-u-CA-tion*. Misplacing word stress often causes greater communicative breakdown than mispronouncing an isolated vowel sound.
- **Sentence Stress:** In connected speech, content words (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs) receive stress, while grammatical function words (articles, prepositions, auxiliary verbs, pronouns) are unstressed and often reduced (e.g. "a cup of tea" sounds like /ə kʌp əv tiː/).
- **Intonation:** Intonation refers to the melodic rise and fall of the voice pitch across a phrase or sentence. Intonation communicates grammatical sentence types, speaker attitudes, and politeness:
  - *Falling Intonation (↘):* Standard for definitive statements ("The school is closed. ↘"), commands ("Open your books. ↘"), and wh- questions ("Where do you live? ↘"). It signals finality and certainty.
  - *Rising Intonation (↗):* Standard for yes/no questions ("Are you ready? ↗"), expressions of surprise, and polite requests ("May I come in? ↗"). It signals that the speaker is inviting confirmation or awaiting more information.

### Conversation and dialogue

Dialogue is the natural social vehicle for developing conversational competence. Nolasco and Arthur (1987) emphasize that conversation requires learners to manage turn-taking, open and close interactions politely, and negotiate meaning collaboratively.

To transition pupils from structured repetition to communicative fluency, teachers should employ a three-tier dialogue progression:

```
+------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                      DIALOGUE TEACHING PROGRESSION                     |
+------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 1. Scripted Dialogue: Rehearse natural conversational model             |
| 2. Substitution Dialogue: Swap keywords using visual cue cards          |
| 3. Information-Gap Role Play: Exchange genuine unknown information      |
+------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

#### Worked Example: A Dialogue Teaching Progression for Grade 4
- **Stage 1: Scripted Model (Teacher and Pupil Demonstration)**
  - *Person A:* "Hello, Rashid. May I borrow your ruler?"
  - *Person B:* "Sure, here you are. Please give it back after math class."
  - *Person A:* "Thank you very much. I will."
  - Pupils practice the dialogue in pairs, focusing on polite rising intonation on "May I borrow your ruler? ↗" and falling intonation on "Thank you very much. ↘".
- **Stage 2: Substitution Cues (Controlled Variation)**
  - Teacher provides flashcards depicting different school objects: *eraser*, *blue pen*, *sharpener*, *coloured pencils*.
  - Pupils substitute the object and the corresponding context:
    - "Hello, Rashid. May I borrow your *blue pen*?"
    - "Sure, here you are. Please give it back after *English class*."
- **Stage 3: Information-Gap Role Play (Authentic Transfer)**
  - Pupil A receives a shopping list: "You need 2 notebooks, 1 pencil, and 1 eraser. Ask the shopkeeper for prices."
  - Pupil B plays the shopkeeper with a price list. Pupil A does not know the prices; Pupil B does not know what Pupil A wants to buy.
  - Pupils must ask, answer, calculate totals, and negotiate using spoken English.

### Language games for oral expression

Language games are not frivolous fillers; they are highly disciplined pedagogical instruments. Games create a genuine communicative imperative: pupils want to participate, guess, win, or solve a puzzle, which compels them to use English spontaneously. Games introduce natural repetition without the soul-crushing boredom of rote recitation.

Key language games suitable for Pakistani elementary schools include:

1. **Twenty Questions (Guess the Object):** One pupil chooses a familiar classroom item or local animal (e.g. *goat*, *tractor*, *mango*). The rest of the class or small group can ask up to twenty yes/no questions to deduce the mystery item: "Is it an animal?" "Can it fly?" "Is it bigger than a desk?" "Does it live on a farm?" This game reinforces auxiliary verbs (*Is it...?*, *Does it...?*, *Can it...?*) and adjective vocabulary.
2. **Describe and Draw:** Pupils work back-to-back in pairs. Pupil A holds a simple line drawing of geometric shapes or household scenes (e.g. a circle inside a square next to a tree). Without showing the picture, Pupil A describes it in English: "Draw a large square in the middle. Inside the square, draw a circle." Pupil B listens and draws. At the end, they compare drawings. This game intensely develops prepositions of place and descriptive clarity.
3. **Chain Story:** The teacher begins a story: "Yesterday morning, a small green parrot flew into our classroom." The next pupil adds a sentence: "It landed on the teacher's table." The third adds: "The parrot looked at Ali's notebook." This game practices past tense narrative forms and cohesive discourse.
4. **Find Someone Who:** Pupils receive a grid containing statements: "Find someone who has two brothers," "...walks to school," "...likes cricket," "...can ride a bicycle." Pupils stand up, move around the room, and ask each other in English: "Do you have two brothers?" When a peer answers "Yes, I do," the interviewer writes down their name. This gets every child moving and asking repetitive question forms naturally.
5. **Spot the Difference:** In pairs, pupils receive two slightly different versions of the same illustration (e.g. in Picture A, the boy wears a hat; in Picture B, he does not). Without looking at each other's sheets, they talk to discover five differences: "In my picture, the boy is holding a book. What about your picture?"

### Vocabulary building

Oral fluency depends directly on the depth and availability of a speaker's mental lexicon. When children pause excessively or switch to their native language, the root cause is frequently a vocabulary deficit. Celce-Murcia (1991) distinguishes between **receptive vocabulary** (words a learner recognises when listening or reading) and **productive vocabulary** (words a learner can retrieve and pronounce accurately in spontaneous speech).

To build strong oral vocabulary, teachers should use multi-sensory techniques:
- **Semantic Mapping:** Draw a central theme on the chalkboard (e.g. *Transport in Sindh*) and brainstorm radiating categories with pupils: land (*bus*, *rickshaw*, *cart*, *train*), water (*boat*, *ferry*), air (*airplane*).
- **Word Walls:** Maintain an interactive vocabulary display on classroom chart paper where new words encountered in stories and dialogues are recorded with small pictorial symbols.
- **Realia and Flashcards:** Always pair new concrete nouns with tangible objects or clear picture cards. Seeing, touching, and naming a piece of clay pottery (*matka*) or a woven basket creates strong sensory anchors.
- **Collocations and Chunks:** Teach words in natural partnerships rather than isolated lists. Teach *make tea*, *catch a cold*, *take a bus*, *heavy rain*, and *brush your teeth*. Formulaic chunks allow children to speak fluently without having to construct every phrase from scratch.

## Activity: Information-gap speaking game

**Work in pairs.** Time allocation: approximately 20 minutes.

Design a classroom information-gap speaking activity for Grade 4 pupils titled **"The Lost Animal."**
1. Create two distinct role cards:
   - *Card A (Farmer Asif):* Describes a missing animal (type, color, special marks, location last seen).
   - *Card B (Village Scout):* Has pictures of four different animals with varying features.
2. Write the exact question frames that Farmer Asif must use (e.g. "Have you seen my...?", "What color is it?", "Does it have...?").
3. Write the exact response frames that the Village Scout must use (e.g. "Yes, I saw a...", "It has...", "It was near the...").
4. Rehearse the game with your partner. One person plays Farmer Asif; the other plays the Village Scout.
5. Exchange your game with another pair. Evaluate whether the activity genuinely requires oral communication to solve the problem or whether a pupil could guess the answer without speaking English.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State the difference between rising intonation and falling intonation, giving one example of an English sentence for each.
2. *(Understand)* Explain how conducting dialogue practice in pairs reduces a learner's affective filter compared to calling on individual pupils to recite before the entire class.
3. *(Apply)* In a Grade 3 class in Mirpurkhas, pupils frequently pronounce the English word *pen* as /bɛn/ and *pin* as /bɪn/. Design a five-minute minimal-pair pronunciation game that helps pupils distinguish and produce the sounds /p/ and /b/ accurately.

## Summary

Speaking is a vital productive skill requiring a supportive, low-anxiety classroom climate where errors are treated as constructive stepping stones toward fluency. Accurate pronunciation and intonation provide the acoustic foundation for mutual intelligibility, with sentence stress and pitch contours signaling grammatical meaning and attitude. Interactive dialogues scaffold learners from controlled mechanical rehearsal to purposeful communication. Structured language games generate authentic communicative motivation, while systematic vocabulary building equips young learners with the lexical resources needed to express ideas with confidence.

## Summative task

Develop a comprehensive 20-minute speaking lesson plan for Grade 5 focused on oral expression, titled **"Planning Our School Exhibition."** Your plan must include:
1. Targeted communicative functions and vocabulary chunks.
2. A warm-up pronunciation drill focusing on word stress in school-related vocabulary (*pro-ject*, *pic-ture*, *ex-hi-bi-tion*).
3. A pair-work information-gap task with explicit pupil prompt cards.
4. Specific guidelines for teacher monitoring and delayed error feedback.
5. An analytic assessment rubric evaluating pupil speaking performance.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1 mark) | Proficient (2 marks) | Exemplary (3-4 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Communicative task structure** | The activity is purely a mechanical drill with no genuine information exchange or pupil choice. | Includes structured pair work with clear roles and communicative goals. | Highly authentic communicative task; information-gap design guarantees mutual dependence and active oral negotiation. |
| **Phonological focus** | Pronunciation focus is absent or limited to unguided chorus repetition. | Targets specific stress or intonation patterns relevant to the lesson dialogue. | Explicitly teaches segmental or suprasegmental features with clear visual/acoustic models and structured contrastive drills. |
| **Error correction analysis** *(Analyze)* | Prescribes immediate whole-class correction for every mistake without justification. | Distinguishes between fluency and accuracy, recommending delayed feedback. | **Analyze:** Critically evaluates the psychological impact of error correction on second-language learners, justifying specific timing, techniques, and feedback formats that preserve learner confidence while promoting linguistic accuracy. |
