## A real classroom situation

Tariq is a prospective teacher completing his practicum at a government primary school in Mirpurkhas. During a Grade 4 English lesson, he wants his pupils to understand a short story about a farmer who visits a busy market. Tariq stands at the front of the classroom and reads the paragraph in English at a rapid pace. When he finishes, he asks the class: "Where did the farmer leave his donkey?"

The classroom falls completely silent. A few children in the front row repeat the words "farmer" and "donkey" in chorus, but nobody can answer the question. Tariq assumes the pupils simply failed to pay attention. He reads the passage again, even louder, but the result is the same: blank stares and hesitant murmurs.

During the break, Tariq speaks with his cooperating teacher, Sir Ayaz. "They simply cannot listen in English," Tariq remarks with frustration. "I spoke clearly, but they retained nothing." Sir Ayaz smiles gently and offers an important correction. "Listening to a second language is not a passive sponge that absorbs speech automatically," Sir Ayaz explains. "Your pupils heard your voice, but hearing sounds is not the same as listening for meaning. You did not prepare their minds before reading, you gave them no specific focus while you read, and you asked a detail question without helping them grasp the main idea first."

## Explanation

Listening is the fundamental receptive language skill through which learners acquire linguistic input. In first-language development, children listen to meaningful spoken language for years before they learn to speak fluently or read print. In English as a second language (ESL) or foreign language (EFL) classrooms across Pakistan, listening instruction is frequently neglected. Teachers often speak English only to give administrative orders or conduct round-robin reading from the textbook.

A widespread misconception among teachers is that "listening is learned automatically and does not need deliberate teaching." Many educators assume that if pupils sit quietly while English is spoken, comprehension will happen naturally. This assumption is false. Second-language listening is an active, demanding cognitive process. A listener must recognise unfamiliar sound patterns, segment continuous streams of speech into separate words, interpret grammatical structures, extract meaning, and connect spoken messages to personal background knowledge. Without deliberate scaffolding and structured guidance, pupils become overwhelmed by spoken English and switch off their attention.

### Techniques of developing listening ability

To build genuine listening comprehension, teachers must use targeted instructional techniques that support second-language learners. Cook (1991) emphasizes that comprehensible input is essential for language acquisition; learners understand spoken messages when language is accompanied by concrete visual and physical support.

1. **Total Physical Response (TPR):** Developed by James Asher, TPR links spoken commands with physical actions. For young learners in Grades 1 to 3, the teacher gives direct spoken instructions in English: "Stand up," "Touch your desk," "Point to the window," "Pick up your blue pencil." Initially, the teacher performs the action while speaking. Next, pupils perform the action together with the teacher. Finally, pupils carry out the command independently without visual imitation. TPR bypasses the need for translation into Sindhi or Urdu and establishes direct neural associations between English phrases and physical actions.

2. **Listen and Draw:** The teacher gives simple, step-by-step descriptive instructions while pupils draw on paper or slate boards. For example: "Draw a small house in the centre. Draw a tall neem tree to the right of the house. Draw two birds in the sky above the roof." This technique provides immediate diagnostic feedback: the teacher can walk around the classroom and see instantly whether pupils understood prepositions of place and descriptive nouns.

3. **Listen and Identify / Point:** Pupils listen to short descriptive sentences while looking at pictures on the chalkboard or in their textbooks. When the teacher says, "The girl is carrying a red umbrella," pupils point to the corresponding picture.

4. **Storytelling with Realia and Puppets:** Teachers narrate short, vivid stories using real objects (realia) such as clay pots, coins, or leaves, accompanied by simple hand puppets. Gestures, vocal modulation, and facial expressions provide contextual scaffolding that enables young learners to infer the meaning of unfamiliar English vocabulary.

### Careful listening habits

Careful listening does not happen by accident; it must be cultivated through consistent classroom norms. In many Pakistani classrooms, especially in crowded urban centers or multi-grade rural schools, ambient noise from traffic, ceiling fans, or adjacent classes creates significant acoustic distraction. Teachers must actively train pupils to practice mindful, attentive listening habits.

- **Establishing Listening Posture and Readiness:** Train pupils with a clear visual signal (such as raising a hand or tapping a small chime) to stop writing, place pens down, turn their bodies toward the speaker, and maintain gentle eye contact.
- **Listening for Specific Acoustic Cues:** Before speaking, alert pupils to transition words and vocal emphasis. Explain that phrases such as "most importantly," "first," "however," and "in conclusion" signal key ideas.
- **Non-Interruptive Attention:** Teach children to listen to a complete sentence or thought without jumping in or raising their hands prematurely. Encourage the habit of processing the full message before formulating a response.
- **Predictive Listening:** Encourage pupils to look at titles, illustrations, or context clues before listening to anticipate what the speaker might discuss. This activates mental schemas and prepares working memory to process incoming vocabulary.

### Use of the cassette player (audio recordings) for developing listening ability

Historically, curriculum guidelines in Pakistan specified the cassette player as the primary audio aid for English classrooms. While magnetic tape cassettes have been largely superseded by modern digital tools, the pedagogical principle remains vital: exposing learners to recorded audio voices beyond their classroom teacher.

Today, primary and elementary teachers utilize recorded audio played through mobile phones, Bluetooth speakers, USB audio players, or computer tablets. Celce-Murcia (1991) points out that relying exclusively on the teacher's voice limits learners' listening competence. In real life, English is spoken by diverse individuals with differing pitch, speech rates, accents, and vocal characteristics.

Audio recordings offer distinct pedagogical advantages:

- **Standardized Acoustic Models:** Recordings provide reliable examples of natural English rhythm, word stress, sentence stress, and clear intonation contours.
- **Repeatability Without Distortion:** A live teacher often unintentionally changes their phrasing, speed, or tone when repeating a story for the third time. An audio track can be replayed identically, allowing pupils to verify specific details on subsequent listenings.
- **Authentic Speech Cadence:** High-quality recordings expose pupils to natural conversational pacing, including pauses, linking of sounds, and conversational contractions (such as "I'll" or "don't").

To use audio recordings effectively in a Grade 4 or 5 classroom, keep audio segments short (under 90 seconds). Always check the volume and acoustic clarity before the lesson begins, and ensure that ambient classroom noise is minimized during playback.

### Using video cassettes (video) for effective listening

Just as cassette players provided audio models, video cassettes represented the early form of multi-sensory instructional technology. In contemporary schools, video is delivered through digital smartboards, mobile devices, classroom projectors, or educational television broadcasts.

Video provides powerful multi-modal support that significantly enhances listening comprehension for second-language learners. Daulay (2025) demonstrates in recent empirical research that audio-visual media enhances EFL listening effectiveness by providing paralinguistic and contextual scaffolding that purely acoustic media lacks.

When pupils watch an English video clip, comprehension is supported by several visual channels:

- **Facial Expressions and Mouth Articulation:** Seeing a speaker's mouth shape helps learners distinguish subtle English phonemes (such as /f/ and /v/ or /w/ and /v/).
- **Kinesics and Gestures:** Body language, hand gestures, and physical reactions reveal the emotional tone and pragmatic intent of characters (e.g. excitement, hesitation, or disagreement).
- **Environmental Context:** The physical background (a market, a bus terminal, a hospital, or a farm) immediately anchors the vocabulary within a recognisable cultural setting.

Effective video listening requires active viewing strategies. Teachers should not treat educational video as passive entertainment. Useful techniques include:
- *Silent Viewing:* Play a 30-second video clip without sound; ask pupils to predict what the characters are discussing based on their actions and setting, then play the clip with sound to verify predictions.
- *Freeze-Frame:* Pause the video at a critical moment and ask pupils to predict what the character will say next.
- *Sound Only:* Play the audio of a video clip while the screen is covered, asking pupils to deduce where the scene takes place from acoustic cues (footsteps, bell ringing, animal sounds) before revealing the image.

### Methods of teaching listening

The universally recognized standard for conducting an effective listening lesson is the **Three-Stage Framework**: Pre-Listening, While-Listening, and Post-Listening. Each stage serves a distinct pedagogical function.

```
+------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                      THREE-STAGE LISTENING LESSON                      |
+------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 1. Pre-Listening:  Activate schema, teach key words, set purpose       |
| 2. While-Listening: Focus attention, complete structured task (gist)    |
| 3. Post-Listening: Check meaning, analyze language, extend to speaking |
+------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

#### 1. Pre-Listening (Orientation and Purpose)
The objective of pre-listening is to activate pupils' prior knowledge (schema activation), remove blocking vocabulary barriers, and establish an explicit reason for listening.
- *Actions:* Show a picture related to the topic, ask two or three guiding questions in simple English, pre-teach two or three essential vocabulary items that pupils cannot deduce from context, and state the listening task clearly before playing the audio.
- *Rule:* Never play audio without first giving pupils a clear question to answer. If pupils do not know why they are listening, their cognitive attention scatters.

#### 2. While-Listening (Guided Comprehension)
The while-listening phase guides pupils through the text from global understanding to specific analysis. Pupils should listen to the passage two or three times, with a distinct task for each listening.
- *First Listening (Global / Gist):* Pupils listen for the general topic or main idea. The task must be simple and non-intrusive (e.g. "Which city did Sana visit?" or "Put these three pictures in order").
- *Second Listening (Specific Details):* Pupils listen carefully to extract specific facts, numbers, names, or events (e.g. filling in a missing word on a worksheet, ticking items on a shopping list, or true/false checks).
- *Third Listening (Inference / Language Features):* For older primary pupils, a third listening examines speaker attitudes, feelings, or how specific phrases were used.

#### 3. Post-Listening (Reflection and Extension)
Post-listening moves beyond the audio text, allowing pupils to respond to the content, examine language features, and integrate listening with speaking or writing.
- *Actions:* Pupils compare their while-listening answers in pairs before whole-class confirmation. They discuss open-ended response questions ("Would you like to live in that village? Why?"), conduct a mini-role play based on the dialogue, or write two sentences summarizing what they heard.

#### Worked Example: A Complete Three-Stage Listening Lesson for Grade 4
To see how this framework functions in practice, study this worked example designed for a rural Sindh school setting:

- **Target Passage (60 words):** "Yesterday morning, Bilal rode his bicycle to the village canal. The sun was hot and bright. Under a large banyan tree, he saw his classmate Aslam fishing with a wooden rod. Aslam caught three small silver fish in a metal bucket. Bilal shared two sweet mangoes with Aslam before they cycled home together."
- **Pre-Listening Stage (4 minutes):**
  - Teacher shows a sketch of a banyan tree and a boy with a fishing rod on the chalkboard.
  - Teacher asks: "Have you ever seen anyone fishing in the canal?" Pupils give short answers.
  - Teacher pre-teaches the word *rod* by showing a wooden stick.
  - Teacher sets the purpose: "Listen carefully to find out who Bilal met under the tree."
- **While-Listening Stage (6 minutes):**
  - *First playback:* Pupils listen and answer the gist question: "Who did Bilal meet?" (Answer: Aslam).
  - *Second playback task:* Teacher gives pupils a mini-worksheet with three multiple-choice statements:
    1. Where was Aslam sitting? (Under a neem tree / Under a banyan tree / On the bridge)
    2. How many fish were in the bucket? (Two / Three / Four)
    3. What did Bilal share? (Mangoes / Guavas / Bread)
  - Pupils tick their answers during and immediately after listening.
- **Post-Listening Stage (5 minutes):**
  - Pupils turn to their bench partner and compare their choices.
  - Teacher confirms answers with the whole class.
  - Pair speaking extension: "Tell your partner about your favourite fruit or outdoor game."

### Listening sub-skills

Skilled listeners deploy different cognitive sub-skills depending on their communicative purpose. In the classroom, teachers must design tasks that deliberately practice these distinct sub-skills rather than testing memory in a generalized way.

1. **Listening for Gist (Global Comprehension):** The listener aims to grasp the overall message or main idea of the text without worrying about every individual word. In the real world, we listen for gist when listening to the news to know what major event occurred. In the classroom, a gist task asks: "Is the speaker happy or sad?" or "Match the speaker to the correct job."
2. **Listening for Specific Detail:** The listener ignores general descriptions and concentrates on locating precise pieces of information, such as names, dates, times, prices, or locations. For example, at an airport or railway station, travelers filter out ambient announcements until their flight or train number is called. Classroom tasks include filling in a bus schedule, noting a telephone number, or writing down the price of a ticket.
3. **Listening for Inference:** The listener reads between the lines, interpreting clues about emotion, attitude, social relationships, or unspoken intentions. If a speaker sighs and says, "Well, the weather could certainly be better," the listener infers disappointment even though the word "sad" was never spoken. In Grade 5, teachers can ask: "How does the father feel when he sees the broken window? What words tell you that?"
4. **Following Spoken Instructions:** The listener processes a sequential set of steps to complete a physical task, assemble an item, solve a puzzle, or follow directions along a map. The cognitive requirement is strict procedural sequencing.

## Activity: Three-stage listening lesson design

**Work in pairs.** Time allocation: approximately 20 minutes.

Review the following short spoken announcement (read by a train conductor):
*"Attention passengers on Platform 2. The morning express train from Karachi to Sukkur is delayed by twenty minutes due to track maintenance near Kotri. The train will arrive at 8:40 AM instead of 8:20 AM. Passengers for Rohri and Hyderabad should remain on Platform 2."*

1. Formulate one pre-listening schema-activation question and one pre-listening vocabulary focus.
2. Design one while-listening task focusing on **listening for specific detail** (such as a gap-fill table with train name, arrival time, and platform).
3. Design one while-listening task focusing on **listening for gist**.
4. Create one collaborative post-listening pair activity where pupils use the information to role-play a passenger phoning a relative.
5. Exchange your lesson plan with another pair. Evaluate whether the while-listening tasks are simple enough to be completed without interfering with the listening process itself.

## Summary

Listening comprehension is an active, meaning-making receptive skill that requires explicit instructional scaffolding. Second-language listeners do not absorb speech automatically; they must be guided to segment spoken English, recognise phonological patterns, and deduce meaning. Effective lessons follow a three-stage sequence: pre-listening activates prior knowledge and sets a clear purpose; while-listening structures attention from gist to specific details; post-listening consolidates meaning and connects listening to oral or written expression. Utilizing recorded audio and video exposes pupils to authentic pronunciation, natural speech cadence, and vital paralinguistic cues.

## Summative task

Develop a detailed 15-minute listening lesson plan for Grade 4 based on an audio passage describing "A Market Day in Hala". The plan must include:
1. Target grade level and specific listening sub-skills addressed.
2. Pre-listening phase: Schema-activation prompt, two pre-taught vocabulary words, and the explicit purpose statement.
3. While-listening phase: First listening task (gist) and second listening task (specific detail worksheet).
4. Post-listening phase: A collaborative pair-speaking extension activity.
5. An analytic rubric for assessing pupil performance on the while-listening tasks.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1 mark) | Proficient (2 marks) | Exemplary (3-4 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lesson phase alignment** | Stages are blended together with unclear boundaries and missing purpose statements. | Three distinct stages are present with appropriate time allocations and clear tasks. | All three stages are meticulously scaffolded; pre-listening directly primes working memory for while-listening success. |
| **Sub-skill appropriateness** | Tasks test general memory rather than specific listening sub-skills; requires heavy writing during listening. | Distinguishes between gist and detail; tasks require minimal writing during audio playback. | Tasks perfectly isolate gist and detail sub-skills; includes visual or tick-box formatting that minimizes cognitive interference while listening. |
| **Diagnostic analysis** *(Analyze)* | Gives generic praise or criticism without identifying root causes of learner comprehension difficulties. | Identifies common listening hurdles in ESL classrooms with plausible solutions. | **Analyze:** Critically evaluates how acoustic, linguistic, and affective barriers impact second-language listeners, justifying specific scaffolding choices to support struggling pupils. |
