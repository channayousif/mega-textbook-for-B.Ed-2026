## A real classroom situation

Rasheed is observing an English reading lesson in Grade 4 at a government boys' primary school in Sukkur. The teacher, Master Ghulam, calls on pupils to stand up one by one and read a passage about the Indus River aloud from the textbook.

A pupil named Jameel stands up. He reads slowly and loudly: "The... In-dus... Ri-ver... is... the... lon-gest... ri-ver... in... Pa-kis-tan." Jameel's voice is strained; his finger traces every syllable on the printed page. Whenever he encounters a longer word like "irrigation," Master Ghulam interrupts to correct his pronunciation. The other thirty-five boys in the class sit passively. Some stare out the window; others whisper quietly or count how many paragraphs remain before their turn.

After fifteen minutes of round-robin reading, Master Ghulam closes his book and asks the class: "Why is the Indus River important for agriculture in Sindh?" A heavy silence settles over the room. Jameel looks bewildered. Even though he read the exact sentence that contained the answer, he has no idea what the passage meant. He was channeling every ounce of his mental energy into pronouncing the sounds correctly, leaving no cognitive bandwidth to comprehend the message. Rasheed realizes that loud oral pronunciation does not equate to reading comprehension.

## Explanation

Reading is an active cognitive process of decoding written symbols and constructing meaning from written text. It is not merely the mechanical translation of graphemes (letters) into phonemes (speech sounds). In second-language classrooms, reading provides the richest source of comprehensible English input, exposing learners to new vocabulary, syntactical patterns, and cultural knowledge.

A pervasive misconception in Pakistani primary schools is that "loud reading in chorus shows that pupils understand the text." When a classroom of forty children recites a textbook chapter in unison, the loud volume creates an illusion of learning. In reality, choral loud reading merely practices vocal rhythm and choral mimicry. It actually hampers comprehension because it splits working memory between vocal tract motor control and semantic interpretation. Authentic reading comprehension happens when the mind interacts directly with text, usually through silent reading guided by purposeful comprehension questions.

### The importance of silent and loud reading

Both loud reading (oral reading) and silent reading have distinct, legitimate pedagogical functions in English language teaching, but they must not be confused or misapplied.

#### 1. Loud Reading (Reading Aloud)
Loud reading involves vocalizing written text with appropriate pronunciation, phrasing, stress, and intonation.
- **Primary Purpose:** Developing phonological awareness, checking sound-symbol correspondences, practicing speech rhythm, and assessing oral decoding accuracy.
- **Appropriate Classroom Use:** Reading aloud is valuable when the teacher reads aloud as an expressive acoustic model, or when the teacher listens to an individual child read a short excerpt during one-on-one diagnostic assessment. Short, prepared oral readings (such as performing a character's lines in a play) also build oral confidence.
- **Pedagogical Limitations:** In whole-class round-robin loud reading, only one child is actively engaged while the rest of the class is bored or anxious. Furthermore, the reader's cognitive capacity is consumed by pronunciation anxiety, leading to near-zero comprehension of content.

#### 2. Silent Reading
Silent reading is the internal processing of written text without vocalization or sub-vocal lip movement.
- **Primary Purpose:** Deep comprehension, information extraction, critical analysis, and developing reading speed. Silent reading mirrors real-world reading: outside of school, adults and children read books, newspapers, instructions, and messages silently.
- **Pedagogical Advantages:** Silent reading allows pupils to read at their individual natural pace. A reader can slow down over complex sentences, pause to reflect on unfamiliar vocabulary, re-read ambiguous phrases, and connect ideas across paragraphs. Cognitive resources are focused entirely on extracting and evaluating meaning.
- **Instructional Best Practice:** Always precede silent reading with clear guiding questions (signpost questions). When pupils read silently with a specific question in mind, their attention is purposeful and focused.

### Methods of teaching reading

Teaching children to read in English requires structured, evidence-based instructional methodologies. Historically, language teaching debates have contrasted bottom-up phonics approaches with top-down whole-word approaches. Contemporary reading pedagogy recognizes that balanced reading instruction integrates both decoding and meaning-making strategies. Hartati et al. (2024) document that effective EFL reading teachers employ varied strategies that support both bottom-up word recognition and top-down comprehension monitoring.

```
+------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                     METHODS OF TEACHING READING                        |
+------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 1. Phonic Method: Sound-letter blending (c-a-t -> cat)                 |
| 2. Look-and-Say: Whole-word visual recognition of sight words          |
| 3. Sentence Method: Whole meaningful sentences linked to experience    |
| 4. Interactive Approach: Integrating bottom-up decoding & schema       |
+------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

1. **The Phonic Method:** This method teaches the systematic relationships between letters (graphemes) and sounds (phonemes). Pupils learn the 44 basic phonemes of English and practice blending sounds to read words: /b/ - /æ/ - /t/ -> *bat*. Phonics equips children with a generative decoding mechanism: when they encounter an unfamiliar regular word, they can sound it out independently. It is particularly effective for regular monosyllabic words and early decoding in Grades 1 and 2.
2. **The Look-and-Say (Whole-Word) Method:** English contains numerous irregular words that defy standard phonetic rules (e.g. *one*, *two*, *said*, *was*, *through*, *daughter*). In the look-and-say method, words are taught as unanalyzed visual units using flashcards. Pupils see the word shape, associate it with an image or concept, and memorize it as a whole. High-frequency "sight words" (such as *the*, *is*, *they*, *have*) are taught through this approach so that children can recognize them instantaneously without halting to sound out each letter.
3. **The Sentence Method and Language Experience Approach:** This approach begins with whole meaningful sentences rather than isolated letters or words. The teacher displays a complete sentence connected to a vivid picture: "The cat sits on the mat." In the Language Experience Approach, the teacher writes down a sentence dictated by pupils based on a shared classroom experience (e.g. "We planted three sunflower seeds today"). Because the sentence expresses the children's own thoughts in familiar vocabulary, reading is immediately experienced as meaningful communication.
4. **The Interactive Reading Model:** Celce-Murcia (1991) explains that skilled reading is an interactive process: the reader simultaneously uses bottom-up processing (decoding letter patterns and syntax) and top-down processing (using background knowledge, context clues, and predictions). Balanced literacy instruction provides explicit phonics instruction for decoding while teaching comprehension strategies (predicting, questioning, summarizing) to extract deeper meaning.

### Reading sub-skills

Effective reading is not a monolithic activity; skilled readers flexibly adapt their reading style and speed depending on their reading purpose. Teachers must train primary pupils in four primary reading sub-skills:

1. **Skimming (Reading for Gist):** Skimming involves glancing rapidly over a text to obtain the main idea or general gist without reading every word. When skimming, the reader looks at the title, headings, opening and closing sentences of paragraphs, and accompanying illustrations.
   - *Classroom Task Example:* Give pupils 30 seconds to look over a short article about a solar energy plant in Quetta. Ask: "Is this text about farming, clean electricity, or cricket?"
2. **Scanning (Reading for Specific Information):** Scanning is searching quickly through a text with a specific visual target in mind (a number, date, name, or key term), while ignoring all unrelated material. The reader's eyes glide down the page looking for capital letters, digits, or distinctive word shapes.
   - *Classroom Task Example:* Display a bus timetable or an advertisement. Ask pupils: "What time does the morning bus leave Larkana?" Pupils scan directly for time digits (e.g. *07:30 AM*) without reading the names of all the intermediate stops.
3. **Intensive Reading (Reading for Detail):** Intensive reading is slow, careful, analytical reading of a short text to achieve complete comprehension of facts, causal arguments, vocabulary nuances, and text structure.
   - *Classroom Task Example:* Pupils read a paragraph on how honeybees pollinate crops, analyzing step-by-step how pollen travels from flower to flower and answering detailed cause-and-effect questions.
4. **Extensive Reading (Reading for Pleasure and Fluency):** Extensive reading involves reading longer, accessible texts (storybooks, graded readers, children's magazines) for enjoyment and general understanding. Texts should contain few unfamiliar words so that reading flows smoothly without stopping to consult a dictionary. Extensive reading builds reading stamina, implicit grammatical intuition, and vocabulary growth.
5. **Inferential Reading (Reading Between the Lines):** Inferring means drawing logical conclusions from clues in the text that are not explicitly stated by the author. If a story states that "Babar put on his heavy woollen coat, wrapped his muffler around his neck, and blew on his cold fingers," the reader infers that the season is winter, even though the word "winter" never appears.

#### Worked Example: Applying Reading Sub-Skills to One Text in Grade 4
To see how a teacher guides pupils through these sub-skills using a single short reading passage, examine this worked instructional example:

- **Passage (85 words):**
  *"The Thar Desert in southern Sindh is one of the most unique ecosystems in Pakistan. It covers thousands of square kilometres of golden sand dunes. In the summer, temperatures often exceed 45 degrees Celsius. However, when the monsoon rains fall between July and September, the desert undergoes a magical transformation. Green grass sprouts rapidly, acacia trees bloom with yellow flowers, and peacocks dance across the hills. Local farmers quickly sow millet and cluster beans in the moist soil before the hot sun dries the ground."*

- **Task 1: Skimming for Gist (Time limit: 30 seconds)**
  - *Prompt:* "Read the title and first two lines quickly. In your own words, what is this passage about?"
  - *Model Pupil Answer:* "It describes the Thar Desert and how rain changes it."
- **Task 2: Scanning for Specific Facts (Time limit: 45 seconds)**
  - *Prompt 1:* "Find the number: What temperature does the summer heat exceed?" (Answer: 45 degrees Celsius).
  - *Prompt 2:* "Find the months: During which months do the monsoon rains fall?" (Answer: July and September).
- **Task 3: Intensive Reading for Detail**
  - *Prompt:* "Which two crops do local farmers plant when the rains arrive?" (Answer: Millet and cluster beans).
- **Task 4: Reading for Inference**
  - *Prompt:* "Why do the farmers sow their seeds quickly instead of waiting a few weeks?"
  - *Model Pupil Answer:* "Because the passage says the hot sun dries the ground quickly, so the seeds need water in the moist soil to grow."

## Activity: Designing a reading sub-skills sequence

**Work in pairs.** Time allocation: approximately 20 minutes.

Review the following informational text (suitable for Grade 5):
*"Mohenjo-daro, located near Larkana in Sindh, was built around 2500 BCE. It was one of the largest settlements of the ancient Indus Valley Civilization. Archaeologists discovered that the city had straight paved streets, double-storey brick houses, and an advanced drainage system covered with clay tiles. At the center of the city stood the Great Bath, a large waterproof pool built with baked bricks and sealed with natural tar."*

1. Formulate one **skimming task** (with a 30-second time limit) to verify that pupils identify the overall topic.
2. Formulate two **scanning questions** that target specific dates or archaeological features.
3. Formulate one **intensive comprehension question** that requires pupils to explain how the city's builders managed sanitation.
4. Formulate one **inferential question** that asks pupils to deduce what the Great Bath tells us about the technical skills or social life of the people.
5. Exchange your task set with another pair and provide peer feedback on whether the questions strictly match their intended reading sub-skills.

## Summary

Reading is an active, meaning-making receptive skill that requires both accurate decoding and cognitive comprehension. Loud reading serves a valuable purpose in early phonological assessment and pronunciation modelling, but it should not be used as a proxy for comprehension. Silent reading allows learners to allocate full cognitive capacity to extracting meaning, pacing themselves, and re-reading challenging passages. Balanced literacy instruction combines phonics for decoding regular sound patterns with whole-word recognition for high-frequency irregular sight words. Developing targeted sub-skills - skimming for main ideas, scanning for specific details, intensive reading for deep relationships, and inferential reading - equips primary pupils to become versatile, independent readers.

## Summative task

Develop a 25-minute reading lesson plan for Grade 5 based on an informational passage about "The Mangroves of the Indus Delta." Your plan must include:
1. Target grade level and explicit student learning outcomes.
2. A pre-reading schema-activation activity and two pre-taught vocabulary words with contextual examples.
3. Guided while-reading tasks that systematically practice skimming, scanning, and intensive comprehension.
4. A post-reading pair discussion or graphic organizer task.
5. An analytic assessment rubric for evaluating pupil comprehension.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1 mark) | Proficient (2 marks) | Exemplary (3-4 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reading phase structure** | Lesson lacks pre-reading orientation or plunges immediately into testing comprehension without guided reading. | Features clear pre-, while-, and post-reading phases with appropriate time allocations. | Perfectly scaffolded lesson; pre-reading directly primes working memory, while-reading supports self-pacing, and post-reading synthesizes learning. |
| **Sub-skill differentiation** | Comprehension questions are generic recall checks that fail to distinguish skimming from scanning. | Explicitly includes distinct skimming (gist) and scanning (detail) tasks with sensible time limits. | Exemplary task design that guides pupils from global gist to rapid visual scanning, followed by deep inferential text analysis. |
| **Cognitive analysis** *(Analyze)* | Asserts that loud reading is sufficient for comprehension without providing theoretical justification. | Compares silent and loud reading, recognizing that silent reading supports deeper meaning-making. | **Analyze:** Critically examines the cognitive trade-offs between oral phonological decoding and silent semantic processing, justifying why structured silent reading is essential for second-language reading development. |
