## Unit summary

Projects use investigation to guide purposeful action or a product; inquiry uses a question and evidence to build an explanation. Both require teacher support, safe methods and opportunities for pupils to reflect. Their value depends on what learners understand and can justify, rather than the expense of materials or the appearance of the final display.

Systems thinking examines relationships that help produce recurring patterns. A causal loop is a proposed explanation to test, not proof. Futures thinking compares what seems likely, what could happen and what people would prefer under stated conditions. It helps a class justify a present choice while keeping uncertainty and differing needs visible.

Lesson planning connects an observable outcome with activities and evidence. A short sustainability lesson can teach mathematics or language as well as science. It needs realistic timing, accessible participation and a check of each learner's reasoning. Evidence from that check informs the next lesson. These connections matter more than fitting many activities or SDG labels into one period.

## Summative assessment

Attempt the questions before consulting the final guidance. All school records below are invented for assessment practice. The unit's default balance is 60% summative and 40% formative; this practice bank does not replace the course guide's mid-term, final-term, assignment/presentation and attendance marks. For RRQs, keep to the stated scope. For ERQs, use about 300-450 words or an equivalent labelled plan, and justify your choices.

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. Which feature most clearly distinguishes project-based learning from making a display after copying notes? *(Remember)*
   - A) Pupils decorate a product before displaying it.
   - B) Pupils investigate a question that guides a purposeful product.
   - C) Pupils complete the same product during a single period.
   - D) Pupils divide the colouring between several group members.

2. What is the immediate purpose of an inquiry-based task? *(Remember)*
   - A) To build an explanation using evidence about a question.
   - B) To complete a product chosen before asking a question.
   - C) To collect a wide range of facts without selecting a focus.
   - D) To demonstrate that the teacher's first prediction is reliable.

3. A teacher supplies comparable water-use records, models reading one row and asks groups to explain a pattern. Which description best fits? *(Apply)*
   - A) Unguided discovery, because pupils discuss a pattern.
   - B) A completed project, because a practical issue is named.
   - C) Guided inquiry, because pupils interpret evidence with support.
   - D) Copying practice, because the teacher provides the records.

4. A group counts fewer wrappers during a three-day trial of a reminder. Which conclusion is best supported? *(Apply)*
   - A) The reminder has changed pupils' habits for the full year.
   - B) The lower count proves that the reminder caused the change.
   - C) The group should replace observation with an opinion poll.
   - D) Counts were lower during the trial, but other causes need checking.

5. In a causal diagram, what does a negative link from workload to time for checks mean? *(Understand)*
   - A) More workload tends to leave less time for checks, other things equal.
   - B) More workload tends to create more time for checks, other things equal.
   - C) Workload is a morally worse issue than checking time.
   - D) Workload and checking time have no proposed relationship.

6. Which test identifies a reinforcing feedback loop? *(Understand)*
   - A) Count whether its diagram contains more than two arrows.
   - B) Check whether all its labels describe desirable outcomes.
   - C) Trace an initial change and see whether its effects strengthen that change.
   - D) Check whether all its factors fall within the school boundary.

7. A class describes a cleaner gate next year if adult collection becomes regular. What makes this a useful possible-future scenario? *(Apply)*
   - A) It makes cleanliness certain once the class prefers it.
   - B) It connects an alternative outcome to an explicit condition.
   - C) It treats the current observation as a complete prediction.
   - D) It replaces the need to compare different people's needs.

8. Which outcome is most directly observable in a 35-minute Grade 6 lesson? *(Understand)*
   - A) Pupils will appreciate sustainability in all daily decisions.
   - B) Pupils will become responsible citizens throughout adulthood.
   - C) Pupils will recognise the importance of every SDG equally.
   - D) Pupils will justify a paper-use choice using two recorded numbers.

9. A group reporter gives a strong explanation, but the teacher needs evidence about each pupil. Which check best fits? *(Apply)*
   - A) Each pupil gives a short evidence-based explanation, written or oral.
   - B) Each group redraws the reporter's poster with more careful lettering.
   - C) Each pupil copies the group's conclusion into the same notebook format.
   - D) Each group votes on which pupil contributed the most ideas.

10. A trainee has a mathematics lesson on paper use. Which addition makes its sustainability connection most meaningful? *(Apply)*
   - A) Adding green borders around every calculation on the worksheet.
   - B) Asking pupils to copy the names of all seventeen SDGs.
   - C) Asking pupils to justify reduced waste while protecting access to learning materials.
   - D) Giving the most decorative notebook the highest participation mark.

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

1. In three sentences, distinguish inquiry from a project and explain how an inquiry can support a project's action. *(Understand)*
2. A school cannot buy project materials. Give two feasible supports for a paper-use inquiry and explain each in one sentence. *(Apply)*
3. In three sentences, explain why an attractive group product does not establish that every member learned. Name one individual check. *(Analyze)*
4. A group observes a lower wrapper count on one day after moving a reminder sign. In four sentences, identify two alternative explanations and propose one fairer comparison. *(Analyze)*
5. In three sentences, explain a model boundary and give one outside influence that a school-gate waste model should acknowledge. *(Understand)*
6. Trace the following loop in four sentences: more uncollected waste increases clean-up work; more work reduces checking time; more checking time reduces uncollected waste. Explain why it reinforces an initial increase. *(Analyze)*
7. In three short statements, distinguish probable, possible and preferable one-year futures for classroom paper use. State a condition for one future. *(Apply)*
8. Rewrite "pupils will know about water saving" as one observable Grade 6 outcome. Give a matching individual check and one access adaptation in three sentences. *(Apply)*
9. A plan has five activities but no final learning check. In three sentences, analyze the weakness and propose one revision that protects its outcome within 35 minutes. *(Analyze)*
10. Week A used 50 new sheets and Week B used 40. In three sentences, interpret the difference, state one limit of the comparison and explain one meaningful sustainability connection for mathematics. *(Analyze)*

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. A Karachi class makes expensive models of a clean neighbourhood. Families buy materials, the teacher chooses every design, and no evidence is collected. Evaluate the task as project-based learning. Redesign it with a guiding question, safe inquiry, purposeful product, fair access, individual evidence and reflection. Justify the two most important changes. *(Evaluate)*
2. Develop a three-factor causal loop explaining a possible recurring problem with shared classroom resources. Trace the effects of an initial change, state the model boundary and identify a link needing evidence. Compare probable, possible and preferable one-year futures and defend an intervention, including one possible unwanted effect. *(Analyze)*
3. Create a 35-minute sustainability lesson for a Pakistani Grade 6 class in mathematics, language or another named subject. Include prior knowledge, an observable outcome, available materials, timed steps, a meaningful SDG link, accessible participation and an individual learning check. Explain how you will use the check to revise the next lesson. *(Create)*
4. Design a one-week school paper-use project that includes one short inquiry lesson. Use a systems relationship to explain why paper waste may recur. Compare two conditional futures, then justify a feasible, inclusive action and the evidence needed before recommending wider use. Show what can happen within the lesson and what needs later follow-up. *(Create)*
5. A head teacher asks for a single clean-up lesson that will "solve school waste permanently". Evaluate that claim using the distinction between projects and inquiry, a causal relationship and uncertainty about future conditions. Propose an achievable lesson outcome, an individual check and a modest follow-up. Defend how these choices improve learning without claiming a permanent solution. *(Evaluate)*

## Answers and marking guidance

This guidance is intentionally public for self-study. It is not the protected graded LMS bank. Accept alternative responses that meet the reasoning criteria and fit the question. A realistic limitation earns credit when it identifies what cannot be concluded, rather than dismissing all evidence. Treat the extended-task totals as practice marks; an instructor may select items and scale them for a specified assessment.

### MCQ answer key

1. B - investigation guides the purpose and product rather than merely decorating copied content.
2. A - inquiry's immediate purpose is an evidence-based explanation of a question.
3. C - the teacher scaffolds the method while pupils interpret evidence.
4. D - a short comparison supports a limited observation, not a certain causal or lasting claim.
5. A - a negative causal link proposes opposite directions of change, not a moral judgement.
6. C - a reinforcing loop returns effects that strengthen the initial direction of change.
7. B - the condition makes the alternative discussable and testable rather than certain.
8. D - a pupil's justification using numbers is observable within the lesson.
9. A - the same brief reasoning requirement can be checked for each pupil in accessible formats.
10. C - the decision connects resource use with fair access, rather than adding decoration or labels.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. Inquiry seeks a justified explanation from a question and evidence. A project connects investigation to a purposeful product or action with reflection. An inquiry into where wrappers gather can guide a project's proposal for bin placement. **4 marks:** 1 for inquiry; 1 for project; 2 for a coherent connection between evidence and action.

2. The teacher can provide a small invented data table on the board so no photocopies or internet are needed. She can model a tally using existing notebooks so groups use a common method. **4 marks:** 1 per feasible support and 1 per explanation of how it enables inquiry. Other existing materials or safe classroom records are acceptable.

3. One member may have produced the product or supplied its explanation. Appearance gives no reliable evidence of each pupil's interpretation. Ask each pupil to justify one choice using a recorded observation. **4 marks:** 1 for uneven participation; 1 for the limitation of the product; 2 for an individual check that measures understanding rather than copying.

4. Attendance may have been lower, or cleaning may have happened earlier. These could lower the observed count without a sign effect. Compare several similar days at the same place and time using a consistent method. Record relevant changes so the conclusion remains qualified. **4 marks:** 1 per alternative explanation; 1 for a comparable method; 1 for repeated observations or a relevant recorded condition. Credit weather or different snack sales when plausibly explained.

5. A boundary states what the model includes for a particular question. A class might focus on its gate during break. Municipal collection outside the school still affects how waste leaves that area. **3 marks:** 1 for the boundary's meaning; 1 for a specific scope; 1 for a relevant outside influence. A boundary must not be described as proof that outside factors do not matter.

6. Begin with more uncollected waste, which increases clean-up work. More work reduces time for checking. Less checking tends to increase uncollected waste because the link from checking to waste is negative. The effects strengthen the initial increase, making this a reinforcing loop under the stated assumptions. **4 marks:** 1 for each of the three correctly traced effects; 1 for explaining the return to the initial direction. A plausible challenge to an arrow is welcome but does not replace tracing the supplied model.

7. Probable: paper use stays similar if present tasks and routines continue. Possible: a scrap-paper tray reduces some new-sheet use if it is maintained and suitable paper is available. Preferable: pupils have enough paper to learn while avoidable waste declines. **4 marks:** 1 for each distinct lens and 1 for an explicit condition. The preferable future may also be possible; do not require mutually exclusive categories.

8. Outcome: given two classroom water-use records, each pupil uses one comparison to justify a feasible change and names one uncertainty. Check: a short individual explanation using the comparison. Adaptation: allow an oral explanation or read the records aloud while retaining the evidence requirement. **4 marks:** 2 for an observable outcome with evidence and reasoning; 1 for a matching check; 1 for access support that preserves the learning demand.

9. Completing five activities does not show whether pupils achieved the intended learning. Remove or shorten one activity whose purpose duplicates another and keep a brief individual exit response. Use that response to decide whether pupils need more modelling. **4 marks:** 2 for explaining the activity-versus-evidence weakness; 1 for a feasible timed revision; 1 for preserving the link to the outcome. Accept another justified revision without an exit slip.

10. Week B used ten fewer new sheets. The difference alone does not show that a paper-saving routine caused it, because attendance or tasks may differ. Pupils can use subtraction to compare resource use while asking whether everyone still had needed paper. **4 marks:** 1 for the accurate difference; 1 for a relevant limit; 2 for connecting mathematical evidence to resource use and fair access. Fewer sheets is not automatically better if learning needs were unmet.

### ERQ rubrics

For each criterion below, award **0** for absent, contradictory or irrelevant work, **1** for a relevant but incomplete account, and **2** for a clear, accurate, justified account that meets the stated full-credit descriptor. Each task totals **10 marks**. Naming a term without applying it to the case earns at most 1 for that criterion. These criteria reward reasoning rather than expensive materials or polished drawings.

1. **Evaluation of the model task (0-2):** identifies the missing investigation and the exclusion created by purchases, using details of the case. **Question and inquiry (0-2):** proposes a manageable question with safe, relevant evidence and teacher support. **Purposeful project (0-2):** links evidence to a product for an audience or feasible action with reflection. **Access and individual evidence (0-2):** provides available materials, equitable roles and a check of each pupil's reasoning. **Justification (0-2, Evaluate):** compares the two main changes against learning and inclusion, explaining why they matter more than cosmetic changes. A strong response might replace purchased models with a board-based waste investigation and a class proposal; this is an example, not a compulsory design.

2. **Causal model (0-2):** names three meaningful factors, a boundary and consistently directed links. **Tracing (0-2, Analyze):** follows an initial change around the loop and explains the resulting feedback rather than relying on its circular shape. **Evidence (0-2):** distinguishes a proposed causal claim from an observation and gives a suitable check. **Futures (0-2):** compares the three lenses with assumptions, allowing overlap. **Intervention (0-2):** defends a feasible action and a plausible unwanted effect using the model and affected people's needs. Do not require a reinforcing loop if the learner supplies and correctly explains another coherent feedback structure.

3. **Outcome and subject learning (0-2):** gives an observable Grade 6 outcome with suitable prior knowledge and a clear subject focus. **Sequence and resources (0-2):** steps total 35 minutes, use available materials and include sufficient guidance. **Sustainability and access (0-2):** the SDG connection explains a choice or effect and the plan removes a realistic participation barrier. **Assessment alignment (0-2):** each pupil produces evidence of the stated reasoning in a feasible format. **Evidence-based revision (0-2, Create/Analyze):** anticipates a specific difficulty and explains how actual responses would guide a justified next step. A list of interesting activities without a matching check cannot receive full credit for alignment.

4. **Project structure (0-2):** includes investigation, purposeful action or product, sharing and reflection across the week. **Inquiry lesson (0-2):** distinguishes a limited classroom question and evidence task from the wider project. **Systems explanation (0-2, Analyze):** links recurring waste to a defensible relationship and marks uncertainty. **Futures and decision (0-2):** compares two conditional alternatives and uses their likely effects to defend a choice. **Evaluation plan and equity (0-2):** proposes comparable evidence for later judgement, protects access and identifies what would trigger revision before wider adoption. Full credit requires a feasible follow-up rather than claiming that one lesson completes the project.

5. **Evaluation of the claim (0-2, Evaluate):** uses the head teacher's promise to distinguish a limited action from a permanent outcome that the evidence cannot establish. **Pedagogical distinction (0-2):** explains how inquiry informs understanding and how a longer project could support action. **Systems and futures (0-2):** supplies a plausible causal relationship and a condition that could change the result later. **Lesson outcome and check (0-2):** states an achievable outcome with matching evidence from each pupil. **Follow-up and defence (0-2):** proposes modest, safe later checking and justifies why the revised claim is useful while remaining honest about uncertainty. Credit reasoned disagreement with a proposed solution; do not require pupils to promise a particular behaviour.
