## A real classroom situation

In this teaching case, a Grade 6 teacher in Hyderabad asks groups to make posters about waste. The posters look attractive, but pupils cannot explain where the waste comes from. One pupil asks whether changing how snacks are sold would help more than another clean-up. The teacher has paper and a board, but no money for equipment. How can she turn this question into learning that uses evidence?

## Explanation

### Project-based learning

**Project-based learning** organises learning around an extended question and a purposeful product or action. Pupils investigate, make choices, produce something for an audience and reflect on what they learned. The product could be a proposal, an improved classroom routine or a short presentation. A model or poster can be part of a project, but making it is not enough. The learning lies in the questions, evidence and reasons that shape it.

UNESCO (2017, section 2.4) describes active, collaborative learning linked to reflection as useful for ESD. This supports designing projects around a real issue rather than using practical work as a reward after the lesson. A sustainability project should connect effects on people, resources and livelihoods. It should also allow pupils to question a proposed solution. A clean playground matters, but it does not prove that pupils understand why waste keeps returning.

Consider this worked project for the Hyderabad class. Its driving question is: "How could our class reduce disposable snack wrapping without making lunch harder for anyone?" The class first discusses what it needs to find out. Groups use teacher-prepared records or observe clean, visible wrappers from a safe distance. They do not touch mixed waste. They record the same place at the same time on three comparable school days. The teacher helps them distinguish a count of wrappers from a claim about every child's habits.

The teacher gives the following invented practice data: 18 wrappers on Monday, 20 on Tuesday and 16 on Wednesday, each after the same break. The total is 54 and the mean is 18 per break. The class chooses a small trial: invite pupils who already have a suitable container to use it, without requiring purchases. A group designs a reminder, another records counts and another asks whether the routine causes difficulty. Roles rotate so that drawing skill or confidence does not decide who learns mathematics or speaks to the class.

Suppose the three trial counts are 12, 14 and 13. The mean is 13, five fewer than before. Pupils can report a lower count during this short trial. They cannot yet claim that their reminder caused the change. Attendance, snack choices or cleaning times might have changed. They share both the result and its limits, then recommend another observation period. This is a stronger project than a poster announcing that the problem is solved.

A useful project sequence is question, plan, investigate, act, share and reflect. Planning identifies a manageable question, safe methods, roles and evidence of success. Investigation checks assumptions before action. Sharing makes thinking visible to an audience, perhaps the class teacher rather than a public event. Reflection asks what changed in the pupils' understanding and what they would revise. A class may return to planning when evidence challenges its first idea. These stages guide learning; they need not become six separate worksheets.

**Misconception: project work needs expensive materials.** Money can support a project, but it does not define one. A class can investigate paper use with existing exercise books, compare shaded and unshaded waiting areas through observation, or propose fair access to a shared resource. Teachers should avoid setting a project whose success depends on family purchases. Equal learning opportunities matter more than an impressive display.

Before starting, decide what each pupil should learn. For this project, an outcome might be: "Use two observations to justify a feasible change and identify one uncertainty." The teacher can collect each pupil's short explanation alongside the group product. A polished display can hide uneven participation. An individual explanation shows whether the quieter pupil understands the evidence, even if another pupil delivered the presentation.

The teacher still teaches. She models counting consistently, supplies a recording sheet, checks calculations and asks groups why their proposed action follows from the evidence. She may give a short explanation when pupils lack needed knowledge. Pupils have meaningful choices within a clear structure. For a crowded classroom, groups can share one board table and report orally, rather than needing a separate booklet for each child.

### Inquiry-based learning

**Inquiry-based learning** begins with a question that learners investigate using evidence. Its immediate aim is a justified explanation, though it may lead to a project or action. A useful question is limited enough to investigate and open enough that evidence matters. "Is waste bad?" invites agreement. "Which of these three observed places has the most visible snack wrapping after break?" tells pupils what evidence could help answer it.

<Figure id="fig-U4-2" src="/img/figures/efmp-407/unit-04/fig-U4-2.svg" alt="Comparison of projects and inquiry by starting question, immediate purpose, teacher support and typical time." caption="Original teaching schematic, textbook.com.pk." kind="table" />

**Misconception: inquiry means leaving pupils alone to find answers.** Guided inquiry includes teacher support. The teacher can supply the question, choose safe materials and model a method while leaving pupils to interpret the evidence. As pupils gain experience, they can make more decisions. Freedom without a usable question or method can produce confusion rather than independence.

Here is a worked inquiry using the same waste issue. The question is: "Does the doorway or the bench area have more visible wrappers after break in our practice record?" The teacher supplies counts for two equally sized observation areas over three days: doorway 8, 9, 7; bench 3, 5, 4. Pupils calculate totals of 24 and 12. They conclude that the doorway had more observed wrappers during these records. They do not conclude that children using the doorway care less about cleanliness. Wind, bin location or cleaning routines offer other explanations.

The teacher then models a claim, evidence and reasoning. The claim states the limited conclusion. The evidence names the counts and observation period. The reasoning explains why comparable counts support the comparison. Pupils also state a limitation: these observations do not tell them who dropped the wrappers or why. A limitation is not a reason to abandon evidence. It marks the boundary of what that evidence can support.

Inquiry can be short. In one lesson, groups can compare teacher-prepared records, identify a pattern and suggest what further observation would test their explanation. Projects usually take longer because planning an action, making a product and reviewing it require time. Neither method is automatically better. Choose inquiry when the immediate need is to understand a pattern. Choose a project when learners can investigate and use their learning in a purposeful product or action. Combine them when the project needs an inquiry to guide its next step.

For example, pupils might first investigate where wrappers accumulate. Their project could then propose a change to the position of a bin, subject to school permission. The inquiry supports the project's choice. A teacher should not promise that any practical activity improves sustainability simply because pupils enjoy it. Boeve-de Pauw and colleagues (2015) examine holistic content and pluralistic teaching, meaning teaching that takes more than one perspective seriously. Their Swedish school study provides reasons to examine how teaching works, rather than assuming a sustainability label guarantees learning. It does not test this invented Pakistani project.

Both approaches need fair discussion. A vendor may rely on inexpensive packaging, a pupil may lack a reusable container and a cleaner may face an unrealistic workload. Ask pupils to compare these needs before proposing action. Do not turn an investigation into blame or require children to disclose family spending. Teachers can provide fictional role cards instead of sending pupils to interview unfamiliar adults. Evidence-based learning can be participatory without exposing children to risk or shame.

## Activity: Repair the poster task

**Work in groups of four for about 20 minutes.** Without looking back, each member names one feature that separates a project from a craft exercise. Assign rotating roles: questioner, evidence planner, inclusion checker and reporter. Redesign the opening poster task around one question about school paper or snack wrapping. Agree on a safe source of evidence, a purposeful product and one individual learning check.

Exchange plans with another group. That group must identify one unsupported claim and one participation barrier. Revise the plan in response. Report which feedback changed your choice and why. Use existing paper and teacher-supplied records; no field visit or purchase is required.

## Check your understanding

1. Define project-based learning and name two features beyond making a product. *(Remember)*
2. Explain how a teacher can guide an inquiry while leaving pupils responsible for interpreting evidence. *(Understand)*
3. A class copies facts onto a waste poster without investigating anything. State two changes that would turn the task into an evidence-based project. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Projects connect investigation to purposeful action or a product. Inquiry develops an explanation from a question and evidence. Both need guidance, reflection and fair participation. Neither expensive materials nor an attractive display proves that learning occurred.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can distinguish a project from a practical task with no investigation.
- [ ] I can explain the support pupils need during inquiry.
- [ ] I can use observations to justify a conclusion and state its limit.

## Try this at your practicum school

With your cooperating teacher, examine one existing classroom task. Write a possible inquiry question and name evidence already available in the classroom. Note one support a pupil would need. Discuss the proposal without collecting personal data or changing school routines.

## Summative task

Write a 250-word redesign of a school waste poster task. Include a question, guided investigation, product or action, individual check and reflection. Analyze one reason why the evidence might not support the group's first proposal. Explain how your design includes a pupil unable to buy materials.
