## A real classroom situation

In this teaching case, a trainee in a government school in Larkana has 35 minutes to teach Grade 6 mathematics. She wants pupils to think about paper use, but her first plan includes a survey, a waste walk, a debate and a poster. Her mentor asks what each pupil should be able to explain when the bell rings. The trainee realises that a busy lesson could end without evidence of learning. She must choose a smaller task and make every part serve it.

## Explanation

### Developing sustainability lesson plans

<Glossary term="Lesson planning" /> means deciding the intended learning, activities, materials and timing. A **lesson plan** states the intended learning and how the teacher will help pupils achieve and demonstrate it within a particular time. A sustainability lesson needs subject learning as well as a meaningful sustainability connection. UNESCO (2017, section 2.4) argues for methods suited to learners, context and resources. Its learning objectives are suggestions to adapt, not a script requiring every activity in every lesson.

Begin by identifying what pupils already know. In this example they can read a small table and compare whole numbers, but have not yet practised qualifying a claim. The class includes pupils who need help reading English and one who finds moving between groups difficult. These details affect materials, grouping and evidence. Writing "group work" does not by itself make a lesson accessible or collaborative.

Then state one observable learning outcome. Avoid "pupils will understand sustainability", which does not tell the teacher what to observe. A usable outcome is: "Given two short records of paper use, each pupil will use two numbers to justify a feasible paper-saving choice and name one limitation of the comparison." This outcome joins mathematical comparison to a reasoned sustainability decision. It can be checked in a short individual response.

Plan that evidence before choosing the main activity. At the end, each pupil will complete: "I suggest ... because the records show ... and ... . We still need to check ... ." A pupil may write, dictate to the teacher or point to the numbers and explain orally. The response must contain the same reasoning in each format. Providing another way to respond changes the barrier to participation, not the intended learning.

Now choose a safe, small evidence set. The teacher prepares the following invented records for two five-day weeks in the same classroom. Week A used 60 new sheets for the task and 20 of those had a usable blank side when discarded. Week B used 45 new sheets and 5 had a usable blank side when discarded. In Week B the class also used a tray of safe, clean scrap paper for rough work. These are practice data, not measured results from Larkana.

Work through the mathematics first. Week B used 15 fewer new sheets. The count of discarded sheets with a blank side was also 15 lower. These are different measures; do not add them and claim a saving of 30 sheets. The same sheet may contribute to both records. The teacher models how choosing a measure carefully prevents double counting. At Grade 6, a correct subtraction is useful, but interpreting what the numbers represent is the deeper teaching move.

Then work through the decision. The records support trying a clean scrap-paper tray for rough work. They do not prove that the tray alone caused the reduction. Attendance, task type or the number of lessons may have differed. Ask whether fewer new sheets could also mean pupils were denied paper they needed. A good decision reduces avoidable waste while protecting learning. This connects environmental care, limited classroom spending and fair access.

The causal relationship can stay simple. Using a clean blank side for suitable rough work may reduce demand for new sheets. If the tray becomes disorganised, pupils may spend longer finding paper and stop using it. A possible future is a workable shared tray if someone maintains it and all pupils can reach it. A preferable future includes both reduced waste and enough suitable paper for learning. This short link draws on Topic 4.2 without turning a 35-minute lesson into a full systems project.

**Misconception: a sustainability lesson must be taught in science.** This lesson teaches mathematics through counts, comparison and evidence. A language lesson could examine the reasons in a proposal, and social studies could compare responsibilities for a shared resource. Keep the subject outcome visible. Adding a green title to a worksheet does not create sustainability learning if pupils never consider effects, choices or fairness.

Here is the filled 35-minute plan. The teacher writes the outcome and the three-part exit response on the board before class. Materials are one board table, clean sample paper and pupils' existing notebooks. No internet, printing, purchase or waste handling is needed. The clean samples contain no names or private information.

| Time | Teaching and learning | Evidence and support |
|---|---|---|
| 0-4 minutes | Show one clean sheet with a blank side. Ask when reusing it is suitable and when new paper is needed. | Hear two contrasting reasons. Accept brief answers in familiar languages before restating key terms. |
| 4-9 minutes | Model the comparison of the two weekly records and distinguish the two measures. | Ask one pupil to explain why subtracting gives 15 but adding both differences would mislead. |
| 9-21 minutes | Groups compare the records, propose a choice and challenge one another's reasons. | Assign reader, calculator, challenger and reporter; allow seated roles and rotate who explains. |
| 21-28 minutes | Groups share two different proposals. Discuss evidence, fairness, one systems link and a conditional future. | Ask whose learning needs might be missed. Check that a claim does not become a certainty. |
| 28-33 minutes | Each pupil completes the exit response using two numbers and a limitation. | Use written or brief oral responses with the same criteria; do not let one group response stand for everyone. |
| 33-35 minutes | Restate the learning and note one question for a later check. | Sort responses after the lesson to decide whether to reteach interpretation or move on. |

The time adds to 35 minutes: 4 + 5 + 12 + 7 + 5 + 2. The group task has time for thinking, discussion and disagreement, rather than many rapid activities. A full project cannot be finished in this period. This lesson can supply an inquiry within a longer project, such as improving paper-use routines across a week. The distinction protects the lesson from promising more than its time allows.

The link to the SDGs should explain learning rather than decorate the board. UNESCO's (2017) SDG 12 examples connect responsible consumption with education, and target 4.7 includes learning for sustainable development. In this plan, the link is pupils using evidence to propose responsible paper use while considering access. Pupils do not need to memorise a target number to demonstrate that reasoning. Neither a short exit response nor reduced sheet use alone proves a lasting change in values or behaviour.

Plan how to respond to likely difficulties. If a pupil says "Week B saved 30 sheets", return to the meaning of the two counts. If another says "use no new paper", ask what happens when a task needs a clean full sheet. If a confident speaker supplies every reason, ask each member to point to a number and explain it. These planned responses help the teacher use mistakes as information rather than merely complete the schedule.

An assessment criterion describes what counts as success. For the exit response use three: a feasible proposal, an accurate interpretation using two numbers, and a sensible limitation. Give feedback that names the next move, such as "Your subtraction is accurate; explain what was counted before drawing your conclusion." Praise for neat writing does not tell a learner whether the reasoning works. Keep the check short enough to read or hear within the actual classroom conditions.

Reflection closes the planning cycle. After teaching, compare the intended outcome with the responses. Perhaps most pupils subtract correctly but cannot name a limitation. The next lesson should model the difference between an observed change and its possible cause. Do not decide that pupils are careless or that group work failed on the basis of a single task. Record what they showed, which support helped and one justified revision.

A plan also needs a fallback. If the board is hard to see, read the small data set aloud twice and ask pupils to repeat the quantities before calculating. If time is lost, shorten group reports and retain the individual check. If pupils lack the subtraction skill, model it with tally marks and reduce the number of comparisons. The core reasoning can remain, even as the representation changes. This is adaptation guided by the outcome rather than improvising unrelated extra work.

## Activity: Audit the 35 minutes

**Work in pairs for about 20 minutes.** One partner explains the worked plan from memory; the other checks the outcome, evidence and time total. Change the issue to classroom water use or shared-book care. Write one observable outcome and a matching individual check before choosing an activity. Use teacher-supplied observations rather than collecting new data.

Exchange plans with another pair. They must identify one activity that does not help the outcome and one access barrier. Revise the sequence, show that it totals 35 minutes and explain which change matters most for learning. Keep a record of the feedback rather than presenting the first draft as finished.

## Check your understanding

1. State the purpose of a lesson plan and name its three central parts: intended learning, learning activity and evidence. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why a sustainability slogan and a group poster may fail to show whether each pupil met the learning outcome. *(Understand)*
3. A 35-minute plan runs short of time. Suggest one change that protects individual evidence and explain why it serves the stated outcome. *(Apply)*

## Summary

A lesson plan joins observable learning, supported activity and evidence within realistic time. Sustainability can be taught through mathematics, language and other subjects when the connection is meaningful. Individual checks and post-lesson reflection show what needs to change in the next lesson.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can state an observable sustainability learning outcome within a school subject.
- [ ] I can align an inclusive activity and individual check with that outcome.
- [ ] I can justify a revision using evidence of pupils' learning.

## Try this at your practicum school

Discuss one existing lesson plan with your mentor. Identify its outcome and the evidence each pupil could produce. Propose one feasible adaptation for a learner facing a reading, movement or materials barrier. Write a short reason; do not implement a change without the teacher's agreement.

Create a 35-minute Grade 6 sustainability lesson for a Pakistani classroom. Analyze one likely difficulty and explain how evidence from the check would guide your next lesson.
