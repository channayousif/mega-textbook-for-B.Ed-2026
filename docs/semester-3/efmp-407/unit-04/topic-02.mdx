## A real classroom situation

In this teaching case, pupils in Sukkur propose cleaning wrappers from the school gate each Friday. Their teacher asks why the wrappers return by Monday. One group blames careless children; another points to snack packaging and an overflowing bin. A third predicts that one clean-up will keep the gate clean for five years. The class needs a way to explain relationships now and discuss different futures without pretending to know what will happen.

## Explanation

### Systems thinking in the classroom

**Systems thinking** means studying how parts interact to produce a pattern. A system is a set of connected parts considered together for a purpose. In a school waste inquiry, parts might include packaging, purchasing routines, bin capacity, cleaning and decisions about spending. A list of these parts is a start. Thinking in systems adds explanations of how one part affects another, what feeds back and what lies outside the class's current view.

UNESCO (2017, Box 1.1) presents systems thinking as understanding relationships and complexity, including uncertainty. In the classroom, this means asking "How might this change affect something else?" before calling it a solution. Pupils do not need a large technical model. A few clearly named factors and justified arrows can expose a weak explanation better than a page of disconnected causes.

Start with a clear **boundary**, the limit of what the class includes in its model. For one lesson, use the gate area during school breaks, not the whole city's waste system. Name what is outside the boundary but still relevant, such as municipal collection and the packaging available to vendors. A boundary makes work manageable. It does not mean that outside influences disappear.

Next distinguish an event from a pattern. "The bin overflowed today" is an event. "The bin overflows after several busy breaks" describes a repeated pattern to investigate. A systems question asks what routines and relationships might produce that pattern. It does not assume that one person caused it. The aim is explanation that can be checked, rather than replacing one blame story with another.

Here is a worked, deliberately simplified causal loop. A **causal link** states how a change in one factor could change another. Imagine that more uncollected plastic increases clean-up work. If staff time is fixed, more clean-up work leaves less time for checking bins and arranging timely collection. Less timely collection can increase uncollected plastic. The effects return to the first factor, so this is a feedback loop.

Write the chain with precise variable names:

- Uncollected plastic increases clean-up workload: a positive link.
- Clean-up workload reduces time for routine bin checks: a negative link.
- Time for routine bin checks reduces uncollected plastic: a negative link.

A positive link means the two quantities move in the same direction, other things being equal. A negative link means they move in opposite directions. Positive does not mean morally good, and negative does not mean bad. Two negative links in this loop combine with the positive link to reinforce an initial change. More plastic can lead back to still more plastic; a reduction could also work through the same relationships to support a further reduction.

Call this a **reinforcing loop**, a cycle whose effects strengthen the starting change. Trace every arrow before naming it. Starting with more uncollected plastic, predict more workload, then less time for checks, then more uncollected plastic. The final direction matches the start. If you simply label a circular drawing "reinforcing" without following its effects, the label teaches little. The figure is a proposed explanation, not evidence that this exact loop operates at every school.

A possible intervention is an agreed class reminder to report a nearly full bin to the teacher before it overflows. Adults remain responsible for handling waste and collection. The reminder could protect time for routine checks, weakening the build-up. But a reminder cannot create collection capacity where none exists. Pupils should ask what adults can actually change and whose workload the suggestion adds. A low-cost intervention is still a demand on somebody's time.

To test the proposed relationships, use safe observations and staff explanations gathered by the teacher. Does overflow follow missed collection? Does workload actually reduce checking time? Does the gate receive wind-blown waste from elsewhere? These questions may change the model. Arrows describe claims to investigate, not established facts merely because they appear in a diagram. Label an uncertain arrow as uncertain and name what would help check it.

A teacher can also demonstrate a **delay**, a gap between an action and a visible effect. A new reporting routine may take time to become familiar. One unchanged observation on the first day does not prove failure. Conversely, one unusually clean day does not prove success. Choose a suitable observation period and compare similar times. Systems thinking helps pupils resist immediate verdicts and look for explanations that fit a longer pattern.

Boeve-de Pauw and colleagues (2015) examine teaching that connects sustainability dimensions and allows different perspectives. Apply that lens to the model: plastic affects the environment, collection uses labour and money, and access to affordable snacks matters to families. A proposal that hides waste beyond the gate changes its location rather than solving the wider issue. Ask who benefits, who does the work and where the material goes next.

### Futures thinking

**Futures thinking** explores different ways a situation could develop and what present choices might contribute to them. It deals with uncertainty explicitly. UNESCO (2017, Box 1.1) includes understanding and evaluating multiple futures within anticipatory competence, meaning the ability to consider what may come next and act thoughtfully. This is useful when a class must decide before every fact is known.

**Misconception: futures thinking is fortune-telling.** A classroom scenario is a reasoned account of what could happen under stated conditions. It is not a prophecy. Lim and Allan (2016) discuss scenarios in university legal education as a way to work with interacting issues and uncertain futures. The school task below adapts that general method. Their study does not establish that these particular Grade 6 activities produce a measured outcome in Pakistan.

<Figure id="fig-U4-4" src="/img/figures/efmp-407/unit-04/fig-U4-4.svg" alt="Three lenses compare a probable future of recurring litter, possible reliable collection and a preferable future with less waste and fair access, each with a question." caption="Original teaching schematic, textbook.com.pk." kind="table" />

Use three questions about the gate area one year from now. A **probable future** is what seems likely if stated current conditions continue. A **possible future** is an alternative that could happen under different conditions. A **preferable future** is one people judge worth working toward, with reasons. These categories overlap: a preferable future may also be possible and become more probable if conditions change. They are lenses for discussion, not three sealed boxes.

In the worked example, suppose the class's practice record shows repeated overflow and no change in collection. A probable scenario is recurring litter near the gate, assuming those conditions continue. A possible scenario is a regular adult collection arrangement that reduces overflow, assuming the school can secure it. A preferable scenario combines less disposable wrapping, reliable collection and fair access to food. It is preferable because it considers cleanliness, affordability and workload together. None of these descriptions guarantees what will happen.

A useful scenario states a time horizon, assumptions, likely consequences and uncertainties. "Everything will be clean" lacks these. "In one year, the gate has fewer visible wrappers if adult collection stays regular and fewer disposable wrappers enter; costs and participation still need checking" is a clearer conditional account. Ask pupils which part is an observation, which is an assumption and which expresses a value. This separates what is happening from what the group hopes will happen.

People may disagree about a preferable future. A pupil may value convenience, a cleaner manageable work and a vendor affordable packaging. Discuss the reasons without treating all factual claims as equally supported. A proposal that requires every family to buy a container may look clean in a drawing while excluding pupils. Compare alternatives and state whose needs still require attention. Futures thinking includes ethical judgement as well as imagination.

Finally work backward from the preferable future. If reliable collection is necessary, what small first step could a class propose now? It might ask the teacher to check the existing routine before planning another campaign. Name a sign of progress, such as fewer overflowing-bin observations across comparable days. Name a reason to revise the plan, such as repeated missed collection. The future remains uncertain, but the next action becomes more purposeful and open to correction.

## Activity: Trace, challenge and revise

**Work in groups of three for about 20 minutes.** From memory, sketch the waste loop and explain the direction of each link. One member traces the loop, one challenges an assumption and one checks whose needs are missing. Rotate roles after five minutes. Choose one safe intervention and predict an intended effect and a possible unwanted effect.

Now write three one-year scenarios for the same issue. Exchange them with another group, which identifies a hidden assumption or an excluded person. Revise one scenario and one arrow. Explain what evidence could make you revise them again. No observation outside the classroom is required.

## Check your understanding

1. Define a reinforcing loop and explain what a negative causal link means. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why a circular diagram is not enough to establish that a feedback loop exists. *(Understand)*
3. A pupil predicts that one clean-up will keep the gate clean next year. Rewrite the prediction as a conditional scenario with one assumption and one uncertainty. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Systems thinking traces relationships that can maintain a pattern, while checking assumptions and boundaries. Futures thinking compares conditional alternatives and the reasons for preferring one. Both approaches help pupils connect present choices to wider effects without making certainty or blame the starting point.

## Try this at your practicum school

Choose a familiar classroom routine with your cooperating teacher, such as distributing shared books. Sketch three connected factors and ask which link is least certain. Write a possible future if the routine changes, plus one condition it needs. Use ordinary classroom information without recording pupil names.

## Summative task

Produce a one-page analysis of recurring paper waste in a classroom. Include a three-factor causal loop, explain whether it reinforces change, and identify one link needing evidence. Compare three one-term futures and justify a preferred action using both the model and the needs of people affected.
