# Topic 7.2 - Curriculum reform and teacher education for sustainability

## A real classroom situation

In this fictional case, a B.Ed group in Sukkur rewrites a lesson on household waste. Their first version adds a paragraph about recycling to the textbook reading. Their mentor asks how pupils will compare choices when collection is irregular and some families cannot buy separate bins. The trainees realise that changing content alone has not prepared them to teach a reasoned decision. They need to change the task, its assessment and their own preparation together.

## Explanation

### Curriculum reforms

A **curriculum** is the planned set of learning aims, content, experiences and assessment. A textbook is one resource within it. <Glossary term="Curriculum reform" /> means revising that plan to improve what learners experience and learn. A new book cover or an added chapter may be part of reform, but it is not evidence that teaching and assessment have changed.

For sustainability, start with what pupils should understand and be able to judge. Then choose a task that makes that understanding visible. In the Sukkur case, a suitable outcome is: pupils compare two waste-reduction choices using safety, fairness and resource use. Copying a definition of recycling does not show that outcome. Comparing a reusable container with a disposable wrapper in a supplied scenario gives pupils a decision to explain.

The National Education Policy (2009) provides historical entry points for such teaching through its curriculum discussion. It does not supply this particular task. The example below is an author-designed application for trainee practice. Before teaching it, check the class's applicable subject outcomes, available time, language needs and school safety arrangements with the mentor. Do not claim that the example is a required provincial lesson.

#### Align outcome, task and evidence

**Alignment** means that the learning aim, teaching task and assessment ask for the same kind of learning. If the aim says "compare", the task must give pupils alternatives and useful criteria. If the assessment asks only for a definition, it cannot show whether comparison has been learned. There is nothing wrong with recalling vocabulary, but recall alone does not fulfil a more demanding aim.

Consider two lesson versions. In Version A, the teacher explains recycling and pupils copy five benefits. In Version B, pupils receive two fictional school snack routines. One uses individually wrapped portions; another uses containers that need cleaning. Groups compare waste, water for cleaning, cost and safe handling before proposing a change. Version B creates space for reasoning about linked consequences. It still needs accurate information and a teacher who challenges weak explanations.

Neither option is automatically best in every setting. A reusable container is not helpful if safe cleaning is unavailable. Pupils should state assumptions rather than repeat a preferred slogan. The teacher can supply simple facts for the scenario so that household income or private family habits do not become a public classroom test. Sustainability learning should not shame a child for the packaging their family can afford.

#### Worked example: revise a waste lesson

First, the trainees select an existing subject outcome involving comparison or explanation. Second, they translate it into a manageable lesson aim: "Give two reasons for a safer way to reduce snack waste." Third, they prepare picture cards representing two choices. The class uses fictional information about cost, cleaning and waste; no pupils sort real refuse.

Fourth, the trainees model a comparison aloud: "This option produces less packaging, but it needs clean water. I would check the cleaning arrangement before recommending it." The model shows both a reason and a condition. Fifth, groups make their own judgement. Sixth, each pupil writes or explains one choice and the evidence behind it. A pupil who needs language support may point to the cards and give an oral explanation.

The trainees assess three things: the pupil identifies a consequence, connects it to a criterion, and acknowledges a condition or trade-off. A **trade-off** is a choice in which improving one feature may make another harder to achieve. For example, a cleaning routine may reduce waste while adding work. The question becomes how to reduce that burden fairly, rather than pretending it does not exist.

Finally, the trainees review the explanations with their mentor. If pupils mention only colour or attractiveness, the task needs clearer modelling of relevant criteria. If they reason well orally but write little, language or writing demands may be hiding understanding. Reform requires this feedback. Replacing a task without checking its effects simply substitutes one assumption for another.

### Teacher education initiatives

**Teacher education** is planned learning that prepares or supports teachers. **Pre-service** education happens before entering the profession; **in-service** learning supports practising teachers. An **initiative** is an organised effort to improve some part of this preparation or support. Distinguish an actual documented programme from an improvement you propose. This unit does not claim that every example below is already operating across Pakistan.

Hopkins and McKeown's UNESCO guidance (2005) addresses reorienting teacher education through changes within institutions and links with communities and wider systems. It supports treating sustainability as more than an isolated classroom topic. Its international recommendations are not proof that a particular Pakistani college adopted them. Jumani and Abbasi (2015) raise the need for deliberate preparation in Pakistan; their abstract's proposed research design does not establish current programme coverage.

A common misconception is that teacher education does not need to change for ESD: teachers can simply explain the new chapter. Yet the Sukkur task asks the teacher to support comparison, handle uncertainty and judge evidence. A teacher who has practised only factual explanation may need modelling and supported rehearsal. More content knowledge helps, but practice is needed to use that knowledge in a discussion where several choices have strengths and weaknesses.

#### A supported preparation cycle

A practical initiative for a B.Ed group could combine four steps: plan, rehearse, observe and revise. This is a proposed local design. In planning, the group selects an outcome and anticipates a misconception. In rehearsal, one trainee teaches ten minutes while peers play pupils who offer different reasons. In observation, peers record what the teacher asks and whose ideas receive attention. In revision, the trainee changes one part of the task and tries again.

For example, a peer acting as a pupil says, "Recycling solves every waste problem." The trainee asks what can be reduced before anything becomes waste, and what conditions recycling needs. Another peer records whether the question invites explanation or merely signals the teacher's preferred answer. The trainee then revises the prompt so pupils compare prevention and later treatment without treating either as a magic solution.

In-service support can use the same logic with a mentor and an existing lesson. A short workshop may introduce an approach, but attendance shows participation rather than teaching competence. A lesson plan, observed rehearsal and subsequent revision provide different evidence. These are assessment choices for the proposed support cycle, not a claim about a national training standard.

#### Resources, inclusion and partnerships

A useful initiative needs time, materials, feedback and a responsible person. A college might propose a mentor meeting around one pupil task rather than a large event with no follow-up. A school-community partnership could invite an adult to explain local collection arrangements, with school permission. The teacher should prepare questions and treat the adult's account as one source to examine. Community participation does not remove the need for safe practice and critical thought.

Use accessible materials: large picture cards, oral explanations, paired discussion and familiar languages where appropriate. Assign roles so that quiet pupils and pupils with disabilities have a meaningful contribution. Do not equate fluent English with good sustainability reasoning. Ask whose participation the task makes easy and whose it makes difficult. This connects social inclusion with the environmental issue rather than adding it as an afterthought.

The route from policy to classroom is not a conveyor belt. Curriculum aims influence textbooks and teacher preparation. Teachers also learn from pupil work and report problems with materials. A lesson can expose an unrealistic assumption in a task, such as reliable collection or available cleaning water. Recording that issue gives a mentor or materials team something specific to improve.

#### Judge an initiative by its purpose

Suppose the group holds a rehearsal and all trainees say they feel confident. Confidence matters, but it does not show that their questions improved. Compare a sample of questions before and after feedback. Look for requests for reasons, evidence and conditions. Then check whether pupils or peers actually use those prompts to explain choices. A stronger claim needs stronger evidence and a clearly stated scope.

Set a feasible improvement rather than promising to transform an entire system. A trainee can revise one lesson with a mentor this week. A college leader may decide whether rehearsal time can enter a course. A curriculum team may consider changes to materials. Name the actor for each action. Teacher education connects these levels, while each level has responsibilities that a trainee alone cannot discharge.

## Activity: Rehearse the reform

Work in pairs for about 25 minutes. Recall the three dimensions without looking back. Choose a familiar fictional waste or water scenario and agree on one comparison outcome. One partner teaches a five-minute worked example; the other offers two plausible pupil responses and records the teacher's follow-up questions. Swap roles. Together revise the task and its assessment so both test the same outcome. Show another pair one change and explain the evidence that led to it.

## Check your understanding

1. Define curriculum reform and name one thing it includes beyond replacing a textbook. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why workshop attendance does not establish improved teaching. *(Understand)*
3. An outcome asks pupils to compare water-saving choices, but the test asks them to copy a definition. Propose one assessment change and explain its link to the outcome. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Curriculum reform links aims with tasks, materials and assessment. Teacher education must support the knowledge and teaching moves those tasks require. A feasible local initiative uses rehearsal and feedback, and distinguishes participation from evidence of better teaching or learning.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your mentor to select one suitable existing outcome. Propose a safe sustainability comparison task and rehearse it before teaching. If permission is granted, keep anonymous notes on pupil reasoning and revise one question afterwards. If teaching is unavailable, rehearse with peers and report the limits of that evidence. Do not collect household income information or handle real waste.

## Summative task

Design a one-page curriculum-to-classroom improvement plan for the Sukkur case. Include the outcome, revised task, individual assessment, teacher preparation and follow-up. Justify two design choices using the case constraints and explain one limit of your evidence.
