# Topic 7.1 - ESD in Pakistan's education policy

## A real classroom situation

In this fictional case, trainee Ayesha visits a government school in Hyderabad. A poster asks pupils to save water, but the tap beside the classroom leaks. Pupils copy the poster's slogan and receive marks for neat writing. Ayesha's mentor asks whether the lesson fulfils the school's sustainability aims. Ayesha realises that a policy statement, a visible activity and evidence of learning are three different things.

## Explanation

### National education policy and ESD integration

<Glossary term="National education policy" /> means a government's stated direction for education. It identifies aims and proposed action. <Glossary term="Policy implementation" /> means putting that direction into practice through decisions, resources, teaching and follow-up. A written promise is the starting point for investigation. It is not evidence that every pupil has received the promised learning opportunity.

The Government of Pakistan's *National Education Policy* (2009, section 6.1, policy actions) includes environmental learning within schooling. Its curriculum discussion also addresses rights and safety. These are historical entry points for discussing ESD. Do not rename the document a complete ESD programme or assume it tells us what every school teaches today. Read a dated policy for what it actually says, then investigate the relevant current curriculum and school arrangements separately.

**ESD integration** means connecting sustainability learning with the aims, content, teaching and assessment of education. Recall the three dimensions from earlier units: environmental care, social well-being and economic life. A lesson on school water use can connect limited water, fair access and repair costs. The environmental dimension gives the issue physical meaning. The social dimension asks whose needs count. The economic dimension asks what different choices cost and whether they can continue.

A common misconception is that ESD in Pakistan means only environmental studies. Environmental learning matters, but the lens is wider. In our water case, pupils might calculate water use yet overlook children who cannot reach the tap. Alternatively, they might debate fairness without understanding the water problem. Integration asks them to connect both. It does not require adding an unrelated sustainability slogan to every lesson.

#### Read a policy claim carefully

Use four questions when reading any policy extract. Who issued it? When was it issued? What action does it propose? What evidence would show that action reaching learners? Record the document title and section so another reader can check your interpretation. Distinguish the wording of the source from your proposed classroom application.

For example, an intention to include environmental learning may justify looking for relevant curriculum outcomes and materials. It does not prove that a specific school has a water-conservation lesson. The school may teach the idea through science, mathematics or another subject. You must look rather than guess. Nor does the national wording alone settle which office supplies repairs or which textbook is currently used in a particular Sindh school. Check with your mentor and the applicable local documents.

A timeline helps prevent a different error: placing later ideas inside earlier documents. UNESCO's teacher education guidance appeared in 2005; the policy considered here is dated 2009; Jumani and Abbasi's paper appeared in 2015. These are different kinds of source, written for different purposes. Their sequence does not demonstrate a single continuous implementation programme. A policy history needs evidence of links, not merely dates in order.

#### Follow intention into practice

For classroom inquiry, distinguish five levels. A **commitment** names an aim. A **planned provision** allocates learning outcomes, time or support. An **activity** is something people do. A **learning product** is pupil work that can be examined. A **learning outcome** is what pupils can understand or do. This is an author-designed reading aid, not a government monitoring framework.

In the Hyderabad case, the poster is a resource. Copying is an activity. The exercise books are products. To infer understanding, Ayesha needs a task that asks pupils to explain or apply the idea. Asking why a leaking tap matters and how to compare two responses gives stronger evidence than counting posters. Even then, one good pupil explanation does not prove a permanent change in school water use.

Jumani and Abbasi (2015) argue for deliberately including sustainability in teacher preparation. Their published abstract sets out a proposed investigation involving teacher educators and teacher education programmes. It should not be cited as a completed national survey or a measured success rate. It supports the question of how teachers are prepared, not a claim that every Pakistani training college has the same weakness.

#### Worked example: evaluating a gap

Here is Ayesha's initial claim: "The school has failed to implement sustainability policy because its tap leaks." The claim goes beyond her evidence. A leaking tap shows a maintenance problem. It does not tell her everything pupils have been taught or why the repair has not happened. She rewrites the claim as a question: "How well does this observed lesson connect water-saving knowledge with informed action?"

She records three pieces of case evidence: pupils copied a slogan; the observed task did not ask for reasons; the mentor reports that repairs require approval. She separates direct observation from the mentor's account. Her judgement is narrow: this lesson provided little evidence of pupils' reasoning about water use. She has not proved that the school never teaches such reasoning.

Ayesha proposes a supervised classroom task. Pupils use a supplied drawing of a leaking tap and compare two responses: request a repair, or place a reminder nearby. They explain which response addresses the leak and what each needs to work. No child handles plumbing or collects unsafe water. The mentor can forward a repair request through the school's normal process. Classroom inquiry and maintenance action have different owners.

Her follow-up has two checks. First, can pupils explain their choice using environmental and cost considerations? Second, has the request reached the responsible person? A delay in the second check should not erase improvement in the first. Equally, a repaired tap should not be counted as proof that all pupils understand sustainability. This distinction makes the evaluation useful rather than accusatory.

#### Judge fairly under limited evidence

A further misconception is that a policy written in Islamabad is automatically taught in every classroom. Between intention and learning lie materials, teacher understanding, language, available time and organisational decisions. Treat these as possible influences to investigate, not excuses you assign without evidence. The same missing activity could have several causes. A teacher may lack materials, may not see the curriculum connection or may have planned the topic for another week.

Compare explanations before recommending action. If the barrier is unclear learning aims, a new poster is unlikely to solve it. If the barrier is a dangerous water point, a pupil discussion alone cannot make it safe. Choose an action that matches the evidence and the actor's authority. A trainee can revise a lesson with a mentor. A trainee cannot promise a budget decision on the school's behalf.

Keep evaluation proportionate. State the class, task and observation period. Say what you did not observe. Use anonymous examples and allow the mentor to correct factual errors. A fair report can identify a weakness without blaming a teacher for circumstances that the report has not investigated. Its purpose is improvement supported by evidence.

## Activity: Build an evidence ladder

Work in groups of three for about 20 minutes. One person reads a short policy extract supplied by the tutor, one examines the fictional Hyderabad case and one challenges unsupported claims. Without looking back initially, recall the difference between intention, activity and learning. Make three columns: commitment, observed evidence, and what remains unknown. Place the poster, copied work and mentor's account in the appropriate places. Agree on one limited judgement and one follow-up question. Exchange sheets with another group and revise any claim that exceeds its evidence.

## Check your understanding

1. Define policy implementation in one sentence and distinguish it from a written commitment. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why a water lesson needs social and economic questions as well as environmental facts. *(Understand)*
3. Pupils attend a tree-planting event. Give one question that would reveal learning and one thing attendance alone cannot establish. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Policy gives direction, while implementation requires action and support. ESD connects environmental, social and economic concerns. Evaluate a specific classroom opportunity with suitable evidence, and distinguish an observed limit from an unsupported national claim.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can separate a policy's wording from my classroom interpretation.
- [ ] I can explain why environmental content alone may not show full ESD integration.
- [ ] I can make a bounded judgement and name evidence still needed.

## Try this at your practicum school

With permission, select one existing lesson about resources or community well-being. Note its stated aim, one observed task and an anonymous example of pupil reasoning. Ask the mentor what support the lesson needs. Write a short judgement limited to this evidence. If observation is unavailable, use the opening case and clearly label it fictional.

## Summative task

Write 300–400 words evaluating the gap between one stated sustainability intention and one classroom task. Use the fictional case or approved practicum evidence. Identify two possible explanations, judge which your evidence supports and propose a feasible action and follow-up check. Do not generalise to all schools.

Mini-rubric, 10 marks: distinguishes commitment and practice (0–2); analyses evidence and competing explanations (0–3, Analyze); justifies a proportionate judgement and acknowledges limits (0–3, Evaluate); assigns a feasible action and follow-up (0–2).
