# ESD in Pakistan - assessment and review

## Unit summary

A policy commitment gives education a direction. It does not by itself establish classroom implementation. When evaluating ESD, distinguish the policy text, the planned learning opportunity, the observed activity and evidence of pupil understanding. A poster can communicate a message; copying it may show writing practice; explaining a choice using evidence can reveal reasoning. These are different claims and need different evidence.

ESD connects environmental, social and economic concerns. In a water lesson, pupils can examine resource use, access and repair costs together. Historical policy entry points do not prove that every school has integrated those concerns. A fair evaluation states the document, class, observation period and limits of its evidence. It considers possible barriers without assigning blame on the basis of one visit.

Curriculum reform involves aims, content, teaching, materials and assessment. A comparison outcome needs a task with alternatives and an assessment that asks for reasons. Teacher education can prepare trainees to model such reasoning, support participation, question assumptions and learn from pupil work. Deliberate rehearsal and feedback offer evidence beyond workshop attendance. International guidance, a Pakistani research agenda and a proposed local initiative have different roles; none should be presented as proof of nationwide success.

## Summative assessment

Attempt the questions before reading the final guidance. MCQs 1–5 and RRQs 1–5 focus on Topic 7.1; MCQs 6–10 and RRQs 6–10 focus on Topic 7.2. ERQs 1–2 focus on policy, 3–4 on curriculum and teacher education, and 5 integrates both. Use original examples. All school scenarios below are fictional.

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. What does policy implementation mean? *(Remember)*
   - A) Publishing the final policy document.
   - B) Putting stated aims into supported practice.
   - C) Announcing a consultation about aims.
   - D) Listing recommended policy readings.

2. Which evidence best shows that a pupil can reason about saving water? *(Understand)*
   - A) A photograph of the pupil beside a poster.
   - B) A register recording attendance at an event.
   - C) A slogan copied neatly into an exercise book.
   - D) An explanation comparing two responses to a leak.

3. A lesson considers water use and repair costs but ignores access for pupils with disabilities. Which sustainability concern needs more attention? *(Apply)*
   - A) Social inclusion in the use of resources.
   - B) The physical quantity of water available.
   - C) The price of replacement materials.
   - D) The speed at which the leak is measured.

4. A trainee sees no sustainability discussion during one mathematics lesson. Which statement matches the evidence? *(Apply)*
   - A) The school has rejected national policy.
   - B) The teacher has received no ESD preparation.
   - C) That observed lesson provided no such discussion.
   - D) The curriculum contains no relevant outcomes.

5. What role can the National Education Policy (2009) play in this unit's inquiry? *(Understand)*
   - A) A record of current practice in all Sindh schools.
   - B) A historical statement whose classroom links need investigation.
   - C) A replacement for checking applicable class outcomes.
   - D) A report of the learning results of a particular lesson.

6. Which description best defines curriculum reform? *(Remember)*
   - A) Revising learning aims, experiences and assessment.
   - B) Reprinting the same tasks with a new cover.
   - C) Adding a slogan without changing lesson aims.
   - D) Replacing a book while retaining each old task.

7. Pupils should compare waste-reduction choices. Which assessment fits that aim? *(Apply)*
   - A) Copy the heading from a waste poster.
   - B) Spell three words from the textbook.
   - C) State the date of a school clean-up.
   - D) Defend a choice using two relevant criteria.

8. What distinguishes pre-service teacher education? *(Remember)*
   - A) It follows a teacher's first school appointment.
   - B) It consists of meetings for school leaders.
   - C) It prepares people before entry to teaching.
   - D) It assesses only experienced classroom mentors.

9. A mentor wants evidence that a trainee improved questioning after feedback. Which source is most useful? *(Apply)*
   - A) A comparison of questions in two recorded rehearsals.
   - B) A certificate showing attendance at a workshop.
   - C) A list of the books placed in the staffroom.
   - D) A photograph showing the training venue.

10. Which statement accurately uses UNESCO's 2005 guidance? *(Understand)*
   - A) It establishes how every Pakistani college now teaches ESD.
   - B) It supplies recommendations to consider in local teacher preparation.
   - C) It verifies the outcomes of a Sukkur trainee rehearsal.
   - D) It replaces the need for local outcome and resource checks.

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

1. In two sentences, distinguish a policy commitment from implementation. Give one school example. *(Understand)*

2. A school displays a water-saving poster. In three sentences, state what that establishes, what it does not establish and one further piece of evidence you need. *(Analyze)*

3. In three short points, explain how environmental, social and economic questions can fit the same school-water lesson. *(Understand)*

4. A trainee writes, "The policy failed because one teacher omitted this topic today." In two or three sentences, identify the unsupported inference and rewrite it as a bounded claim. *(Analyze)*

5. In three sentences, explain how you would use a dated policy extract responsibly when planning a Sindh classroom inquiry. Include one check beyond reading the extract. *(Apply)*

6. In two or three sentences, distinguish a textbook change from broader curriculum reform, using one waste-lesson example. *(Understand)*

7. An outcome requires comparison, but pupils are assessed only on definitions. Propose one revised question and explain its alignment in two sentences. *(Apply)*

8. Give three steps in a mentor-supported rehearsal cycle and explain, in one further sentence, how feedback changes the next attempt. *(Apply)*

9. A workshop report lists 40 participants and claims that their teaching improved. In three sentences, identify the evidence gap and propose a stronger check. *(Analyze)*

10. In three short points, identify one safety adaptation, one participation adaptation and one evidence check for a fictional waste-comparison task. *(Apply)*

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. A school has a conservation poster and one set of copied slogans. The trainee observed a single lesson. Evaluate the claim that the school has fully implemented ESD. Write 300–400 words distinguishing what the evidence establishes, alternative explanations and two further checks. *(Evaluate)*

2. A mentor says the leak outside a classroom is awaiting approval for repairs. Pupils can repeat a water-saving slogan but have not been asked to explain it. In 300–400 words, analyse the difference between a maintenance issue and a learning gap. Recommend actions with separate owners and justify a limited policy-to-practice judgement. *(Analyze)*

3. Design a revised lesson for a fictional Sukkur class comparing snack-waste options. In 300–400 words, include an existing-subject outcome to verify with the mentor, a worked example, a group task, an individual assessment and safety and access adaptations. Defend the alignment. *(Create)*

4. A college proposes one lecture on sustainability followed by certificates of attendance. Evaluate this as teacher preparation in 300–400 words. Design a feasible extension involving rehearsal, feedback and revision. Distinguish international recommendations from evidence of what the college achieves. *(Evaluate)*

5. A school wishes to connect a stated environmental-learning intention with fair access to drinking water. It can provide mentor time but cannot promise immediate repairs. Develop a 400–500-word improvement plan linking policy interpretation, curriculum, teacher education and classroom evidence. Compare two feasible actions, defend a choice and state what your evidence could not establish. *(Create)*

## Answers and marking guidance

These are public self-study suggestions. Reasoned alternatives can earn credit when they fit the question and evidence. Formal graded assessment uses the teacher's approved arrangements. Do not treat the examples as reports of real schools.

### MCQ answer key

1. B. Implementation puts aims into supported practice; publication and consultation alone do not demonstrate it.
2. D. An explanation of alternatives reveals reasoning more directly than attendance, copying or a photograph.
3. A. Access for pupils with disabilities raises a social-inclusion concern alongside water and cost.
4. C. The evidence covers one observed lesson, not the whole school, teacher preparation or curriculum.
5. B. A dated policy supports historical interpretation and a question for investigation, not a current national classroom claim.
6. A. Reform concerns the planned learning experience and assessment, beyond a new cover or slogan.
7. D. Defending a choice using criteria makes comparison visible.
8. C. Pre-service preparation precedes entry to the teaching profession.
9. A. Comparing questions before and after feedback examines the teaching move that was meant to improve.
10. B. International guidance can inform local planning without verifying local implementation.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

Each response carries four marks. Award marks for meaning rather than exact wording. Examples must fit the stated scope.

1. A commitment states an intended direction, such as environmental learning within schooling. Implementation involves arranging and supporting opportunities for pupils to learn, such as an aligned water-use lesson with suitable materials. Marks: intention distinguished (1); supported practice distinguished (1); relevant example (1); distinction made clear through the example (1).

2. The poster establishes the presence of a communication resource. It does not establish that pupils can explain water-saving choices or that water use changed. Ask pupils to compare two responses and inspect their reasons. Marks: limited evidence identified (1); unsupported learning inference identified (1); relevant further evidence (1); explanation of why it helps (1).

3. Environmental: what water is wasted or available? Social: can all pupils reach safe water fairly? Economic: what resources and ongoing costs would the response require? Marks: each relevant dimension (3, one each); clear connection to the same school-water decision (1).

4. The trainee moves from one omission to a judgement about the whole policy. A bounded claim is that no relevant discussion was observed in this lesson; checking the planned sequence and other evidence is needed before a wider judgement. Marks: inference identified (1); scope limit explained (1); bounded rewrite (1); suitable further check (1).

5. Record the policy's issuer, date and relevant section, and distinguish its actual wording from a proposed application. Check the applicable class outcome and discuss school arrangements with the mentor. Do not assume that a historical national commitment describes current local implementation. Marks: source identification (1); intention/application distinction (1); local check (1); avoids unsupported currency or implementation claim (1).

6. A textbook change alters one resource; curriculum reform can change aims, teaching experiences and assessment together. A waste lesson that moves from copied definitions to comparing options, with assessment of reasons, changes more than the book. Marks: resource distinguished (1); broader curriculum explained (1); meaningful example (1); link between task and assessment (1).

7. Ask, "Which of these two water-saving responses would you choose, and why, using access and cost?" It aligns because pupils compare alternatives with relevant criteria rather than reproduce a definition. Marks: alternatives present (1); reasons required (1); relevant criteria (1); alignment explained (1).

8. Plan an outcome and model question, rehearse with peers, and review observation notes with a mentor. Revise a question that elicited only slogans, then try it again to check whether reasons improve. Marks: three suitable steps (3, one each); feedback leads to a specific revision and renewed check (1).

9. Attendance counts participation, not changed teaching. Compare observed questioning or lesson plans before and after support, using the same criterion. State the sample and avoid extending a small observed improvement to all participants. Marks: evidence gap (1); suitable direct check (1); common comparison criterion (1); scope limit (1).

10. Use picture cards rather than handling refuse. Permit oral explanation and assign meaningful roles so writing fluency does not determine participation. Collect each pupil's reason for a choice to check individual understanding. Marks: safe adaptation (1); access adaptation (1); aligned individual evidence (1); all three fit the same task (1).

### ERQ rubrics

Each extended response carries 10 marks. For each criterion, zero means absent, irrelevant or contradicted by the case. In a two-mark criterion, one means partial and two means accurate and supported. In a three-mark criterion, one means a relevant assertion, two means an explained link to case evidence, and three means a sustained, justified treatment that addresses limits or alternatives. Length alone earns no marks.

1. Policy implementation judgement: distinctions among intention, resource, activity and learning (0–2); analysis of the poster, copied work and observation limits (0–3, Analyze); evaluation of the full-implementation claim against alternatives (0–3, Evaluate); two relevant further checks (0–2). A strong response rejects an unsupported comprehensive claim without replacing it with an equally unsupported claim of total failure. Suggested checks include pupil explanations and the planned lesson sequence, with a reason for selecting each.

2. Maintenance and learning: distinguishes the two issues and their evidence (0–2); analyses what the mentor's account and pupil responses show (0–3, Analyze); justifies feasible responses and their separate responsibilities (0–3, Evaluate); states a bounded judgement and follow-up (0–2). A strong response combines a mentor-approved reasoning task with a repair request through normal channels. It does not ask children to repair equipment or assume that a repair itself demonstrates learning.

3. Revised lesson: clear, locally verifiable outcome and coherent sequence (0–3, Create); explicit alignment of comparison task and individual assessment (0–3, Analyze); useful worked example with reasons and a condition (0–2); realistic safety and participation adaptations (0–2). A strong response compares consequences such as packaging, cleaning and cost. It protects pupils from unsafe waste handling and from being judged through private family purchasing choices.

4. Teacher preparation: distinguishes participation evidence from competence (0–2); evaluates the lecture proposal and its limits using the stated purpose (0–3, Evaluate); designs a feasible rehearsal, feedback and revision extension (0–3, Create); separates recommendations from verified local outcomes (0–2). A strong response identifies the teaching move to practise, who observes it, what evidence is collected and how the next attempt uses that evidence. A certificate alone is insufficient evidence of changed practice.

5. Integrated plan: accurately links policy interpretation, curriculum and preparation (0–3, Analyze); compares two actions and defends a feasible choice under the case constraints (0–3, Evaluate); uses aligned learning evidence and proportionate follow-up (0–2); states responsibilities, inclusion and limits (0–2). A strong response may pair a water-access comparison lesson with a documented request for maintenance, while separating the purposes and owners. It does not claim that one lesson or request establishes school-wide implementation, permanent behaviour change or national policy success.
