# Topic 8.2 - Working with the community and reporting results

## A real classroom situation

Tariq is an enthusiastic trainee teacher placed at a government boys' middle school on the outskirts of Larkana. Hoping to improve the school's barren grounds, Tariq planned a vegetable and herbal garden project with his Class 7 students. Because the school had no discretionary budget for seeds, gardening tools, or soil fertilizer, Tariq drafted a formal letter to send home to parents, asking each family to contribute 200 rupees.

When he showed the draft letter to his cooperating teacher, Mr. Memon, the older teacher smiled gently and advised him to pause. "Tariq," Mr. Memon explained, "many of our pupils come from families of day labourers and tenant farmers who struggle to buy wheat flour. If you ask them for cash, parents will feel burdened and defensive. Instead, come with me to tomorrow's School Management Committee meeting."

At the meeting, Tariq presented his garden idea directly to the assembled parents, the village elder, and the local vegetable grower who sat on the committee. The reaction was immediate. A parent who ran a small nursery offered 50 surplus tomato and chilli seedlings for free; another parent volunteered to deliver a cartload of cured cow manure; and an elder advised the pupils on how to plant seasonal gourds that flourish in the local soil. By shifting from asking for money to inviting partnership, Tariq unlocked community expertise and goodwill that money could never have bought.

## Explanation

### Community participation

#### Why schools and communities must collaborate

Environmental challenges do not respect the boundary wall of a school. A school cannot eliminate mosquito breeding if open sewage pools immediately outside its gate; a school cannot teach clean water practices if children return home to contaminated wells; and school trees will not survive summer breaks if neighbours do not care for them.

Wals et al. (2024) explain that sustainable development education is inherently relational. A school operates as an anchor institution within its neighbourhood. When schools invite community members into sustainability projects, two vital benefits emerge:
1. **Contextual ecological knowledge:** Local farmers, elders, and artisans possess generations of accumulated knowledge about local soil conditions, groundwater fluctuations, seasonal weather patterns, and native plant resilience that no generic textbook contains.
2. **Social ownership and sustained protection:** When community members help build a school facility - whether a rainwater harvesting tank, a compost bed, or a tree grove - they protect it from vandalism, animal grazing, and neglect during holidays.

#### The legal framework: School Management Committees (SMCs)

In Sindh, community participation in government schooling is not an informal favour; it is a statutory right and obligation. Under Section 16 of the *Sindh Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2013*, every government school is legally required to establish a **School Management Committee (SMC)**.

The SMC has a clearly mandated structure and set of functions:
- **Equal representation:** The committee consists of nine members headed by the school principal or head teacher, with equal representation drawn from teachers, parents of enrolled children, local government officials, and school management.
- **Regular governance:** The committee must convene at least three times each academic year.
- **Mandated responsibilities:** The SMC is legally tasked with monitoring the overall functioning of the school, overseeing the proper utilization of government development grants, ensuring pupil attendance, and preparing and recommending the formal **School Improvement Plan (SIP)**.

For student teachers, the SMC is the primary institutional gateway for sustainability projects. When a sustainability project is integrated into the school's annual School Improvement Plan, it gains official authority, community oversight, and access to approved school maintenance grants.

#### Degrees of participation: from audience to co-ownership

Community involvement exists along a spectrum (Tilbury & Wortman, 2004). Student teachers must recognize which level of participation their projects foster:

1. **Information (Passive audience):** The school simply informs community members of decisions already made. For example, parents are invited to sit in rows during an annual function, listen to speeches, and applaud student performances. The community has no voice, no decision-making power, and no ongoing responsibility.
2. **Consultation:** The school asks community members for their opinions through a survey or discussion, but school staff retain total control over whether those opinions are used.
3. **Collaboration and co-ownership (Active partnership):** Parents, local elders, and civic leaders work side by side with teachers and pupils from the beginning. They help identify local sustainability priorities, share physical and technical resources, make joint management decisions, and co-evaluate project results.

Frameworks for engaging communities in sustainability (Tilbury & Wortman, 2004) emphasize that lasting initiatives occur when participation progresses toward active collaboration. If community members feel that a project belongs to them rather than being an external imposition by the education department, they invest their time and care in maintaining it.

#### Correcting the misconception: "The community is an audience, not a partner"

A persistent misconception among novice teachers is viewing community members merely as an audience to be lectured or entertained. Teachers often think: "We are the educated professionals; our job is to teach the uneducated community how to save water and keep the village clean."

This patronizing mindset breeds resentment and passivity. Villagers and urban neighbours often understand local hydrological constraints, waste flows, and economic pressures far better than a newly posted teacher. When teachers position community members as equal partners, they acknowledge local wisdom. Instead of delivering a sermon on hygiene, a teacher invites parents to examine the school's water drainage alongside pupils. True partnership is dialogic: both school and community teach and learn from each other.

### Reporting project outcomes

#### Why reporting matters

Projects often fizzle out silently because nobody outside the classroom knows what was accomplished. **Reporting** is the vital bridge between action and sustained institutional habit. 

In a whole-school sustainability framework, reporting serves four distinct purposes in school practice:
1. **Transparency and accountability:** Showing parents, the SMC, and district officials that school resources and grants were used honestly and productively (Wals et al., 2024).
2. **Pedagogical validation:** Giving pupils authentic pride and public recognition for their hard work, reinforcing their civic identity as change-makers.
3. **Evidence-based improvement:** Analyzing collected data to identify what succeeded, what failed, and what adjustments are required in the next school term (Wals et al., 2024).
4. **Community mobilization:** Inspiring neighbouring schools, homes, and businesses to adopt similar sustainability practices in their own daily lives.

#### Correcting the misconception: "Reporting means writing a long document nobody reads"

Many teachers believe that reporting requires compiling a 40-page technical English document full of bureaucratic jargon that is filed in an office cupboard and forgotten. 

This misconception turns reporting into a tedious chore that excludes the very people who matter most. In reality, effective reporting is **purposeful, accessible, and multimodal**. A photograph display on a courtyard notice board, a five-minute student demonstration at morning assembly, or a lively bilingual discussion at an SMC meeting is far more powerful than an unread paper binder.

#### Tailoring reporting formats to diverse audiences

Different stakeholders need different types of information presented in formats they can readily digest:

1. **Visual campus notice boards (For pupils, teachers, and daily visitors):**
   - *Format:* A prominent wooden or cardboard bulletin board in the school veranda or entrance hall.
   - *Content:* Before-and-after photographs, colourful student-drawn charts showing weekly water savings, sapling growth curves, and the weekly pupil duty roster.
   - *Frequency:* Updated weekly by student project leaders.
2. **Morning assembly presentations (For the entire school body):**
   - *Format:* A 3 to 5-minute verbal presentation, student role-play skit, or awards ceremony during the daily morning gathering.
   - *Content:* Celebrating student achievements, highlighting practical conservation milestones (for example, "Our Class 8 greywater filter saved 1,000 litres of hand-pump water this month!"), and announcing upcoming tasks.
   - *Frequency:* Once a month or to celebrate project milestones.
3. **Concise written briefs (For the head teacher, SMC, and District Education officers):**
   - *Format:* A crisp, professional 1 or 2-page printed brief, written in plain language.
   - *Content:* A structured summary stating the original problem, key interventions completed, financial summary of grants used, quantitative indicators achieved, and three concrete recommendations for the School Improvement Plan.
   - *Frequency:* Submitted at the end of each term or project phase.
4. **Community meetings and exhibition days (For parents, local elders, and neighbours):**
   - *Format:* An interactive open day held in the school courtyard, conducted bilingually in Sindhi and Urdu.
   - *Content:* Guided student tours of the garden or water systems, physical displays of compost and seedlings, open question-and-answer sessions, and formal words of gratitude to community contributors.
   - *Frequency:* Once or twice per project cycle (a mid-way check-in and a celebratory project finale).

#### Worked example: Reporting a school tree-nursery project

Consider how a trainee teacher at a government high school in Badin reported on a native tree-nursery initiative:

Instead of writing a lengthy academic narrative, the teacher and pupils prepared two coordinated communications:
- For the **School Management Committee**, they prepared a single-page table showing that 150 neem and moringa seeds were sown, 128 saplings sprouted (85% success rate), zero grant money was spent (all seeds and milk bags were donated by families), and 50 saplings were ready for planting along the perimeter wall.
- For the **village elders and parents**, Class 8 pupils organized a 20-minute demonstration after Friday prayers. Two pupils explained in Sindhi how drip-irrigation bottles cut water needs by half, showed their healthy seedlings, and presented each visiting parent with one sapling to plant outside their home. 

The result was immediate: parents felt respected, pupils experienced public affirmation, and the community agreed to supply organic compost for the upcoming school vegetable beds.

## Activity: Developing a stakeholder reporting strategy

**Context:** Work in small groups of three or four student teachers. Allow about 25 minutes.

**Scenario:** Your school has completed a 10-week project titled "Clean Water and Safe Sanitation". Pupils identified that dirty washrooms and contaminated drinking water caused frequent stomach illnesses and pupil absenteeism. Through pupil monitoring, minor plumbing repairs by the SMC, and clean water storage practices, the school achieved zero leaking pipes and pupil illness absences dropped by 30%.

**Instructions:**
1. Your group must design a multi-format reporting strategy for this project.
2. Create an outline specifying what information you will share and what format you will use for three different audiences:
   - **Audience A:** Primary school children (Classes 1 to 5) who need to learn how to keep taps and cups clean.
   - **Audience B:** Parents and local community elders, many of whom cannot read written English or Urdu text.
   - **Audience C:** The District Education Officer (DEO) who has the authority to allocate future school maintenance funds.
3. Explain one concrete technique to ensure that Audience B (the community) has a genuine voice to ask questions and offer feedback, rather than merely listening silently.

## Summary

School sustainability projects cannot succeed in isolation from their social context. Following Wals et al. (2024) and Tilbury and Wortman (2004), genuine community participation transforms local families and neighbours from passive spectators into active co-investigators and guardians of school initiatives. In Sindh, the School Management Committee (SMC) provides a mandated legal framework under the 2013 Act to anchor sustainability within the official School Improvement Plan. Communicating project outcomes must not become a burden of unread bureaucratic paperwork; effective reporting is visual, dialogic, and tailored to specific audiences through notice boards, assemblies, concise executive briefs, and community gatherings.

## Try this at your practicum school

Investigate community links during your teaching practicum:
1. Speak with your cooperating teacher or school head to find out who the parent members of the School Management Committee (SMC) are and when they last met.
2. Examine the school's communication methods: how does the school share news or achievements with parents who cannot read written circulars?
3. Identify one local artisan, shopkeeper, or farmer in the immediate school neighbourhood and note two ways their knowledge or materials could support a science or sustainability lesson.

## Summative task

**Task:** Design a comprehensive Community Engagement and Reporting Strategy for a school-based sustainability project in Sindh (such as a plastic-free canteen drive, a school composting system, or a solar-powered study corner). 

Write a 400 to 500-word blueprint addressing:
1. **Stakeholder analysis:** Identify at least three community stakeholder groups (including the SMC), their local knowledge assets, and their specific collaborative roles in the project.
2. **Participation strategy:** Detail how you will move beyond passive one-way communication to foster active co-ownership and shared decision-making.
3. **Multimodal reporting plan:** Design a clear reporting schedule detailing the medium, language, content, and frequency for pupils, school administrators, and community parents.
