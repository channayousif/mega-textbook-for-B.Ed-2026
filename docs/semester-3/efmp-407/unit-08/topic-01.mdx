# Topic 8.1 - Planning and running a sustainability project

## A real classroom situation

Farida is completing her B.Ed teaching practicum at a government girls' high school in Badin district. To mark World Environment Day, she organized a tree-planting drive with her Class 8 pupils. The school bought 40 neem saplings. On Tuesday morning, the head teacher gave a speech, the local education officer planted the first tree, and the girls happily planted the remaining 39 saplings in the dusty school yard. Everyone took photographs, and Farida felt proud of a successful project.

Two weeks later, Farida walked across the yard. The summer sun was blazing, and cows had wandered through a broken section of the perimeter wall. Twenty-five of the saplings had dried up and withered into brittle sticks, and several others had been trampled. Nobody had watered them after Tuesday's ceremony, and no pupil or caretaker had been assigned daily responsibility. Farida realized with a sinking heart that planting saplings was only a single morning activity. It was not a complete sustainability project. This topic explains how to plan and run projects that actually last.

## Explanation

### Planning sustainability projects

#### Activities versus sustainability projects

Schools in Pakistan frequently hold one-off events: a campus cleaning morning, a poster competition on water conservation, or a tree-plantation photograph. While such events create initial enthusiasm, they rarely change institutional habits or improve local environments. 

A **school sustainability project** is fundamentally different from a standalone event. A project is a structured, collaborative investigation and intervention where pupils identify a genuine environmental or social challenge, design solutions, take collective action, monitor measurable indicators over time, and evaluate their long-term impact.

Wals et al. (2024) explain that sustainable schooling requires a **Whole School Approach** (WSA). In this approach, sustainability is not confined to textbook chapters or isolated speeches. Instead, learning connects four interrelated dimensions of school life:
1. **Curriculum and pedagogy:** Classroom lessons engage pupils in authentic inquiry and critical problem-solving.
2. **Campus operations:** Physical resources - including drinking water, electricity, vegetation, and solid waste - are managed responsibly.
3. **School ethos and culture:** Teachers, pupils, and staff build daily habits of care, equity, and shared responsibility.
4. **Community links:** The school works actively with families, local elders, and civic institutions.

When student teachers plan sustainability projects through a whole-school lens, the campus itself becomes a living laboratory. Pupils do not merely read about water scarcity; they audit the school's water taps, measure waste, and construct simple conservation systems.

#### The six stages of the project cycle

To ensure that projects succeed and endure, teachers guide pupils through a systematic six-stage cycle, elaborating the five project phases of Wals et al. (2024) by distinguishing public reporting from institutional reflection:

1. **Identify a need:** Rather than copying a generic idea from a textbook, pupils investigate their immediate school and local surroundings. They collect baseline evidence through observation walks, surveys, or interviews. For example, pupils might count how many litres of clean water are lost each day from leaking school taps, or calculate how much plastic wrapper waste is generated at the canteen.
2. **Plan the project:** Together with the teacher, pupils define clear, realistic objectives. They break the work down into specific tasks, assign roles to teams or pairs, create a realistic timeline, identify necessary materials, and decide on measurable indicators of success.
3. **Take action:** Pupils and teachers execute the planned tasks collaboratively. Because sustainability projects emphasize learner agency (Tilbury & Wortman, 2004), pupils take active leadership in digging beds, installing drip containers, repairing fixtures, or running peer awareness sessions.
4. **Monitor progress:** This critical stage tracks whether activities are functioning as planned. Pupils collect data weekly or daily using simple logbooks, charts, or photo journals. Monitoring catches problems early - such as pest attacks on saplings or broken pipes - before the project fails.
5. **Report outcomes:** Pupils share their evidence, progress, and challenges with the school community, parents, and local stakeholders through assemblies, notice boards, and short briefs.
6. **Reflect and improve:** The cycle concludes with critical reflection. Pupils and teachers evaluate what worked, what went wrong, and what institutional changes are needed to keep the benefits alive into the following school year.

Teacher education programmes in Pakistan increasingly emphasize bridging the gap between abstract policy rhetoric and practical school implementation. Equipping prospective teachers with concrete project-planning skills ensures that environmental education moves from memorization to tangible community transformation.

#### Worked example: Building a project plan matrix

Before launching an initiative, a teacher should construct an explicit planning matrix with the class. Consider how a Class 7 teacher in Sukkur designed a "School Greywater and Kitchen Garden Project":

- **Identified need:** Water from the school hand-pump washing station pooled into stagnant, mosquito-breeding mud puddles outside the boundary wall, while school grounds remained barren.
- **Project goal:** Channel wastewater safely through gravel filter trenches to irrigate a native vegetable and shade-tree plot.
- **Task breakdown:**
  1. Measure daily wastewater volume and dig a 10-metre shallow drainage trench filled with gravel and charcoal. *Responsible:* Senior pupil team and school caretaker. *Timeline:* Weeks 1 to 2. *Resources:* Shovels, local gravel, discarded charcoal. *Indicator:* No standing stagnant water around the hand-pump.
  2. Plant drought-tolerant native vegetables (spinach, gourd, okra) and neem saplings along the trench line. *Responsible:* Class 7 science groups. *Timeline:* Weeks 3 to 4. *Resources:* Native seeds from pupil homes, organic manure from local farms. *Indicator:* 40 healthy sprouted plants established.
  3. Form daily pupil maintenance pairs to clear debris and record plant growth in a wall chart. *Responsible:* Class duty roster pairs. *Timeline:* Weeks 5 to 12. *Resources:* Ruler, record notebook, watering cans. *Indicator:* 85% plant survival rate and daily log completed.

Notice how every task has a named person, a bounded time, low-cost local materials, and a clear verification indicator.

### Implementing sustainability projects

#### Managing practical challenges and low-cost resources

A frequent excuse for inaction in government schools is a lack of financial budget. However, effective sustainability projects thrive on low-cost, local resources. Successful projects use:
- Discarded plastic soda bottles punctured with small holes to create slow-drip root irrigation for young trees.
- Recycled plastic milk bags and discarded wooden crates as seedling nursery containers.
- Fallen leaves, vegetable peels from pupils' homes, and livestock dung from neighbouring plots to establish rich organic compost piles.
- Indigenous drought-resistant plants (such as neem, amaltas, keekar, and moringa) that require minimal water once rooted, rather than exotic decorative plants that demand constant irrigation.

Pupil safety and task suitability are paramount. Teachers should ensure that pupils are never exposed to hazardous chemicals, sharp scrap metal, or unhygienic sewage. Tasks must be divided equitably so that boys and girls share leadership, physical tasks, and monitoring roles equally.

#### Continuous monitoring versus post-event inspection

Monitoring is not a single examination at the end of the year; it is an ongoing habit of scientific observation and civic stewardship. Without ongoing monitoring, enthusiasm fades within days of the initial event.

Teachers must establish simple, verifiable indicators that pupils can record independently:
- **Resource indicators:** Litres of water saved, number of leaking taps repaired, kilograms of organic waste composted, or units of electricity reduced by turning off fans in empty rooms.
- **Ecological indicators:** Number of saplings surviving, height growth in centimetres, presence of birds or beneficial insects in the school garden.
- **Social and behavioural indicators:** Number of pupils actively participating in care rosters, cleanliness of classroom bins, and attendance at project briefings.

#### Correcting the misconception: "A project is finished when the activity is done"

The most damaging misconception in school sustainability work is believing that once an activity is finished - when saplings are placed in the ground, trash is gathered in bags, or posters are hung - the project is complete.

This thinking treats the physical event as the destination. In reality, the physical event is merely Stage 3 (Take action) of a six-stage cycle. If teachers and pupils do not monitor growth (Stage 4), report progress to the wider community (Stage 5), and embed maintenance into the ongoing school routine (Stage 6), the saplings die, the cleaned area fills with litter again, and pupils learn a cynical lesson: that sustainability is just an empty performance for guests and cameras. True sustainability requires continuous care, systematic reflection, and long-term stewardship.

## Activity: Designing a school water-conservation plan

**Context:** Work in small groups of three or four student teachers. Allow about 25 minutes.

**Scenario:** You are posted to a government high school in a rural district of Sindh. The school has two operational hand-pumps and four bathroom taps. Because washers are worn out, two taps drip continuously, wasting an estimated 150 litres of clean water every day. Meanwhile, the school compound is completely barren and dusty, with no shade for pupils during hot break times.

**Instructions:**
1. Work together to draft a five-column Project Plan Matrix (Task, Who is responsible, Timeline, Resources needed, Success indicator) for a 6-week project titled "Save Every Drop: Water Conservation and Shade".
2. Your plan must include:
   - A method for auditing and fixing the leaking taps using local low-cost materials.
   - A system for capturing hand-pump wash-water to nurture five native shade saplings.
   - A pupil duty rota to monitor both the taps and the saplings daily.
3. Compare your group's indicators with another group: are your indicators measurable, and do they test whether the project lasts beyond Week 6?

## Summary

A school sustainability project is a sustained cycle of inquiry, collaborative action, continuous monitoring, and institutional reflection. Following Wals et al. (2024), a Whole School Approach embeds sustainability into classroom learning, campus resource management, school culture, and community ties. Projects must not be treated as isolated publicity events; without pupil-led daily monitoring and institutional maintenance, physical activities quickly fail. By establishing clear responsibilities, low-cost local resources, and verifiable indicators of success, student teachers empower pupils to become active stewards of their immediate educational environment.

## Try this at your practicum school

During your next visit to your practicum school, conduct a 20-minute "Campus Sustainability Walk" with a colleague or mentor teacher:
1. Walk systematically around the school grounds, observing water sources, waste collection bins, electrical switches in empty rooms, and vegetation or shade trees.
2. Identify one specific, manageable sustainability problem (for example, a dripping tap, unshaded assembly areas, or burning of paper waste).
3. Draft a three-sentence project proposal identifying who in the school (which class, club, or caretaker) could lead an initiative to solve it, what local zero-cost resource could be used, and what visible sign would prove success after two months.

## Summative task

**Task:** Design a comprehensive 8-week school sustainability project plan for an elementary or secondary school in Sindh. Your plan must address either **solid waste management (reduction and composting)** or **water conservation and greening**. 

Write a 400 to 500-word project blueprint containing:
1. A brief baseline diagnosis of the school problem and local justification.
2. A complete Project Plan Matrix containing at least four distinct tasks covering planning, execution, and monitoring.
3. A daily/weekly monitoring protocol explaining exactly what data pupils will collect and how maintenance will continue during school vacations.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing (0-1 mark) | Competent (2 marks) | Advanced (3 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Project cycle alignment** *(Understand)* | Mentions isolated activities without clear stages; skips monitoring or reflection. | Covers the project stages in logical order, linking need identification to action and outcomes. | Synthesizes all six stages into a coherent Whole School Approach, linking classroom inquiry, campus operations, and pupil agency. |
| **Contextual feasibility** *(Apply)* | Relies on expensive commercial materials or unrealistic external funding. | Selects feasible, low-cost local materials and specifies sensible pupil/teacher roles suitable for a Pakistani government school. | Ingeniously repurposes indigenous, low-cost community resources and establishes robust, safe pupil leadership routines. |
| **Monitoring and sustainability rigor** *(Analyze)* | States vague success signs (such as "the school looks clean" or "pupils are happy") without verifiable data. | Specifies measurable indicators (such as litres saved, survival rates, or kilograms diverted) and assigns monitoring roles. | Rigorously analyzes potential failure points (such as holiday neglect, water shortages, or cattle intrusion) and justifies concrete operational mechanisms to ensure long-term continuity. |
