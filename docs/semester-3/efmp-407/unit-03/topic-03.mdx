# Topic 3.3 - Educating global citizens

## A real classroom situation

During a morning school assembly at a government boys high school in Hyderabad, the senior English teacher announced a special speech competition on "Our Responsibilities as Global Citizens". In the classroom discussion that followed, Grade 9 student Danish raised his hand and asked frankly: "Sir, we are Pakistani citizens living in Sindh. Why should our school teach us to be 'global citizens'? Does global citizenship mean adopting foreign cultural lifestyles and prioritizing international concerns over the struggles of our own people?"

Student teacher Bilal observed the exchange from the back of the classroom. He noticed that several pupils nodded in agreement with Danish's question, while the teacher struggled to provide an answer that sounded neither unpatriotic nor indifferent to the wider world. Bilal realized that without clear pedagogical guidance, many teachers and learners perceive global citizenship as a hollow abstraction or an imported Western concept that conflicts with national loyalty, rather than recognizing that ethical commitment to one's own country and solidarity with our shared planet are deeply complementary.

## Explanation

### Education for global citizenship

Global Citizenship Education (GCED) is one of the foundational pillars of the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, prominently embedded within SDG Target 4.7 alongside Education for Sustainable Development (ESD). UNESCO (2015, 2017) defines global citizenship education as a transformative approach that equips learners of all ages to understand local and global challenges, recognize our common humanity, and become active, responsible contributors to more peaceful, tolerant, inclusive, and secure societies.

GCED does not confer a legal passport or replace sovereign nationality. There is no world government that grants citizenship papers. Rather, global citizenship refers to a **civic mindset, a moral outlook, and an ethical mode of engagement**. It involves recognizing that our lives, economies, health systems, and natural environments are inextricably linked to the lives of fellow human beings across the globe.

#### Addressing a common misconception: Global citizenship versus national loyalty

The most persistent misconception among educators and communities in Pakistan is that **"global citizenship means loyalty to the world instead of to Pakistan"**, or that it requires pupils to discard local cultural and religious traditions in favor of westernized habits.

Scholars of critical global citizenship education decisively refute this false dichotomy (Akkari & Maleq, 2020). Far from weakening national commitment, authentic global citizenship is deeply grounded in local roots. A person cannot be an effective global citizen if they are indifferent to the poverty, water scarcity, or social injustice in their own village, town, or province.

Consider the severe challenges Pakistan faces today:
- **Climate vulnerability:** Pakistan contributes less than one percent of global greenhouse gas emissions, yet it ranks among the nations most severely impacted by catastrophic climate change, as evidenced by recurring heatwaves and the devastating 2022 Indus floods. Addressing climate resilience requires Pakistani citizens who understand both local flood drainage systems and international climate finance agreements.
- **Public health:** Diseases like polio, dengue fever, and viral epidemics do not respect national borders. Protecting children in Sindh requires local community vaccination campaigns informed by global scientific cooperation.
- **Economic livelihoods:** Pakistani agriculture and textiles depend on international trade, water flows from regional mountain ranges, and global commodity markets.

Therefore, educating global citizens in Pakistan means preparing young people who love and serve their country, cherish their linguistic and cultural heritage, and possess the critical thinking and international awareness needed to protect their nation within an interconnected world (Akkari & Maleq, 2020).

#### The three domains of Global Citizenship Education

Like ESD, global citizenship education engages three core learning domains (UNESCO, 2017):

1. **Cognitive domain:** Developing knowledge and critical thinking about local, national, and global issues, governance structures, and the interconnected drivers of inequality, poverty, and ecological harm. Learners investigate whose voices are represented in media, how global supply chains operate, and how international human rights frameworks protect human dignity.
2. **Socio-emotional domain:** Cultivating emotional intelligence, empathy, and a profound sense of shared humanity. Learners explore diverse perspectives, recognize universal values of justice, fairness, and compassion, and develop the emotional capacity to respect cultural differences without feeling threatened.
3. **Behavioural domain:** Fostering practical action and civic responsibility. Learners work collaboratively to address real-world community dilemmas, resolve peer conflicts non-violently, and engage in informed, ethical social action both inside and outside the school.

#### Intersections between ESD and GCED under Target 4.7

Historically, Education for Sustainable Development and Global Citizenship Education developed along distinct paths (Khoo & Jørgensen, 2021). ESD emerged primarily from environmental education, ecology, and concerns over resource exhaustion and economic models. GCED emerged from human rights education, peace studies, and international development education.

Under SDG Target 4.7, these two streams converge into a powerful, mutually reinforcing partnership. Both engage the same three learning domains (cognitive, socio-emotional, and behavioural); the difference is one of emphasis, not a rigid division:
- **ESD emphasizes humanity's relationship with natural systems:** It teaches learners about the safe limits of Earth's ecosystems, resource cycles, biodiversity, and sustainable consumption, while also addressing the social and economic choices that drive environmental harm.
- **GCED emphasizes the social, political and ethical dimensions:** It teaches learners about power structures, human rights, social equity, democratic participation, and intercultural dialogue, while also connecting these to the environmental systems that sustain life.

Without GCED, sustainability education risks degenerating into purely technical resource management (such as merely fixing water pipes without asking who has equitable access to the water). Without ESD, global citizenship education risks remaining abstract political rhetoric without understanding the natural systems that sustain life. When integrated, they enable students to recognize that a healthy environment and human dignity are indivisible.

#### Pedagogical strategies for diverse Pakistani classrooms

To teach global citizenship effectively across different grade bands in Pakistan, teachers should adopt participatory, learner-centred strategies (Figure 3.6):

- **Primary grades (Grades 1-5):** Focus on concrete empathy, fair play, and appreciating differences. Teachers use traditional storytelling from different provinces of Pakistan (Sindh, Balochistan, Punjab, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Gilgit-Baltistan), establish democratic classroom rules, and teach children to resolve playground disagreements through calm dialogue rather than aggression.
- **Middle grades (Grades 6-8):** Broaden horizons to community resource mapping and national diversity. Pupils investigate where their daily food, clean water, and clothing come from, tracing cotton from Sindh farms to global markets. They engage in jigsaw reading activities exploring how families in other parts of Pakistan and the world adapt to environmental and social challenges.
- **Secondary grades (Grades 9-10):** Engage critical inquiry, structured controversy, and community action research. Trainees and students examine complex dilemmas, such as balancing urban industrial growth with agricultural land conservation, evaluating news sources for bias, and leading student-run community campaigns on waste reduction and peace-building.

#### Worked example: A secondary school cotton inquiry connecting local work to global systems

Consider a Grade 9 Social Studies class in a cotton-growing district of Sindh, such as Sanghar:

1. **Local investigation:** Pupils interview local farmers and family members about cotton sowing, irrigation water availability, pesticide costs, and the health effects of chemical spraying on women cotton pickers.
2. **Connecting to global systems:** In the classroom, the teacher guides pupils to examine how cotton harvested in Sanghar travels to textile mills in Karachi, is manufactured into clothing, and is exported to international markets across Europe and Asia.
3. **Critical reflection:** Pupils discuss fair wages for farm workers, the ecological consequences of excessive chemical fertilizers on local soil, and the global consumer demand for organic, ethically produced garments.
4. **Action:** The class writes a collaborative wall magazine titled *"From Our Fields to the World: Cotton, Water, and Fair Work"*, inviting parents and community members to discuss water-conserving irrigation methods and safe farming practices.

This worked example shows how a teacher can connect immediate provincial agriculture to global economics, environmental science, and social justice, making global citizenship tangible, practical, and personally meaningful.

## Activity: Structuring a classroom dialogue on a community dilemma

**Format:** Groups of 4 student teachers.  
**Time:** 25 minutes.

1. **Scenario:** In an interior Sindh town, the local municipal council plans to drain a natural freshwater wetland on the edge of town to construct a commercial wholesale market. The market will create local jobs for merchants and youth (economic prosperity), but draining the wetland will destroy bird habitats, eliminate grazing areas used by poor pastoralists, and increase summer heat and flood risks (ecological integrity and social equity).
2. **Assign stakeholder roles:**
   - Role A: Local shopkeepers and unemployed youth seeking jobs.
   - Role B: Pastoralist families who depend on the wetland for cattle grazing.
   - Role C: Environmental science teachers concerned about biodiversity and flood risks.
   - Role D: The municipal council mayor seeking revenue.
3. **Conduct a 10-minute structured dialogue:** Each role presents their perspective using respectful, evidence-based arguments.
4. **Consensus building:** In the final 5 minutes, step out of your roles and collaboratively draft a compromise proposal that balances economic livelihoods with environmental protection and social justice.
5. **Debrief:** Reflect on how structured role-play develops pupils' socio-emotional empathy, perspective-taking, and peace-building skills.

## Summary

Global citizenship education is a transformative pedagogical approach that cultivates the critical knowledge, ethical values, and collaborative skills needed to build peaceful, just, and sustainable societies. Far from competing with national identity, authentic global citizenship begins with deep local responsibility in our own Pakistani communities and extends to universal solidarity with all humanity. Integrated with Education for Sustainable Development under SDG Target 4.7, GCED equips learners across primary, middle, and secondary grades to analyze interconnected social and ecological challenges, practice non-violent dialogue, and take constructive civic action for the common good.

## Try this at your practicum school

During your practicum placement, spend 20 minutes observing student interactions during recess, sports, or an unstructured classroom activity:

1. Observe how pupils from different linguistic, regional, or socio-economic backgrounds interact. Do they mix naturally, or do you observe social grouping, exclusion, or stereotyping?
2. Note how pupils handle minor disagreements (for example, over sports equipment, turns in a game, or shared desks). Do they use dialogue or resort to verbal taunting and aggression?
3. Design a simple 15-minute cooperative classroom activity (such as a paired interview where pupils find three shared values and one unique cultural strength in their partner) and trial it with your practicum mentor's permission.
4. Write a brief reflection in your journal describing how the activity influenced classroom climate and pupil empathy.
