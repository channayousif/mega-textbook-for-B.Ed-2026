# Topic 3.2 - Pakistan's National SDG Framework

## A real classroom situation

During a monthly staff meeting at a government higher secondary school in Mirpurkhas, the headteacher shared a directive from the District Education Authority. The notification urged all school heads to align their annual school development plans with provincialized Sustainable Development Goal targets, placing special emphasis on drinking water hygiene (SDG 6), student health (SDG 3), and girls' transition to secondary grades (SDG 4 and SDG 5).

Senior teacher Tariq shook his head in frustration and remarked to his colleagues: "These SDG targets are formulated in Islamabad and agreed at international summits in New York. We are dealing with teacher shortages, broken boundary walls, and power outages in interior Sindh. How can a secondary school in Mirpurkhas with limited operational funds be expected to deliver a national planning framework?"

Student teacher Asma, who was observing the meeting, listened attentively. She recognized that Tariq viewed national policy as an external statistical burden rather than an enabling framework. She began to reflect: how does Pakistan's National SDG Framework actually reach from federal ministries to provincial departments, and how can local schools use it to legitimize and support the very improvements they need most?

## Explanation

### Pakistan's National SDG Framework

Many educators assume that global development declarations remain purely aspirational documents with no formal domestic legal or policy standing. In Pakistan, however, the adoption of the 2030 Agenda followed a deliberate legislative and institutional pathway.

In February 2016, the National Assembly of Pakistan passed a unanimous resolution adopting the 17 Sustainable Development Goals as **Pakistan's National Development Goals**. This landmark parliamentary commitment made Pakistan one of the earliest countries in the world to integrate the 2030 Agenda directly into its national sovereign policy architecture.

To translate these 17 global goals and their 169 targets into an achievable roadmap, the Ministry of Planning, Development and Special Initiatives (the federal Planning Commission), in partnership with provincial governments and United Nations agencies, formulated the **National Sustainable Development Goals Framework: Prioritization and Localization** (Government of Pakistan, 2018). The framework addresses a fundamental practical question: given resource constraints and acute developmental challenges, how should Pakistan sequence and prioritize its efforts?

#### The three-tier national priority categorisation

The National Framework categorizes the 17 SDGs into three distinct, phased priority tiers based on baseline national data, urgency, and resource availability:

1. **Category 1 (Immediate / High Priority):** Comprises the urgent baseline goals directly affecting human survival, basic dignity, and social foundations. This category includes SDG 1 (No Poverty), SDG 2 (Zero Hunger and Malnutrition), SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being), SDG 4 (Quality Education), SDG 6 (Clean Water and Sanitation), SDG 7 (Affordable Clean Energy), and SDG 16 (Peace, Justice, and Strong Institutions).
2. **Category 2 (Emerging Priority):** Focuses on systemic enablers and structural transformation, including SDG 5 (Gender Equality), SDG 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth), SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation, and Infrastructure), SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities), and SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities).
3. **Category 3 (Long-Term and Partnership Priority):** Addresses planetary ecosystems, sustainable consumption, and global collaboration, encompassing SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production), SDG 13 (Climate Action), SDG 14 (Life Below Water), SDG 15 (Life on Land), and SDG 17 (Partnerships for the Goals).

It is crucial to note that categorising a goal as Category 2 or 3 does not mean it is unimportant. Rather, the framework recognizes that without urgent investments in basic education, health, clean water, and poverty alleviation (Category 1), long-term ecological and industrial transformation cannot be sustained.

#### Provincial localization and the 18th Constitutional Amendment

A central constitutional reality governs how the National SDG Framework operates in Pakistan: the **18th Constitutional Amendment** of 2010. Under this constitutional reform, legislative and executive authority over key social sectors - particularly school education, literacy, public health, and local government - was fully devolved to the provinces. Under Article 25-A of the Constitution of Pakistan, the provision of free and compulsory education for all children aged 5 to 16 is a direct legal obligation of the provincial governments.

Consequently, Pakistan cannot implement the SDGs through a single, centralized federal edict. The national framework must be localized. To achieve this, dedicated **Provincial SDG Support Units** were established within the Planning and Development (P&D) Boards of all four provinces, including Sindh.

In Sindh, the Provincial SDG Support Unit collaborates with the School Education and Literacy Department (SELD) to:
- Align provincial educational budgets and the Sindh Education Sector Plan with SDG 4 and Target 4.7.
- Establish district-level baseline indicators for out-of-school children, gender parity in secondary schooling, and school drinking water availability.
- Integrate sustainability, climate resilience, and civic values into provincial teacher training curricula at Government Elementary Colleges of Education (GECEs).

#### Addressing common misconceptions about the national framework

Two widespread misconceptions often discourage teachers and school leaders from engaging with the SDGs:

- **Misconception 1: "Pakistan has no SDG plan of its own."** As explained above, Pakistan does not rely on generic international mandates; it established its own National SDG Framework through a unanimous parliamentary resolution in 2016 and published comprehensive national and provincial priority frameworks (Government of Pakistan, 2018).
- **Misconception 2: "The SDGs are a high-level government matter that individual teachers cannot act upon."** National targets are not abstract mathematical formulas; they represent aggregated human realities. When a teacher in Dadu organizes a handwashing routine that prevents student absenteeism, that teacher directly advances SDG 3 and SDG 6. When a school head in Larkana ensures that girls receive equal encouragement to participate in school leadership and mathematics, that school advances SDG 4 and SDG 5. The national framework succeeds only when local schools translate macro priorities into micro-level classroom routines.

#### Worked example: Translating national priorities into a school development plan

Consider a government high school in a flood-prone district of lower Sindh. The school development committee, comprising teachers, the headteacher, and parents, decides to align its annual work plan with Pakistan's priority SDGs:

1. **Identifying priority targets:** The committee reviews national priorities and selects three relevant challenges: child health and water contamination (SDG 3 and 6), out-of-school girls in the village (SDG 4 and 5), and recurring monsoon heatwaves (SDG 13).
2. **Formulating school-level interventions:**
   - *Water and health:* The school installs a sand-and-charcoal water filter, tests water quality twice monthly, and assigns pupil health monitors to lead 2-minute morning handwashing demonstrations.
   - *Girls' retention:* The school ensures functional, private sanitation facilities with running water, and teachers conduct community home visits to reassure parents about safety and dignity.
   - *Climate adaptation:* The school plants 50 native Neem and Peepal trees in the schoolyard to provide natural shade during extreme summer heatwaves.
3. **Evaluating impact:** Within six months, pupil attendance increases by 14 percent, reported cases of stomach illness drop significantly, and eight previously out-of-school girls enroll in Grade 6.

This worked example demonstrates how national policy provides legitimacy and clear direction for grassroots school improvement without requiring expensive external technology.

## Activity: Designing a school-level SDG action plan

**Format:** Pairs or trios of student teachers.  
**Time:** 25 minutes.

1. **Select a school context:** Imagine you are appointed as teachers in a government secondary school in an interior Sindh town facing irregular electricity, saline tap water, and low female attendance in Grades 9 and 10.
2. **Select two priority SDGs:** Review Figure 3.4 and choose two priority goals that would address this school's most critical challenges (for example, SDG 4 and SDG 6, or SDG 5 and SDG 13).
3. **Draft a 3-month action plan:**
   - Formulate one low-cost, achievable school intervention for each selected goal.
   - Specify the role of the teachers, the students, and the School Management Committee (parents).
   - Define one simple, observable indicator of success (for example, "number of functioning clean water stations" or "percentage reduction in unexcused girl-student absences").
4. **Exchange and critique:** Swap plans with another pair. Provide constructive feedback on whether the proposed actions are realistic, low-cost, and culturally respectful.

## Summary

Pakistan demonstrated early international leadership by unanimously adopting the SDGs as its National Development Goals in 2016. The National SDG Framework prioritizes urgent baseline challenges across health, education, poverty, and water (Category 1) while establishing enablers for structural and planetary transformation. Following the 18th Amendment, education is an exclusively provincial responsibility, making provincial SDG units and departments of education the engines of implementation. Far from being a distant bureaucratic exercise, the national framework provides headteachers and classroom educators with a coherent roadmap to design practical, low-cost interventions that improve child wellbeing, equity, and learning quality in every community.
