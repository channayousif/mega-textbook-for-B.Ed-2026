# Topic 6.1 - A green economy and sustainable consumption

## A real classroom situation

In this illustrative teaching case, Sumera is a trainee teacher in a Class 9 lesson at a government school in Tando Allahyar. She writes "green economy" on the board. Asif raises his hand. "My uncle repairs diesel generators," he says. "If everyone buys solar panels, he has no work. So green means fewer jobs." Sana answers from the back. "My father says green things are for rich people. A good fan costs far more than a cheap one." Sumera had planned a lesson about trees. Now she has two serious questions about money and work, and she must answer them honestly.

## Explanation

### Green economy

An **economy** is the whole system by which people produce, buy, sell and use goods and services. The United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) gives a working definition of a **green economy**: one that improves human well-being and social equity while greatly reducing environmental risks and shortages of natural resources (UNEP, n.d.). **Social equity** means fairness in who gains and who pays.

So the definition sets two conditions, and an economy must meet both:

1. **People and fairness.** Do people live better, and are the gains shared fairly?
2. **Nature.** Are the risks to the environment, and the pressure on natural resources, falling?

A project that cuts pollution but pushes poor families out of work does not meet the first condition. A project that creates jobs and poisons a canal does not meet the second. UNEP's later term, the *inclusive* green economy, stresses that it must provide jobs and income as well as health and a safe environment (UNEP, n.d.).

#### From a line to a circle

One useful picture comes from the idea of a circular economy. In a **linear economy**, we take materials from the earth, make products and throw them away. In a **circular economy**, we design out waste, keep materials in use and restore nature (Ellen MacArthur Foundation, n.d.). The figure above compares the two. A circular economy says little about fairness, so it is not the whole of a green economy, but it shows what the "nature" condition can look like. A bazaar mechanic who rewinds an old fan's motor is part of such a loop.

#### Correcting a common idea

**"A green economy means slower growth and fewer jobs."** Asif's worry is real, because some jobs do end. But the general claim of fewer jobs is not what the ILO's modelling shows.

The International Labour Organization (ILO) modelled action in the energy sector to keep global warming to 2°C, and compared it with carrying on as usual. By 2030, it estimated about 24 million jobs created and about 6 million lost, a net gain of about 18 million worldwide (ILO, 2018). Read that result carefully:

- It is a **model** of the future, not a count of jobs that already exist.
- The **people who lose are often not the people who gain.** Oil refining and crude oil extraction lost the most. The ILO also estimated net job losses in Africa and the Middle East unless their economies change.
- It is a world figure. It tells us **nothing exact about Pakistan** or about Asif's uncle.
- It is about jobs, so it does not settle the question of growth. Whether growth slows depends on the country, the sector and the policy.

An honest reply to Asif is this: "Work is likely to move, more than it is likely to vanish. A generator mechanic has skills that are useful for solar systems, but somebody has to pay for the retraining." The fairness condition asks exactly that question.

#### Worked example: testing the solar boom

Pakistan gives us a real case. Ember, an energy think tank, reports that Pakistan imported 17 gigawatts of solar panels in 2024, double the amount of the year before. Homes and businesses installed rooftop panels, mainly to escape high electricity prices (Arab News, 2025). Is this a green economy? Use the two conditions.

**Step 1: State the case.** Households and firms bought panels with their own money, because grid electricity was expensive.

**Step 2: Test nature.** Sunlight replaces some electricity from fuel, so this looks positive. *To find out:* how much fuel was actually replaced, and what happens to old panels and batteries when they fail? A linear ending would still be a waste problem.

**Step 3: Test people and fairness.** Families with panels may pay less, and installers have new work. *To find out:* who could not afford panels? Do they end up carrying more of the cost of the grid? Our source only says that planning and rules must catch up.

**Step 4: Judge.** The motive was mainly price, but a motive is not a test. We can call the case "partly green, fairness unknown". This is a stronger answer than a simple yes or no.

### Sustainable consumption

To **consume** is to use up goods and services. **Sustainable consumption** means using products and services that meet basic needs and improve the quality of life, while keeping natural resources and environmental harm low over the product's whole life, so that future generations can meet their needs too (Al-Nuaimi & Al-Ghamdi, 2022). It is part of Goal 12 of the Sustainable Development Goals, on sustainable consumption and production (United Nations, n.d.).

*Meet basic needs* tells us this is not about going without food or medicine. *Over the product's whole life* tells us to look past the shop: how it is made, used and thrown away.

#### Why people do not simply choose well

Al-Nuaimi and Al-Ghamdi (2022) sort the research into two kinds of consumption:

- **Immediate consumption** is what a person controls directly, such as what and where to buy.
- **Contextual consumption** is what surrounds that choice, such as rules, taxes, labels and what shops stock.

Polythene bags in Pakistan show why both matter. In 2019 the Guardian reported fines in Islamabad and bans announced in Sindh and Punjab. It also reported that provinces had imposed such bans for more than a decade without enforcing them, and a shopper who was offered no alternative at the supermarket (Baloch, 2019). A shopper can decide to carry a cloth bag. Whether cheap alternatives exist, and whether a ban is enforced, is decided elsewhere.

The same review warns teachers about one assumption. More knowledge raises awareness, but it does not always change behaviour, because cost and daily habits get in the way. Knowledge about *what to do* links to action more clearly than plain facts do (Al-Nuaimi & Al-Ghamdi, 2022). Its largest shares of studies by country come from the United States, China and Spain, and its examples are not Pakistani, so treat them as a guide and not as a finding about your own class. So a school that only teaches facts about plastic may change little. A school that also removes polythene from its canteen teaches through its routines. In one university case from Malaysia, a campus programme cut paper use by an average of 43 percent over three years.

#### Correcting a common idea

**"Sustainable products are always more expensive."** Sana's father is right about some first prices. He is wrong about "always", for two reasons. Some sustainable choices cost almost nothing, such as using less, repairing or planning meals to waste less. Other choices cost more at first and less over time. The table shows both.

A cloth bag has a small first cost, a fan with a higher star rating costs more at first and lowers bills, refilling a bottle costs less if safe water is at hand, repair usually costs less than buying new, and planning food saves money at once." />

A cost that pays back later still has to be paid now, and a poor household may not have the money. Research in the review links higher income with higher consumption (Al-Nuaimi & Al-Ghamdi, 2022). So never teach this topic in a way that shames a pupil's family for buying the cheaper item.

#### Worked example: what does a fan really cost?

A presentation at a 2019 South Asian (SAARC) energy workshop estimates an average saving of 40 to 60 watts for each ceiling fan that meets a higher efficiency rating (Mahmood, 2019). We will use the lower figure. The price and the tariff below are assumed round numbers for practice. Replace them with your own.

**Step 1: Units saved.** 40 watts for 12 hours a day is 480 watt-hours, or 0.48 units a day. Over 30 days, that is 14.4 units.

**Step 2: Money saved.** Assume Rs 40 a unit. Then 14.4 × 40 = Rs 576 a month.

**Step 3: Payback.** Assume the efficient fan costs Rs 5,000 more. Then 5,000 ÷ 576 is about 9. It pays back after about 9 months of such use.

**Step 4: Judge.** Over the years of a fan's life, the dearer fan may be the cheaper one. But a family must find Rs 5,000 first, and the sum changes if the fan runs fewer hours. So the result is "cheaper over time, for those who can pay at the start".

## Activity: Move the cost

Work in groups of three or four. About 25 minutes.

Each group takes one household item used in your area, such as a fan, a light bulb, a water bottle, a shopping bag or a school bag. Without looking back, one person states the definition of sustainable consumption. Together, list a more sustainable alternative, then estimate its first cost and its cost over one year from what you know of local prices. Mark clearly which numbers are guesses. Next, decide who in your area could not make this change, and what would have to change around them (a rule, a shop, a loan). Another group must then challenge one of your numbers.

## Check your understanding

Answer from memory, then check against the explanation.

1. State the two conditions in UNEP's definition of a green economy. *(Remember)*
2. Explain, in your own words, the difference between immediate and contextual consumption, with one example of each. *(Understand)*
3. A shop sells an LED bulb for Rs 400 more than an ordinary bulb. It saves Rs 100 a month in electricity. Work out how many months it takes to pay back, and name one family for whom this is still hard. *(Apply)*

## Summary

A green economy must improve people's lives fairly and also reduce harm to nature. Modelling suggests that action on energy moves jobs more than it destroys them, but those who lose still need help. Sustainable consumption means meeting real needs with less harm over a product's whole life, and it depends on the rules and shops around a person as well as on personal choice. Work out the cost before making a claim, because the sustainable choice may be cheaper at once, cheaper over time or out of reach.

## Try this at your practicum school

Walk through your practicum school with your cooperating teacher's permission. List three things the school buys or uses up, such as chalk, paper, water or electricity. For one of them, ask who decides the purchase, what it costs each month and what happens to the waste. Note one change that the school could make at no cost.

## Summative task

Choose one business or public project in your district that claims to be green, such as a solar scheme, a recycling shop or a tree-planting drive. In about 350 words, test it against both conditions of a green economy. Then choose one household choice connected with it and work out its cost over one year. Show your arithmetic and mark your assumptions.

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Use of the two conditions (3 marks) | Both conditions applied to the case with evidence | Both named, one applied thinly | One condition only |
| Analysis of who gains and who pays (4 marks) | Separates groups, and shows how a gain for one may be a cost for another | Lists gains and costs without linking them | No analysis |
| Cost calculation and honesty about evidence (3 marks) | Sound arithmetic; assumptions marked; known separated from unknown | Arithmetic with hidden assumptions | No calculation, or invented facts |

The second criterion asks for analysis. Explain how your evidence leads to your judgement.
