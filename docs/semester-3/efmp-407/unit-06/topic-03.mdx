# Topic 6.3 - Ethical consumerism

## A real classroom situation

In this illustrative teaching case, a B.Ed class in Sukkur has Rs 6,000 to buy gift bags for a school fair. One shop offers thin plastic bags at a low price. A women's sewing group in the next street offers cloth bags at more than double the price. Aliya wants the cloth bags. "We should buy ethically," she says. Bilal shakes his head. "One small order changes nothing. And my mother buys the cheapest of everything because she must. Is she unethical?" The class goes quiet, because nobody wants to answer that. This topic helps them make a decision without judging Bilal's mother.

## Explanation

### Ethical consumerism

**Ethical consumerism** means letting your values about people, animals and the environment guide what you buy and do not buy. It has two main forms (Szmigin et al., 2009):

- **Positive choice:** buying something because of how it was made, such as a fairly traded or environmentally friendly product.
- **Avoidance or boycott:** refusing to buy a certain product, or from a certain company.

Szmigin et al. (2009) quote an earlier study saying that every purchase has "ethical, resource, waste, and community implications". An ethical consumer is a person who keeps this in mind. It overlaps with sustainable consumption from Topic 6.1, but the focus differs. Sustainable consumption asks about harm and resources over time. Ethical consumerism asks what *I* should do, given what I value. It often includes questions about fairness, such as who made a product and how they were treated.

#### Real people are inconsistent

Szmigin et al. (2009) interviewed nine people in England who regularly bought ethical products. They called them **conscious consumers**: people who know the issues and care, yet do not always choose the ethical item. Price, taste, convenience, family wishes and even mood often won. One participant said she would bend her principles at the end of the month, just before pay day. The authors do not call this failure. They call it flexibility, and they say that it helps people to manage budgets, families and values at the same time.

This was a small study of British shoppers, and its authors do not claim that it applies to everyone. It does not tell us about shoppers in Sukkur. But it gives us a useful idea: ethical buying is a series of separate decisions, not a badge that a person either has or lacks. It also tells us how to answer Bilal. Consumption is often shaped by obligations to our families (Szmigin et al., 2009). A mother who buys the cheapest food so that her children can eat is acting on her duty to them. Choice needs money and options, and she may have little of either. Ethical consumerism is a question about what a person can reasonably do, not a test that the poor fail.

#### Correcting a common idea

**"One person's shopping choices cannot matter."** Part of this is true, so do not simply deny it. One purchase will rarely change how a factory behaves. But the sentence leaves out how choices spread.

- **Habits spread within a person.** Al-Nuaimi and Al-Ghamdi (2022) report research on "behavioural spillover": a person who makes one sustainable choice is more likely to make another.
- **Choices are social.** People buy for households, and children and neighbours notice.
- **Some people choose for many.** A head teacher, a canteen owner or a shop decides for everyone who buys there. Szmigin et al. (2009) point to British supermarkets that stocked only fair trade bananas or chocolate, so that customers did not have to choose. A class of 30 trainees buying for a school fair already has more weight than one person.

There is a limit on the other side too. The review reports a criticism that talk of shopping choices can put moral pressure on individuals, and so draw attention away from governments and companies (Al-Nuaimi & Al-Ghamdi, 2022). So the balanced answer is this: your choices matter somewhat, and more when they are shared, but they do not replace rules or company responsibility from Topic 6.2.

#### Four questions before buying

The flowchart above sets out four questions that an ethical consumer can ask:

1. **Do I need it?** If not, the least harmful purchase may be none. Could I borrow it, repair it or make do?
2. **Who made it?** Is there a named maker? Can I learn anything about them?
3. **How was it made?** What does the label or seller say about materials, energy and workers?
4. **What happens after use?** Can it be reused, repaired or recycled, or does it end up in a canal?

No shopper can answer all four questions every time. Often the honest answer to "how was it made?" is "I do not know". Then you decide on what you do know and note the gap.

#### Reading labels

A **label** or mark on a product is a claim. The important thing to ask is **what it claims, and who checked it**. Each trustworthy label answers a narrow question. It says nothing about other questions.

<Figure id="fig-U6-6" src="/img/figures/efmp-407/unit-06/fig-U6-6.svg" alt="Table of five labels and signs, with what each claims, who checks it and how far to trust it. The Pakistan Energy Label is strong on energy, the PS mark is good for its standard, the halal logo is serious on its own question, the Fairtrade mark covers certified products only, and words like eco-friendly are weak until there is evidence. None of the labels says everything about a product." />

Look at the table with care.

- The **Pakistan Energy Label** on a fan gives a rating of 1 to 5 stars, and a higher rating means a more efficient fan. Fan models are registered with the National Energy Efficiency and Conservation Authority (NEECA) and tested, and the label carries a QR code for checking (NEECA, n.d.). It tells you about electricity. It tells you nothing about how the workers were treated.
- The **PS mark** from the Pakistan Standards and Quality Control Authority (PSQCA) shows that a product is meant to meet a Pakistan Standard. PSQCA publishes a list of items that must meet these standards (PSQCA, n.d.). This concerns quality and safety for that product.
- The **halal logo** signifies that an article or process conforms to the relevant Pakistan Halal Standard, and a certificate comes from an accredited body (Pakistan Halal Authority Act, 2016). For a Muslim consumer that is a serious and specific assurance. It is not a claim about wages or packaging, and no one should read it as one.
- A **Fairtrade** mark relates to producers receiving at least a minimum price, plus a premium, for certified products (Fairtrade International, n.d.). Check whether it appears on the product at all, because it covers certified products only.
- Words like **"eco-friendly"**, "natural" or "green" printed by a seller, with no named checker, have no clear meaning (Delmas & Burbano, 2011). They are not worthless, but they prove nothing until there is evidence.

Remember the Ali Enterprises case in Topic 6.2. Even a real certificate is evidence and not a guarantee. Also, a real label can be applied wrongly. Delmas and Burbano (2011) report refrigerators in another country that carried a respected energy label and did not meet its standard. That is why a QR code you can check is worth more than a logo you cannot.

#### Worked example: buying a fan for a classroom

A head teacher in Sukkur has money for one ceiling fan. Two models are on offer in the bazaar. Walk through the questions.

**Question 1: Do I need it?** The old fan was repaired twice, and the mechanic says the motor is finished. The class is hot. So yes.

**Question 2: Who made it?** Both fans show the maker's name and address. One maker is in Gujrat and one is an importer. *Unknown:* working conditions at either.

**Question 3: How was it made?** Fan A has a Pakistan Energy Label with more stars, and the QR code works. Fan B says only "energy saver" on the box. So Fan A has a claim that someone has checked, and Fan B has only a word. Neither tells us about workers.

**Question 4: What happens after use?** The local mechanic can repair both, and copper from a dead motor has a resale value. No difference here.

**Decision.** Choose Fan A, as long as the extra cost fits the budget. Use Topic 6.1's calculation to check the payback. Write in the record: "Chosen for checked energy rating. Workers' conditions not known." A decision like this is defensible. It uses the evidence that exists and admits what is missing.

## Activity: The fair's gift bags

Work in groups of four. About 20 minutes.

You are the Sukkur class with Rs 6,000. Without looking back, write the four questions. Answer them for the plastic bags and for the cloth bags, marking anything you would have to find out. Then look for a third way: fewer bags, a mix, asking guests to bring their own, or paying the sewing group partly by asking the school committee for support. Agree on one plan. Write one sentence that explains your plan to Bilal's mother respectfully. Read it aloud to another group, who must tell you if it sounds like blame.

## Summary

Ethical consumerism means letting your values guide what you buy and refuse. Real people are inconsistent because money, family and convenience all count, and people with less money have fewer choices. One choice rarely changes a company, yet choices spread, and some people choose for many. Four questions help: do I need it, who made it, how, and what happens after use. A label is a claim about one narrow thing, so ask what it claims and who checked it, and record what you still do not know.

- [ ] I can say what a label does and does not claim, and how to check it.

## Try this at your practicum school

Look at the school canteen, or a shop beside the school. Choose three products that pupils often buy. Copy down every label, mark and claim on the packets. For each one, note what it claims and who checked it, if anyone. Ask the canteen owner or shopkeeper why they stock the product. Then write one activity you could use with pupils on these packets.

## Summative task

You must buy one item for your practicum school or your home, such as a fan, a water cooler, notebooks or a school bag. Compare two real options. In about 350 words, apply the four questions to both, and report every label on them. Make a decision, and defend it to someone who would have chosen differently. State clearly what you could not find out.

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Use of the four questions (3 marks) | All four applied to both options with real details | Applied to one option, or without details | Questions not used |
| Reading of labels (3 marks) | States what each label claims, who checked it and what it does not say | Names labels without their limits | Labels ignored or misread |
| Reasoned decision (4 marks) | Weighs the options, answers the other view, and separates known from unknown | Decision with general reasons | No decision, or invented facts |

The decision criterion needs analysis and evaluation. Show how your evidence and values led to your choice.
