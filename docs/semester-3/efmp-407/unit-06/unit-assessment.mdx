# Economic Dimensions - Assessment and Review

## Unit summary

Sustainability is also about how people earn, spend and throw away. A green economy must improve people's lives fairly and also reduce environmental risks and resource shortages. Modelling for the energy sector suggests that action moves jobs more than it destroys them, yet workers who lose their jobs still need help. Sustainable consumption means meeting real needs with less harm over a product's whole life. It depends on personal choices and also on the rules, shops and prices around them. A sustainable choice may be cheaper now, cheaper over time, or out of reach for a poor family.

Corporate social responsibility is a company's responsibility for how its business affects workers, communities, the environment and customers. Charity is only what it gives away, and a gift does not cancel harm. Pakistani law increasingly asks companies to disclose CSR, but disclosures and certificates are evidence and not proof. Greenwashing joins poor performance with positive talk. A careful person asks what is claimed, what can be checked, how the scale compares and who benefits.

Ethical consumerism means letting values guide what we buy and refuse. Real people are inconsistent, and people with less money have fewer choices, so it is not a test that the poor fail. One choice rarely changes a company, but choices spread, and some people choose for many. Four questions and a careful reading of labels lead to a decision that can be defended, along with an honest note of what remains unknown.

## Summative assessment

Attempt every question before you read the final guidance section. Each MCQ has one best answer. For restricted responses, keep to the length stated. For extended responses, build an argument with evidence, not a list of definitions. All named pupils, teachers, villages and companies in the questions are illustrative.

MCQs 1-4 and RRQs 1-4 address Topic 6.1; MCQs 5-7 and RRQs 5-7 address Topic 6.2; MCQs 8-10 and RRQs 8-10 address Topic 6.3. Extended tasks 1, 2 and 3 address Topics 6.1, 6.2 and 6.3 in turn, and tasks 4 and 5 draw on the whole unit. Your course teacher decides which tasks to collect and how the course's 60% summative and 40% formative weighting applies.

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. In UNEP's working definition, a green economy is one that: *(Remember)*
   - A) Grows faster than other economies by selling environmental products
   - B) Improves well-being and social equity while greatly reducing environmental risks and scarcities
   - C) Replaces all private business with public ownership of natural resources
   - D) Reduces the use of energy, whatever the effect on people's income

2. What is the main difference between a linear and a circular economy? *(Understand)*
   - A) A linear economy uses machines and a circular economy uses hand labour
   - B) A linear economy is found in poor countries and a circular economy in rich ones
   - C) A linear economy takes, makes and throws away, while a circular economy keeps materials in use
   - D) A linear economy is owned by the state and a circular economy by companies

3. According to the ILO (2018) model of action in the energy sector, what happens to jobs worldwide by 2030? *(Understand)*
   - A) Jobs lost are far more than jobs created, in every region
   - B) Jobs are created and lost, with a net gain, though some regions and industries lose
   - C) Jobs are created in every industry and no worker loses a job
   - D) Total jobs stay exactly the same because no industry changes

4. A family in Larkana hears that a costly water-saving tap will pay back within a year, but it has no savings. Which statement best applies sustainable consumption here? *(Apply)*
   - A) The family is acting unethically until it buys the tap
   - B) The tap is not more sustainable, because it has a higher first price
   - C) The family should stop using water until it has saved enough money
   - D) The choice may be cheaper over time, but the first cost is a real barrier for this family

5. Which situation is the clearest example of CSR that goes beyond charity? *(Understand)*
   - A) A factory donates money to a local mosque committee each Ramzan
   - B) A factory treats its waste water and gives its workers safe equipment and contracts
   - C) A factory pays for a large banner welcoming guests to a school event
   - D) A factory's owner personally gives scholarships to two pupils

6. Delmas and Burbano (2011) describe greenwashing as the joining of which two things? *(Remember)*
   - A) High prices and good quality
   - B) Good environmental performance and silence about it
   - C) Poor environmental performance and positive talk about that performance
   - D) Government rules and voluntary reports

7. A company advertises "We care for the Indus" after one riverside cleaning day. Which is the best first step in testing this claim? *(Apply)*
   - A) Accept it, because a cleaning day did take place
   - B) Reject it, because all company advertising is false
   - C) Ask how the company's advertisement was designed and printed
   - D) Compare the cleaning day with the company's own effect on the river, using evidence you can check

8. Which is an example of ethical consumerism in the form of avoidance? *(Remember)*
   - A) Refusing to buy from a company because of how it treats its workers
   - B) Choosing a cloth bag from a local sewing group
   - C) Comparing prices of the same fan in two shops
   - D) Buying a larger pack because it costs less per item

9. What did Szmigin et al. (2009) conclude about "conscious consumers"? *(Understand)*
   - A) They always pick the ethical product, regardless of price
   - B) They do not really care about ethical issues
   - C) They know the issues but are flexible, because price, family and convenience also count
   - D) They are mostly people with very low incomes

10. A fan shows a Pakistan Energy Label with a working QR code. A second fan's box says "eco-friendly". What can a careful buyer conclude? *(Apply)*
    - A) The first fan was made by well-treated workers
    - B) The first fan has a checkable energy claim; the second fan's words alone prove little
    - C) Both fans make equally strong claims about energy use
    - D) The second fan must be less efficient than the first

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

Answer each question within the length stated. Marks are shown in brackets.

1. Explain the two conditions of a green economy in your own words, with one example of a project that meets one condition but not the other. Use no more than three sentences. [3] *(Understand)*
2. A pupil says, "Going green means my uncle will lose his job." Write a reply in three or four sentences that takes his worry seriously and uses the ILO estimate accurately. [4] *(Understand)*
3. Use polythene bags in Pakistan to show the difference between immediate and contextual consumption. Use no more than four sentences. [4] *(Understand)*
4. A household replaces a fan at an extra cost of Rs 4,000 and saves Rs 500 a month. Work out the payback time, then give one reason why this result does not settle what the household should do. [4] *(Analyze)*
5. Explain why a company's donation of school desks does not show that it is socially responsible. Use no more than three sentences. [3] *(Understand)*
6. A bank publishes a CSR report about its tree-planting, but says nothing about its own staff. Use Zulfiqar et al. (2019) to analyse what is missing from this report. Use no more than four sentences. [4] *(Analyze)*
7. A factory holds a valid certificate from an outside auditor. Give two reasons why this is good evidence but not proof of safe conditions. Use no more than four sentences. [4] *(Analyze)*
8. Give one reason why a person who regularly buys ethical products might sometimes not do so, and explain why this need not be hypocrisy. Use no more than three sentences. [3] *(Understand)*
9. A friend says, "One person's shopping cannot change anything." Give a balanced reply: one point on which the friend is right, and two ways in which choices can still matter. Use no more than four sentences. [4] *(Analyze)*
10. A packet of biscuits carries a halal logo and the word "natural". Analyse what each sign claims and name one thing that neither tells the buyer. Use no more than four sentences. [4] *(Analyze)*

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

Write about 250-350 words for each task. Each task is marked out of 12.

1. A local politician says, "A green economy is a luxury. Pakistan needs jobs first." Evaluate this claim, using the two conditions of a green economy, the ILO estimate and the solar boom in Pakistan. Reach a clear judgement, and say what evidence would change it. *(Evaluate)*
2. Choose a CSR claim made by a real company operating in Pakistan, or use this one: "Company K, a large packaging firm, says: *We are Pakistan's greenest packaging company. We gave 500 dustbins to schools in Hyderabad.*" Judge the claim, using the four questions and evidence you can check. Name at least two checks. Separate what is shown from what is not known. *(Evaluate)*
3. A Class 8 teacher tells pupils, "Ethical people always buy the ethical product." Analyse what is wrong with this statement, using Szmigin et al. (2009) and the situation of low-income families. Then rewrite the message that the teacher should give. *(Analyze)*
4. A shop in your town sells "eco-friendly" school bags, made by a company with a strong CSR report, at twice the normal price. Evaluate whether a school should tell parents to buy these bags. Use sustainable consumption, CSR and ethical consumerism in your answer, and take account of low-income families. *(Evaluate)*
5. Design a 40-minute Class 8 lesson on one economic decision in your district, such as what the school canteen sells or which fans the school buys. Your plan must use cost over time, at least one label or company claim and the four questions, and it must include one pupil task. Justify your choices. *(Create)*

## Answers and marking guidance

Use this section only after attempting the questions. It explains the reasoning behind each answer so that you can check and improve your own. Your teacher may use different marks for formal assessment.

### MCQ answer key

1. **B.** This follows the UNEP wording. Speed of growth (A), ownership (C) and energy use alone (D) are not its conditions.
2. **C.** Linear means take, make and throw away; circular means keeping materials in use. The other options are not what the terms mean.
3. **B.** The ILO estimated about 24 million jobs created and about 6 million lost, with net losses in some regions and industries.
4. **D.** A choice can be cheaper over time and still be out of reach at the start. Sustainable consumption does not mean blaming the family (A) or going without basic needs (C).
5. **B.** This concerns how the factory earns its money, its waste and its workers. The other options are gifts or publicity.
6. **C.** This is their description. Option B describes a company doing well but staying silent, which is not misleading in this way.
7. **D.** Comparing scale with evidence tests the claim. Accepting it (A) or rejecting all advertising (B) skips the evidence.
8. **A.** Refusing to buy is avoidance or boycott. Option B is a positive choice; C and D are about price alone.
9. **C.** The participants knew the issues and cared, yet price, taste, convenience and family also counted.
10. **B.** The label is a checked claim about energy only. The word on the second box proves little, but it does not show that the fan is worse (D).

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. A green economy must make people's lives better and share the gains fairly, and it must also reduce harm to nature. A factory that creates jobs but poisons a canal meets the first in part and fails the second. **Marks (3):** 1 for each condition; 1 for a fitting example.
2. Your uncle's worry is fair, because some jobs do end. The ILO estimated about 24 million jobs created and 6 million lost worldwide by 2030 in its model, so work is likely to move more than it vanishes. That is a world estimate and does not promise his job is safe, so he may need help to retrain. **Marks (4):** 1 for taking the worry seriously; 1 for the figures; 1 for the point that it is a model or a world figure; 1 for retraining or support.
3. Carrying a cloth bag is an immediate choice that a shopper controls. Whether shops offer alternatives, and whether a ban is enforced, is contextual. Baloch (2019) reported bans that were not enforced and a shopper offered no alternative. **Marks (4):** 2 for each kind with its example.
4. 4,000 ÷ 500 = 8 months. This does not settle the matter because the household must find Rs 4,000 first, and other needs may come first, or the fan may run fewer hours than assumed. **Marks (4):** 2 for the working and result; 2 for a reason that is explained.
5. A donation shows what the company gave away, while responsibility also concerns how it earned the money. A company may give desks and still harm workers or the environment. The gift is real but does not cancel the harm. **Marks (3):** 1 for each point.
6. The report leaves out internal CSR, meaning how the bank treats its own employees. Zulfiqar et al. (2019) argue that employees are key to CSR, and that CSR without them risks becoming marketing. Their study links employees' view of internal CSR to their engagement. So the report tells us little about a central group. **Marks (4):** 1 for naming internal CSR or employees; 1 for the source's argument; 2 for applying it to this report.
7. A certificate reports what an auditor found at one time, and conditions can change after that. An audit can also miss things, as the disputed Ali Enterprises case shows, so workers' accounts and direct observation still matter. **Marks (4):** 2 for each reason, explained. Credit any sound reason. An answer that treats the disputed audit as settled loses 1 mark.
8. Price, family wishes or convenience may win on a given day, such as the end of the month. This need not be hypocrisy, because the person is balancing values against real duties and limits. **Marks (3):** 1 for the reason; 2 for the explanation.
9. One purchase will rarely change a company. But a person's habits can spread to further choices and to others in the household, and people such as head teachers or shopkeepers choose for many. **Marks (4):** 2 for the fair point; 1 for each way, up to 2.
10. The halal logo claims that the article conforms to the relevant Pakistan Halal Standard, under a certificate from an accredited body. "Natural" has no clear meaning and no named checker. Neither tells the buyer how workers were treated or what happens to the packet. **Marks (4):** 1 for each sign's claim; 1 for contrasting their strength; 1 for one unanswered question.

### ERQ rubrics

Each task uses four criteria worth **0-3 marks each**, totalling 12. Across criteria, 3 means accurate, developed and supported; 2 means mostly accurate with an incomplete link; 1 means a relevant point with little explanation; 0 means absent, wrong or unrelated.

**ERQ 1: Jobs first?**

| Criterion | What a 3 looks like |
|---|---|
| Use of the two conditions | Applies both conditions, and notes that a green economy includes jobs and income |
| Use of evidence | Reports the ILO figures with their limits, and uses the solar case accurately |
| Analysis of the claim (**Analyze**) | Shows that "jobs first" assumes that jobs and greening are opposed, and considers who loses work |
| Judgement (**Evaluate**) | Reaches a clear judgement that follows from the evidence, and says what would change it |

**ERQ 2: Judging a company's CSR claim**

| Criterion | What a 3 looks like |
|---|---|
| Understanding of CSR | Separates CSR from charity, and considers workers, community, environment and customers |
| Checkable evidence | Names at least two specific checks, and separates the company's words from independent evidence |
| Analysis of scale and benefit (**Analyze**) | Compares the action with the company's effects, and asks who benefits and who is left out |
| Judgement (**Evaluate**) | Judges the claim, not the people; makes no unsupported accusation; says what is not known |

**ERQ 3: "Ethical people always buy the ethical product"**

| Criterion | What a 3 looks like |
|---|---|
| Use of the source | Reports the idea of conscious consumers and flexibility accurately, with the limits of a small study |
| Attention to low-income families | Shows that choice depends on money and options, and that duties to family count |
| Analysis of the statement (**Analyze**) | Shows the hidden assumptions and the harm the statement could do to pupils |
| Rewritten message | Gives a respectful message that pupils could use and that keeps the ethical question open |

**ERQ 4: The "eco-friendly" school bags**

| Criterion | What a 3 looks like |
|---|---|
| Use of all three ideas | Uses sustainable consumption, CSR and ethical consumerism accurately |
| Evidence and labels | Questions "eco-friendly" and the CSR report, and names what could be checked |
| Fairness (**Analyze**) | Analyses the effect of the price on low-income families and considers what the school could do instead |
| Judgement (**Evaluate**) | Reaches a practical decision for the school, and defends it against the other view |

**ERQ 5: Lesson on an economic decision**

| Criterion | What a 3 looks like |
|---|---|
| Content | Cost over time, one label or company claim, and the four questions all used correctly |
| Pupil task | A task for Class 8 that is doable in the time and has pupils reason with evidence |
| Sensitivity | Does not shame pupils' families and takes care over real companies |
| Justification (**Create**) | Explains why each choice suits these pupils and this local decision |

Answers that show real checking should earn credit even when the evidence is incomplete. Reward a learner who writes "not known" over one who invents a fact.
