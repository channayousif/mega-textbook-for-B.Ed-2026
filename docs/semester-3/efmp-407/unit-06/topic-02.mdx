# Topic 6.2 - Corporate social responsibility

## A real classroom situation

In this illustrative teaching case, a mill near Nooriabad gives twenty new desks to a government primary school. There is a ceremony, a banner and a photographer. Hina, a trainee teacher, thanks the mill's manager on behalf of the staff. Next morning a pupil, Rafiq, puts up his hand. "Miss, the mill's dirty water runs past our field, and my brother works there with no contract. Are they good people or bad people?" Hina starts to answer and stops. The desks are real and pupils need them. So is the water. This topic gives her a better question than "good or bad".

## Explanation

### Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)

A **corporation** or company is a business set up to earn money for its owners. **Corporate social responsibility (CSR)** is a company's responsibility for how its business affects people and the environment. Zulfiqar et al. (2019) describe it as requiring a safe and fair workplace, environmentally friendly policies, a fair sharing of benefits in society and ethical business practice. They also report one scholar's short definition, "international private business self-regulation". That reminds us of something important: a company often sets and judges its own CSR.

The figure above shows four groups of people and things that a company's work affects:

- **Workers:** safe conditions, fair pay and treatment, and steady work.
- **Community:** the neighbours who live with its noise, traffic, water and jobs.
- **Environment:** the air, water, land and resources that it uses or pollutes.
- **Customers:** safe products and honest information.

#### Inside and outside the company

Researchers separate **internal CSR**, which concerns the company's own employees, from external CSR, which concerns everyone else. Zulfiqar et al. (2019) studied 530 employees of the top five banks in Pakistan. Employees who saw their bank as responsible towards them felt more part of it, and this went with greater engagement in their work. The link was stronger for people with a more group-centred outlook. The authors conclude that companies in Pakistan should treat employees as central to CSR. Their study has limits. It measured people at one point in time in one industry, so it cannot prove what caused what, and it may not apply to a brick kiln or a textile mill.

#### Correcting a common idea

**"CSR is the same as charity."** This is a very common idea, and it is easy to see why. When the Securities and Exchange Commission of Pakistan (SECP) issued its 2009 order on CSR, its press release spoke of "charitable and philanthropic activities", while also listing environmental protection and occupational safety (SECP, 2009). Zulfiqar et al. (2019) describe collectivist countries like Pakistan as "still very philanthropic" in their CSR, which means mostly about giving money away.

Here is the difference. **Charity asks what a company gives away from its profits. CSR also asks how the company earned those profits.** A mill can give desks and still pollute a canal. The gift is real, but it does not cancel the harm. If workers are left out, Zulfiqar et al. (2019) warn, CSR becomes merely a marketing strategy. Charity is not wrong. It is just a small part of the question.

So Hina can drop "good or bad" and help Rafiq to ask: "What did the mill give, and what does its work do to the water and to its workers?"

#### What the law asks in Pakistan

The law on this is changing, so read this as a snapshot and check it again.

- **2009:** the SECP's General Order required public companies to report their CSR activities in the directors' report (SECP, 2009). Even so, the Free and Fair Election Network (FAFEN, 2026) describes CSR under this order as a voluntary framework, because what a company does remains its own choice.
- **2017:** the Companies Act mentions CSR once, requiring listed companies to include their CSR activities in a business review (FAFEN, 2026).
- **2026:** the National Assembly passed a Corporate Social Responsibility Bill on 21 January. It requires listed companies and large unlisted companies to have a CSR policy and to disclose their CSR activities, with fines for failing to disclose. It encourages, but does not require, spending one percent of net profit after tax (FAFEN, 2026). The Senate was reported to have passed a CSR bill on 11 May 2026 (Associated Press of Pakistan, 2026). We could not confirm that it has become law, so look up its current status before you teach this.

Notice what such a law does. It makes companies *tell*. It does not prove that what they tell is true or enough.

#### Greenwashing

**Greenwashing** means misleading people about a company's environmental practices or about the environmental benefits of a product. Delmas and Burbano (2011) describe it as poor environmental performance joined with positive talk about that performance. They list forms that range from words with no clear meaning, such as "eco-friendly", to false claims. Other forms include giving no proof, and praising one narrow feature while saying nothing about the rest. A company can do the same with its social record, by praising a donation while hiding poor conditions. The table compares a real CSR effort with greenwashing.

#### A certificate is evidence, not proof

A certificate from an outside auditor is stronger evidence than a company's own words, yet it has limits too. In September 2012, a fire at the Ali Enterprises garment factory in Karachi killed more than 250 people (ECCHR, 2020). The factory held an SA8000 social certificate, after an audit in August 2012 (RINA, n.d.).

The two sides disagree about this audit. The labour rights groups who brought a complaint say that the auditor overlooked fire alarms and emergency exits (ECCHR, 2020). The auditor, RINA, says that the factory complied when audited, that conditions had changed by the time of the fire, and that it bears no responsibility (RINA, n.d.). A mediation in Italy ended without RINA signing the proposed agreement (ECCHR, 2020). We cannot settle that dispute here. We can draw a narrower lesson: a certificate shows what someone reported at one time. It should be read together with what workers and neighbours say, and with what can be seen.

#### Worked example: testing a company's claim

Use four questions. Here is an invented case: "Company R, a large cement firm, says in an advertisement: *We are a green company. We planted 1,000 trees in Jamshoro last year.*"

**Question 1: What exactly is claimed?** Two claims. A broad one ("a green company") and a narrow one (1,000 trees).

**Question 2: What can I check?** The trees: where are they, and how many are alive? Search the company's annual report for its emissions, water use and waste. Ask the provincial environment agency for any approval or complaint.

**Question 3: How does the scale compare?** Compare the action with the effect of the business. Planting 1,000 trees is small beside the kilns of a large cement plant. A small good act cannot support the large word "green".

**Question 4: Who benefits, and who was left out?** The advertisement benefits the company. It says nothing about workers, dust in nearby villages or customers.

**Judgement.** The tree-planting may be real, but the claim "green company" is not supported by this evidence. On what we have, it shows signs of greenwashing. The judgement is about the claim, and it could change if the company publishes its figures. Notice that we did not call anyone good or bad, and we did not say more than we could show.

## Activity: Desks and water

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

Return to the mill near Nooriabad. Two people prepare the mill manager's case, and two prepare the case of Rafiq's family. Without looking back, each side must use the words "charity" and "CSR" correctly. Each side speaks for three minutes. Then step out of your roles. Together, write three things the school could ask the mill to show, and decide whether the school should accept the desks. Give a reason that you could say aloud in front of pupils. Be ready to explain your decision to another group.

Answer from memory, then check against the explanation.

2. Explain the difference between CSR and charity, using one example. *(Understand)*
3. A soap company prints "100% eco-friendly" on its wrapper, and gives no other information. Use the four questions to say what you would check first and why. *(Apply)*

## Summary

CSR is a company's responsibility for how its business affects workers, communities, the environment and customers. Charity is what it gives away, and it cannot cancel harm done in earning the money. Pakistani law increasingly asks companies to disclose CSR, but disclosure and certificates are evidence, not proof. A careful person asks what is claimed, what can be checked, how the scale compares and who benefits. Then that person judges the claim, not the people.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find one sign of a company's support in or near your practicum school, such as a banner, a donated water cooler or a sponsored event. If there is none, use an advertisement from a newspaper. Ask your cooperating teacher what the school received. Write down the company's claim, one thing you could check and one thing you do not know. Do not contact the company, and do not raise this with pupils without your teacher's agreement.

## Summative task

Choose one CSR claim made by a real company operating in Pakistan, from its website, an annual report or an advertisement. In about 400 words, use the four questions to evaluate it. Name at least two pieces of evidence that you could check, and report what you found. State your judgement of the claim and what would change your mind. Keep to what your evidence supports, and do not accuse the company of anything you cannot show.

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Understanding of CSR (2 marks) | Separates CSR from charity, and considers workers, community, environment and customers | Uses CSR accurately but only for one group | Treats CSR as charity |
| Use of checkable evidence (4 marks) | Two or more sources checked; company's words separated from independent evidence | Evidence named but only from the company | No evidence |
| Evaluation of the claim (4 marks) | Clear judgement that follows from the evidence, compares scale, and says what would change it | Judgement with general reasons | No judgement, or unsupported accusations |

The evaluation criterion needs analysis and judgement. Explain how the evidence leads to your conclusion.
