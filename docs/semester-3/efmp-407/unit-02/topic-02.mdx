# Topic 2.2 - ESD and environmental education: what is the difference?

## A real classroom situation

In a Karachi primary class, pupils sort clean paper and plastic cards into groups and explain how waste can harm animals. A second class studies the same materials, then compares reuse costs and asks who can afford a reusable lunch container. Its pupils revise a school proposal after hearing different needs. Both teachers call their work ESD. A trainee must explain what each lesson actually includes without dismissing useful environmental learning.

## Explanation

### ESD compared with environmental education

**Environmental education (EE)** develops understanding, concern and action about the environment and people's relationships with it. **ESD**, introduced in Topic 2.1, explicitly connects environmental questions with social and economic questions about sustainable futures. These are overlapping traditions, not two sealed boxes. Maurer and Bogner's (2019) study of university entrants examines both this distinction and its contested boundaries. Their study is not evidence about the understanding of Sindh schoolchildren ([article](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0208910)).

A common misconception is that **ESD is just a new name for environmental education**. The name alone does not tell us the breadth of a lesson. A lesson about animals can develop valuable environmental knowledge. An ESD approach to the same issue might also ask how a decision affects livelihoods, fair access and future users. However, EE can itself include social questions, values, participation and action. Calling it “only facts about nature” misrepresents it.

Use the following comparison as a planning aid. It shows differences of emphasis, not a rule that prevents a teacher from crossing a boundary.

| Feature | EE emphasis | ESD emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| Starting focus | Environmental relationships and protection | Linked sustainability questions |
| Scope | People and their environment, including human causes and responses | Explicit links across environmental, social and economic dimensions |
| Aim | Understanding and responsible participation in environmental matters | Informed choices about shared needs, limits and futures |
| Possible activity | Explain waste effects and investigate ways to prevent harm | Compare waste options for harm, cost and fairness, then justify a feasible response |

Here is a worked classification of the opening case. In the first lesson, pupils identify materials and explain harm to animals. The evidence supports an EE emphasis. We should not claim that pupils considered affordability, because the case does not say they did. This does not make the lesson poor. It may be an appropriate first step before a wider decision-making task.

In the second lesson, pupils retain the environmental concern and add costs and access. A rule requiring every child to buy an expensive container might reduce some waste but exclude a child who cannot buy one. Pupils compare alternatives and revise a proposal. This is stronger evidence of an ESD approach because the dimensions affect the decision. Merely listing “environment, society, economy” on a poster would be weaker evidence.

Follow four moves when comparing lessons. First, describe the actual pupil work. Second, identify the dimensions that the work makes pupils connect. Third, examine whether pupils explain a choice and its effects. Fourth, give a qualified judgement. “This account shows an EE emphasis, with scope to develop an ESD task” is more careful than claiming the lesson could never count as ESD. Missing description is not proof that the teacher omitted something in practice.

Consider another fictional example from Mirpurkhas. A school plans to replace shade trees with a paved assembly space. An EE lesson could investigate shade, birds and soil. A wider ESD task could preserve that evidence while comparing heat, cost, accessibility and space for different activities. Pupils might suggest keeping trees and improving a smaller path. Their proposal still needs to explain the environmental effects, rather than using a social benefit to avoid them.

Suppose pupils argue that paving is cheaper to maintain. Ask what is included in “cheaper”. Is it only this year's cleaning cost? Does the comparison include future repairs, lost shade or an accessible route? The class need not invent prices. The teacher can supply a clearly labelled hypothetical cost card and ask which missing information would matter. A request for better evidence can be a sound conclusion.

This is where a trade-off becomes visible. A **trade-off** occurs when gaining one benefit brings a cost or loss elsewhere. Finding a trade-off does not mean every option is equally good. A plan may still be unacceptable if it creates avoidable harm or excludes pupils. The learner should identify the affected people or living things, compare options and explain why a chosen response is reasonable under the stated conditions.

The nested-circle figure is one way to organise a lesson, not a settled history of the two fields. It helps a novice remember to add connected questions. It can also mislead if it suggests that EE has no concern for people or that ESD automatically improves environmental protection. Maurer and Bogner (2019) describe disagreement about their relationship. Keep the environmental evidence visible when widening the discussion.

For example, imagine a group proposes replacing every paper item with plastic because plastic lasts longer. The claim needs investigation: which item, how often used, what cleaning is possible, and what happens after use? “Economic benefit” is not a reason to ignore waste or damage. A useful comparison has conditions, not an automatic winner. The teacher can accept a reasoned provisional choice and require a review after more evidence.

For your own planning, distinguish the **topic**, the **method** and the **learning evidence**. “Trees” is a topic. “Group discussion” is a method. A pupil's explanation of how a path affects roots, access and cost is evidence. Neither an environmental topic nor a group method is sufficient by itself to identify the whole approach. Read the objectives, questions and pupil products together.

Assessment should preserve that same care. A learner who questions an expensive reuse rule may understand sustainability well. Mark the link between evidence and judgement. Do not mark agreement with the teacher's preferred purchase. Let pupils propose different options if they account for environmental limits and other people's needs. This makes comparison an exercise in reasoning rather than a contest to use the newest label.

## Activity: Compare two school plans

**Work in pairs for about 20 minutes.** Plan A asks a Larkana class to identify damage caused by litter and explain two ways to reduce it. Plan B adds a comparison of school-bin locations, who can reach them and who will empty them. Both use drawings and clean paper cards, not collected waste.

Close the book and recall the four comparison moves. One partner describes the pupil evidence in Plan A; the other does the same for Plan B. Exchange notes and challenge any unsupported claim. Together, write a four-sentence comparison and one revision to Plan A. The revision must preserve its environmental learning. Swap your comparison with another pair and check whether its judgement follows from the described work.

## Check your understanding

1. Define environmental education in your own words and give one classroom focus. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why an ESD lesson must do more than add three dimension labels to an environmental poster. *(Understand)*
3. A school garden lesson examines soil and insects. Add a question about access and a question about costs that would change the planting decision. *(Apply)*

## Summary

EE and ESD share concern, inquiry and action. ESD makes links across sustainability dimensions explicit, but EE can also address society and choices. Classify a lesson through what learners actually examine and justify, and state where the description leaves uncertainty.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can compare EE and ESD without reducing EE to memorising nature facts.
- [ ] I can identify an environmental, social and economic link in a lesson decision.
- [ ] I can justify a qualified classification using the pupils' work as evidence.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher for one environment-related activity description. In a brief private note, separate its stated evidence from questions you would need to ask. Suggest one connected question that could widen the learning. Do not describe an unmentioned dimension as definitely absent, and do not label the teacher's competence from this single example.

## Summative task

Write 250-300 words comparing two fictional lessons. Lesson A asks pupils to record shade and explain why trees matter. Lesson B asks them to use that record while comparing a tree-protection plan with a paved route for pupils with mobility needs and a limited repair budget. Classify each emphasis, explain the overlap, and propose a revision that protects access and environmental learning. Do not assume either plan has a complete solution. *(Analyze)*

**Mini-rubric, 10 marks:** distinctions and overlap (0-2: inaccurate, partly accurate, accurate with qualification); evidence-based comparison (0-3: labels only, one supported contrast, contrasts tied to pupil work); linked effects (0-3: isolated concerns, two linked dimensions, three linked dimensions); revision (0-2: missing, vague, feasible and responsive to the case).
