# Topic 2.1 - UNESCO's framework for ESD

## A real classroom situation

At a government primary school near Hyderabad, a trainee gives a clear lesson about saving water. Pupils can repeat three facts, yet the school tank still overflows at break. One pupil says that reporting a broken tap is an adult's job. Another worries that closing the tap quickly will leave younger children without time to wash. The trainee asks what a good lesson should help pupils know, consider and do.

## Explanation

### The UNESCO framework of ESD

**Education for Sustainable Development (ESD)** helps learners make informed and responsible choices about linked environmental, social and economic concerns, including effects on future generations. **UNESCO** is the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization. Its 2017 learning-objectives guide offers a framework, or an organised way to plan learning, for the Sustainable Development Goals. It is guidance to adapt, rather than one fixed lesson script for every school ([UNESCO, 2017](https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000247444), introduction).

A useful way into this framework is through three learning domains. **Cognitive** learning concerns knowledge and thinking. **Socio-emotional** learning concerns values, feelings and relationships. **Behavioural** learning concerns action. A **competency** is the ability to use learning in a situation. UNESCO identifies eight cross-cutting competencies: systems thinking, anticipatory, normative, strategic, collaboration, critical thinking, self-awareness and integrated problem-solving (2017, pp. 10-11).

Here is an original planning example. The trainee replaces the broad instruction, “Understand water conservation”, with three observable objectives. After the lesson, a pupil can explain why a tank overflows, listen to a younger child's access concern, and help prepare a report to the school adult responsible for repairs. The objectives connect a cause, another person's needs and a possible action. Repair work itself stays with adults.

These are different learning demands. A pupil who explains the leak has not yet shown that they can listen fairly. A pupil who cares about water may still need help reading a tally. A pupil who follows a teacher's instruction once has not necessarily learned to choose an action independently. Plan evidence for each demand rather than treating one correct definition as proof of all three.

In a short lesson, the teacher might provide an invented tank record. Before break, the tank contains 80 litres. During break, 15 litres enter, pupils use 10 litres, and 5 litres overflow. Pupils find the final amount and explain the loss. They then compare two proposed responses: stop all washing at break, or ask an adult to check the inlet while protecting time for washing. The arithmetic alone cannot decide which response is fair.

For the social discussion, the teacher gives role cards without naming real pupils. A younger child needs help reaching the tap. A caretaker cannot watch the tank every minute. A teacher needs washing to finish before class. Groups listen to each role and revise their proposal. No child has to disclose a private household shortage. For action evidence, pupils produce a short report with the observation, the possible cause, the responsible adult and a date to check what changed.

The eight competencies help the trainee examine the quality of this work. The classroom examples below are author-created applications, not UNESCO's prescribed activities.

| Competency | A question a trainee can use | Possible classroom evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Systems thinking | What affects what? | A pupil links the inlet, tank level and overflow. |
| Anticipatory thinking | What might happen next? | A group compares a dry-week and a normal-week plan. |
| Normative thinking | Which values guide this choice? | Pupils explain why younger children's access matters. |
| Strategic thinking | How could a group carry out a change? | A report names a responsible adult and follow-up date. |
| Collaboration | Can we work with different needs? | A group revises a plan after hearing another role. |
| Critical thinking | What claim should we question? | Pupils ask whether stopping washing solves the problem. |
| Self-awareness | What is my own role or assumption? | A pupil explains a change in their first proposal. |
| Integrated problem-solving | Can we bring these ways of thinking together? | A group combines evidence, fairness and a feasible action. |

<Figure id="fig-U2-2" src="/img/figures/efmp-407/unit-02/fig-U2-2.svg" alt="Eight sustainability competencies connect tracing water relationships, considering futures and values, planning, collaboration, questioning, reflection and combined problem-solving." />

Do not turn this table into eight definitions to memorise in a single primary lesson. Choose the competencies that help with the task. In the first lesson, a simple relationship and respectful group discussion may be enough. Later, pupils can compare future possibilities or revisit a plan. Competency grows through use across situations, not through a teacher's tick beside a word.

A common misconception is that **ESD is a separate subject added to the timetable**. The school-water example can sit within existing mathematics, science and language work. Pupils calculate amounts in mathematics, explain a cause in science, and write a clear report in language. The teacher still needs a clear subject purpose. A sustainability label does not excuse an unclear calculation or a weak explanation.

Another mistake is to count any visible activity as successful ESD. A class may chant a slogan while the teacher makes every decision. To examine learning, ask a pupil to explain the choice, describe a competing need, and say what evidence could change the proposal. The result may be a modest report rather than an impressive display. This distinction matters where schools have limited money or time.

Suppose an adult repairs the inlet and the overflow stops. That is a useful school outcome, but it does not prove every pupil met the objectives. Conversely, a pupil may reason carefully while a repair is delayed. Judge the pupil's explanation and planning separately from the adult-controlled repair. Avoid giving higher marks to pupils whose families can buy equipment or bring resources to school.

A final check is to ask whether the three domains support one another. An action without knowledge may miss the cause. Knowledge without attention to other people can produce an unfair plan. Concern without a feasible next step can leave pupils frustrated. The trainee's task is to connect these demands at a level pupils can manage, then collect small pieces of evidence that show what they learned.

## Activity: Build a three-domain lesson

**Work in groups of three for about 20 minutes.** Use this fictional case: a Sukkur school wants to reduce discarded paper, but some pupils need larger writing sheets. Start with three minutes of closed-book recall of the learning domains. Then give each member a planning role: knowledge evidence, fairness evidence, or action evidence.

Draft one objective per domain and one way to observe it. Exchange roles and point out where the evidence does not match the objective. Agree on a revised plan that protects access for pupils who need more paper. Select two competencies that the plan would develop and explain your choices to another group. Keep the original plan beside the revision so the change is visible.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the three learning domains and explain one in your own words. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why a pupil's accurate account of water loss does not, by itself, show learning in all three domains. *(Understand)*
3. A class must decide where to place a shared reading box. Write one objective about knowledge, one about others' needs and one about a feasible action. *(Apply)*

## Summary

ESD planning connects what learners understand, how they consider other people and how they act. Competencies describe ways of using learning across situations. A useful lesson has evidence of learning that pupils can produce, even when adults control the final school decision.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can distinguish the three learning domains using classroom evidence.
- [ ] I can explain two sustainability competencies without merely repeating their names.
- [ ] I can connect a subject objective, a fairness concern and a feasible action in one lesson.

## Try this at your practicum school

With your cooperating teacher, examine one existing lesson plan for ten minutes. Identify which learning domain has clear evidence and which has less. Suggest one small addition using the materials already available. Record the suggestion without pupil names. You are examining a plan, not rating the teacher or checking children's home behaviour.

## Summative task

Write a 250-300-word analysis of a lesson in which pupils copy water-saving slogans and are then told to stop using the tap at break. Identify what the lesson does teach, what evidence is missing, and who might be affected by the proposed rule. Revise it with three connected objectives and two competency choices. Justify how your revision preserves washing while investigating water loss. *(Analyze)*

**Mini-rubric, 10 marks:** domain distinctions (0-3: absent, partly separated, all three clearly evidenced); analysis of the rule's effects (0-3: assertion, one supported effect, competing needs examined); revised objectives and evidence (0-2: missing, partly matched, fully matched); competency choices (0-2: labels only, one reason, two task-specific reasons).
