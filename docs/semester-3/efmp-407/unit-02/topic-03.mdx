# Topic 2.3 - Learning that transforms, and thinking in systems

## A real classroom situation

At a teacher-training class in Khairpur, a trainee says families waste water because they do not care. A classmate asks whether an unreliable supply might make people store extra water. The tutor gives a fictional village tank record instead of asking anyone to disclose home conditions. The group discovers that a leaking pipe and uncertain delivery times could affect its first judgement. Its next task is to reconsider the judgement and trace the connections.

## Explanation

### Transformative learning

**An assumption** is something we accept as true before checking it. **Transformative learning** involves reconsidering a basic assumption that shapes how we understand a situation. Learning a new fact can help this process, but a changed explanation matters more than repeating a new slogan. UNESCO's *Greening Curriculum Guidance* (2024) connects transformative learning with reflection and different perspectives. It provides a sustainability-education basis for examining assumptions ([official PDF](https://www.unesco.org/sites/default/files/medias/fichiers/2024/09/Greening%20curriculum%20guidance%20Teaching%20and%20learning%20for%20climate%20action.pdf)).

In the opening case, the assumption is that apparent waste proves a lack of care. The tutor does not announce that all storage is good. Instead, learners compare reasons and evidence. Perhaps some storage responds to unreliable delivery, while a damaged container loses water unnecessarily. A revised judgement can hold both possibilities. It becomes more careful because it distinguishes causes and asks what would settle uncertainty.

Use a simple teaching cycle: experience something puzzling, question the assumption behind your first explanation, reflect with evidence and other people, and try a different action. Then examine the new experience. This cycle is a classroom scaffold, not a claim that everyone changes through four fixed stages. A learner may return to an earlier question or keep an earlier view after examining good reasons.

Here is a worked reflection. A trainee first writes, “A family with several containers is careless.” After reading the fictional supply record, she writes, “I assumed storage meant excess use. The record suggests that uncertain delivery can make storage reasonable. I still need evidence about leaks and how much water is needed.” Her proposed action changes from blaming families to asking how the school can report losses and communicate delivery times. The revision names the assumption, the evidence and the limit of the new claim.

A common misconception is that **transformative learning means changing pupils' beliefs for them**. A teacher may challenge an unsupported claim, but should not reward a pupil for accepting the teacher's personal opinion. Give learners room to ask questions and disagree with reasons. Mark how they use evidence, consider another perspective and explain a revision or a justified decision to retain their view. A forced confession of being wrong is poor evidence of reflection.

For a school-age class, keep the issue concrete and emotionally manageable. Use a fictional case rather than a child's household. Give quiet writing time before discussion. A pupil can revise an explanation privately without speaking about family habits. If learners disagree, return to the question and available evidence rather than deciding which pupil is a better person.

### Systems thinking

**Systems thinking** means examining relationships among parts and how they affect the whole over time. A **system boundary** states what your model includes and leaves outside. A **stock** is an amount held at a given time, such as water in a tank. A **flow** adds to or removes that stock, such as a delivery, use or leakage. These simple modelling terms let us apply UNESCO's systems-thinking competency to a classroom example (UNESCO, 2017, p. 10).

Take a fictional village tank used for a school and nearby homes. Our first boundary includes the tank, its inlet, outlets, users and the person who reports repairs. It leaves out the full regional supply network. That makes the model manageable, but means it cannot explain every interruption. Draw arrows from delivery to tank, from tank to use and leakage, and from observations to a repair request. State what each arrow means. A line without an explanation can hide a guess.

Start with numbers the class can check. The tank holds 100 litres at the start of a period. A delivery adds 40 litres. Users take 30 litres, and 10 litres leak. The end stock is 100 + 40 - 30 - 10 = 100 litres. These are invented quantities, not measurements of a real village. An unchanged stock does not mean no water was lost. Water entered and left during the period, including a preventable loss.

Now keep delivery and use the same but reduce leakage to 2 litres after an adult repairs the pipe. The end stock becomes 108 litres. That comparison holds other quantities constant to show the effect of one change. In reality, users might change how much they take, or the delivery might arrive late. A model should identify these conditions rather than promise that a repair guarantees a particular future stock.

A **feedback loop** occurs when a change travels through linked effects and returns to influence its starting condition. For a simple balancing loop, suppose a lower tank level triggers a low-level report. The report leads an adult to arrange an extra delivery. The delivery raises the level and reduces the shortage. Trace the whole loop aloud: shortage increases, reporting increases, supply response increases, shortage decreases. Reporting alone cannot restore water unless somebody can respond.

<Figure id="fig-U2-6" src="/img/figures/efmp-407/unit-02/fig-U2-6.svg" alt="Water enters a village tank and leaves through use and leaks. A low level prompts a report and delayed adult delivery that restores the level, forming a balancing loop." />

A **delay** is the time between an action and its effect. If delivery takes two days, a report today cannot solve today's shortage. A class might therefore compare a timely report with a later report. It should also ask who can report, who hears the message and whether the response helps everyone. These links connect the physical system with social access and available resources.

The misconception that **systems thinking needs advanced mathematics** can prevent trainees from trying it. An arrow diagram with clearly stated reasons is a good beginning. Yet a diagram must do more than list “water, people, school”. Show a direction, explain the effect, and trace at least one consequence. More arrows do not automatically produce a better model. Remove a link you cannot explain, or mark it as a question to investigate.

Compare two interventions. A poster telling children to use less water addresses use, but leaves a leaking pipe unchanged. A repair addresses the leak, but may still leave a reporting delay. Neither is a complete explanation on its own. A reasoned proposal could combine an adult repair request, a simple observation routine and protection for essential washing. It should name which part each action can change and what it cannot control.

Do not confuse an association with a demonstrated cause. A tank level that falls on a hot day may reflect increased use, a late delivery, a leak or several influences. The model proposes relationships to examine. Ask what observation could distinguish them. A record of delivery times might test one explanation; an adult's maintenance check might test another. Pupils need not enter unsafe areas or inspect pipes themselves.

Reflection and systems thinking support each other. The model can reveal that a personal judgement ignored a supply constraint. Reflection can reveal that the model ignored a quieter group of users. Neither removes responsibility for action. Together, they help a learner choose an action that addresses a plausible cause while remaining willing to revise it after new evidence.

## Activity: Trace and revise

**Work in groups of four for about 25 minutes.** Use the invented tank figures above. Assign a stock recorder, an arrow explainer, an assumptions checker and a spokesperson. First, recall the meanings of stock and flow without looking. Reconstruct the calculation and draw a boundary around your model.

Add the low-level reporting loop and mark the delay. Compare a poster-only response with a repair-and-report response. Each member must name one assumption before the group chooses a plan. Rotate the spokesperson and explain the plan to another group. After hearing a challenge, record one revision or explain with evidence why you retained the plan. No group needs to investigate an actual water source.

## Check your understanding

1. State what an assumption is, then distinguish a stock from a flow using water examples. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why being told to agree with a teacher is weak evidence of transformative learning. *(Understand)*
3. A tank starts with 60 litres, receives 20, supplies 15 and loses 5 through a leak. Calculate the end stock and describe a reporting response with a possible delay. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Transformative learning questions the assumptions behind a judgement while preserving the learner's ability to reason. Systems thinking traces relationships, amounts, feedback and delays. Together they help trainees move from blame or isolated actions towards explanations that can be checked and revised.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can identify an assumption and explain how evidence might change it.
- [ ] I can distinguish stocks, flows and a complete feedback loop.
- [ ] I can propose an action while naming a delay, a limit and a way to check its effect.

## Try this at your practicum school

With the cooperating teacher, sketch a safe classroom system such as borrowing and returning books. Identify the stock, two flows and a reporting response when books are missing. Discuss one assumption in your sketch. Record no borrower names. Keep the exercise on paper; do not inspect tanks, pipes or electrical equipment.

## Summative task

Analyse this claim in 250-300 words: “More water-saving posters will solve the tank shortage.” Use the opening case to draw a stock-and-flow model and a complete feedback loop. Identify one assumption, explain an alternative cause and propose a safe action with a delay and a follow-up check. Explain how you would invite a pupil to reconsider the claim without demanding agreement. *(Analyze)*

**Mini-rubric, 10 marks:** causal model (0-3: disconnected list, some explained links, clear stock/flows and complete loop); assumption and evidence (0-3: blame, assumption named, alternative explanation tested); action and limits (0-2: vague, action linked, action with delay and check); learner agency (0-2: imposed opinion, invitation without evidence, reasoned dialogue).
