# Topic 1.1 - What sustainable development means

## A real classroom situation

In this illustrative teaching case, Sadia is a trainee teacher at a government girls' school in Thatta district. Her Class 8 is talking about a new fish-processing unit near the creek. Marvi says, "My uncle got a job there, so this is development." Nasreen says, "My father says the waste is killing the fish he catches. How is that development?" Both girls look at Sadia. Sadia has used the words "sustainable development" many times, but she has never had to explain them to a child. Can one project be development for one family and harm for another? This topic gives you a clear way to answer.

## Explanation

### Definitions and evolution of sustainable development

#### What the words mean

**Development** means change that makes people's lives better: more income, better health, more schooling, more choices. **Sustainable** means able to continue without using up what the activity depends on. A well is sustainable if the rain refills it as fast as people draw from it.

**Sustainable development** joins the two words. It asks whether a better life today can continue, and whether it can reach everyone, now and later.

#### The best-known definition

The World Commission on Environment and Development (1987) described sustainable development as development that meets "the needs of the present" without harming "the ability of future generations to meet their own needs". In plain words: meet today's needs, and leave your grandchildren able to meet theirs.

The Commission said the definition holds two key ideas (World Commission on Environment and Development, 1987, ch. 2).

1. **Needs.** People's essential needs come first, above all the needs of the world's poor. Food, safe water, shelter, work and schooling are not luxuries.
2. **Limits.** The environment can only meet present and future needs up to a point. That point depends on our technology and on how society is organised. A new way of irrigating can move the limit. It cannot remove it.

The definition therefore does not say "stop development". It says that development must be judged by who benefits and by what is left behind.

#### How the idea evolved

The idea did not appear in one day. Purvis, Mao and Robinson (2019) trace it through these steps.

1. **Growth as the goal.** After the Second World War, many governments treated economic growth as the main meaning of development.
2. **Two complaints.** By the late 1960s and early 1970s, one group of critics said growth was damaging nature on a finite planet. Another said growth was not reaching the poor. Purvis et al. (2019) argue that sustainable development grew from these two complaints together.
3. **The name.** The phrase first appeared prominently in 1980, in the *World Conservation Strategy* (Purvis et al., 2019).
4. **Wide use.** The 1987 report of the World Commission on Environment and Development carried the idea into international policy. Topic 1.2 explains this report.
5. **Political promise.** At the 1992 Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro, world leaders pledged support for the principle (Purvis et al., 2019).

Each step added something. The idea began with concern for nature, then took in people's needs, and then became a promise made by governments.

Education has been part of this story too. Secondary accounts of Orr (1992) describe his concept of ecological literacy, which means understanding how the natural world supports human life (the original book was not directly consulted for this unit). These accounts report his argument that what we include or leave out of our lessons teaches pupils whether they are part of nature or separate from it. So a teacher is already teaching something about sustainability, even without using the word.

#### There is no single agreed definition

The figure above shows three definitions, and there are many more. The Commission itself said that interpretations "will vary" (World Commission on Environment and Development, 1987, ch. 2). Purvis et al. (2019) show that writers have understood sustainability in conflicting ways. This does not make the idea useless. It means you should always ask a speaker, "What do you mean by sustainable here, and for whom?"

#### Two ideas to correct

**"Development and sustainability are opposites."** They are not. The term was made to join them. A family without clean water needs development, and it needs that development to last. The real opposite of sustainable development is development that cannot last or that leaves some people to pay for it.

**"Sustainability is a concern for rich countries only."** The Commission wrote that sustainability applies to "all countries", developed or developing (World Commission on Environment and Development, 1987, ch. 2). Pakistan shows why. The 2022 floods affected 33 million people and hit the poorest districts especially hard (World Bank, 2022). People with the least savings have the most to lose when land, water and weather change. Topic 1.3 returns to these floods.

#### Worked example: testing a decision against the definition

Here is a way to use the definition. Take this illustrative decision: a village council near Mirpurkhas wants to install a large motor pump on its well, so that every home gets water within a year.

| Question | Applied to the pump | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Whose needs does it meet now? | All homes, including poor ones who now walk far for water | Strong: an essential need |
| What limit does it press on? | The groundwater. Faster pumping may lower the water level or draw in salty water | Unknown: the council must measure it |
| What is left for later? | If pumping is faster than refilling, children may inherit a dry or salty well | A risk |
| Verdict | Development, yes. Sustainable only if pumping stays within what the well can refill | Conditional |

The definition did not give a simple yes or no. It told the council what to find out next. Now look again at Sadia's class. Marvi is right that jobs meet a need. Nasreen is right that the waste presses on a limit and takes away another family's living. Using the definition, Sadia can tell them that both are describing the same project, and that the project is not yet sustainable.

## Activity: Three decisions, four questions

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

Your teacher gives each group these three illustrative decisions.

- A school drills a borehole and pumps water much faster than the rain refills it.
- A neighbourhood in Karachi collects roof rainwater in shared tanks, but only families who can pay join.
- A new road links villages to a market and to a high school, and the builders cut down the old roadside trees.

Each person takes one role: needs, limits, future generations or verdict. For each decision, the first three people state what they see from their role, and the fourth person gives a verdict with a reason. Rotate roles after each decision. Finally, swap one verdict with another group and try to change their mind, using the words "needs" and "limits".

## Check your understanding

Answer from memory, then check against the explanation.

1. State the Brundtland definition of sustainable development in your own words, in one sentence. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why the two ideas of "needs" and "limits" must be used together, not one at a time. *(Understand)*
3. A friend says, "Building this new market is development, so it must be sustainable." Use the four questions from the worked example to tell your friend what they still need to find out. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Sustainable development means meeting people's needs today, the needs of the poor first, while staying within limits so that later generations can meet their needs too. The idea grew out of two complaints about growth: it harmed nature and it missed the poor. There is no single agreed definition, so the useful habit is to ask who benefits, what limit is pressed and what is left for later.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask three people at your school (a teacher, a member of staff and a pupil) what "development" means for your area. Note one example from each. Then write two lines: whose needs each example meets, and what limit it may press on. Bring your notes to class.

## Summative task

Choose one real or planned change in your own area, such as a road, a market, a water scheme or a housing colony. In about 300 words, test it against the definition, using the four questions. End with a verdict and the one thing someone must find out before the verdict can be final. Separate what you know from what you assume.

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Use of the definition (2 marks) | Uses needs, limits and future generations accurately | Uses one or two of them accurately | Definition missing or wrong |
| Analysis of the case (4 marks) | Breaks the case into who benefits, what limit is pressed and what is left, with evidence | Describes the case with little breakdown | Only restates the case |
| Verdict and next step (4 marks) | Gives a reasoned verdict, and separates knowledge from assumption | Gives a verdict with general reasons | No verdict or no reasons |

The analysis and verdict criteria need real analysis. Show how the evidence leads to your conclusion. A longer description does not meet them.
