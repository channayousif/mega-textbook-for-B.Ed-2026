# Topic 1.3 - The three dimensions of sustainability

## A real classroom situation

In this illustrative teaching case, a primary school near Dadu was badly damaged in the 2022 floods. Shazia, a trainee teacher, sits in on the School Management Committee as it plans the rebuilding. The contractor says, "Rebuild on the old floor. It is cheapest and you will be back in class within three months." An older teacher says, "The water will come again. Raise the floor and plant trees to hold the bank." A mother says, "Whatever you do, build a boundary wall and a toilet, or my daughter will not come back." Then the chairperson asks, "Whose advice do we follow?" Shazia notices that each speaker is right about something and silent about the rest. This topic gives her a way to hold all three in view.

## Explanation

### Dimensions of sustainability: environmental, economic, social

A **dimension** here means one side of a question that you must look at. Sustainability is usually discussed through three dimensions. The Rio+20 outcome document speaks of "the three dimensions" of sustainable development and asks countries to integrate them (United Nations, 2012, paragraphs 3 and 75).

#### The three dimensions

**The environmental dimension** asks: does this protect the land, water, air and living things that life depends on? In Shazia's meeting, the older teacher is speaking from this dimension.

**The economic dimension** asks: does this support livelihoods, and can people carry its cost over time? It is wider than the first price. The cheapest floor today can be the most expensive floor if it must be rebuilt after the next flood. The contractor speaks from this dimension, but only about the first price.

**The social dimension** asks: is this fair? Does it support health, education and safety? Do the people affected have a voice? The mother is speaking from this dimension. A school that girls cannot attend has not met its purpose.

#### Why the three depend on each other

The three are not separate boxes. A change in one moves the others.

- In the Indus Delta, mangrove species have been lost, catches of valuable fish have fallen, and the coastal families who depend on these resources have become poorer (IUCN Pakistan, 2003). An environmental change has become an economic and a social one.
- If a family loses its income, its children may leave school. An economic change has become a social one.

The common picture is three overlapping circles with sustainable development where they all meet. Purvis et al. (2019) trace this picture to an economist, Barbier, writing in 1987. Barbier described three systems (biological, economic and social) and the trade-offs between them. A **trade-off** means gaining something on one side by giving up something on another.

The picture is helpful, but do not treat it as a law. Purvis et al. (2019) found no single origin for the three-pillar idea and no rigorous theory behind it. Some writers add a cultural or institutional dimension. Others draw the economy inside society, and society inside the environment, because no economy can exist without nature. In this course we use the three circles because your course guide and the UN use them. Remember that they are a tool for thinking, not a fact of nature.

#### Correcting a common idea

**"Sustainable development only means protecting the environment."** Many people hear "sustainable" and think of trees. But protecting a forest by removing the people who live from it, with no other income, fails on two of the three dimensions. The environment is one dimension, not the whole.

The 2022 floods show all three together. The World Bank (2022) reports that they affected 33 million people, caused damage above USD 14.9 billion and economic losses of about USD 15.2 billion, and fell hardest on Sindh, with close to 70 percent of the damage and losses. The poorest districts were especially affected. A flood is a natural event, but who suffers and how much it costs depend on earlier decisions about where and how we build.

#### Worked example: analysing a dam against all three

Take the Diamer Basha Dam, a real project on the Indus. Follow these steps, and separate what the source says from what you still need to find out.

**Step 1: State the decision and its aim.** Build a large dam to deal with shortages of energy and water. The planned figures are 4,500 MW of electricity and 6.4 million acre-feet of storage (Ilyas et al., 2025).

**Step 2: Economic.** Power and stored water could support farms, homes and industry. But the work has been delayed for years. *To find out:* what does it cost, who pays, and who gets the power and the water?

**Step 3: Social.** The reservoir will flood 31 villages and affect 28,650 people, displacing more than 4,135 households (Ilyas et al., 2025). In a survey of 425 affected household heads, Ilyas et al. (2025) found that the delay itself increased psychological and development harm. The waiting itself has a cost. *To find out:* what happens to their land, income and schools?

**Step 4: Environmental.** Our source does not study this dimension, so we must not guess. *To find out:* what happens to the river, the sediment and the living things downstream? Remember the Indus Delta. A full analysis needs an environmental study.

**Step 5: Judge.** An honest verdict at this stage: strong possible economic benefit, serious social cost that is already being paid, and an environmental question this source cannot answer. So the decision cannot be called sustainable or unsustainable until the gaps are filled. Notice that a good analysis can end with a precise list of what is missing.

**A law you should know.** In Sindh, the Sindh Environmental Protection Act 2014 (section 17) says that the proponent of a project in a prescribed category cannot start construction or operation without filing an environmental examination or impact assessment with the provincial agency and getting its approval. Reviews of impact assessments must include public participation. A law like this brings the environmental and social dimensions into the decision before work begins.

## Activity: One school, three chairs

Work in groups of three. About 25 minutes.

Return to the Dadu school. Each person sits in one "chair": environmental, economic or social. Without looking back at the text, each person gives the committee one recommendation from their chair, in two minutes. Next, as a group, list one trade-off between each pair of chairs. Finally, agree on a single plan the committee can afford. (Assume a tight emergency budget that covers only two of the three proposals, or state your group's own financial constraints and assumptions.) Write down what your plan gives up. Share your plan with another group. They must name the dimension they think you treated least well, with a reason.

## Check your understanding

Answer from memory, then check against the explanation.

1. Name the three dimensions, and write the question each one asks. *(Remember)*
2. Explain, using the Indus Delta, how a change in one dimension can become a change in another. *(Understand)*
3. A new brick kiln near a village gives 50 jobs, but its smoke drifts over the primary school. Say what each dimension would ask about the kiln. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Sustainability has three commonly used dimensions: environmental, economic and social. They depend on each other, so a decision that looks good on one side can cause harm on another. Sound analysis looks at all three, names the trade-offs and says honestly what is still unknown. The three-circle picture is a useful tool, not a fact of nature.

## Try this at your practicum school

Choose one recent decision at your practicum school, such as a new tap, a repair, a fee, a rule about plastic or a change of timetable. Ask the head teacher or your cooperating teacher why it was made. Then note which dimension the reasons came from, and which dimension nobody mentioned.

## Summative task

Choose one Pakistani development decision: a dam, a housing scheme, a road or a factory. In about 400 words, analyse it against all three dimensions, following the steps of the worked example. Use at least one checked source, and label anything you cannot verify as unknown. End with a judgement and one change that would make the decision more sustainable.

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coverage of the dimensions (3 marks) | All three treated accurately with case evidence | Two treated well, or all three thinly | One dimension only |
| Analysis of links and trade-offs (4 marks) | Shows how the dimensions affect each other and names at least one trade-off | Lists effects without linking them | No analysis |
| Judgement and honesty about evidence (3 marks) | Reasoned judgement and a realistic change; separates known from unknown | Judgement with general reasons | No judgement, or invented facts |

The analysis and judgement criteria need analysis and evaluation. Explain how the evidence leads to your judgement.
