# Topic 5.2 - Human rights and inclusion

## A real classroom situation

In a government primary and lower secondary school in Badin district, trainee teacher Tariq is assigned to teach Class 7. His classroom has 48 pupils with wide differences in life circumstances. Three pupils have physical impairments: Asif uses crutches because of a childhood illness, and two pupils have mild visual impairments but cannot afford corrective eyeglasses. Several children belong to families displaced by the 2022 monsoon floods who currently live in temporary reed-thatched dwellings near the canal bank; these pupils often arrive late because they help their parents with morning agricultural labor. Furthermore, while the medium of instruction is Urdu and Sindhi, five newly enrolled children from migrant Baloch and Pashtun pastoralist families speak only Balochi or Pashto at home and struggle to follow textbook instructions.

During a lesson on energy conservation, the school headmaster conducts an inspection. Observing that Tariq's lesson moves at a brisk pace, the headmaster remarks approvingly: "Good work, Tariq. You have enrolled all these children in school, so our school is completely inclusive. Just focus on the top ten students who can pass the board examinations; the others will drop out to work in the fields anyway." Tariq looks at Asif, who cannot reach the raised chalkboard because there is no ramp, and at the flood-displaced children sitting on the bare floor without textbooks. He realizes that having children physically present in the room does not mean they are included in learning. How can a teacher move beyond mere enrollment to build genuine inclusion grounded in human rights? This topic shows how human rights and the three pillars of inclusion transform classroom practice.

## Explanation

### Human rights and inclusion

#### The human rights foundation of education

Education is not a charitable privilege bestowed by the state; it is a fundamental, inalienable human right. The international foundation of this right was established in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR, 1948) and codified in the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC, 1989).

Pakistan ratified the UNCRC in 1990. Under Article 28 of the UNCRC, the government commits to making primary education compulsory and available free to all, and to taking measures to encourage regular attendance and the reduction of drop-out rates. Under Article 29, the education of the child must be directed to developing the child's personality, talents, and abilities to their fullest potential, fostering respect for human rights and for the natural environment (United Nations, 1989).

At the national level, the Eighteenth Amendment to the Constitution of the Islamic Republic of Pakistan made education an enforceable fundamental right through Article 25A:

> "The State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children of the age of five to sixteen years in such manner as may be determined by law." (Government of Pakistan, 1973)

Article 25A places an explicit legal duty on provincial governments, education departments, and school administrations. When a school turns away a child because of poverty, disability, lack of birth documentation, or linguistic background, it is not merely demonstrating poor customer service; it is breaching a constitutional guarantee. In education for sustainable development, human rights provide the non-negotiable benchmark: every child has the right to understand their local environment, develop critical problem-solving skills, and participate in community decision-making.

#### Broadening the concept of inclusion: "All means all"

What does inclusive education mean? For many decades, educators treated inclusion as a medical or specialized concept concerning only children with diagnosed physical or intellectual disabilities. In traditional practice, children with disabilities were either segregated into special schools or accommodated on the sidelines of mainstream classrooms as passive observers.

The UNESCO Global Education Monitoring Report (UNESCO, 2020) established a vital shift in international educational policy: **inclusion means all**. The report defines inclusion as a continuous process of removing barriers and responding to the diverse needs of all learners. Disability is an important dimension of inclusion, but it is not the only one. Inclusion embraces:

1. **Socio-economic background:** Children from landless agricultural families, urban informal settlements, and daily-wage households who lack uniforms, bags, or meals.
2. **Gender:** Girls in rural communities whose schooling is cut short due to safety concerns, lack of boundary walls, or household care expectations.
3. **Linguistic and ethnic minorities:** Children whose mother tongue differs from the provincial medium of instruction.
4. **Displaced and climate-affected learners:** Children whose families have migrated due to seasonal work, droughts, or climate disasters like the 2022 floods.
5. **Diverse learning paces:** Learners who need differentiated instructional scaffolds, tactile materials, or more time to grasp scientific concepts.

#### The central misconception: "Inclusion is only about disability"

**"Inclusion is only about disability."** This belief is widespread in Pakistani schools and must be actively corrected. When teachers believe inclusion is limited to physical impairments, they assume that if their classroom has no wheelchair user or blind child, they do not need to worry about inclusive pedagogy.

In reality, an exclusionary classroom can be full of able-bodied children. When a teacher only asks questions of wealthy students sitting in the front rows, scolds a child for speaking a home dialect, assigns textbook tasks that assume home internet access, or ignores the quiet confusion of first-generation learners, that teacher is practicing exclusion. Inclusion is not an add-on program for a few individuals; it is the universal pedagogical approach of welcoming and supporting every child who enters the classroom door.

#### The three pillars of the inclusive classroom

To make inclusion actionable, UNESCO (2020) models the inclusive classroom across three concentric dimensions: **access**, **participation**, and **achievement**.

<Figure id="fig-U5-4" src="/img/figures/efmp-407/unit-05/fig-U5-4.svg" alt="Diagram of the inclusive classroom showing three concentric layers: access at the foundation, participation in the process, and achievement at the centre for every learner." />

##### 1. Access (physical, legal and institutional entry)

Access is the essential baseline: can every child physically, safely, and legally enter the school and classroom?
- Physical access includes wheelchair ramps, wide doorways, level paths, clear signage, and functional, separate toilets with privacy and clean water.
- Legal and economic access includes fee-free enrollment under Article 25A, free textbooks, no hidden charges for sports or stationery, and flexible attendance policies that accommodate seasonal agricultural harvests.

##### 2. Participation (belonging, voice and collaborative engagement)

Getting into the classroom is useless if a learner is treated as invisible. Participation means that every learner has a voice, feels valued, and actively contributes to classroom life:
- Safe, non-coercive classroom climate where mistakes are treated as learning opportunities rather than occasions for public humiliation or corporal punishment.
- Collaborative group work where tasks are rotated so that marginalized learners hold genuine responsibility (such as recording data or presenting findings).
- Linguistic bridges that allow learners to discuss ideas in their home language (such as Sindhi, Balochi, Punjabi, or Pashto) before transitioning to written Urdu or English.

##### 3. Achievement (learning progress and competence)

Inclusion is fulfilled only when every child actually learns and masters the curriculum. Low expectations for disadvantaged pupils represent structural discrimination:
- Differentiated instruction: providing multiple entry points into a concept (visual diagrams, spoken explanations, concrete objects, and peer support).
- Formative feedback: checking understanding frequently through non-threatening retrieval methods rather than high-stakes ranking tests.
- Fostering sustainability competencies: ensuring that all children develop systems thinking, collaboration, and practical problem-solving for community environmental issues.

#### Worked example: redesigning an exclusionary lesson

Here is a worked example demonstrating how a teacher can analyze an exclusionary lesson plan and redesign it across access, participation, and achievement.

**Initial Lesson:** A Class 7 teacher introduces a unit on "Water Pollution in Our Canal". The teacher reads three dense paragraphs aloud from the textbook in English, writes five technical definitions on the chalkboard, and commands students to copy the text into their notebooks. For homework, students are instructed to download and print two color pictures of factory water pollution from the internet and write a 200-word essay in English.

**Diagnostic Evaluation:**
- *Access:* Several students cannot see the small chalkboard writing from the back of the room; students from flood-affected settlements do not have electricity, computers, or internet access at home.
- *Participation:* The teacher spoke for 35 minutes; only two confident boys answered questions; no collaborative dialogue occurred.
- *Achievement:* Copying textbook sentences tests motor speed, not comprehension of water quality; children who struggle with English reading fail entirely, despite living beside the polluted canal every day.

**Redesigned Inclusive Lesson Plan:**

| Pillar | Specific inclusive adaptation | Rationale grounded in rights |
|---|---|---|
| **Access** | Bring three clear glass jars of real canal water samples into class; read instructions aloud clearly; replace internet printouts with direct observation of local puddles, ponds, or irrigation ditches within walking distance. | Removes economic barriers and guarantees equal opportunity for children without home internet or electricity (UNCRC Article 28). |
| **Participation** | Structure a 15-minute collaborative think-pair-share activity in mixed-gender, mixed-ability groups of three; allow initial discussions in the pupils' shared home language before reporting in the class medium. | Ensures every student speaks, engages peer support, and contributes community observations (UNCRC Article 29). |
| **Achievement** | Provide a visual observation chart with clear icons (color, smell, floating debris, aquatic plant life) alongside short written descriptions; assess understanding through oral explanation or diagram labeling rather than an English essay alone. | Differentiates learning modes, enabling all learners to demonstrate analytical grasp of water quality regardless of reading fluency (Article 25A). |

## Activity: Auditing an environmental lesson for inclusion

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Read the following classroom scenario from a rural middle school:

> "Teacher Munir is teaching a lesson on biodiversity. He tells the class that they will spend the next hour walking through a rocky hillside forest trail 1.5 kilometers from the school. Students must bring a pair of binoculars and a purchased field guide from the city bookstore. In class, students who can afford the guide read the bird species names aloud, while Asif, who uses crutches, is told to stay behind in the empty staffroom with the school guard to avoid slowing down the class."

1. **Audit (10 minutes):** Work with your partner to identify the specific access, participation, and achievement barriers in Munir's plan. Which constitutional and human rights principles are violated?
2. **Redesign (10 minutes):** Propose three specific modifications so that the entire class, including Asif and learners without money to buy field guides, can investigate local biodiversity around the school compound itself.

## Summary

Education for sustainable development is grounded in fundamental human rights, guaranteed internationally by the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child and nationally by Article 25A of the Constitution of Pakistan. Inclusion goes far beyond physical disability; it is a universal commitment to ensure that "all means all", including children marginalized by poverty, gender, linguistic barriers, and environmental displacement. Genuine inclusion requires systematic attention to three concentric pillars: securing physical and economic access to schooling, fostering authentic active participation in classroom life, and ensuring high achievement and sustainability competence for every learner through differentiated instruction.

## Try this at your practicum school

During your next visit to your practicum placement school, conduct a simple accessibility and participation walk:
1. Check physical access: Are there steps without ramps, narrow doorways, broken desks, or unlit corners that disadvantage certain children?
2. Check linguistic access: How does the teacher handle a child who uses a home language word instead of the textbook term? Is the child corrected with warmth or scolded?
3. Design one simple differentiated visual aid (such as a chart with illustrations or real-world local materials) for your next teaching practice lesson to help struggling learners participate.
