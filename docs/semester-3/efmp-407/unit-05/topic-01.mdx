# Topic 5.1 - Peace, gender equality and social justice

## A real classroom situation

In a government higher secondary school in Larkana district, trainee teacher Parveen coordinates a student committee for a new rainwater-harvesting and tree-plantation project. During the first planning meeting, an argument breaks out. Several older boys take charge of the meeting, decide where the trees will be planted, and announce that the girls will be responsible for sweeping the courtyard and carrying water buckets. When Nadia, a Class 9 pupil, objects that girls should help choose the plant varieties and manage the storage tank, one of the boys replies, "Tree planting is hard work outside; girls should look after the cleaning." At the same time, two students whose families are landless farmworkers sit silently at the back, hesitant to speak because they feel their views do not matter to the village elders on the school council.

Parveen steps in. She realises that while the students are eager to plant trees for environmental sustainability, their school culture currently reproduces the very inequities that cause community conflict: unequal voice for female learners, exclusion of less privileged families, and adversarial shouting instead of peaceful problem-solving. Can a tree-planting project truly be sustainable if it reinforces social division? This topic provides the conceptual tools to understand why environmental care requires peace, gender equality, and social justice.

## Explanation

### Peace

**Peace education** is the teaching and learning that helps learners prevent conflict, resolve disputes without violence, and build the social conditions for lasting peace. It is a core part of education for sustainable development.

#### Negative peace and positive peace

Many people think of peace simply as the absence of war, physical violence or riots. In peace education and sustainability studies, scholars call this **negative peace** (UNESCO, 2017). Negative peace means guns are quiet, but underlying tensions, discrimination, and grievances remain unresolved beneath the surface.

In contrast, **positive peace** refers to the presence of constructive social conditions: mutual trust, social justice, the protection of human dignity, fair sharing of common resources, and established channels for non-violent conflict resolution (UNESCO, 2017). Positive peace addresses the root causes of conflict before they escalate into violence.

#### Peace as an ecological imperative

Why is peace an essential pillar of sustainable development? Natural resources such as irrigation water, grazing pastures, firewood, and fertile agricultural land are finite. When environmental degradation reduces available resources, competition intensifies. In rural Sindh and Balochistan, disputes over canal water turns (*waari*) or access to tube-wells frequently pit neighbouring families or villages against one another. If communities lack peaceful mechanisms to negotiate water distribution, competition leads to legal battles, economic sabotage, or physical confrontation.

Sustainable development requires cooperation across generations and social groups. Armed conflict and social hostility destroy ecosystems, consume public funds that could support schooling or healthcare, and displace communities. As declared in Sustainable Development Goal 16 (SDG 16), sustainable development cannot be achieved without peace, justice, and inclusive institutions (UNESCO, 2017).

Article 29 of the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC, 1989), ratified by Pakistan in 1990, explicitly mandates that education must prepare the child for a responsible life in a free society in the spirit of understanding, peace, tolerance, and respect for the natural environment. In schools, peace education teaches pupils active listening, perspective-taking, and peer mediation over shared classroom resources.

### Gender equality

#### Gender as a determinant of environmental experience

Gender refers to the socially constructed roles, responsibilities, and opportunities assigned to women, men, girls, and boys in a given society. **Gender equality** means that women and men enjoy equal rights, social opportunities, economic resources, and voices in decision-making (UNESCO, 2017).

In Pakistani communities, environmental degradation and climate disasters are not experienced identically by men and women. Consider daily resource management:

1. **Water collection:** In drought-prone areas of Tharparkar, Kohistan, and rural Sindh, fetching domestic drinking water is primarily the daily task of women and school-age girls. When shallow wells dry up, girls must walk longer distances each morning, often leading to school absenteeism or dropping out altogether.
2. **Fuel and household health:** In homes lacking gas infrastructure, women and girls collect biomass and firewood and cook over open biomass stoves, inhaling harmful particulate smoke that damages respiratory health.
3. **Disaster vulnerability:** During the devastating 2022 floods in Pakistan, women faced severe challenges regarding personal privacy, safe maternal healthcare, and emergency relief distribution, despite bearing heavy burdens of household care and child protection.

Yet, despite managing domestic water, food preparation, and household hygiene, women and girls are often excluded when village committees make decisions about irrigation water schemes, disaster mitigation plans, or local school council priorities.

#### Constitutional and educational obligations

Article 25(1) of the Constitution of the Islamic Republic of Pakistan guarantees that all citizens are equal before the law, while Article 25(2) explicitly commands: "There shall be no discrimination on the basis of sex" (Government of Pakistan, 1973). Furthermore, Sustainable Development Goal 5 (SDG 5) sets the international target of eliminating all forms of discrimination against women and girls (UNESCO, 2017).

In the classroom, gender equality requires teachers to dismantle unconscious stereotypes. A teacher promotes gender equality not just through textbooks, but through daily routines: ensuring equal questioning time for girls and boys, appointing girls to lead scientific inquiry projects, assigning cleaning and gardening duties equitably, and encouraging female pupils to speak with authority on environmental topics.

### Social justice

#### What social justice means in sustainable development

**Social justice** means the fair and equitable distribution of social benefits, educational opportunities, and environmental protections across all segments of society, with particular care for the vulnerable, poor, and marginalized (UNESCO, 2017).

In environmental debates, scholars frequently identify **environmental injustice**. Environmental injustice happens when the burdens of industrial development (such as chemical pollution, smoke, untreated municipal sewage, and toxic garbage dumps) are concentrated near low-income settlements, while the benefits (such as corporate profits, clean suburban air, and paved commercial markets) are enjoyed by affluent groups. For example, industrial chemical waste discharged into water canals in Kotri or Lyari affects the drinking water and health of low-income families who cannot afford commercial water filtration or private healthcare.

Article 37 of the Constitution of Pakistan sets forth the state's obligation to promote social justice and eradicate social evils, directing the state to promote with special care the educational and economic interests of backward classes and areas (Government of Pakistan, 1973).

#### Two common misconceptions to correct

**"Sustainability has nothing to do with peace or gender."** Some people imagine that sustainability is strictly a biological or engineering subject: planting saplings, designing solar panels, and checking soil salinity. This belief is mistaken. Natural resources are stewarded by human beings organized in societies. If a community is fractured by ethnic conflict or gender oppression, community members will not cooperate to maintain a shared solar pump, protect an urban park, or preserve irrigation canals. Ecological breakdown fuels resource competition, and social injustice undermines the collective trust needed for environmental stewardship.

**"Social justice is a controversial political topic that teachers must avoid."** Some educators believe that discussing fairness or marginalized groups in school creates trouble. However, in education, social justice is fundamentally about constitutional values and classroom fairness. As a teacher, social justice means ensuring that the child of a daily-wage laborer receives the same high expectations, learning materials, and respect as the child of an influential landlord or government officer. Far from being forbidden, fostering fairness is a core constitutional duty mandated by Article 25 and Article 37 of Pakistan's Constitution.

#### Worked example: analyzing a school environmental dilemma

Here is how a teacher can apply the three lenses of peace, gender equality, and social justice to analyze a concrete school situation.

**Scenario:** A government high school receives a grant to install a solar-powered drinking water filtration plant. The school head appoints the three most vocal boys from prominent local families to manage the distribution tokens. Within two weeks, younger students and girls report that the boys only distribute tokens to their personal friends, lines are disorderly, and water containers brought by children from a nearby brick-kiln settlement are pushed aside.

| Analytical lens | Evaluative question | Diagnostic finding | Recommended pedagogical action |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Peace** | Is there positive peace and orderly conflict resolution? | Negative peace was assumed, but structural unfairness created shouting and resentment. | Form a transparent, multi-member water committee with a clear schedule and student-led peer mediation for dispute resolution. |
| **Gender equality** | Do female learners have equal voice and access? | Girls were entirely excluded from operational decisions and faced intimidating crowds at the tap. | Appoint an equal number of female and male student monitors, and create designated, safe filling stations with equal queue priority. |
| **Social justice** | Are vulnerable learners protected and treated fairly? | Children from brick-kiln families faced open discrimination and unequal access to clean water. | Establish a universal, fee-free entitlement where every enrolled child receives an identical token regardless of family status or wealth. |

By breaking the issue into these three distinct dimensions, Parveen avoids vague scolding and instead builds an educational routine that models justice, gender parity, and peaceful cooperation.

## Activity: Three lenses on a school project

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

Your teacher provides this illustrative case: A middle school in Sukkur wants to create a community kitchen garden to teach sustainable agriculture and provide fresh spinach and vegetables for school meals.

1. **Role allocation:** Each member takes one primary analytical role:
   - Member 1: Focus on **Peace** (how to prevent disputes over garden plots, tools, and harvest).
   - Member 2: Focus on **Gender equality** (how to ensure girls and boys share both decision-making and physical tasks).
   - Member 3: Focus on **Social justice** (how to ensure children from the poorest homes have equal access to the harvest and learning).
   - Member 4: Synthesizer and reporter.
2. **Task (15 minutes):** For each dimension, develop two concrete rules for the school garden project that turn abstract principles into daily classroom routines.
3. **Plenary sharing (10 minutes):** Swap your four-point project charter with another group. Test whether their rules prevent the Larkana dilemma from recurring.

## Summary

Sustainable development is fundamentally a social enterprise that requires positive peace, gender equality, and social justice. Negative peace is merely the absence of violence, whereas positive peace builds fair cooperative structures. Gender equality ensures that women and girls, who often bear the heaviest burdens of resource scarcity, possess equal voice and leadership in sustainability decisions. Social justice demands that environmental benefits and costs be shared equitably, protecting vulnerable groups from exploitation. When teachers embed these three lenses into daily classroom routines, they transform schools into living models of sustainable communities.
