# Cultural and Social Dimensions - Teacher Notes

These notes support the teacher educator teaching Unit 5 of Education for Sustainable Development. They contain no assessment items. The course guide allocates Weeks 9 and 10 to this unit (`3rd 2026.txt:357-360`), specifying lectures, discussions, question-answer sessions, and brainstorming as teaching strategies, alongside group work, practical assignments, and presentations.

## Sequencing

Teach the three topics in logical sequence across the two allotted weeks:

1. **Week 9, first session (Topic 5.1):** Introduce the social pillar of sustainability. Begin with Parveen's Larkana dilemma before defining terms. Brainstorm why an environmental initiative fails when gender inequality or social hierarchy divides students. Contrast negative peace with positive peace, then walk through the worked example on school water distribution.
2. **Week 9, second session (Topic 5.1 & Topic 5.2):** Run the "Three lenses on a school project" activity in small groups. Transition to Topic 5.2 by examining the constitutional foundation of education under Article 25A and the UNCRC. Assign Topic 5.2 for evening reading.
3. **Week 10, first session (Topic 5.2):** Unpack the three pillars of inclusion: access, participation, and achievement. Directly confront the misconception that inclusion is only about physical disability. Run the "Auditing an environmental lesson for inclusion" pair activity.
4. **Week 10, second session (Topic 5.3):** Open with Gul Khan's story in Mastung. Discuss the difference between empirical traditional knowledge and superstition. Compare the Karez system and motorized tube-wells in Balochistan. Introduce "two-eyed seeing" and organize the community elder inquiry activity.

## Discussion prompt and a likely wrong answer

Ask trainees: *"If a school installs a high-technology solar energy system but only boys are allowed to monitor the dials while girls sweep the dust off the panels, is that school teaching sustainable development?"*

Many student-teachers will initially say: *"Yes, because solar energy is renewable and green, which is the main goal of sustainability."*

Do not immediately correct them. Ask: *"What are the girls in that classroom learning about their own worth and future in a green economy? What will happen when those boys become community leaders and make municipal water or energy plans?"* Guide them to see that sustainability is not merely about kilowatt-hours or physical technology; it is about human dignity, shared stewardship, and equity. An environmentally green project that reinforces gender subordination undermines the social foundation of sustainable development.

## Likely misconceptions

- **"Sustainability has nothing to do with peace or gender."** Use the canal water dispute case in rural Sindh. Ask trainees how a community can maintain irrigation infrastructure if ethnic or tribal tensions prevent neighbors from talking, or if women who gather water are excluded from planning decisions.
- **"Inclusion is only about disability."** Ask trainees how many children in their practicum districts are out of school because of extreme poverty, child labor, minority language barriers, or post-flood displacement. Help them recognize that "all means all".
- **"Indigenous knowledge is backward superstition."** Use the Karez and legume crop rotation examples. Highlight that while modern diesel pumps caused water tables to collapse across Balochistan, the traditional Karez system sustainably supplied clean water for centuries within natural recharge limits.
- **"Social justice is a controversial political topic teachers must avoid."** Remind trainees that Articles 25 and 37 of the Constitution of Pakistan make equality, non-discrimination, and protection of disadvantaged classes a fundamental national duty. In classrooms, social justice is about fairness, high expectations for all children, and equal opportunity.

## A caution on sensitive cases

Discussions of gender roles, caste discrimination, tribal traditions, and religious minorities can provoke defensive reactions. Maintain an educational, inquiry-based stance:
- Anchor all discussions in the **Constitution of Pakistan (Articles 25, 25A, and 37)** and international treaties ratified by Pakistan (such as the UNCRC).
- Focus on pedagogical and community solutions rather than personal finger-pointing.
- When examining traditional ecological practices, avoid uncritical romanticization of all past customs; teach trainees to evaluate traditional techniques using rigorous ecological and scientific criteria (two-eyed seeing).

## Practical work

- **Group work:** The three topic activities (Three lenses on a school project, Auditing a classroom for inclusion, and Investigating local community ecological knowledge).
- **Group assignment:** A two-page illustrated chart for a school staffroom comparing traditional and modern ecological practices across Pakistan's four provinces.
- **Individual assignment:** The summative task in Topic 5.2 (redesigning an exclusionary lesson plan) or Topic 5.3 (analyzing a local piece of traditional knowledge).
- **Presentation:** A five-minute presentation analyzing an environmental problem in the student-teacher's home district through the lenses of peace, gender equality, and social justice.
