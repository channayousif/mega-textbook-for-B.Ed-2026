# Topic 5.3 - Indigenous knowledge systems

## A real classroom situation

In a government high school near Mastung in Balochistan, trainee teacher Bilal is teaching a general science lesson on water resources and groundwater conservation. He projects diagrams from a textbook showing modern diesel-powered tube-wells, electric pumps, and deep boreholes. During the class discussion, a student named Gul Khan raises his hand and shares a story from his village:

"Sir, in our ancestral village, we do not need electric pumps to get fresh water. Our elders built a *karez* more than a hundred years ago. It brings clean mountain water through a tunnel underground all the way to our fields, using only gravity. We never have to buy diesel, the water never runs out, and the village elders divide the water turns fairly among families according to the size of their land."

Before Bilal can respond, another student sitting in the front row snickers and says: "Sir, that is just backward village talk from old uneducated people. Real modern science is about motorized tube-wells and modern electric technology, not digging holes in the dirt." Several pupils laugh, and Gul Khan looks down at his desk in embarrassment.

Bilal pauses the lesson. He realizes that standard curriculum materials often treat science as something invented exclusively in Western laboratories, ignoring centuries of sophisticated, place-based ecological knowledge developed by Pakistan's own communities. By ridiculing local traditions, schooling alienates rural learners and discards proven models of sustainable resource management. How can educators value indigenous knowledge while teaching rigorous modern science? This topic explains the nature of indigenous knowledge systems and how to integrate them into sustainability education.

## Explanation

### Indigenous knowledge systems

#### What are indigenous knowledge systems?

**Indigenous knowledge systems (IKS)**, often referred to as **traditional ecological knowledge (TEK)**, represent cumulative bodies of knowledge, practices, skills, and beliefs regarding the relationship of human beings with one another and with their local ecosystems (Zidny, Sjöström, & Eilks, 2020; Owuor, 2008).

This knowledge does not come from laboratory textbooks. Instead, it evolves through hundreds or thousands of years of direct empirical observation, trial, adaptation, and intergenerational transmission through oral history, community customs, practical apprenticeship, and cultural storytelling (Owuor, 2008). Indigenous knowledge is intimately tied to specific geographical places, local microclimates, soils, vegetation, and wildlife.

While modern industrial models often view nature as a collection of separate raw materials to be extracted for immediate economic profit, indigenous knowledge systems typically embody a **circular, reciprocal worldview** (Zidny et al., 2020). In traditional worldviews, human welfare is fundamentally inseparable from the health of land, rivers, and non-human living species.

#### The central misconception: "Indigenous knowledge is mere superstition"

**"Indigenous knowledge is mere superstition and unscientific backwardness."** This common misconception causes severe harm in education. It arises from colonial and Eurocentric historical attitudes that equated formal Western schooling with enlightenment and dismissed community-based knowledge as primitive folklore (Owuor, 2008).

In truth, traditional ecological practices are deeply empirical. When an indigenous farming community selects specific seed varieties, observes the flight patterns of migratory birds to predict seasonal monsoon rains, rotates crops according to lunar cycles, or manages communal pastures with seasonal grazing bans, they are not acting on blind superstition. They are applying conclusions verified by generations of observation and natural experimentation (Zidny et al., 2020).

Consider what happens when traditional ecological knowledge is discarded in favor of uncritical modernization. Across Balochistan, motorized tube-wells were installed alongside traditional gravity-fed *karez* systems (Kahlown & Hamilton, 1994). Motorized pumps extracted groundwater much faster than the rain and mountain snowmelt could refill the aquifers, depleting water tables through uncontrolled pumping (Kahlown & Hamilton, 1994). Modern engineering without ecological limits produced an environmental crisis that traditional systems had prevented for centuries.

#### Traditional ecological knowledge across Pakistan's regions

Pakistan's geographic and cultural diversity, from deserts and alluvial river basins to arid plateaus and glaciated mountain ranges, has nurtured rich, specialized ecological knowledge systems.

##### 1. The Karez system of Balochistan

A *karez* is a traditional subterranean aqueduct system engineered to capture groundwater from alluvial fans at the base of mountains and transport it kilometers downhill to agricultural fields entirely by natural gravity (Kahlown & Hamilton, 1994).
- *Scientific and ecological principle:* Because the canal runs underground, water loss from evaporation in arid desert heat is virtually zero. Because water extraction is powered solely by gravity, the rate of flow naturally adjusts to the height of the water table. If rainfall is low, flow slows; if rainfall is high, flow increases. Unlike a diesel motor, a *karez* cannot drain an aquifer dry.
- *Social sustainability:* Karez water is distributed through customary community governance known as *shabana*, where each family receives an allocated time turn based on agreed community shares and shares in the physical maintenance of the shafts (Kahlown & Hamilton, 1994).

##### 2. Legume crop rotation in Sindh and Punjab

For generations, peasant farmers in the Indus basin have practiced *chana* (chickpea), *masoor* (lentil), or *mung* bean rotation following winter wheat harvests.
- *Scientific principle:* Legumes possess symbiotic *Rhizobium* bacteria in their root nodules that capture inert atmospheric nitrogen and convert it into bioavailable soil nitrates (biological nitrogen fixation).
- *Sustainability lesson:* Rotating legumes naturally replenishes soil nutrients, breaks insect pest cycles, and builds soil organic carbon without dependency on synthetic chemical inputs.

##### 3. Rod-Kohi and Sailaba spate irrigation in KP and Sindh

In the piedmont plains of Dera Ghazi Khan, Dera Ismail Khan, and parts of western Sindh, seasonal monsoon cloudbursts generate torrential flash floods from the Sulaiman and Kirthar mountain ranges. Local communities practice *Rod-Kohi* (spate irrigation), constructing temporary earthen embankments (*gandi*) to divert floodwaters onto terraced agricultural fields.
- *Scientific principle:* The floodwaters carry rich volcanic and mineral silt eroded from mountain peaks. By trapping the muddy water in deep earthen basins, farmers simultaneously harvest moisture deep into the subsoil and deposit natural nutrient-rich topsoil.
- *Sustainability lesson:* Instead of treating seasonal flash floods purely as catastrophic disasters, traditional engineering transforms flood pulses into fertile agricultural production while reducing downstream flood risk.

##### 4. Glacier grafting and contour water channels in Gilgit-Baltistan

In high-altitude Karakoram and Himalayan valleys (such as Hunza, Skardu, and Nagar), indigenous mountain communities have long practiced glacier grafting (*phaalo*), a technique in which ice is transported and combined at high altitude to build a small ice reservoir that feeds gravity-contoured meltwater canals (*kuhl*) during the dry season (Munir et al., 2021). This deliberate seeding fosters snow accumulation that sustains irrigation water for agriculture and food security in water-stressed valleys (Munir et al., 2021).
- *Sustainability lesson:* Demonstrates community-governed climate adaptation and collective water allocation without centralized external technology (Munir et al., 2021).

#### Integrating IKS into formal education: "Two-eyed seeing"

How should a school teacher handle the relationship between traditional community knowledge and formal science?

Scholars emphasize the principle of **two-eyed seeing** (Zidny et al., 2020). Two-eyed seeing means learning to see from one eye with the strengths of indigenous knowledge and ways of knowing, and from the other eye with the strengths of Western modern scientific tools, and using both eyes together for the benefit of all.

<Figure id="fig-U5-6" src="/img/figures/efmp-407/unit-05/fig-U5-6.svg" alt="Diagram showing indigenous ecological knowledge and modern scientific inquiry converging as two complementary streams into integrated sustainability education." />

When teachers bring indigenous knowledge into the classroom:
1. **Validation of learner identity:** Rural, minority, and working-class students see their families' lived heritage recognized as valuable and intellectually dignified, increasing academic motivation and self-esteem (Owuor, 2008).
2. **Contextualizing abstract science:** Scientific concepts like nitrogen cycles, groundwater dynamics, thermal insulation, and ecological succession become concrete and immediately visible through local community practices.
3. **Restoring ethical limits:** Modern technological science often asks "Can we do this?" while indigenous wisdom asks "Should we do this, and what will happen to our grandchildren and the land if we do?" (Zidny et al., 2020).

#### Worked example: comparing traditional and industrial pest control

Here is a worked example showing how a teacher can guide students to analyze a local traditional practice alongside modern technology.

**The Case:** An agricultural community in Khairpur district seeks to control insect pests damaging stored grain (wheat and rice) in school and household storage rooms.

| Analytical dimension | Traditional practice: Earthen granary (*kothi*) with dried neem leaves | Modern industrial practice: Chemical pesticide fumigation tablets (aluminum phosphide) |
|---|---|---|
| **Mechanism** | Earthen granary breathes, maintaining even temperature and low humidity; active biological compounds in neem (*azadirachtin*) repel weevils and disrupt insect reproduction. | Chemical tablets react with moisture to release lethal phosphine gas, killing all insects immediately. |
| **Ecological sustainability** | 100% biodegradable; utilizes local clay, straw, and renewable tree leaves; zero chemical residue in food or soil. | Toxic chemical residue; accidental ingestion causes severe human poisoning; improper disposal contaminates local soil and water. |
| **Economic cost and access** | Virtually free; made by local artisans using readily available community resources; zero cash debt. | Requires recurring monetary purchase; vulnerable to market inflation, adulterated counterfeit chemicals, and cash shortages. |
| **Sustainability verdict** | Highly resilient, low-risk, self-reliant ecological technique for household and small-scale food preservation. | High acute effectiveness but carries dangerous human toxicity risks and economic dependency; requires strict professional handling. |

By leading students through this comparative inquiry, the teacher does not reject modern chemistry, but demonstrates that the traditional method possesses significant ecological, economic, and safety advantages for local storage.

## Activity: Investigating local community ecological knowledge

Work in small groups of three or four. About 25 minutes.

Your group represents a curriculum committee designing a middle-school science and social studies inquiry project called "Our Grandparents' Science".

1. **Selection (5 minutes):** Choose one traditional practice from your own community or region from the following list:
   - Traditional architecture for thermal cooling (mud walls, wind towers, thatched roofs).
   - Natural food preservation techniques (sun-drying vegetables, pickling, earthen cool-pots / *matka*).
   - Traditional herbal remedies and plant conservation (neem, moringa, tulsi).
   - Traditional animal grazing and rangeland conservation practices.
2. **Pedagogical planning (15 minutes):** Draft an outline for a class assignment where pupils interview an elder relative or community member about this practice. Formulate:
   - Three structured interview questions pupils will ask the elder.
   - The underlying scientific principle that explains why the traditional practice works.
   - The specific sustainability lesson (needs, limits, energy efficiency, or resource conservation) pupils will learn.
3. **Sharing (5 minutes):** Share your inquiry plan with a neighbouring group. Critique each other's questions to ensure they treat the community elder with respect as a knowledgeable co-teacher.

## Summary

Indigenous knowledge systems and traditional ecological knowledge represent centuries of place-based empirical observation, circular ecological values, and communal resource governance. Far from being unscientific superstition, traditional practices across Pakistan - such as Balochistan's Karez water systems, Indus basin legume crop rotation, Rod-Kohi spate irrigation, and northern glacier grafting - embody proven principles of ecological limits, zero-carbon energy, and soil preservation. Rather than rejecting either modern science or local heritage, effective sustainability education adopts "two-eyed seeing", bringing community wisdom and scientific inquiry into productive dialogue to prepare learners for genuine environmental stewardship.

## Summative task

1. Describes the traditional practice, its geographical context, and the ecological challenge it addresses.
2. Analyzes the scientific and ecological principles underpinning the practice, demonstrating why it is empirical rather than superstitious.
3. Evaluates what this traditional practice offers modern sustainability education, and outlines one concrete inquiry-based classroom activity in which student-teachers or pupils investigate this practice with their local community.
