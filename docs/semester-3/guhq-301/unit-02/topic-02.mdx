## A real classroom situation

Nasreen is a B.Ed trainee at a government girls' middle school in Mirpurkhas. She is helping the Islamiat teacher explain a verse to Class 6. The verse is 3:195: فَٱسْتَجَابَ لَهُمْ رَبُّهُمْ أَنِّىٓ لَآ أُضِيعُ عَمَلَ عَـٰمِلٍ مِّنكُم مِّن ذَكَرٍ أَوْ أُنثَىٰ, "And their Lord responded to them: 'Never will I allow to be lost the work of any worker among you, whether male or female.'" A girl, Zainab, points at رَبُّهُمْ and asks: "Miss, whose Lord?" Nasreen knows that *rabbuhum* means "their Lord", but she cannot explain what *-hum* does, or why the verse also says ذَكَرٍ أَوْ أُنثَىٰ ("male or female"). By the end of this topic, you will understand what *-hum* is, how Arabic marks gender and number on nouns, and how pronouns attach to words to show "his", "their" or "your".

## Explanation

This topic teaches you three features of Qur'anic nouns: gender, number and pronouns. Together they tell the reader who or what a noun refers to.

### Nouns: gender (masculine and feminine)

**Every Arabic noun is either masculine or feminine.** There is no "it". This is different from English, where only people and some animals have gender, but similar to Urdu, where nouns also carry gender.

**Signs of the feminine.** Most feminine nouns end in one of three markers:

1. **Tā' marbūṭa** (ة) - the most common sign. Example: رَحْمَة (*raḥmah*, mercy), مَدْرَسَة (*madrasah*, school), صَلَوٰة (*ṣalāh*, prayer).
2. **Alif maqṣūra** (ى) - an alif shape at the end. Example: أُنثَىٰ (*unthā*, female), دُنْيَا (*dunyā*, world).
3. **Alif ممدودة** (اء) - a long alif-hamza. Example: سَمَاء (*samāʾ*, sky).

**But some nouns are feminine by meaning, not by marker.** Words for mothers, female relatives and paired body parts are feminine even without ة. Example: أُمّ (*umm*, mother), أَرْض (*arḍ*, earth), عَيْن (*ʿayn*, eye), يَد (*yad*, hand). The Quranic Arabic Corpus labels each noun's gender, so you can check.

**A worked example from the Qur'an.** In 2:286, the word نَفْس (*nafs*, soul/self) is feminine despite lacking ة. When a verb or adjective describes it, the verb takes a feminine marker. Knowing this helps you spot which noun a verb refers to.

**A Pakistan-grounded example.** Urdu speakers often guess correctly. *Madrasah* is feminine in Urdu, and it is feminine in Arabic. *Kitāb* is feminine in Urdu, and also in Arabic (by convention, not by marker). But *yawm* (day) is masculine in Arabic while Urdu does not give it a clear gender. Watch for these differences when you translate.

### Number: singular, dual, plural

**Arabic keeps three numbers apart.** Urdu has two (singular and plural). English has two. Arabic has three:

1. **Singular** (*mufrad*) - one. عَبْد (*ʿabd*, one servant).
2. **Dual** (*muthannā*) - exactly two. عَبْدَانِ (*ʿabdāni*, two servants, in the nominative case).
3. **Plural** (*jamʿ*) - three or more. عِبَاد (*ʿibād*, servants).

The table at the top of this topic shows all six forms for one noun.

**Correcting a common misconception: "a dual is the same as a plural."** Many Urdu speakers skip the dual because Urdu does not have one. But in the Qur'an the dual is a separate form with its own ending (-āni/-ayni for the masculine, -atāni/-atayni for the feminine). It appears in important places. In 55:46 and throughout Surah al-Rahman, the dual is used for "two gardens" (جَنَّتَانِ, *jannatāni*) promised to those who fear their Lord. Reading the ending correctly tells you whether the verse speaks of one, two, or many.

**Types of plural.** Arabic has two main plural types:

1. **Sound plural** - formed by adding an ending to the singular. Masculine: -ūna / -īna (مُسْلِمُونَ, *muslimūna*, Muslims). Feminine: -āt (مُسْلِمَات, *muslimāt*, Muslim women).
2. **Broken plural** - the word's internal pattern changes. كِتَاب → كُتُب (*kutub*, books). رَسُول → رُسُل (*rusul*, messengers). These must often be learned one by one, but the Corpus lists them.

**A worked example from the Qur'an.** In 2:285, three plurals appear in one verse: رُسُلِهِ (*rusulihi*, His messengers), كُتُبِهِ (*kutubihi*, His books) and مَلَـٰٓئِكَتِهِ (*malāʾikatihi*, His angels). All three are broken plurals with an attached pronoun meaning "His". Spotting the plural pattern tells you the verse speaks of many messengers, many books and many angels.

### Pronouns in Quranic Arabic

**Two sets of pronouns.** Arabic pronouns come in two forms:

1. **Detached pronouns** - they stand alone as separate words, usually at the start of a clause. They work like "he", "she", "they" in English.
2. **Attached pronouns** - they join the end of a noun, verb or particle. They work like "his", "her", "their" or "him", "her", "them".

<Figure id="fig-U2-4" src="/img/figures/guhq-301/unit-02/fig-U2-4.svg" alt="Table of detached and attached pronouns with a Qur'anic example for each" />

**A worked example: pronouns in 1:5.** إِيَّاكَ نَعْبُدُ وَإِيَّاكَ نَسْتَعِينُ, "It is You we worship and You we ask for help."

| Piece | Type | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *iyyāka* | detached object pronoun (special emphatic form) | You (alone) |
| *naʿbudu* | verb with attached subject pronoun *-u* (we) | we worship |
| *nastaʿīnu* | verb with attached subject pronoun *-u* (we) | we ask for help |

The *-ka* in *iyyāka* is an attached pronoun meaning "you". The same *-ka* appears in *rabbuka* (your Lord, as in 93:5). The *na-* at the start of each verb is a prefix pronoun meaning "we".

**Attached pronouns on nouns.** When a pronoun attaches to a noun, it means "his", "her", "their" and so on. You saw this in Nasreen's verse:

| Arabic | Parts | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| رَبُّهُمْ | *rabb* + *-hum* | their Lord |
| رَبُّكَ | *rabb* + *-ka* | your Lord |
| رَبُّنَا | *rabb* + *-nā* | our Lord |

A Pakistan-grounded example: in Urdu we say *Rab-ul-ʿālamīn* without thinking. It means "Lord of the worlds". The *-ul-* is the Arabic *al-* joining two nouns. Knowing how pronouns and the article work together helps you read longer phrases.

## Activity: Pronoun hunt

Work in groups of three. About 20 minutes.

Open Surah al-Fatiha (1:1-7) and 2:285-286 on Quran.com with the word-by-word display.

1. **Find (8 minutes).** List every pronoun you can find - both detached and attached. Write each one with its meaning and the word it is attached to (if attached).
2. **Sort (5 minutes).** Sort your list into detached and attached. For the attached ones, note whether the pronoun is on a noun ("His books"), a verb ("we worship") or a particle ("for them").
3. **Teach (7 minutes).** Each group member takes one pronoun and explains it to the other two, giving the Arabic, the meaning and the verse. Then quiz each other: one member says a pronoun, and the others say what it means and find it in the text.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the three signs that mark a noun as feminine in Arabic, with one example of each from the Qur'an.
2. *(Understand)* In three sentences, explain why the dual matters in Qur'anic Arabic, using an example.
3. *(Apply)* Take the word مُسْلِمَات (*muslimāt*). State its gender, its number and the type of plural it uses. Then give its singular form.
4. *(Apply)* In 2:286, find the word رَبَّنَا (*rabbanā*). Break it into its two parts, name the pronoun, and state whose Lord the verse is speaking of.

## Summary

Every Arabic noun has a gender (masculine or feminine) and a number (singular, dual or plural). Feminine nouns usually carry a marker such as tā' marbūṭa, but some are feminine by meaning alone. The dual is a number Urdu does not have, used in the Qur'an for exactly two. Plurals may be sound (an ending is added) or broken (the internal pattern changes). Pronouns are either detached (standing alone) or attached (joined to a noun, verb or particle), and they tell the reader who is doing, owning or receiving.

## Summative task

Write 250-300 words. Choose **two** of these Qur'anic words: مَلَـٰٓئِكَتِهِ (His angels, 2:285), جَنَّتَانِ (two gardens, 55:46), مُسْلِمُونَ (Muslims, 3:52).

1. For each word, identify the gender, the number (singular, dual or plural) and the type of plural if it is a plural.
2. If the word has an attached pronoun, name it and explain what it means.
3. Analyse how knowing the number and pronoun of one of these words helps a reader understand what the verse is saying.

*(Analyze)*

**Mini-rubric (10 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gender and number identified (3 marks) | Correct gender, number and plural type for both words (3) | Correct for one word (1-2) | None correct |
| Pronoun identified and explained (3 marks) | Names the pronoun, states its meaning and explains its role (3) | Names the pronoun without explaining its role (1-2) | Absent |
| Analysis of meaning (4 marks) | Shows with the verse how the number or pronoun shapes the reader's understanding, and draws a conclusion (3-4) | Notes the number or pronoun without connecting it to the verse's meaning (1-2) | No analysis |

The analysis criterion asks you to connect grammar to meaning. Writing "the dual means two" without showing what it means in the verse does not meet it.
