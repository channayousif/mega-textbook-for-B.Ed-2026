## A real classroom situation

Mariam is a B.Ed trainee at a government girls' high school in Khairpur. She is helping Class 8 pupils read Surah al-Ikhlas (112:1-4). One girl, Hira, reads verse 112:3: لَمْ يَلِدْ وَلَمْ يُولَدْ, "He neither begets nor is born." Hira asks, "Miss, what does *lam* do? Why is it not just *yalid* by itself?" Mariam knows that *lam* means "not" in some way, but she is not sure how it differs from لَا (*lā*), which she met in Surah al-Kafirun. By the end of this topic you will know the difference, and you will be able to use particles, negation and emphasis as tools for translating a verse word by word.

## Explanation

This topic covers the third part of speech - particles - and shows how they build meaning. It ends with a grammar-based translation exercise that brings everything in this unit together.

### Particles and prepositions (huruf)

**What particles do.** A particle has no meaning by itself. It works only when it connects to a noun or a verb. But its effect on meaning is large. Take the particle فَ (*fa*, then/so). In 94:5-6, the Qur'an says: فَإِنَّ مَعَ ٱلْعُسْرِ يُسْرًا, "For indeed, with hardship comes ease." The فَ links this verse to what came before and adds "so, therefore". Remove it and the connection is lost.

**Common prepositions.** Prepositions are particles that come before a noun and show its relationship to the rest of the sentence.

| Arabic | Transliteration | Meaning | Example verse |
|---|---|---|---|
| بِ | *bi* | with, by | بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ (1:1), "in the name of Allah" |
| مِن | *min* | from | مِنكُم (3:195), "from among you" |
| فِي | *fī* | in | فِي ٱلْأَرْضِ (2:30), "in the earth" |
| إِلَىٰ | *ilā* | to, towards | إِلَيْهِ رَٰجِعُونَ (2:156), "to Him we return" |
| عَلَىٰ | *ʿalā* | on, upon | عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ (2:20), "over all things" |
| لِ | *li* | for, to | لِلَّهِ (1:2), "for/to Allah" |
| عَن | *ʿan* | from, about | عَنِ ٱلسَّاعَةِ (79:42), "about the Hour" |

**A Pakistan-grounded example.** Most of these particles are already in Urdu: *bismillāh* uses بِ and the particle اسم; *fī amān Allāh* uses فِي; *ilā* appears in *ilā mashāʾ Allāh*. When you see them in the Qur'an, your Urdu ear has already met them.

### Negation and emphasis

**Four negation words.** Arabic has several ways to say "not". Each works with a different tense and carries a different shade of meaning.

| Particle | Works with | Effect | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| لَا (*lā*) | present tense | "does not" or "do not" (prohibition) | لَا أَعْبُدُ (109:2), "I do not worship" |
| لَمْ (*lam*) | present-tense form, but shifts it to past meaning | "did not" (negation of the past) | لَمْ يَلِدْ (112:3), "He did not beget" |
| لَنْ (*lan*) | present tense | "will never" (strong negation of the future) | لَن تَرَىٰنِي (7:143), "You will never see Me" |
| مَا (*mā*) | past tense (usually) | "did not" or "not" | مَا وَدَّعَكَ رَبُّكَ (93:3), "Your Lord has not taken leave of you" |

<Figure id="fig-U2-8" src="/img/figures/guhq-301/unit-02/fig-U2-8.svg" alt="Diagram showing how la, lam, lan and ma each change the verb yaktubu (he writes) to a different negated meaning" />

**A worked example: negation in Surah al-Ikhlas.** Hira's question about لَمْ يَلِدْ can now be answered.

| Word | What it does | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *lam* | takes a present-form verb and negates it with a past meaning | "did not" |
| *yalid* | present form of the verb from root و ل د (birth, begetting) | "(He) begets" |
| *lam yalid* together | "He did not beget" | a past negation |

The same pattern repeats: وَلَمْ يُولَدْ, "and He was not born." The لَمْ plus the present form (*yūlad*, passive) gives a past negation. If the verse had used لَا instead, it would mean "He does not beget" (present), which is also true but not what this verse says. لَمْ makes it about the nature of Allah, something that never happened.

**Correcting a common misconception: "particles are too small to matter for meaning."** Many learners skip particles when they translate, treating them as filler. But changing one particle changes the whole sentence. Compare:

- فِي ٱلْأَرْضِ (*fī al-arḍ*) = **in** the earth
- مِنَ ٱلْأَرْضِ (*min al-arḍ*) = **from** the earth
- عَلَى ٱلْأَرْضِ (*ʿalā al-arḍ*) = **on** the earth

The noun is the same. Only the particle changes. Each particle places the noun in a different relationship to the action.

**Emphasis with إِنَّ and قَدْ.** Two particles add force:

- **إِنَّ** (*inna*) = "indeed, surely". It introduces a statement with emphasis. إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدِيرٌ (2:20), "Indeed, Allah is over all things competent."
- **قَدْ** (*qad*) = "certainly" (before past tense, confirming it happened) or "may" (before present, expressing possibility). قَدْ أَفْلَحَ ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ (23:1), "Certainly, the believers have succeeded."

### Grammar-based translation practice

**Putting it all together.** You now have three tools from this unit: parts of speech (Topic 2.1), nouns with gender, number and pronouns (Topic 2.2), and verbs with tense, plus particles (Topics 2.3 and 2.4). Grammar-based translation means using all of these to translate a verse word by word and check each word against its grammar.

**A worked example: 2:285 (second half).** وَقَالُوا۟ سَمِعْنَا وَأَطَعْنَا ۖ غُفْرَانَكَ رَبَّنَا وَإِلَيْكَ ٱلْمَصِيرُ, "And they said, 'We hear and we obey. [We seek] Your forgiveness, our Lord, and to You is the [final] destination.'"

| Arabic word | Part of speech | Gender/number/tense | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| وَ | particle (conjunction) | - | and |
| قَالُوا۟ | verb | past, they (masc. pl.) | they said |
| سَمِعْنَا | verb | past, we | we heard |
| وَ | particle | - | and |
| أَطَعْنَا | verb | past, we | we obeyed |
| غُفْرَانَ | noun | masc. sing. | forgiveness |
| ـكَ | attached pronoun | 2nd person masc. sing. | Your |
| رَبَّ | noun | masc. sing. | Lord |
| ـنَا | attached pronoun | 1st person pl. | our |
| وَ | particle | - | and |
| إِلَيْ | preposition | - | to |
| ـكَ | attached pronoun | 2nd person masc. sing. | You |
| ٱلْمَصِيرُ | noun | masc. sing., with *al-* | the destination |

Every word is labelled. Every pronoun is named. Every verb's tense and subject are stated. This is what grammar-based translation looks like. It is slower than reading a finished translation, but it shows you exactly how the Arabic works.

## Activity: Translate and label a verse

Work in pairs. About 25 minutes.

Choose **one** of these verses: 2:286 (the first sentence only: لَا يُكَلِّفُ ٱللَّهُ نَفْسًا إِلَّا وُسْعَهَا) or 112:1-4.

1. **Write (5 minutes).** Copy the Arabic words in a column, one word per row.
2. **Label (10 minutes).** For each word, write its part of speech (noun, verb, particle), its gender and number (if a noun), its tense and subject (if a verb), and its meaning.
3. **Check (5 minutes).** Use Quran.com word-by-word and the Corpus to verify. Mark any word you labelled wrong.
4. **Reflect (5 minutes).** Together, write two sentences: one thing the grammar showed you that the English translation does not make visible, and one word that surprised you.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name four common prepositions in Qur'anic Arabic, give the meaning of each and give one verse where it appears.
2. *(Remember)* What is the difference between لَا and لَمْ in negation? State the tense each one works with.
3. *(Understand)* In two sentences, explain what إِنَّ (*inna*) does in a Qur'anic sentence, using an example.
4. *(Apply)* Take the phrase لَمْ يَلِدْ from 112:3. Identify the negation particle, state the tense of the verb, and translate the phrase.

## Summary

Particles are the smallest words in the Qur'an but they carry large effects. Prepositions such as *bi*, *min*, *fī* and *ʿalā* connect nouns to the rest of a sentence. Negation particles change a verb's meaning: *lā* negates the present, *lam* uses a present form to negate the past, *lan* strongly negates the future, and *mā* negates the past directly. Emphasis particles such as *inna* and *qad* add force to a statement. Grammar-based translation brings all the unit's tools together: parts of speech, gender, number, pronouns, tense and particles, applied word by word to the Arabic text.

## Try this at your practicum school

Choose one short verse the class already knows (such as the opening of Surah al-Ikhlas or al-Fatiha). Write three words from it on the board: one noun, one verb and one particle. Ask the class to guess which is which and why. Note which kind of word the pupils found hardest to identify and write a short reflection on what that tells you about how to teach vocabulary.

## Summative task

Write 300-350 words. Take 2:286, first sentence: لَا يُكَلِّفُ ٱللَّهُ نَفْسًا إِلَّا وُسْعَهَا, "Allah does not burden a soul except [within] its capacity."

1. Translate the sentence word by word, labelling each word's part of speech, gender/number (if a noun), and tense/subject (if a verb).
2. Identify the negation particle, state which tense it negates, and explain the effect.
3. Analyse how the grammar of this sentence - the negation, the verb, the noun *nafs* and the attached pronoun *-hā* - builds the meaning. What does each grammatical feature contribute?

**Mini-rubric (10 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Word-by-word translation with labels (3 marks) | Every word correctly labelled with part of speech and grammatical features (3) | Most words correct but one or two errors (1-2) | Absent or mostly wrong |
| Negation identified and explained (3 marks) | Names the particle, states the tense it negates and explains the effect on the sentence (3) | Names the particle without explaining the tense or effect (1-2) | Absent |
| Analysis of grammar and meaning (4 marks) | Shows how negation, verb form, noun gender and pronoun together build the sentence's message, and draws a conclusion about what the grammar reveals (3-4) | Notes grammatical features without connecting them to meaning (1-2) | No analysis |

The analysis criterion asks you to show how grammar builds meaning. Listing labels without explaining what they reveal does not meet it.
