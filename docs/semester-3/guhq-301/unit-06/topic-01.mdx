# Topic 6.1 - Revelation in its setting

## A real classroom situation

Tariq is completing his B.Ed practicum at a government high school in Mirpurkhas. During a Class 9 Islamic Studies lesson on the Holy Qur'an, a student named Bilawal raises his hand and asks: "Sir, when we read Surah al-Duha or Surah al-Kawthar, the verses are very short, rhythmic, and poetic, talking about patience, the morning brightness, and comfort. But when our teacher reads Surah al-Baqarah, there are long, detailed verses about commercial contracts, marriage, inheritance, and fasting. Why are some surahs so different from others?"

Before Tariq can answer, another student, Zeeshan, interjects: "And sir, if a verse was revealed because someone in seventh-century Makkah mocked the Prophet (PBUH) after his infant son passed away, does that verse only speak about that seventh-century man, or does it have a message for us in Sindh today?"

Tariq realizes that these two students have touched upon two fundamental concepts of Quranic studies: the distinction between the Makki and Madani revelation periods, and the science of asbab al-nuzul (the occasions of revelation). To teach the Qur'an meaningfully, a teacher must know not only the vocabulary and grammar of the text, but also the historical, cultural, and human environment into which it was communicated over twenty-three years.

## Explanation

This topic explores the historical and cultural context of the Qur'an across four dimensions: the concept of asbab al-nuzul, the characteristics and distinction between Makki and Madani surahs, the social and cultural environment of pre-Islamic Arabia, and the impact of context on balanced interpretation.

### Concept of asbab al-nuzul (reasons of revelation)

The term *asbab al-nuzul* (singular: *sabab al-nuzul*) literally translates to the "causes", "occasions", or "reasons" of revelation. In the sciences of the Qur'an (*Ulum al-Quran*), it denotes the specific historical occurrences, inquiries, or circumstances that directly coincided with the revelation of a particular verse or group of verses during the lifetime of the Prophet Muhammad (PBUH).

Classical scholars, such as al-Wahidi and al-Suyuti, compiled dedicated treatises on asbab al-nuzul based on authenticated reports transmitted by the Companions, who witnessed the events firsthand. As Abdul Kadir, Muhamed Ali, Md Ariffin, and Usman (2022) note, knowledge of asbab al-nuzul constitutes an indispensable pillar of sound exegesis (*tafsir*). It clarifies the immediate historical context, identifies the individuals or communities involved, explains why a legal decree or moral admonition was communicated at that precise moment, and reveals the divine wisdom underlying the text.

Consider two concrete examples of asbab al-nuzul:

1. **Surah al-Duha (93:1-3):** Following an initial period of revelation, communication paused for a brief interval (*fatrat al-wahy*). Opponents in Makkah began to jeer and mock the Prophet (PBUH), claiming that his Lord had abandoned him. In response to this painful emotional trial, the opening verses of Surah al-Duha descended as tender divine comfort: "By the morning brightness, and by the night when it grows still, your Lord has not forsaken you, nor is He displeased." Knowing this occasion transforms our reading from an abstract poetic reflection into an intimate reassurance given in the depths of sorrow.
2. **Surah al-Kawthar (108:1-3):** When the Prophet's infant son died, an opponent named al-As ibn Wa'il mocked him publicly as *abtar* (one cut off from lineage, legacy, and posterity). In Arabian society, where male progeny was seen as the sole measure of honor and continuity, this was a cruel insult. The surah descended immediately to comfort the Prophet: "Indeed, We have granted you al-Kawthar (abundance). So pray to your Lord and sacrifice. Indeed, your enemy is the one cut off."

**Correcting a common misconception: "Every verse in the Qur'an has a recorded occasion of revelation."**
A widespread error among students is the belief that every single ayah was prompted by an external event or question. Classical scholars explicitly refute this. The vast majority of the Qur'an was revealed initially (*ibtidā'an*) without any preceding event, dispute, or question. These verses directly introduced the foundations of faith, described the majesty of the Creator, warned of the Day of Judgement, recounted stories of earlier prophets, and outlined universal moral obligations. Asbab al-nuzul applies specifically to those passages that responded directly to historical moments, communal crises, questions posed by believers or opponents, or legal dilemmas.

### Makki and Madani surahs

The Qur'an was revealed gradually over a span of approximately twenty-three years (from 610 CE to 632 CE). Scholars classify its 114 surahs into two major categories: Makki (Meccan) and Madani (Medinan).

The primary and agreed upon classical standard for this classification is **chronological**, anchored by the historic event of the Hijrah (the migration of the Prophet and his companions from Makkah to Madinah in 622 CE):

- **Makki surahs** are those revealed **before the Hijrah**, covering the first thirteen years of prophethood (610-622 CE).
- **Madani surahs** are those revealed **after the Hijrah**, covering the remaining ten years of prophethood in Madinah (622-632 CE).

Under this standard criterion, even if a verse was revealed geographically outside Madinah (for example, at the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah or in Makkah during the Farewell Pilgrimage in 10 AH), it is classified as Madani because it was revealed after the Hijrah.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Makki and Madani refer only to where a surah was revealed geographically."**
Some beginners assume that if a surah was revealed within the physical borders of Makkah, it is automatically Makki. As clarified above, classical scholars established that the dividing line is chronological rather than purely geographical. A verse revealed in Makkah during the Conquest of Makkah (8 AH) or the Farewell Pilgrimage (such as Surah al-Ma'idah 5:3) is considered Madani because it belongs to the post-Hijrah phase of communal governance.

<Figure id="fig-U6-2" src="/img/figures/guhq-301/unit-06/fig-U6-2.svg" alt="Table comparing Makki and Madani surahs across four dimensions: historical period, primary themes, linguistic style and verse length, and audience addressed." />

The differences between Makki and Madani surahs reflect the evolving needs of the Muslim community across two distinct historical stages:

1. **Themes:**
   - *Makki surahs* focused on the fundamentals of faith (*iman*): establishing Tawhid (the absolute oneness of God), refuting polytheism (*shirk*), establishing belief in the afterlife and resurrection (*al-Akhirah*), vindicating prophethood, and building personal moral fortitude amidst persecution.
   - *Madani surahs* built upon this spiritual foundation to establish the legal, institutional, and social framework of a functioning community: civil and criminal legislation, contracts, family law (marriage, divorce, inheritance), acts of collective worship (fasting, zakat, hajj), rules of warfare and peace treaties, and relations with the People of the Book and hypocrites (*munafiqun*).
2. **Style and verse length:**
   - *Makki surahs* are generally characterized by short, rhythmic, intense verses with powerful cadence, evocative oaths by cosmic phenomena (the sun, the night, the dawn), and striking rhetorical questions designed to awaken the slumbering conscience.
   - *Madani surahs* feature longer, comprehensive, and detailed prose sections that explain legal ordinances, communal responsibilities, and institutional covenants with precision.
3. **Forms of address:**
   - In *Makki surahs*, the general address is directed to humanity as a whole: *"Ya ayyuha al-nas"* ("O mankind!").
   - In *Madani surahs*, the direct address frequently calls upon the formed community of faith: *"Ya ayyuha alladhina amanu"* ("O you who have believed!").

### Cultural and social background of revelation

To appreciate why the Qur'an was revealed in the manner and style it assumed, student-teachers must examine the cultural and social world of seventh-century pre-Islamic Arabia, often referred to in Islamic historiography as the era of *Jahiliyyah* (the state of ignorance and unrestrained tribalism).

Pre-Islamic Arabian society had distinctive cultural traits:

1. **Oral mastery and poetic eloquence:** The pre-Islamic Arabs possessed an extraordinary oral culture. Eloquence, poetry (*shi'r*), and rhetoric were the supreme arts of the Arabian peninsula. At seasonal fairs like Suq Ukaz, poets competed for social prestige, and celebrated odes (*Mu'allaqat*) were preserved and honored. The Qur'an met this culture at its peak. It challenged the poets with a divine discourse whose rhythm, diction, and emotional power surpassed human literary invention, establishing the doctrine of *I'jaz al-Quran* (the inimitability of the Qur'an).
2. **Tribalism and social stratification:** Identity was strictly tied to tribal lineage (*asabiyyah*). Loyalty to one's clan took precedence over justice. In this environment, the weak, orphans, slaves, and women possessed few legal protections. Practices such as usury (*riba*), blood feuds, and the burial of newborn female infants (*wa'd al-banat*) reflected deep socio-economic injustice.
3. **Polytheism and ancestral customs:** The Quraysh served as custodians of the Ka'bah, which housed hundreds of idols representing tribal deities. Idolatry was not merely a spiritual practice; it formed the backbone of Makkah's commercial prosperity and political hegemony.

The Qur'an engaged this environment through **gradual revelation** (*tanjim* or *tadrij*). Rather than imposing a complete legislative code overnight on a society accustomed to tribal customs, the Qur'an reformed society systematically over twenty-three years:

- In Makkah, it dismantled idolatry, instilled belief in divine accountability, and awakened compassion for the marginalized: "So as for the orphan, do not oppress him; and as for the beggar, do not repel him" (93:9-10).
- In Madinah, it replaced tribal chauvinism with the brotherhood of faith and codified laws that abolished infanticide, granted legal inheritance and economic rights to women, outlawed usury, and instituted mandatory institutional charity (*zakat*) to protect the vulnerable.

Understanding this cultural backdrop demonstrates to learners that divine revelation did not speak into a vacuum. It spoke directly to real human conditions, transforming a fractured, tribal society into an ethically responsible civilization.

### Impact of context on interpretation

What is the relationship between the historical context of a verse and its meaning for subsequent generations?

A central hermeneutical rule in the science of tafsir, analyzed comprehensively by Abdul Kadir and colleagues (2022), addresses this question:
**"Al-ʿibrah bi ʿumum al-lafz la bi khusus al-sabab"**
(The ruling or lesson is derived from the generality of the wording, not from the particularity of the specific occasion).

When a verse was revealed in response to a particular individual, question, or historical incident, but its phrasing is general and universal, the interpretation is never confined solely to that initial incident. Instead, the general wording governs, extending the guidance to every person, community, and time facing a comparable situation.

Consider how context and wording interact in practice:

<Figure id="fig-U6-3" src="/img/figures/guhq-301/unit-06/fig-U6-3.svg" alt="Diagram showing how a verse, its occasion of revelation, and the general principle connect to draw a timeless lesson." />

As shown in the diagram:
1. **The occasion provides the concrete setting:** Surah al-Kawthar was prompted by al-As ibn Wa'il mocking the Prophet (PBUH) upon the death of his son.
2. **The wording provides the universal principle:** The verse does not name the mocking opponent, nor does it restrict "al-Kawthar" or the command to pray and sacrifice to that afternoon in Makkah. It establishes a universal spiritual reality: material loss and verbal mockery cannot extinguish divine favor, and the righteous response to grief and animosity is prayer, gratitude, and devotion to God.
3. **The lesson applies across time:** A school pupil in Pakistan who is mocked or bullied for their humble background finds direct solace in Surah al-Kawthar. The verse assures them that their dignity rests in their character and connection with God, while malice and mockery ultimately leave the oppressor spiritually bankrupt.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Knowing the historical context limits a verse to its own time."**
Some readers mistakenly believe that if we link a verse to a seventh-century event, we reduce it to an obsolete historical document. Abdul Kadir and colleagues (2022) demonstrate that the reality is the exact opposite. Far from confining the verse to the past, knowledge of the historical context acts as a safeguard against two dangerous extremes:
- **Abstract decontextualization:** Reading verses completely detached from their setting can lead people to tear stern warnings out of context (for example, applying verses about battlefield enemies to peaceful neighbours, or taking specific historical exceptions as universal rules).
- **Historical obsolescence:** Confining a verse strictly to its initial recipients strips the text of its living moral authority.

By holding both the specific occasion and the general wording together, the student-teacher discovers the underlying rationale (*'illah*) of the text, allowing its ethical wisdom to illuminate contemporary classroom and personal challenges.

## Activity: Investigating the setting of a surah

Work in pairs. Allow approximately 20 minutes for this collaborative inquiry.

Select two short surahs that you frequently recite: **Surah al-Kawthar (108)** and **Surah al-Ma'un (107)**.

1. **Classify (4 minutes):** Examine the verses of both surahs. Note down whether each is classified as Makki or Madani (or disputed), and identify the textual indicators (such as focus on the orphan, the needy, prayer, and hypocrisy).
2. **Investigate the context (6 minutes):** Recall or look up the historical occasion of revelation for each surah.
   - For Surah al-Kawthar: What event prompted the revelation, and what emotional state did it address?
   - For Surah al-Ma'un: What social behaviors (denying the Day of Judgement, repelling the orphan, withholding small kindnesses) were prevalent in pre-Islamic Makkah?
3. **Apply the principle of generality (5 minutes):** Apply the rule *"al-ʿibrah bi ʿumum al-lafz"* to Surah al-Ma'un. How does a verse that censured the wealthy Quraysh for refusing small household utensils (*al-ma'un*) translate into an ethical duty for students in a Pakistani school today?
4. **Synthesize and share (5 minutes):** Pair up with another group and compare your conclusions. Discuss: How would you explain to a Class 9 pupil that Surah al-Ma'un connects genuine religious faith directly with caring for vulnerable members of society?

## Check your understanding

2. *(Understand)* Explain why classical scholars state that not every verse in the Holy Qur'an has a recorded occasion of revelation.
3. *(Understand)* Describe three prominent social or cultural characteristics of pre-Islamic Arabian society (*Jahiliyyah*), and explain how the gradual revelation of the Qur'an addressed them.
4. *(Apply)* A student remarks: "Surah al-Kawthar was only meant to comfort the Prophet (PBUH) when his son passed away; it has no application for us today." Drawing upon the hermeneutical rule of general wording, explain how you would help the student recognize the enduring relevance of the surah.
5. *(Apply)* Contrast the typical themes, verse length, and style of a Makki surah (such as Surah al-Inshirah or Surah al-Qari'ah) with those of a Madani surah (such as Surah al-Hujurat).

## Summary

The Holy Qur'an was revealed gradually over twenty-three years into the living cultural and historical setting of seventh-century Arabia. *Asbab al-nuzul* denotes the specific historical moments, disputes, or questions that coincided with the descent of certain verses. Understanding these occasions illuminates the text, revealing its divine wisdom and immediate purpose, though the majority of verses descended initially without a preceding incident to establish universal principles of faith and ethics.

The division of surahs into Makki (pre-Hijrah) and Madani (post-Hijrah) marks two complementary phases of revelation: the first building spiritual foundations, conviction, and moral resilience; the second codifying communal law, social justice, and institutional structures. Knowledge of the pre-Islamic cultural landscape highlights how divine revelation met Arabs at their highest art of poetic eloquence and systematically reformed tribal injustice. In exegesis, the classical principle that "the ruling is derived from the generality of the wording, not from the particularity of the occasion" ensures that understanding historical context does not confine the Qur'an to the past, but rather uncovers its timeless moral guidance for our lives today.
