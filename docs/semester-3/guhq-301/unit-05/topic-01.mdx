# Topic 5.1 - Principles of tafsir

## A real classroom situation

Rabia is a B.Ed trainee at a government girls' high school in Sukkur. In a Class 9 Quran period a pupil reads 22:4 and asks, "Miss, can I explain this verse in my own words?" Rabia wants to say yes, but another pupil says, "Only the scholars can interpret the Qur'an, miss. We should just read it." Rabia knows the verse speaks of those who turn away from the truth, but she is not sure how far she may go. By the end of this topic you will know what tafsir is, where it draws its authority from, and which principles keep it honest.

## Explanation

This topic introduces tafsir, the principles that guide it, and the role of context and language.

### Introduction to tafsir

Tafsir is the effort to understand what the Qur'an means. It is not the same as reciting the text, and it is not a free opinion about it. The classical definition, quoted by Kadir and colleagues (2022), is that tafsir is "a set of knowledge employed to have the best understanding of the Qur'an" by clarifying its meaning, drawing out its rulings and wisdom, with the help of language, grammar, the principles of jurisprudence, the occasions of revelation and the science of abrogation. The same source notes that the Qur'an was revealed for three purposes: to be recited as worship, to be understood, and to be acted on. Tafsir serves the second and third purposes.

The Qur'an itself calls the reader to reflection. 38:29 says it is "a blessed Book which We have revealed to you, that they might reflect upon its verses"; 47:24 asks whether people do not reflect upon the Qur'an; and 54:17 says it was made easy for remembrance.

A teacher does not have to be a mufti to explain a clear verse. A teacher can put a verse's plain meaning into a pupil's own words and connect it to the pupil's life, as long as the explanation rests on the text and the accepted tools. Tafsir as a disciplined field belongs to trained scholars; explaining a clear verse in a class is part of a teacher's work.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Anyone can interpret a verse from its translation alone."** A translation is already someone's interpretation, and it can mislead when one word carries more than one sense. Al-Hilali (2026) showed that English translations of the Qur'an sit on a spectrum between literal and interpretive, which Unit 4 used. A teacher who reads only a translation cannot see the grammar, the context or the reason behind a verse, so an explanation built on it alone is weak. The opposite idea, "tafsir is only for scholars and has no place in a classroom", is also wrong. A teacher who ignores the tools and speaks from impulse is not doing tafsir either; the classroom needs the discipline, not the title.

### Principles of interpreting Quranic verses

The classical discipline that gathered these rules is called qawaʿid al-tafsir, the principles of interpretation. Kadir and colleagues (2022) review them in the work of the Indonesian exegete M. Quraish Shihab. Three principles matter for this unit.

1. **Interpret the Qur'an by the Qur'an.** A verse that is unclear in one place is often clear in another. Read 103:1-3 together: the oath in verse 1 and the exception in verse 3 explain what "loss" means. 38:29 calls the whole Book "blessed", and its verses explain one another.
2. **The decisive point is the general wording, not the particular occasion.** This is the rule Shihab applies most: al-ʿibrah bi ʿumum al-lafz la bi khusus al-sabab. If a verse was revealed about a particular event but its wording is general, the ruling is drawn from the general wording, not only from the event. For example, 22:4 was revealed about a man named al-Nadar ibn al-Harith, but its wording is general, so it covers every stubborn opponent of the truth, in every time (Kadir et al., 2022).
3. **The Sunnah and the language set the bounds.** The Prophet's practice explains and limits a general command, and Arabic grammar decides which reading a sentence allows. A rule that contradicts the language is not tafsir.

The table below sets out the three principles with an example of each. Follow it as a worked example.

Read the second row. 22:4 says, "It has been decreed for him that whoever takes him as a protector, he will misguide him and lead him to the punishment of the Blaze." The verse names no one. If we read only the occasion, we might confine it to one man in the seventh century. If we read the general wording, we see a warning that reaches every age. The principle tells us which reading to prefer, and why.

The third row shows the opposite case. 92:17-18 says, "But the righteous one will avoid it, the one who gives from his wealth to purify himself." Some scholars read these verses as specific to Abu Bakr. Al-Suyuti argued, on the grammar of the word *al-atqa* (the most righteous), that the wording is specific, and Kadir and colleagues (2022) judge his argument stronger. The rule of generality is not a licence to generalise every verse; it applies where the wording itself is general.

### Role of context and language

Context is the arrangement of the text around a verse (siyaq) and the circumstances of its revelation (asbab al-nuzul). Kadir and colleagues (2022) describe asbab al-nuzul as the events or questions that occasioned a verse, and they note that the principle of generality means an occasion explains the verse without confining it. The same article sets out a second rule: an occasion may cover situations before or after it, as long as the verse's wording includes them.

A simple classroom example: 2:114 asks, "who are more unjust than those who prevent the name of Allah from being mentioned in His mosques?" The verse was revealed about a particular place of worship, but its wording is general. The occasion gives the verse a place and a face; the wording makes it speak about every such prevention.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Context does not affect meaning."** Context does not change the wording, but it decides how the wording is heard. A verse about a specific event is applied to that event and to every situation its wording covers. Reading a verse without its context can turn a general warning into a personal attack, or a specific ruling into a universal law. The context and the grammar are the guardrails that keep interpretation on the road.

## Activity: Which principle is at work?

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Take three short passages: 22:4, 92:17-18 and 5:33.

1. **Read (3 minutes per passage).** Read the passage aloud once, then say what its wording actually says, without adding anything.
2. **Classify (3 minutes per passage).** Decide whether the wording is general or specific, and say why.
3. **Match (4 minutes per passage).** Say which principle a reader should apply: interpret the Qur'an by the Qur'an, the generality of the wording, or the occasion and context.
4. **Compare (5 minutes).** Join another pair and defend one answer you disagree on. Say what would change your mind.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Define tafsir in one sentence, and name two tools it uses.
2. *(Remember)* State the rule "al-ʿibrah bi ʿumum al-lafz la bi khusus al-sabab" in your own words.
3. *(Understand)* In two or three sentences, explain why a translation alone is not a sufficient basis for interpreting a verse.
4. *(Apply)* A reader says 22:4 was revealed about al-Nadar, so it applies only to him. Using the principle above, explain what the reader has missed.
5. *(Apply)* Explain in two sentences how the occasion of a verse and its general wording work together.

## Summary

Tafsir is the disciplined effort to understand the Qur'an, grounded in the text itself, the Sunnah, the Companions' usage and the Arabic language. Its principles, above all the rule that the ruling is drawn from the generality of the wording rather than the particularity of the occasion, keep interpretation honest. Context and grammar do not change the wording; they show how far its meaning reaches.

## Summative task

Choose one of 22:4, 92:17-18 or 109:1-6. Write 250-300 words explaining its meaning to a Class 9 pupil. Show how the wording, the occasion and one principle of interpretation shape your explanation, and include one sentence on what a reader who ignored the principle might get wrong.
