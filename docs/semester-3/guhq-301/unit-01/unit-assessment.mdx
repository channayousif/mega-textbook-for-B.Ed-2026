# Introduction to Quranic Arabic - Assessment and Review

## Unit summary

The Qur'an asks to be understood. It says it was sent down "as an Arabic Qur'an that you might understand" (12:2), and it asks its readers to reflect on its verses (38:29, 47:24). Memorising and reciting are valuable acts of worship, but understanding is a separate skill. A teacher needs it for guidance, prayer, reflection and teaching, and Urdu and Sindhi speakers already know many Qur'anic words.

Quranic Arabic is the Arabic of the Qur'an itself. Its words grow from roots; it says much in few words, so that even word order carries meaning; it has rhythm and rhyme but no poetic metre; it gives familiar words exact meanings, such as *ṣalāh* and *kutiba*; and it speaks directly to its listeners. It is the central text of Classical Arabic, the literary Arabic of early Islam and the century before it. It shares that literature's words and images but gives them a new purpose of guidance and reflection. It is not the same as the Arabic spoken in Arab countries today, which is a family of dialects alongside Modern Standard Arabic.

Urdu merges sounds that Arabic keeps apart, such as ع and alif, or ق and ک, and in the Qur'an the difference can change a word: *ʿalīm* means Knowing, *alīm* means painful. Arabic words are built from a root, usually three letters with a core meaning, and a pattern that adds its own meaning, so *kātib*, *kitāb* and *maktūb* form one family. The root guides the reader, but the verse decides. Frequent expressions, learned as wholes, give footholds in any passage.

## Summative assessment

Attempt every question before you read the final guidance section. Each MCQ has one best answer. For restricted responses, keep to the length stated. For extended responses, build an argument with evidence from the Qur'an, not a list of definitions. Translations are Saheeh International (Quran.com).

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. In 12:2, what reason does the Qur'an give for being sent down in Arabic? *(Remember)*
   - A) So that it would be easy to memorise
   - B) So that its listeners might understand
   - C) So that it could be written down quickly
   - D) So that poets would accept it
2. In this course, "Quranic Arabic" means: *(Remember)*
   - A) The Arabic spoken in Makkah and Madinah today
   - B) The Arabic used on Arabic television news
   - C) The Arabic language exactly as it is used in the text of the Qur'an
   - D) Any Arabic written in the Qur'anic script
3. Which statement best describes how Quranic Arabic relates to Classical Arabic? *(Understand)*
   - A) They are two unrelated languages
   - B) Quranic Arabic is a modern form of Classical Arabic
   - C) Quranic Arabic is one part of Classical Arabic, and its most important text
   - D) Classical Arabic is a simplified form of Quranic Arabic
4. A pupil says that 69:41 proves the Qur'an has no rhythm or rhyme. What is the best reply? *(Apply)*
   - A) The pupil is right; the Qur'an is ordinary prose
   - B) The Qur'an has rhythm and rhyme, but not the fixed metres of poetry
   - C) The Qur'an follows the same metres as pre-Islamic poetry
   - D) Rhythm in the Qur'an comes only from the reciter's voice
5. In *iyyāka naʿbudu* (1:5), the translation "It is You we worship" shows "You alone". Which characteristic of Quranic Arabic does this show? *(Understand)*
   - A) Words with new religious meanings
   - B) Its own preserved spelling
   - C) Much meaning in few words, with word order carrying meaning
   - D) Direct address to the believers
6. Which group contains letters that are separate sounds in Arabic but usually sound alike in Urdu? *(Remember)*
   - A) ب پ
   - B) ث س ص
   - C) چ ج
   - D) گ ک
7. A three-letter set such as ع ل م, which carries the core meaning of a family of words, is called a: *(Remember)*
   - A) Pattern (*wazn*)
   - B) Root (*jadhr*)
   - C) Verse ending
   - D) Vowel mark
8. A pupil reads the end of 2:10 with the first sound of *ʿalīm* instead of *alīm*. What has gone wrong? *(Apply)*
   - A) Nothing; the two words are the same
   - B) She has turned "painful" into "Knowing" by using the wrong first letter
   - C) She has missed a *shadda*
   - D) She has read a long vowel as a short one
9. *Maktūb* follows the pattern *mafʿūl*. Using the root k-t-b (writing), what does it mean? *(Apply)*
   - A) A writer
   - B) He wrote
   - C) Something written
   - D) A place of writing
10. Which expression means "Indeed we belong to Allah, and indeed to Him we will return"? *(Remember)*
    - A) *Al-ḥamdu lillāh*
    - B) *Innā lillāhi wa innā ilayhi rājiʿūn*
    - C) *Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū*
    - D) *Wa Allāhu ʿalīmun ḥakīm*

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

1. In three sentences, explain why memorising a surah is not the same as understanding it. Use one verse to support your answer. *(Understand)*
2. Give two reasons, from the four in Topic 1.1, why a school teacher in Pakistan should understand Quranic Arabic. Support each with a verse or a classroom example. *(Understand)*
3. Explain, in two or three sentences, how the Qur'an gave a familiar word a new, exact meaning. Use one example. *(Understand)*
4. A trainee says, "Quranic Arabic and Classical Arabic are opposites." In three sentences, explain what is wrong with this and state the correct relationship. *(Analyze)*
5. Using the example of ruins (*aṭlāl*), analyse in three or four sentences how the same image does a different job in pre-Islamic poetry and in the Qur'an. *(Analyze)*
6. Explain, with one example from the Qur'an, why a teacher of Nazira Quran should notice when pupils merge ق with ک. *(Understand)*
7. In two sentences, explain the difference between a root and a pattern, with one example of each. *(Understand)*
8. Find the root and pattern of *ʿālim*, and explain how its meaning follows from them. *(Apply)*
9. Break *yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū* into its four words, give the meaning of each, and name the root of *āmanū* with one Urdu word from it. *(Apply)*
10. *Kutiba* in 2:183 comes from the root for writing but means "decreed". In three sentences, analyse what this shows about using roots to find meaning. *(Analyze)*

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. A parent at a school meeting says: "My son has memorised Juz Amma. He does not need to understand Arabic; that is for scholars." Write a respectful reply of about 250 words. Use at least two verses and evaluate the parent's view fairly, saying what is right in it as well as what it leaves out. *(Evaluate)*
2. A trainee claims: "Quranic Arabic is the same as Classical Arabic, and both are the same as the Arabic spoken in Saudi Arabia today." In about 250 words, analyse this claim. Show what is accurate in it, what is not, and give evidence for each point. *(Analyze)*
3. Choose three words from this list: *kātib* (2:282), *ʿalīm* (2:32), *raḥmah* (2:157), *maʿlūm* (15:21), *kitāb* (2:2), *ʿallama* (96:4). For each, give its root and pattern, and explain how the meaning follows. Then analyse one case where the verse narrows or shifts the meaning that the root suggests. *(Analyze)*
4. Your cooperating teacher asks you to plan a 15-minute warm-up for a Class 6 Nazira Quran group that merges ع with alif and ح with ه. Design the warm-up, using at least two Qur'anic words where the sound changes the meaning, and justify each step. *(Create)*
5. Using everything in this unit, evaluate this statement: "An Urdu-speaking teacher can begin to understand the Qur'an faster than an English-speaking one." Use evidence about vocabulary, roots, letters and frequent expressions, and identify at least one way Urdu can also mislead a reader. *(Evaluate)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### MCQ answer key

1. B - 12:2 says "that you might understand". A is a true benefit (54:17) but not the reason given in 12:2.
2. C - Quranic Arabic is the language of the Qur'an's text. A and B describe Arabic used today.
3. C - Classical Arabic includes poetry, Hadith and early prose; the Qur'an is its central text.
4. B - The Qur'an has rhythm and verse endings that rhyme, but 69:41 says it is not a poet's word, and it follows no fixed metre.
5. C - Putting "You" first in two words gives the meaning "You alone"; word order carries meaning.
6. B - ث, س and ص are three Arabic sounds that Urdu usually pronounces as s. The other pairs are Urdu letters not used in the Qur'an, or different sounds in both languages.
7. B - A root carries the core meaning; a pattern is the frame that turns it into a word.
8. B - *Alīm* begins with a glottal stop and means painful; *ʿalīm* begins with ع and means Knowing.
9. C - *Mafʿūl* names the thing the action is done to, so *maktūb* is something written.
10. B - This is 2:156.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. Model: Memorising preserves the sounds of a surah; understanding means knowing what its words say and what they ask. A child can recite perfectly without knowing a word's meaning. The Qur'an asks for both: it was sent in Arabic "that you might understand" (12:2) and asks readers to reflect (38:29). **Marks (3):** 1 - distinguishes the two skills; 1 - example or explanation; 1 - correctly referenced verse.
2. Model: any two of guidance (2:2, guidance helps only when understood), prayer (1:5 recited in every rak'ah becomes a conscious promise), reflection (38:29, 47:24) and teaching (pupils ask what words mean). **Marks (4):** 1 per reason (2); 1 per supporting verse or classroom example (2).
3. Model: *ṣalāh*, *zakāh* or *ṣawm* existed with general meanings and became exact acts of worship; or *kutiba*, from the root for writing, means "decreed" in 2:183. **Marks (3):** 1 - names a word; 1 - states the general or root meaning; 1 - states the Qur'anic meaning.
4. Model: They are not opposites, because Quranic Arabic is part of Classical Arabic. Classical Arabic is the whole literature of early Islam and the century before, and the Qur'an is its central text. They share words and images, but the Qur'an gives them a new purpose. **Marks (3):** 1 - rejects "opposites"; 1 - states "part of" correctly; 1 - names a shared feature and a difference.
5. Model: In pre-Islamic poetry the ruins of an empty campsite make the poet remember a lost love and feel time passing. In the Qur'an, the ruins of destroyed towns are signs that ask the listener to reflect on earlier peoples and change how they live. The words are shared; the purpose changes from feeling to reflection and action (Yazar & Batur, 2025). **Marks (4):** 1 - poetry's use; 1 - the Qur'an's use; 2 - analysis of the change in purpose.
6. Model: In Arabic, ق and ک are different sounds, and a merged sound can turn one word into another: *qulūb* (hearts, 2:10) begins with ق, while *kalb* (dog, 18:18) begins with ک. A teacher who notices early can correct the habit before it sets. **Marks (3):** 1 - states they are separate sounds; 1 - a correct example; 1 - the teaching reason.
7. Model: A root is usually three letters carrying a core meaning, such as ك ت ب (writing). A pattern is a frame of vowels and extra letters that adds meaning, such as *fāʿil* (the one who does it), giving *kātib*. **Marks (2):** 1 - root with example; 1 - pattern with example.
8. Model: Root ع ل م (knowing); pattern *fāʿil* (the one who does it); so *ʿālim* is one who knows. **Marks (3):** 1 - root; 1 - pattern; 1 - meaning explained from both.
9. Model: *yā* (O), *ayyuhā* (you), *alladhīna* (who), *āmanū* (have believed); root ء م ن; Urdu *īmān*, *muʾmin*, *amānat* or *amn*. **Marks (4):** 2 - all four meanings (1 for two or three); 1 - root; 1 - Urdu word.
10. Model: A root gives a core idea, but a word's Qur'anic meaning can move away from it. Writing something down makes it fixed, and *kutiba* means it has been made a duty. So a reader uses the root as a guide and then checks the verse before deciding. **Marks (3):** 1 - root as guide; 1 - shift explained; 1 - conclusion that the verse decides.

### ERQ rubrics

1. Analytic rubric (10 marks) - *Verses used* (0-3): two or more correctly referenced verses on understanding or reflection · *Fair evaluation* (0-4, **Evaluate**: credits the value of memorisation and shows clearly what the view leaves out, reaching a reasoned judgement) · *Respectful tone suited to a parent* (0-1) · *Clarity and organisation* (0-2).
2. Analytic rubric (10 marks) - *Accurate parts identified* (0-2): Quranic Arabic is part of Classical Arabic · *Inaccurate parts identified* (0-3): the Qur'an's distinct purpose and style; spoken dialects and Modern Standard Arabic differ · *Evidence* (0-3, **Analyze**: uses specific examples, such as 69:41, the ruins image or *ṣalāh*, and explains what each shows) · *Clarity* (0-2).
3. Analytic rubric (12 marks) - *Roots and patterns* (0-3): one mark per word correct · *Meanings explained* (0-3): shows how root and pattern combine for each word · *Context analysis* (0-4, **Analyze**: uses the verse to show how context narrows or shifts the root meaning, for example *maʿlūm* as "a known [specified] measure" in 15:21, or *al-kitāb* as the revealed Book in 2:2) · *Accuracy of references* (0-2).
4. Analytic rubric (10 marks) - *Qur'anic word pairs* (0-3): at least two correct pairs where the sound changes meaning, such as *ʿalīm* and *alīm* · *Design of steps* (0-3): listening, contrast, practice and checking in a sensible order within 15 minutes · *Justification* (0-3, **Create**: each step is explained by how it helps pupils hear and produce the difference) · *Suitability for Class 6* (0-1).
5. Analytic rubric (12 marks) - *Vocabulary and roots* (0-3): Urdu words from Qur'anic roots, with examples · *Letters and expressions* (0-3): familiar script and daily expressions, with the problem of merged sounds · *Ways Urdu misleads* (0-2): merged sounds or different meanings, such as *kitāb* · *Judgement* (0-4, **Evaluate**: weighs advantages against risks and reaches a qualified conclusion supported by the evidence).
