## A real classroom situation

Saima is a B.Ed trainee at a government girls' high school in Hyderabad. She is helping a Class 7 Nazira Quran group. One girl reads 2:10 and says the last words, *ʿadhābun alīm*, with the same sound she uses for *ʿalīm* in 2:32. Saima knows these are different words, but she cannot explain why, because in Urdu both first letters sound the same to her.

At the end of the lesson another girl, Mehwish, points to the board. Saima has written كِتَاب (*kitāb*, book), كَاتِب (*kātib*, writer) and مَكْتُوب (*maktūb*, written) for a spelling exercise. "Miss, why do these words look like cousins?" Saima smiles, because Mehwish has noticed the most useful fact about Arabic words. By the end of this topic you will be able to answer both girls.

## Explanation

This topic gives you three tools for reading Quranic Arabic: the sounds of its letters, the way its words are built, and the expressions it repeats most often.

### Arabic alphabet review and pronunciation (Quranic focus)

**What you already know.** The Arabic alphabet has 28 letters. It is written from right to left, most letters join to the next one, and a letter changes shape at the start, middle and end of a word. Short vowels are marks above or below a letter: *fatḥa* (a), *kasra* (i) and *ḍamma* (u). A *sukūn* shows a letter with no vowel, and a *shadda* shows a doubled letter. Long vowels are written with alif, wāw and yā'. If you read *nazira*, you already use all of this.

**What Urdu adds and what it hides.** Urdu uses the same script with extra letters for its own sounds, such as پ, ٹ, چ, ڈ, ڑ and گ. None of these is in the Qur'an. The bigger problem runs the other way. Urdu kept many Arabic letters in spelling but stopped pronouncing them differently. In everyday Urdu:

| These Arabic letters | usually sound in Urdu like |
|---|---|
| ث س ص | s |
| ذ ز ض ظ | z |
| ت ط | t |
| ح ه | h |
| ع ا | a (a plain vowel) |
| ق ک | k |

In Arabic, every letter in each row is a separate sound, made at a different place in the throat or mouth.

**Why it matters: the sound can change the word.** Here is a worked example with two words that both appear in the Qur'an.

| Word | First letter | Meaning | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| عَلِيم (*ʿalīm*) | ع, a sound from the middle of the throat | Knowing, All-Knowing | "it is You who is the Knowing, the Wise" (2:32) |
| أَلِيم (*alīm*) | أ, a plain glottal stop | painful | "for them is a painful punishment" (2:10) |

If a reader says ع like alif, a verse about Allah's knowledge can sound like a word for pain. A second pair: قُلُوب (*qulūb*, hearts) in 2:10 begins with ق, made deep in the throat, while كَلْب (*kalb*, dog) in 18:18 begins with ك, made at the back of the mouth. Saima can now tell her pupil: "These two words begin with different letters, and the Qur'an uses them for different things. Let us practise the sound of ع."

**Tajwid** is the set of rules for reciting the Qur'an correctly. It describes the exact place where each letter is produced (its *makhraj*) and the qualities of its sound (its *ṣifāt*). Abdelfattah, Khalil and Abbas (2025) point out that these rules, set out by Muslim scholars, are so precise that they can even be used to check recitation automatically. A Nazira teacher does not need to be a *qāri*. But a teacher who knows which letters Urdu speakers merge can listen for them and correct them early.

### Word formation in Quranic Arabic

**The root.** Most Arabic words are built on a **root** (*jadhr*): usually three consonants that carry a core meaning. The root ع ل م (ʿ-l-m) carries the idea of knowing. The root ك ت ب (k-t-b) carries the idea of writing.

**The pattern.** A root is not yet a word. To make a word, Arabic puts the root letters into a **word pattern** (*wazn*): a fixed frame of vowels and, sometimes, extra letters. Grammarians write patterns with the letters ف ع ل (f-ʿ-l) standing in for any root. Each pattern adds its own meaning.

| Pattern | What the pattern adds | With ع ل م | With ك ت ب |
|---|---|---|---|
| *faʿala* | he did it (past action) | *ʿalima*, he knew | *kataba*, he wrote |
| *faʿʿala* | he made someone do it | *ʿallama*, he taught | - |
| *fāʿil* | the one who does it | *ʿālim*, one who knows | *kātib*, writer |
| *mafʿūl* | the thing it is done to | *maʿlūm*, known | *maktūb*, written |
| *faʿīl* | one who has the quality, often strongly | *ʿalīm*, all-knowing | - |
| *fiʿl* or *fiʿāl* | a noun naming the idea or thing | *ʿilm* (*fiʿl*), knowledge | *kitāb* (*fiʿāl*), book |

A dash means that this root does not take this pattern in the Qur'an. Not every root fills every pattern.

**How often these words occur.** The Quranic Arabic Corpus (Dukes et al.) lists every word in the Qur'an under its root. It counts 854 words from the root ʿ-l-m, including *ʿalīm* 163 times, *ʿilm* 105 times, *ʿallama* 41 times, *ʿālim* 18 times and *maʿlūm* 11 times. From k-t-b it counts 319 words, of which *kitāb* alone is 260. Learning one root gives you a whole family of words at once. The diagram at the top of this topic shows the family of ʿ-l-m.

**A worked example: from word to meaning.** The flowchart below shows the four steps, using كَاتِب (*kātib*) from 2:282.

<Figure id="fig-U1-4" src="/img/figures/guhq-301/unit-01/fig-U1-4.svg" alt="Flowchart of four steps from a word to its meaning, with a worked example under each step: 1, find the root by removing the extra letters (from kātib, the root k-t-b); 2, name the pattern (fāʿil); 3, join the root meaning to the pattern meaning (writing plus the one who does it); 4, check the meaning in the verse (a writer, as in 2:282). A return arrow from step 4 to step 1 shows that if the meaning does not fit the verse, the reader looks again." />

1. **Find the root.** Remove the letters that belong to the pattern. In كَاتِب, the long ā after the first letter belongs to the pattern. The root is ك ت ب, writing.
2. **Name the pattern.** The word follows *fāʿil*, the one who does the action.
3. **Join the meanings.** Writing plus "the one who does it" gives "a writer".
4. **Check in context.** Verse 2:282 is about recording a debt: "let a scribe write [it] between you in justice". A *kātib* here is a scribe who writes down an agreement, so the meaning fits. If it had not fitted, you would go back to step 1.

The same verse holds two more words from the family: *fa-ktubūhu* ("write it down") and *yaktub* ("let him write"). It also has *ʿallamahu* ("has taught him"), from the root ʿ-l-m. One verse lets you practise two roots. Urdu speakers meet the same steps in مُعَلِّم (*muʿallim*, teacher): the م at the start and the doubled ل belong to the pattern *mufaʿʿil*, the one who does a *faʿʿala* action, so from *ʿallama* (to teach) it means "one who teaches". The word *muʿallim* itself is not in the Qur'an, but its verb *ʿallama* is (96:4).

**Correcting a common misconception: "Arabic words have no system and must each be learned alone."** Many learners try to memorise every Qur'anic word as a separate item, like a list of telephone numbers. The root-and-pattern system shows that Arabic words come in families. Urdu speakers already know many of these families: *ʿilm*, *ʿālim*, *taʿlīm*, *muʿallim* and *maʿlūm* all come from ʿ-l-m, and *kitāb*, *kātib*, *maktab* and *maktūb* all come from k-t-b. Learning roots means learning one key that opens many words.

**But the system guides; it does not decide.** Two warnings. First, a word's meaning in the Qur'an can move away from the core idea. *Kutiba*, from the root for writing, means "decreed" in 2:183. *Al-ʿālamīn*, "the worlds" in 1:2, is listed under ʿ-l-m in the Corpus, but you could not guess "worlds" from "knowing". Second, an Urdu meaning may differ from the Qur'anic one: in Urdu *kitāb* is any book, but in the Qur'an *al-kitāb* usually means the revealed Book, as in "This is the Book about which there is no doubt" (2:2). So always do step 4.

### Frequently occurring Quranic expressions

Some phrases come back again and again in the Qur'an. Learning them as whole phrases gives a reader quick footholds in any passage. Many are already part of daily speech in Pakistan.

| Expression | Meaning | One place it occurs |
|---|---|---|
| بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ | In the name of Allah, the Entirely Merciful, the Especially Merciful | 1:1; it opens every surah except Surah 9 |
| ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ | [All] praise is [due] to Allah | 1:2 |
| يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ | O you who have believed | 2:153, 2:183 |
| ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ | those who have believed and done righteous deeds | 2:25, 103:3 |
| إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدِيرٌ | Indeed, Allah is over all things competent | 2:20 |
| وَٱللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ | And Allah is Knowing and Wise | 4:26 |
| إِنَّا لِلَّهِ وَإِنَّآ إِلَيْهِ رَٰجِعُونَ | Indeed we belong to Allah, and indeed to Him we will return | 2:156 |
| إِلَّآ أَن يَشَآءَ ٱللَّهُ | except [when adding], "If Allah wills" | 18:24 |
| رَبَّنَا | Our Lord | 2:286 |

Translations are Saheeh International, from Quran.com.

**A worked example: opening a phrase.** Take *yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū*, "O you who have believed".

- *yā* - O (a word for calling someone)
- *ayyuhā* - you (the person called)
- *alladhīna* - who, those who
- *āmanū* - they believed, from the root ء م ن (ʾ-m-n), the idea of faith and safety

The root ʾ-m-n is already in your Urdu: *īmān* (faith), *muʾmin* (believer), *amānat* (a trust) and *amn* (peace). When you meet this phrase you know two things at once: who is being spoken to, and that an instruction for believers usually follows, as in 2:153 ("seek help through patience and prayer") and 2:183 (fasting).

**Phrases and roots work together.** *ʿAlīmun ḥakīm* contains the pattern *faʿīl* twice: *ʿalīm* from ʿ-l-m and *ḥakīm* from ḥ-k-m (wisdom, judgement). Each new phrase you learn gives you practice with roots and patterns, and each new root makes more phrases easy to read.

## Activity: Root families

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

Each group takes one root: **ع ل م** (knowing), **ك ت ب** (writing), **ر ح م** (mercy) or **ء م ن** (faith).

1. **Urdu round (5 minutes).** Without books, list every Urdu or Sindhi word you know that uses your root.
2. **Corpus round (10 minutes).** Search your root in the Quranic Arabic Corpus dictionary. Find three Qur'anic words from it, with one verse for each. For the root ر ح م, for example, the Corpus lists *raḥmah* (mercy) 114 times and *raḥīm* 116 times.
3. **Pattern round (5 minutes).** For each of your three words, name the pattern from the table in this topic, and say what it adds to the meaning.
4. **Teach round (5 minutes).** Pair with another group. Teach them your root family in two minutes, without notes. Then test them: say one new word from your root and ask them to guess its meaning.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name three pairs of letters that are separate sounds in Arabic but usually sound the same in Urdu.
2. *(Remember)* Write five frequent Qur'anic expressions from memory, with their meanings.
3. *(Understand)* In two or three sentences, explain the difference between a root and a pattern, using one example of each.
4. *(Apply)* Use the four steps to find the root, the pattern and the meaning of مَعْلُوم (*maʿlūm*). Then check it: the Corpus lists it 11 times.

## Summary

The Arabic alphabet is familiar to Urdu readers, but Urdu merges sounds that Arabic keeps apart, and in the Qur'an that difference can change a word, as with *ʿalīm* (Knowing) and *alīm* (painful). Arabic words are built from a root that carries a core meaning and a pattern that adds its own meaning, so words come in families that Urdu speakers often already know. The system guides the reader, but each meaning must be checked in its verse. Frequent expressions, learned as wholes, give a reader footholds in any passage and practice with roots and patterns.

## Try this at your practicum school

Listen to a Nazira Quran class for ten minutes. Note any words in which pupils merge ع with alif, ق with ک, or ح with ه. Then choose one root that pupils know from Urdu, such as ع ل م, and with the class teacher's permission, write three of its words on the board for the pupils to group as a family. Write a short note: the sounds you heard merged, the root you used, and how the pupils responded.

## Summative task

Write 300-350 words. Choose **three** words from this list: *kātib* (2:282), *ʿalīm* (2:32), *raḥmah* (2:157), *maʿlūm* (15:21), *kitāb* (2:2), *ʿallama* (96:4).

1. For each word, find its root and its pattern.
2. Explain how the meaning of each word follows from its root and its pattern.
3. Analyse one case where the root alone would not give you the exact meaning, and explain how the verse decides it.

*(Analyze)*

**Mini-rubric (10 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Root and pattern identified (3 marks) | Correct root and pattern for all three words (3) | Correct for one or two words (1-2) | None correct |
| Meaning explained from root and pattern (3 marks) | Shows clearly how each meaning combines root and pattern (3) | Gives meanings without showing how they follow (1-2) | Absent |
| Analysis of where context decides (4 marks) | Uses a verse to show exactly how context narrows or shifts the root meaning, and draws a lesson for reading (3-4) | Notes that context matters, without a worked case (1-2) | No analysis |

The analysis criterion asks you to show your reasoning from the verse. Writing "the meaning depends on context" without a worked case does not meet it.
