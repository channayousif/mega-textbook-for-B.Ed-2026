## A real classroom situation

Ayesha is a B.Ed trainee on practicum at a government primary school near Larkana. In the morning assembly, a Class 5 boy, Bilal, recites Surah al-Fatiha into the microphone. His recitation is clear and beautiful, and the teachers smile. Later, in the Islamiat period, Ayesha asks him what *al-ʿālamīn* means in the second verse. Bilal is puzzled. "Miss, we recite it for reward. The meaning is for the maulvi sahib."

Ayesha is not sure what to say. She has recited al-Fatiha in every prayer since she was seven. She knows that *al-ḥamdu lillāh* means praise belongs to Allah, but she has never thought about the other words. Is Bilal right that understanding is only for scholars? By the end of this topic you will be able to answer him, and to explain what kind of Arabic he is reciting.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. Why should a Muslim, and especially a teacher, understand the Qur'an? What is special about the Arabic it uses? And how does that Arabic relate to Classical Arabic and to the Arabic spoken in Arab countries today?

### Importance of understanding the Qur'an

**The Qur'an asks to be understood.** Its own words make this point many times. Read these four verses on Quran.com (Saheeh International translation):

- "Indeed, We have sent it down as an Arabic Qur'an that you might understand" (12:2).
- It is "a blessed Book... that they might reflect upon its verses" (38:29).
- "Then do they not reflect upon the Qur'an, or are there locks upon [their] hearts?" (47:24).
- "And We have certainly made the Qur'an easy for remembrance" (54:17).

The first verse gives a reason for the Arabic language itself: so that its listeners would understand. The second and third ask for **reflection** (*tadabbur*), which means thinking carefully about what a verse says and what it asks of us. Nobody can reflect on words they do not understand.

**Four reasons for a teacher.** The concept map above groups the reasons.

1. **Guidance.** The Qur'an calls itself "a guidance for those conscious of Allah" (2:2). Guidance helps only when we know what it says.
2. **Prayer.** We recite al-Fatiha in every *rak'ah* of every prayer. In it we say, "It is You we worship and You we ask for help" (1:5). Knowing the meaning turns a memorised sound into a conscious promise.
3. **Reflection.** As 38:29 and 47:24 show, reflection is something the Qur'an asks of every reader, not only of scholars.
4. **Teaching.** In Pakistan, Islamiat and Nazira Quran are part of the school timetable. Pupils like Bilal will ask their teachers what the words mean. A teacher who can answer, even simply, shows that the Qur'an is a book to be understood.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Memorising the Qur'an means understanding it."** Memorising (*hifz*) and reading with correct pronunciation (*nazira*) are valuable acts of worship, and Muslims have preserved the Qur'an through them for fourteen centuries. But they are different skills from understanding. A child can recite a whole surah perfectly and still not know what one word means, as Bilal shows. The Qur'an joins the two: it asks to be recited, and it also asks to be understood (12:2, 38:29). This course does not replace recitation. It adds understanding to it.

**A worked example: how much do you already understand?** Take the verse Bilal recited: ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ (1:2), "[All] praise is [due] to Allah, Lord of the worlds". Go word by word and ask what Urdu already gives you.

| Arabic word | Meaning | An Urdu word you already know |
|---|---|---|
| *al-ḥamdu* | the praise | *ḥamd*, a poem in praise of Allah |
| *li-llāhi* | for Allah, belongs to Allah | *Allah* |
| *rabbi* | Lord (of) | *Rab*, as in "Ya Rab" |
| *al-ʿālamīn* | the worlds | *ʿālam*, world, as in *ʿālam-e-Islām* |

An Urdu speaker who has never studied Arabic already recognises every word in this verse. That is the good news of this course. Urdu and Sindhi took thousands of words from Arabic, so you begin with a large vocabulary you did not know you had. Now Ayesha can answer Bilal: "*Al-ʿālamīn* means all the worlds. You already use the word *ʿālam*."

### Characteristics of Quranic Arabic

**Quranic Arabic** means the Arabic language exactly as it is used in the text of the Holy Qur'an. Six characteristics stand out for a beginner.

1. **Words are built from roots.** Most Arabic words grow from a root of three letters. *ʿIlm* (knowledge), *ʿālim* (one who knows) and *ʿalīm* (all-knowing) all share the letters ع ل م. Topic 1.2 teaches this system.
2. **Much meaning in few words.** Quranic Arabic is compact. In 1:5, *iyyāka naʿbudu* is only two words. Because "You" comes first, the sentence means "You alone we worship", which is why the translation says "It is You we worship". Word order itself carries meaning.
3. **Sound and rhythm.** Verses often end on the same sound. Surah al-Ikhlas (112) ends each verse on *-ad*: *aḥad*, *aṣ-ṣamad*, *yūlad*, *aḥad*. These verse endings help listeners remember and feel the message.
4. **Neither poetry nor ordinary prose.** The Qur'an has rhythm and rhyme, but it does not follow the fixed metres of Arabic poetry. It says of itself, "it is not the word of a poet" (69:41).
5. **Familiar words with new, exact meanings.** Words such as *ṣalāh*, *zakāh* and *ṣawm* existed before Islam with general meanings. The Qur'an gave them exact meanings as acts of worship. Even *kutiba*, from the root for "write", means "decreed" in "decreed upon you is fasting" (2:183).
6. **It speaks directly to its listeners.** The Qur'an often addresses people: "O you who have believed" (2:153). The reader is spoken to, not only told a story.

The Qur'an is also written in its own old spelling. Look at the word for prayer in 2:153: ٱلصَّلَوٰة (*aṣ-ṣalāh*), written with a wāw shape that is not pronounced. Your copy of the Qur'an keeps this spelling, which is one reason Urdu-printed and Arab-printed copies can look slightly different while the words are the same.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Quranic Arabic is the same as the Arabic spoken today."** Many trainees expect that a worker returning from Dubai or Riyadh can explain any verse. He may help, but spoken Arabic today is a family of local dialects. A taxi driver in Cairo and a shopkeeper in Riyadh speak very differently, and neither speaks like the Qur'an. Arab schools also teach **Modern Standard Arabic**, used for news and books. It keeps much of the old grammar but has a modern vocabulary for things like trains and computers. Quranic Arabic is older than both. An Arab child also has to study to understand the Qur'an well, though the task is shorter for them than for us.

### Difference between Classical Arabic and Quranic Arabic

**Classical Arabic** is the literary Arabic of the early centuries of Islam and the century just before it. It includes pre-Islamic poetry, such as the famous long odes called the *Muʿallaqāt*, the Qur'an, the Hadith, early histories and the first grammar books. So the two terms are not opposites. **Quranic Arabic is one part of Classical Arabic: its most important text, and the model on which the rest was later measured.** Some books use the two names for the same thing. In this course we keep them apart, because the differences help us read.

<Figure id="fig-U1-1" src="/img/figures/guhq-301/unit-01/fig-U1-1.svg" alt="Table comparing Classical Arabic and Quranic Arabic on five points: scope (a whole literature of poetry, Hadith, history and grammar, against one text, the Qur'an); vocabulary (the shared Arabic word stock, against the same words often given new religious meanings); style (poetry with fixed metres and ordinary prose, against rhythm and rhyme without fixed metre); purpose (praise, pride, memory and record, against guidance and reflection); and spelling (the normal spelling of its time, against the Qur'an's own preserved spelling)." />

**What they share.** The Qur'an used the language its first listeners already knew. Yazar and Batur (2025) compare the Qur'an with the *Muʿallaqāt* and find a large shared vocabulary and many shared images of the desert, animals, rain and ruins. The first audience understood the words because they were their own.

**How they differ.** The same researchers show that the Qur'an gives these shared words and images a different job (Yazar & Batur, 2025). Pre-Islamic poetry mostly describes the world as it is, to express a feeling or praise a tribe. The Qur'an places the same images inside a larger message about Allah, human responsibility and the Hereafter. The table above sums up five differences.

**A worked example: one image, two jobs.** Follow one image through both kinds of text, using Yazar and Batur's (2025) analysis.

| Question | In pre-Islamic poetry | In the Qur'an |
|---|---|---|
| What image appears? | The ruins of an empty campsite (*aṭlāl*) | The ruins of destroyed towns and nations |
| What does the image do? | Makes the poet remember a lost love and feel time passing | Makes the listener reflect on what happened to earlier peoples and why |
| What kind of response does it ask for? | A feeling of longing | A change in how one lives |

The words are Classical Arabic in both columns. The difference is in purpose. This is what we mean when we say Quranic Arabic is part of Classical Arabic and yet distinct from it.

**Why the difference matters for a reader.** A dictionary of Classical Arabic is very useful, but it gives a word's general meaning. To understand a Qur'anic word, check how the Qur'an itself uses it, as with *ṣalāh* and *kutiba* above. Later units return to this when we translate and interpret verses.

## Activity: Word hunt in Surah al-Fatiha

Work in groups of three. About 20 minutes.

Open Surah al-Fatiha (1:1-7) on Quran.com with the word-by-word display switched on. Each member takes one job.

1. **The word finder** lists every Arabic word in the surah that is also used in Urdu or Sindhi, such as *raḥmān*, *raḥīm*, *mālik* and *yawm*.
2. **The meaning checker** compares the Urdu meaning of each word with the meaning shown on Quran.com, and marks any word whose meaning differs.
3. **The reason finder** decides which of the four reasons in the concept map (guidance, prayer, reflection, teaching) the surah shows most clearly, and finds the verse that shows it.

Then, as a group, write two sentences that Ayesha could say to Bilal, using one word from your list. Share your sentences with another group and explain, without notes, which reason you chose and why.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Without looking back, give two verses in which the Qur'an asks to be understood or reflected upon, with their references.
2. *(Remember)* List four characteristics of Quranic Arabic.
3. *(Understand)* In three sentences, explain how Quranic Arabic is related to Classical Arabic. Use the words "part of" and "purpose".
4. *(Apply)* A pupil says, "My uncle works in Saudi Arabia, so he knows exactly what every verse means." Explain to the pupil, in simple words, what is right and what is wrong in this idea.

## Summary

The Qur'an asks to be understood: it was sent down in Arabic so that people would understand it, and it asks every reader to reflect on its verses. Memorising and reciting are valuable, but understanding is a separate skill, and Urdu speakers already know many of the words. Quranic Arabic is built from roots, says much in few words, has rhythm without poetic metre, gives familiar words exact meanings and speaks directly to its listeners. It is the central text of Classical Arabic, sharing its words and images but giving them a new purpose, and it differs from the Arabic spoken today.
