## A real classroom situation

The next day Sadiq takes the same Class 9 group. He writes 103:3 on the board and asks the pupils to translate it with him. Word by word they get: "except those who believed and did the righteous deeds and advised each other with the truth and advised each other with the patience". It is correct, but it is heavy, and the pupils laugh at how it sounds.

A girl named Sana puts up her hand. "Sir, the translation on Quran.com says 'advised each other to truth and advised each other to patience'. Why does it say 'to' when the Arabic has *bi*?" Sadiq now has to explain the difference between keeping every word and carrying the meaning. By the end of this topic you will be able to translate for meaning, find the key words of a passage, and show how its verses connect.

## Explanation

This topic takes the second half of understanding: how to carry the meaning into natural English or Urdu, how to find the words a passage turns on, and how its verses hold together.

### Meaning-based translation of selected verses

**Word-by-word translation shows the skeleton; meaning-based translation carries the message.** A meaning-based translation asks, "What is this verse saying to someone who does not know Arabic?" It then says that in natural English or Urdu, even if the word order has to change.

Al-Hilali (2026) surveys English translations of the Qur'an and places them on a spectrum. At one end is a literal rendering that keeps the Arabic word order and the Arabic words as closely as possible. At the other is an interpretive rendering that puts the meaning into idiomatic English and may explain what the Arabic leaves implicit. He uses Eugene Nida's terms: **formal equivalence** keeps the form and content of the source, while **dynamic equivalence** aims at the same effect on the reader. His finding is that no translation is fully literal or fully interpretive. Every translation sits somewhere on the spectrum, and each has strengths and weaknesses. A literal rendering preserves the words; an interpretive rendering carries the spiritual effect and the functional meaning for the reader.

The table above shows the two ends on 103:1-3. Read the word-by-word column first. It is honest about the Arabic, but it is stiff. Now read the meaning-based column. "By time, indeed mankind is in loss" keeps the oath and the emphasis; "except for those who have believed and done righteous deeds" turns *alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū ṣ-ṣāliḥāt* into a natural English clause; "advised each other to truth and advised each other to patience" turns the two *wa-tawāṣaw* clauses into parallel English.

Notice what the meaning-based rendering had to decide. The Arabic *bi-l-ḥaqqi* is literally "with the truth" or "in the truth", but English idiom says "to truth" after "advised". The translator chose the English idiom because the meaning is the same and the sentence now reads. That is the kind of decision a meaning-based translation makes, and it is why Sana's question is a good one.

**Correcting a common misconception: "One translation gives the whole meaning."** No single translation can. Every translation is a rendering on the spectrum Al-Hilali describes, and each makes choices. That is why Muslims have always read the Qur'an with the Arabic in front of them, and why a student of the text compares translations rather than trusting one. In Pakistan, compare the Saheeh International English on Quran.com with an Urdu translation you trust. Where they differ, ask what the Arabic says and why each chose its words.

### Identifying key words and phrases

**Key words are the words a passage turns on.** Find them and you can say what the passage is about in one sentence.

In 103:1-3 the key words are: *al-ʿaṣr* (the time), *al-insān* (mankind), *khusr* (loss), *āmanū* (they believed), *ʿamilū* (they did), *ṣ-ṣāliḥāt* (righteous deeds), *tawāṣaw* (they advised each other), *al-ḥaqq* (the truth) and *aṣ-ṣabr* (patience). Three of them come from roots you met in Unit 1: ء م ن (faith), ص ل ح (righteousness, wholeness) and ص ب ر (patience). Look them up in the Quranic Arabic Corpus and you will see whole families.

Two signs point to a key word. First, **it repeats**. In 103:3 the verb *tawāṣaw* appears twice, in two parallel clauses: "advised each other to truth and advised each other to patience". The repetition tells you the verse is built as a pair, and that truth and patience belong together. Second, **it carries the theme**. In 103 the word *khusr* (loss) is the pivot: the whole surah is about what mankind is in, and the exception is the escape from it.

A Class 9 pupil can do this. Give the class 103:3 and ask them to underline the word that appears twice. They will find *tawāṣaw*. Then ask what the two things are that the believers advise each other to. They will find truth and patience. That is identifying key words and phrases.

### Understanding verse connections

**Verses in a passage are not a string of separate sentences.** They connect, and the connection is part of the meaning.

Look at Surah al-Falaq (113:1-5), drawn in the diagram below.

<Figure id="fig-U4-4" src="/img/figures/guhq-301/unit-04/fig-U4-4.svg" alt="Diagram showing how the verses of Surah al-Falaq (113:1-5) connect: verse 1 asks for refuge in the Lord of daybreak, and verses 2 to 5 each begin with the shared phrase 'wa-min sharri' (and from the evil of), linking every listed evil back to the refuge asked for in verse 1." />

Verse 1 asks for refuge: "Say, I seek refuge in the Lord of daybreak". Verses 2 to 5 each begin with the same phrase, *wa-min sharri*, "and from the evil of". The shared phrase is the link. Each verse adds one evil to the list, and every list hangs on the refuge asked for in verse 1. Read the surah without verse 1 and the list has no anchor; read it with verse 1 and the list is a prayer.

A second example shows a connection of movement rather than repetition. In 102:1-8 the surah moves step by step: "Competition in increase diverts you until you visit the graveyards. No! You are going to know. Then, no! You are going to know. No! If you only knew with knowledge of certainty, you will surely see the Hellfire. Then you will surely see it with the eye of certainty. Then you will surely be asked that Day about pleasure." The repeated "No! You are going to know" pushes the listener forward, and the move from "you will see" to "you will be asked" carries the passage from warning to accountability. The verses are one passage, not eight separate warnings.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Each verse can be understood alone, without its neighbours."** A verse can be translated alone, but it cannot be fully understood alone. Its pronouns point back, its lists hang on an earlier verse, and its meaning grows as the passage builds. Read the verses around it. In a Class 9 Islamiat lesson, ask the pupils what the word "it" or "that Day" refers to in a verse. They will have to look at the verses before it.

## Activity: Two translations, one verse

Work in pairs. About 25 minutes.

Take 103:3 and 113:1-5. For each passage, find the Saheeh International English on Quran.com and one Urdu translation you trust.

1. **Compare (10 minutes).** Write the English and the Urdu side by side. Underline every place where they differ in word order or in the words chosen.
2. **Explain (10 minutes).** For each difference, decide whether it is a word-by-word choice or a meaning-based choice, and say what the Arabic allows. Use Al-Hilali's spectrum if it helps.
3. **Connect (5 minutes).** In 113:1-5, mark the shared phrase *wa-min sharri* and write one sentence on how it links the verses. Then share your sentence with another pair and compare.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* In two sentences, explain the difference between a word-by-word and a meaning-based translation.
2. *(Remember)* Name three key words of 103:1-3 and give the meaning of each.
3. *(Understand)* In three sentences, explain what Al-Hilali (2026) means by a spectrum of translation, and why no translation is fully literal.
4. *(Apply)* A pupil reads 113:2-5 without verse 1. Explain, in two or three sentences, what the pupil misses and how the shared phrase *wa-min sharri* connects the verses.

## Summary

A word-by-word translation shows the skeleton of a verse; a meaning-based translation carries the message into natural English or Urdu. Al-Hilali (2026) places translations on a spectrum between literal and interpretive, and shows that no translation is fully one or the other, so a careful reader compares translations. Key words, especially repeated ones, carry the theme of a passage. Consecutive verses connect through shared phrases, pronouns and movement, so a verse is understood best with its neighbours.

## Try this at your practicum school

Take one short passage, such as 113:1-5 or 102:1-8, into a Class 9 Islamiat period. Write the passage on the board with the Arabic and one translation. Ask the pupils to find the word or phrase that repeats, and to say in one sentence what the passage is about. Note in a short paragraph: the passage, the repeated word or phrase, the pupils' one-sentence theme, and one place where the translation made a meaning-based choice.

## Summative task

Write 300-350 words comparing a word-by-word and a meaning-based translation of 103:1-3. Give both renderings, then analyse at least two places where they differ. For each, explain what the Arabic says, what the meaning-based rendering chose, and why. Finish with one sentence on what a reader gains from each kind of translation.

*(Analyze)*

**Mini-rubric (10 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Both renderings given (3 marks) | Word-by-word and meaning-based renderings of all three verses, accurate (3) | One rendering thin or one verse missing (1-2) | Renderings absent or inaccurate |
| Analysis of differences (4 marks) | Two differences analysed: the Arabic, the choice and the reason, showing how the spectrum works (3-4) | Differences noted without explaining the Arabic or the reason (1-2) | No analysis |
| Conclusion on what each gains (3 marks) | A justified judgement on what word-by-word and meaning-based translation each offer a reader (3) | A general statement without justification (1-2) | No conclusion |

The analysis criterion asks you to show the Arabic behind each difference. Writing "the meaning-based one is smoother" without showing the words does not meet it.
