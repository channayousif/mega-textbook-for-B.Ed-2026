## A real classroom situation

Sadiq is a B.Ed trainee at a government boys' high school in Jacobabad. In the morning assembly a Class 9 boy, Imran, recites Surah al-Asr (103) into the microphone. His recitation is fluent and the teachers nod. After assembly Imran stops Sadiq in the corridor. "Sir, you are doing Arabic in college. What does *wal-ʿaṣr* actually mean?"

Sadiq opens his phone to Quran.com and reads the words. He knows *wa* (and), *al-ʿaṣr* (the time), *inna* (indeed), *al-insān* (man) and *khusr* (loss). But when he strings them together he gets "and the time, indeed the man is in loss", and he can hear how flat it sounds next to the translation "By time, indeed mankind is in loss". He wants to read the verse accurately and then translate it word by word without losing its shape. By the end of this topic you will be able to do both.

## Explanation

This topic gives you three things: a quick check on reading the text accurately, a method for translating a verse word by word, and practice on a short verse.

### Rules for accurate reading of Quranic text

**Reading accurately means reading what is written, the way it is written.** Unit 1 introduced *tajwid*, the set of rules for reciting the Qur'an. Four rules matter most when you read for meaning.

1. **Give each letter its place.** Every Arabic letter is produced from a particular place in the mouth or throat (its *makhraj*). The letter ع is not alif, and ق is not ک. If you merge them, you can change a word, as Unit 1 showed with *ʿalīm* (Knowing) and *alīm* (painful).
2. **Observe the signs on the letters.** A *sukūn* means the letter has no vowel. A *shadda* means the letter is doubled. Reading إِنَّ as *inna* with a doubled nūn is not the same as reading *inā*.
3. **Stop where the meaning allows a stop.** The Qur'an has pause marks (*waqf*). Stopping in the middle of a sentence can cut a verb from its object. Read 103:2 as "Indeed, mankind is in loss", not "Indeed, mankind" and then a confused pause.
4. **Do not add or drop a letter.** The Uthmani text in your mushaf is the text. Read the extra wāw shape in the word for prayer in 2:153 as the script spells it, and do not "correct" it.

Abdelfattah, Khalil and Abbas (2025) describe *tajwid* as a set of rules precise enough that a computer can check a recitation against them. A trainee does not need to be a *qāri*, but a teacher who reads carefully can hear when a pupil has dropped a letter or merged two.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Reading quickly means reading well."** Speed is not accuracy. A fast reader who swallows the ع in *ʿaṣr* or the shadda in *innā* has not read the verse. Read slowly enough to place every letter, especially when you are reading to understand. In a Class 8 Nazira period, a teacher who reads 103:1 slowly and asks the class to repeat it after her is teaching accuracy, not slowness.

### Word-by-word translation techniques

Word-by-word translation is a way of seeing the skeleton of a verse. It has five steps, shown in the flowchart above.

1. **Read.** Read the verse aloud once, with the rules above, so that you are working from sounds you have actually heard.
2. **Split.** Break the verse into its words. In 103:1 there are five: وَٱلْعَصْرِ, إِنَّ, ٱلْإِنسَـٰنَ, لَفِى, خُسْرٍ.
3. **Look up.** For each word, find its meaning and its grammar. The Quranic Arabic Corpus gives the root and the part of speech; a dictionary gives the meaning.
4. **Order.** Give a first rendering that keeps the Arabic order. This is the word-by-word draft.
5. **Check.** Read your draft against the verse. If the order makes English or Urdu sound broken, adjust it and say why.

The table below lays out 103:1 word by word. Follow it as a worked example.

<Figure id="fig-U4-2" src="/img/figures/guhq-301/unit-04/fig-U4-2.svg" alt="Table laying out verse 103:1 word by word: wa-l-'asri (by the time, particle plus noun in the genitive), inna (indeed, an emphasis particle), al-insana (mankind, the noun governed by inna), la-fi (in, preposition plus noun), khusrin (loss, noun in the genitive)." />

Read the first row. وَٱلْعَصْرِ (*wa-l-ʿaṣri*) is "and the time": *wa* is a particle joining the word to what came before, and *al-ʿaṣr* is a noun in the genitive because it follows a particle of oath. The second row, إِنَّ (*innā*), is "indeed", an emphasis particle that governs the noun after it. The third row, ٱلْإِنسَـٰنَ (*al-insāna*), is "mankind", the noun that *innā* governs, so it is in the accusative. The fourth row, لَفِى (*la-fi*), is "in": the *la* adds emphasis and *fi* is a preposition. The fifth row, خُسْرٍ (*khusrin*), is "loss", a noun in the genitive because it follows the preposition.

Now give the word-by-word draft: "And the time, indeed mankind is in loss." That is accurate, and it already carries the meaning. Notice how the Arabic puts *innā* and *la* to press the point home. English does the same work with "indeed" and "is in".

**Correcting a common misconception: "Word-by-word translation is always the most accurate."** Word-by-word translation is honest about the words, but it can be unreadable, and a reader who stops there may miss what the verse is doing. Al-Hilali (2026) surveys English translations of the Qur'an and finds a spectrum: a fully literal rendering keeps the Arabic word order even when the English breaks, while an interpretive rendering puts the meaning into natural English. No translation is fully one or the other. Word-by-word work is the first half of understanding, not the last. Topic 4.2 takes the second half.

### Understanding short verses

A short verse rewards this patient work. You can hold the whole verse in your mind at once, and you can see how its words depend on one another.

Take the whole of 103:1-3, which you have just seen word by word.

| Verse | Word-by-word draft | What the verse does |
|---|---|---|
| 103:1 | And the time, indeed | An oath, calling time as a witness |
| 103:2 | mankind is in loss | The statement the oath supports |
| 103:3 | except those who believed and did righteous deeds and advised each other to truth and advised each other to patience | The exception to the statement |

The three verses are one short surah and, in the mushaf's division, one *ruku'* (ruku 547). Read them together and the shape is clear: an oath, a serious statement about mankind, then the exception that makes the statement bearable. A Class 9 pupil in Jacobabad can learn these three verses in one period and then explain that shape in Urdu. That is understanding a short verse.

The same method works on any short verse. Choose a verse of four to six words, read it accurately, split it, look up each word, give a word-by-word draft and check it. Surah al-Falaq (113) and Surah al-Kawthar (108) are good practice because their words are familiar and their meanings are clear.

## Activity: Word-by-word relay

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

One member is the reader, the other is the recorder. Take turns on two verses: 103:1-3 and 113:1.

1. **Reader (2 minutes per verse).** Read the verse aloud once, slowly. Then say each Arabic word and give its meaning and grammar, one word at a time.
2. **Recorder.** Write the word-by-word draft exactly as the reader gives it, keeping the Arabic order.
3. **Together (5 minutes per verse).** Check the draft. Underline any word whose meaning you are unsure of and look it up in the Quranic Arabic Corpus. Then rewrite the draft so it reads naturally in English or Urdu, and note one place where you changed the order.
4. **Swap roles** for the second verse. When both are done, compare your two final drafts with another pair and explain, without notes, why you changed the order where you did.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* List the four rules for reading the Qur'anic text accurately, in your own words.
2. *(Remember)* Write the five steps of word-by-word translation, in order, from memory.
3. *(Understand)* In two or three sentences, explain why a reader who stops after "Indeed, mankind" in 103:2 has not understood the verse.
4. *(Apply)* A pupil reads إِنَّ as *inā*. What is the difference in meaning, and which rule of accurate reading has been broken?

## Summary

Reading accurately means giving every letter its place and its signs, stopping where the meaning allows, and not adding or dropping anything. Word-by-word translation is a five-step method: read, split, look up, order, check. It shows the skeleton of a verse honestly, but it is the first half of understanding, not the last. A short verse such as 103:1-3 yields to this method: an oath, a statement, and an exception, all in three verses and one ruku'.

## Try this at your practicum school

During a Class 8 or 9 Nazira Quran period, ask the teacher if you may take one short verse, such as 103:1 or 113:1. Read it aloud to the class once, slowly. Then write the five steps of word-by-word translation on the board and work through the verse with the pupils, one word at a time. Note in one short paragraph: the verse you chose, the words the pupils found hardest, and one reading error you heard.

## Summative task

Produce a word-by-word translation of 103:1-3 for a Class 9 notebook. For each verse, give the word-by-word draft and then a meaning-based rendering. Write 250-300 words in total, including a short note on one place where you changed the Arabic word order and why.

*(Analyze)*

**Mini-rubric (10 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accurate reading and word meanings (3 marks) | Every word correctly read and glossed, with grammar named (3) | Most words correct, grammar thin (1-2) | Several words wrong or missing |
| Word-by-word draft and meaning-based rendering (3 marks) | Both drafts given for all three verses, and the difference is clear (3) | One draft missing, or the two not distinguished (1-2) | Only one draft, or drafts inaccurate |
| Analysis of a changed word order (4 marks) | Names a specific change, explains how the Arabic order carries meaning and why the English or Urdu order differs (3-4) | Notes a change without explaining it (1-2) | No analysis |

The analysis criterion asks you to show the reason for the change, not just to make it. Writing "English sounds better" does not meet it.
