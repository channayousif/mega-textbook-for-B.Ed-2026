## A real classroom situation

Imran is a B.Ed trainee on practicum at a government high school in Sukkur. In a Class 9 Nazira Quran period he reads 2:2 with the class: ذَٰلِكَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبُ لَا رَيْبَ ۛ فِيهِ ۛ هُدًى لِّلْمُتَّقِينَ. He asks, "What kind of sentence is this?" A girl, Saba, answers at once: "A verbal sentence, miss, because the Qur'an tells us what to do." Imran smiles, because Saba has made a very common mistake. The sentence does not begin with a verb at all. By the end of this topic you will be able to give Saba a clear answer, and to explain the difference between the two sentence types.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What is a nominal sentence? What is a verbal sentence? And how does the order of the words change what a sentence emphasises?

### Nominal sentences (jumlah ismiyyah)

A **nominal sentence** (جبملة اسمية, *jumlah ismiyyah*) is a sentence that begins with a noun. It has two main parts. The first is the **mubtada'** (مبتدأ): the noun the sentence opens about, usually the first word. The second is the **khabar** (خبر): the predicate that completes the news about it. Together they make a complete statement.

Take 2:2: ذَٰلِكَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبُ (*dhālika l-kitābu*, "That is the Book"). The first word, *dhālika* ("that"), is the mubtada'. The second word, *al-kitābu* ("the Book"), is the khabar. Neither word is a verb, so the sentence is nominal. The Qur'an Arabic Corpus labels *dhālika* a demonstrative pronoun and *al-kitābu* a nominative noun, which is exactly the shape of a mubtada' and khabar.

A second example is 2:157: أُولَـٰٓئِكَ عَلَيْهِمْ صَلَوَٰتٌ مِّن رَّبِّهِمْ وَرَحْمَةٌ ("Those are the ones upon whom are blessings from their Lord and mercy"). The first word, *ulā'ika* ("those"), is the mubtada'. The rest of the sentence, "عَلَيْهِمْ صَلَوَٰتٌ مِّن رَّبِّهِمْ وَرَحْمَةٌ", is the khabar, and it tells what is with them.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Every Arabic sentence starts with a verb."** Many learners assume this because the verb is the most exciting word in a sentence. It is not true. Arabic has a whole sentence type that begins with a noun, and the Qur'an uses it often, especially when it describes Allah, the world or the people of a story. A nominal sentence feels steady and settled, because it begins by naming a thing rather than an action.

### Verbal sentences (jumlah fi'liyyah)

A **verbal sentence** (جبملة فعلية, *jumlah fi'liyyah*) is a sentence that begins with a verb. Its main part is the **fi'l** (فعل), the verb. A verb that has a doer names the **fā'il** (فاعل), the one who does the action, in the nominative. If the verb takes an object, it is the **maf'ūl bihi** (مفعول به), in the accusative.

Take 2:152: فَٱذْكُرُونِىٓ أَذْكُرْكُمْ (*fa-dhkurūnī adhkur-kum*, "So remember Me; I will remember you"). The sentence begins with the verb *udhkurū* ("remember"), an imperative. The waāw at the end of the verb is the fā'il, meaning "you", and the pronoun *nī* ("Me") is the object. The second half, *adhkur-kum* ("I will remember you"), is another verbal sentence with its own doer and object.

Another example is 2:25: وَبَشِّرِ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ (*wa-bashshiri alladhīna āmanū*, "And give good news to those who believe"). The sentence begins with the imperative verb *bashshiri* ("give good news"). Its object is *alladhīna āmanū* ("those who believe"). The doer is hidden in the verb: it means "you give good news".

**Correcting a common misconception: "A verbal sentence always names its doer."** Not always. In an imperative like *bashshiri*, the doer is the person being spoken to, and the verb itself carries it. In a past-tense verb like *kataba* ("he wrote"), the doer is often only a hidden pronoun inside the verb. The doer is always there in meaning, but it is not always a separate word.

### Sentence components and word order

The parts of a sentence have a normal order, but Arabic can move a part forward. The default order in a verbal sentence is verb, then doer, then object. The default order in a nominal sentence is mubtada', then khabar. When a word is moved forward, it usually gains emphasis or restriction.

A famous example is 1:5: إِيَّاكَ نَعْبُدُ وَإِيَّاكَ نَسْتَعِينُ (*iyyāka naʿbudu wa-iyyāka nastaʿīn*, "It is You we worship and You we ask for help"). The object pronoun *iyyāka* ("You") is placed before the verb *naʿbudu* ("we worship"). The normal order would put the verb first. Moving *iyyāka* forward restricts the worship to Allah alone: "You alone we worship". This is why the translation says "It is You we worship" rather than "We worship You".

Word order can also move a khabar in front of its mubtada'. In 2:148, وَلِكُلِّ وِجْهَةٍ هُوَ مُوَلِّيهَا (*wa-li-kulli wijhatin huwa muwallīhā*, "And for every direction is one that He turns it"), the phrase *li-kulli wijhatin* ("for every direction") is a khabar placed before the mubtada' *huwa* ("He"). The meaning is "For every direction there is one to turn it", and the forward placement gives the direction the emphasis.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Word order never changes meaning."** Word order does not change the core parts of a sentence, but it changes what the sentence highlights. In 1:5 the forward object makes the worship exclusive. In 2:148 the forward khabar gives the direction the first place. A reader who ignores word order will still find the words, but will miss what the verse is stressing.

## Activity: Sort the sentences

Work in groups of four. About 20 minutes.

Prepare six slips, each with the opening words of a Qur'anic sentence: 2:2, 2:152, 2:157, 2:25, 2:148 and the opening of 2:91 (وَإِذَا قِيلَ لَهُمْ).

1. **Sort (5 minutes).** As a group, sort the slips into two piles: nominal sentences and verbal sentences. For each slip, write down the first word and say why it belongs in that pile.
2. **Label (10 minutes).** Choose one nominal slip and label the mubtada' and the khabar. Choose one verbal slip and label the verb, the doer and the object, if there is one.
3. **Explain (5 minutes).** Take one sentence and explain to the group how the word order shapes its meaning. Then swap roles with another group and test each other: read a slip and ask the other group to name its type and parts without looking at their notes.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* In two sentences, state the difference between a nominal sentence and a verbal sentence, naming the first word of each.
2. *(Remember)* Name the two parts of a nominal sentence and the three parts of a verbal sentence.
3. *(Understand)* A pupil says 2:2 is a verbal sentence because the word "book" is a thing, not an action. Explain what is wrong with this reasoning.
4. *(Apply)* Look at 2:157. Identify the mubtada' and the khabar, then say what the sentence means in your own words.

## Summary

Arabic has two sentence types, and the first word tells you which one you are reading. A nominal sentence begins with a noun: the mubtada' opens it and the khabar completes it, as in "That is the Book" (2:2). A verbal sentence begins with a verb: the fi'l comes first, and a fā'il and, where the verb takes one, a maf'ūl bihi follow, as in "So remember Me; I will remember you" (2:152). The doer may be hidden inside the verb, especially in an imperative. Word order is not fixed for effect: moving a word forward, as with "You alone" in 1:5, gives it emphasis or restriction.
