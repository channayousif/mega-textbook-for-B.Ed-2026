# Topic 3.7 - Experiential and activity-based learning

## A real classroom situation

It is "activity day" in a Grade 5 class in Sanghar. Sir Javed has brought clay, sticks and paint, and the pupils spend a happy hour making models of the sun, the earth and the moon. The room is noisy and full of colour. At the end, the head teacher asks a pupil, "Why do we have day and night?" The pupil holds up his clay earth and says, "Because the sun goes round the earth."

Sir Javed is puzzled. His pupils were active the whole hour. Why did they not learn the main idea? This topic explains the difference between being busy and learning from experience, and shows how a teacher turns an activity into learning.

## Explanation

### Experiential and activity-based learning

**Activity-based learning.** In *activity-based learning*, pupils learn by doing: handling materials, making models, playing learning games, role-playing, sorting cards or carrying out small investigations. Instead of listening to a description, pupils have a direct experience. Most of the methods in this unit, from laboratory work to projects, are activity-based in this sense.

**Experiential learning.** *Experiential learning* explains how an experience turns into learning. The most cited model is David Kolb's experiential learning cycle (Morris, 2020). Kolb, who drew on the work of John Dewey, Kurt Lewin and Jean Piaget, described learning as a four-stage cycle in which the learner "touches all the bases" of experiencing, reflecting, thinking and acting (Kolb, 2015, quoted in Morris, 2020):

1. **Concrete experience:** the learner takes part in a new experience.
2. **Reflective observation:** the learner looks back on the experience and observes it from different angles.
3. **Abstract conceptualisation:** the learner forms an idea or rule that explains the observations.
4. **Active experimentation:** the learner uses the idea to make decisions, predict and solve problems, which leads to a new experience.

Morris (2020), reviewing studies of experiential learning, found that critical reflection is what turns experience into meaningful learning. Without the reflecting and thinking stages, an experience stays just an experience.

**Worked example: day and night through the cycle.** Here is how Sir Javed could reteach the lesson (Figure fig-U3-13):

1. **Concrete experience.** The room is darkened. One pupil holds a torch (the sun); another slowly turns a ball (the earth) with a small sticker marking Pakistan.
2. **Reflective observation.** Sir Javed asks, "What do you see? How much of the ball is lit? What happens to the sticker?" Pupils notice that half the ball is always lit and the sticker moves from light to dark and back.
3. **Abstract conceptualisation.** "So why do we have day and night?" With his help, pupils reach the idea that the earth spins, and each place turns into and out of the sun's light. He then names it: the earth's *rotation*.
4. **Active experimentation.** "When it is daytime in Pakistan, where is it night?" Pupils predict and mark it on the ball, then test it with the torch.

The clay models could still be used, but now as a way to show and check the idea, not as the whole lesson.

> **Common misconception:** "Activity-based learning means no teacher input." The opposite is true. In Sir Javed's first lesson, pupils were busy, but nobody asked them to observe, reflect or explain, so their wrong idea about the sun going round the earth survived. A science education report puts it simply: hands-on activities are "not enough"; experiences must also be "minds on" (Bybee et al., 2006). The teacher chooses an activity that targets the idea, asks questions at each stage, introduces the scientific explanation and checks understanding. Activity without thinking is just entertainment.

**Low-cost activities.** Activity-based learning does not need expensive kits. Many Grade 4 to 8 topics can be taught with everyday materials found in any Sindh town (Figure fig-U3-14). The rule is to start from the concept you want pupils to understand and choose the simplest activity that makes it visible.

### Choosing a method for a topic and a class

This topic ends the unit, so it is the place to bring the methods together. No method is best for everything. To choose, ask four questions:

1. **What is the learning goal?** New background knowledge suits a chunked lecture. A rule pupils can observe suits the inductive method or guided inquiry. A practical skill needs the laboratory method. A real problem suits problem-solving or a project.
2. **What can pupils already do?** Pupils new to investigating need structured inquiry and guidance before open inquiry.
3. **What does the school have?** One set of apparatus suggests a demonstration; everyday materials allow group practicals; a nearby farm or canal allows a field trip.
4. **How large is the class and how much time is there?** In a class of 60, pair talk and group practicals with shared materials work better than whole-class discussion.

Most good lessons combine methods, as the 5E model does: a demonstration to engage, a group activity to explore, a short explanation, a discussion to elaborate and a check to evaluate.

## Activity: Turn busy into learning

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. Each group receives a description of an activity that keeps pupils busy but may not teach much, for example "Pupils colour a printed diagram of the parts of a plant", or "Pupils paste pictures of animals on a chart".
2. Identify which stages of Kolb's cycle are missing.
3. Redesign the activity so that it goes through all four stages. Write one question the teacher asks at each stage.
4. Choose a low-cost material that could make the concept visible.
5. Present your redesign to the class in two minutes.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the four stages of Kolb's experiential learning cycle in order. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why an activity can keep pupils busy without teaching the main idea. *(Understand)*
3. Plan a low-cost activity for the Grade 4 topic "the parts of a plant" and write one reflection question for it. *(Apply)*
4. A school has no laboratory and 55 pupils in Grade 6. Choose a method for teaching "Plants make food using sunlight", and give two reasons for your choice. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Activity-based learning has pupils learn by doing, and experiential learning explains how doing becomes learning: Kolb's cycle moves from concrete experience through reflective observation and abstract conceptualisation to active experimentation. Reflection is the stage that matters most, so hands-on activity must also be minds-on, and the teacher's questions and explanations are essential. Low-cost materials make activities possible in any school. Choosing a method depends on the learning goal, the pupils, the resources and the class size, and most good lessons combine several methods.
