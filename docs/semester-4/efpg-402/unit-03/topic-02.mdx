# Topic 3.2 - Laboratory and demonstration methods

## A real classroom situation

Sir Waseem teaches Grade 8 science at a government high school in Shaheed Benazirabad (Nawabshah). The school has a laboratory room, but it has been locked for two years and holds only one rack of test tubes and a few bottles. His next chapter is "Acids, bases and indicators". He has 55 pupils and one set of apparatus.

He wonders: "Without a working laboratory, should I just read the chapter aloud? Or should I do the experiment myself in front of the class? Or can my pupils still do practical work?" This topic answers these questions. You will see that "laboratory" is a kind of experience, not only a room.

## Explanation

### Laboratory method

**What it is.** In the *laboratory method*, pupils themselves handle materials and apparatus, usually in small groups, to observe, measure, test or investigate. The teacher plans and supervises, but the hands on the materials are the pupils'. A widely used definition says laboratory experiences let students "interact directly with the material world", or with data drawn from it, using the tools and ideas of science (National Research Council, 2006, cited in Bybee et al., 2006).

**What it is for.** The same report lists the goals of laboratory work: mastering the subject matter, developing scientific reasoning, understanding how complex and uncertain real measurements are, developing practical skills, understanding the nature of science, building interest in science, and learning to work in a team (Bybee et al., 2006). Notice that "practical skills" is only one goal among seven.

**Recipe labs versus thinking labs.** Research summarised by Bybee et al. (2006) found that a typical "recipe" practical, where pupils follow steps without knowing why, is no better than other methods for learning the subject. Practical work helps more when it is linked to what comes before and after: pupils know the question, make a prediction, do the practical, and then discuss and explain their results. So a good practical has three parts:

1. **Before:** state the question, discuss safety, and ask pupils to predict.
2. **During:** pupils work in groups and record observations in a table.
3. **After:** groups share results, explain differences, and reach a conclusion with the teacher.

> **Common misconception:** "No laboratory room means no laboratory method." The laboratory method is about pupils handling materials and gathering evidence, not about a special room. Most Grade 4 to 8 practical work can be done on ordinary desks with low-cost, everyday materials. Pakistan's science curriculum asks pupils to "use equipment to carry out scientific investigations" and to "take measurements and record them" by the end of Grade 5 (National Curriculum Council, 2023). A locked room is not an excuse to skip these outcomes.

**Worked example: an indicator practical with no laboratory.** Sir Waseem can still run the laboratory method:

- **Materials:** turmeric powder dissolved in water (an *indicator*, a substance that changes colour in acids or bases), small plastic cups, a few drops each of lemon juice, vinegar, soap solution, baking soda solution and tap water. All are cheap and safe.
- **Groups:** eleven groups of five; each group shares one set of cups.
- **Before:** "Which of these will change the colour of turmeric water? Write your prediction."
- **During:** each group adds a few drops of each liquid to turmeric water and records the colour in a table.
- **After:** groups report. Turmeric turns reddish-brown in soap and baking soda solution but stays yellow in lemon juice and vinegar. The teacher then names the pattern: turmeric shows bases, not acids. Pupils explain why a turmeric stain turns red when washed with soap.

Every pupil handled materials and recorded evidence, at almost no cost.

**Limits.** The laboratory method takes more time than telling. It needs careful supervision, enough materials for every group, and attention to safety. Some apparatus is too dangerous, delicate or expensive to give to every group. That is where the demonstration method comes in.

### Demonstration method

**What it is.** In the *demonstration method*, the teacher (or a pupil helper) performs an experiment or shows an object or process in front of the class, while pupils watch, predict and answer questions. Pupils observe; they do not each handle the materials.

**When to use it.** A demonstration is the better choice when:

- the apparatus is dangerous, delicate or expensive (for example, heating a substance strongly, or using a single microscope);
- there is only one set of materials for a large class;
- time is short and the point is a single striking observation;
- the teacher wants to start a lesson with a puzzle.

That last use is powerful. Showing a *discrepant event*, something that surprises pupils because it goes against what they expect, is one of the ways the 5E model "engages" pupils at the start of a lesson (Bybee et al., 2006). An older science teaching model also began with pupils observing demonstrations "to raise questions" before testing their own ideas (Bybee et al., 2006).

**A demonstration is not a magic show.** A demonstration fails when pupils only watch. It works when pupils think: predict first, watch closely, then explain. Figure fig-U3-3 shows the planning steps.

**Worked example: a Grade 6 demonstration.** The topic is "air takes up space and has pressure".

1. **Aim:** pupils see that air pushes in all directions.
2. **Apparatus and trial run:** a glass full of water, a piece of stiff card, a bucket. The teacher tries it at home first.
3. **Predict:** "If I turn this glass upside down with a card over it, what will happen to the water?" Most pupils predict that the water will fall.
4. **Show:** the teacher turns the glass over slowly above the bucket and lets go of the card. The card stays and the water stays in.
5. **Question:** "What is holding the card up? Why did the water not fall?"
6. **Conclude:** together the class reaches the idea that air pushes up on the card from below.

The surprise in step 4 is what makes pupils want an explanation.

**Limits.** In a demonstration, pupils do not practise handling apparatus, and pupils at the back may see little. It can slide back into a lecture if the teacher only talks. Figure fig-U3-4 compares the two methods.

## Activity: Lab or demo?

Work in pairs, then join another pair. About 20 minutes.

1. Each pair receives six practical tasks from Grades 4 to 8, for example: "separating sand and salt by dissolving and filtering", "heating sugar until it chars", "testing which objects a magnet attracts", "looking at onion cells under the school's only microscope", "making a simple electric circuit with a cell and bulb", "showing that a candle needs air to burn".
2. For each task, decide whether you would use the laboratory method or the demonstration method in a class of 50 with no laboratory room. Write one reason for each choice.
3. For one demonstration, write the prediction question you would ask before showing it.
4. Join another pair and compare. Where you disagree, decide which reason is stronger.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the main difference between the laboratory method and the demonstration method. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why a "recipe" practical often teaches little, and what a teacher should add before and after it. *(Understand)*
3. A Grade 7 teacher has one spirit lamp and wants to show that metals expand when heated. Which method should she use, and why? *(Apply)*
4. Plan the prediction question and the follow-up question for a demonstration of an egg sinking in tap water and floating in salty water. *(Apply)*

## Summary

The laboratory method has pupils handle materials and gather evidence themselves; it builds understanding, reasoning and skills when it has a clear question before and a discussion after. It does not need a laboratory room: much Grade 4 to 8 practical work runs on desks with everyday materials. The demonstration method has the teacher perform while pupils predict, watch and explain; it suits dangerous, costly or single-set apparatus and is a strong way to open a lesson with a surprise. Both fail when pupils only follow or only watch, and both work when pupils think.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find out what science apparatus and materials your practicum school has and where they are kept. List five practical activities from the Grade 5 to 8 textbooks that the school could run as group practicals with what it has or with low-cost materials. Note which ones would need to be demonstrations instead, and why.

## Summative task

A government middle school in your district has no laboratory room and 45 pupils in Grade 7. The textbook chapter on "Heat" includes three activities: (a) showing that metals conduct heat better than wood using a spoon and a stick in hot water, (b) heating a metal ball and ring to show expansion, (c) measuring the temperature of hot water as it cools every two minutes.

Write 200-250 words. For each activity, decide whether to teach it by the laboratory method or the demonstration method, and justify your decision with reference to safety, materials and what pupils will learn. Then evaluate which of the three activities best develops pupils' scientific reasoning, and explain why.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1) | Secure (2) | Strong (3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Choice of method | Choices given without reasons, or unsafe choices | A sensible method for each activity with one reason | A sensible, safe method for each activity with reasons covering safety, materials and learning |
| Evaluation of reasoning *(Evaluate)* | Names an activity with no argument | Names an activity and gives one reason | Compares all three activities and argues from what pupils predict, measure and explain |
| Use of the topic's ideas | Few or no ideas from the topic | Uses some ideas, such as before-during-after or predict-observe | Uses the topic's ideas accurately and applies them to the school's real conditions |
