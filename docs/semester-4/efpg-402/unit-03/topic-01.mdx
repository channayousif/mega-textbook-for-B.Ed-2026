# Topic 3.1 - Lecture and discussion methods

## A real classroom situation

Sir Imran teaches Grade 7 General Science at a government boys' school in Sukkur. His class has 68 pupils and the chapter is "Immunity and diseases". He talks for 35 minutes, explaining germs, antibodies and vaccines, while the boys copy notes from the board. At the end he asks, "Any questions?" Nobody answers. The next day a short quiz shows that most boys think a vaccine is a medicine that cures a disease after you catch it.

In the staff room, Madam Farzana says, "Lecturing never works. Let them discuss." Sir Imran replies, "With 68 boys and a long syllabus? Discussion is a luxury." Who is right? This topic shows that both methods have a place, and that the real skill is knowing when and how to use each.

## Explanation

### Lecture method

**What it is.** In the *lecture method* the teacher explains a topic orally, usually with the board, while pupils listen and take notes. Information flows mainly one way, from teacher to pupils. It is the most common method in Pakistani classrooms, because it is quick, needs no apparatus and works with any class size.

**Strengths.** A good lecture can do things other methods cannot do easily:

- It gives a whole class the same background knowledge in a short time, for example the story of how vaccines were discovered.
- It can explain an abstract idea that pupils cannot see or handle, such as how antibodies recognise germs.
- It lets the teacher *model* scientific thinking aloud: "Here is the question. Here is the evidence. So we conclude..."

**Limits.** The same features cause the problems Sir Imran met. Pupils sit still and receive; they do not observe, test or explain anything themselves. Many pupils' attention drifts during a long stretch of continuous talk. And the teacher cannot see what is going on in pupils' heads, so wrong ideas, like "a vaccine cures a disease", stay hidden until the test. At its extreme, teaching by telling turns into lecturing plus rote memorisation (Bybee et al., 2006).

> **Common misconception:** "The lecture method is always bad." This is not true. Research on young children aged 7 to 10 found that clear, explicit teaching helped them learn how to design a fair experiment better than leaving them to find out alone (Bybee et al., 2006). Prince and Felder (2006) agree that teachers still need to guide and clarify, and "sometimes even" lecture; they quote the view that "teaching by telling" works very well after learners have first struggled with a question themselves. The problem is not the lecture. The problem is a long, unbroken lecture that is the *only* method used.

**Worked example: chunking a lecture.** Here is how Sir Imran could rebuild his 35-minute lecture on vaccines. Read it before you try the activity.

1. **Open with a question (3 minutes).** "Your little sister got the polio drops. She was not ill. Why did she need medicine?" Pupils say their answer to a partner. The teacher listens to a few answers and notes the wrong idea "it is a medicine".
2. **Chunk 1 (8 minutes).** Explain germs and how the body fights them, with a simple board diagram.
3. **Check (3 minutes).** "In one sentence, what does an antibody do?" Two or three pupils answer.
4. **Chunk 2 (8 minutes).** Explain that a vaccine trains the body *before* the germ arrives. Return to the opening question.
5. **Check (3 minutes).** "Is a vaccine a cure? Show thumbs up or down, then tell your partner why."
6. **Summary (5 minutes).** Pupils write two lines in their notebooks in their own words.

The content is the same. What changed is that pupils now think, speak and are checked every few minutes, so the teacher finds the wrong idea during the lesson rather than after it.

### Discussion method

**What it is.** In the *discussion method*, the teacher and pupils share and test ideas through talk about one question. The teacher plans the question and guides the talk, but pupils do much of the thinking aloud. A discussion can be whole-class, in small groups, or in pairs ("think, pair, share": each pupil thinks alone, then talks with a partner, then pairs report to the class).

**Why it works.** When pupils explain their ideas aloud, hidden wrong ideas come out where the teacher can deal with them. In group discussion, pupils present and defend their ways of solving a task and get feedback from classmates who are close to their own level of understanding (Bybee et al., 2006). This is why the 5E model of science teaching, described in Topic 3.6, places group discussion in its "elaborate" phase, after pupils have explored and been taught a new idea.

**What a good discussion needs.**

- **An open question with more than one reasonable answer.** "What is the boiling point of water?" ends the talk after one answer. "Our school's water comes from a hand pump. Should we boil it before drinking? Give reasons" can carry a ten-minute discussion in Grade 6.
- **Some knowledge to discuss with.** Pupils cannot discuss germs before they know what germs are. A short lecture, a reading or an experiment usually comes first.
- **Simple ground rules.** One speaker at a time; give a reason; respond to the idea, not the person.
- **A teacher who guides, not dominates.** The teacher asks "Why do you think so?" and "Who disagrees?", invites quiet pupils, and keeps the talk on the question.
- **A summary at the end.** The teacher or a pupil pulls together what was agreed and what is still open.

**Limits.** Discussion takes time. A few confident pupils may dominate, and a discussion without a clear question drifts. In a class of 68, whole-class discussion lets only a handful speak, so pair and small-group talk works better. This is why Madam Farzana is also only partly right.

**Putting the two together.** Most good science lessons combine methods. Figure fig-U3-2 shows one 40-minute lesson that uses a short lecture, a demonstration (Topic 3.2) and a discussion in turn.

## Activity: Rebuild a lecture

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. Each group receives one short Grade 5 to 7 textbook passage, for example on the water cycle, magnets, or the parts of a flower.
2. Imagine a teacher lectures on this passage for 30 minutes without a break. Write down two problems this would cause, using ideas from this topic.
3. Rebuild the lesson as a chunked lecture, like the worked example: an opening question, two short chunks, a check after each chunk and a summary. Write the exact check questions.
4. Add one open discussion question that a Grade 6 class could talk about for ten minutes. Test it on the group: does it have more than one reasonable answer?
5. Swap plans with another group. Each group gives one strength and one suggestion.

## Check your understanding

1. State, in one sentence each, what the lecture method and the discussion method are. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why attention and understanding often drop in a long, unbroken lecture. *(Understand)*
3. Turn the closed question "What is photosynthesis?" into an open question that could start a Grade 7 discussion. *(Apply)*
4. A colleague says, "I never lecture; lecturing is always bad." Give one situation in a Grade 4 to 8 science class where a short lecture is the best choice, and explain why. *(Apply)*

## Summary

The lecture method gives a class the same knowledge quickly and lets the teacher model scientific thinking, but long, unbroken lectures leave pupils passive and hide their wrong ideas. Breaking a lecture into short chunks with checks keeps its strengths and removes most of its weaknesses. The discussion method brings pupils' ideas into the open and lets them learn from each other, but it needs an open question, some prior knowledge and a guiding teacher. Good science lessons use both, each for the job it does best.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can describe the strengths and limits of the lecture method.
- [ ] I can break a long lecture into short chunks with check questions.
- [ ] I can write an open question that will start a real discussion.
- [ ] I can explain why "the lecture method is always bad" is a misconception.

## Try this at your practicum school

Observe one science lesson. Every five minutes, note who is talking (teacher or pupils) and what pupils are doing (listening, copying, answering, discussing). At the end, work out roughly what share of the lesson was teacher talk. Note one point where a short check question or a pair discussion could have been added. Bring your notes to the next seminar.

## Summative task

A Grade 6 teacher in a class of 60 must teach "Balanced diet" in two 40-minute periods. She plans to lecture for both periods because "the content is factual".

Write 200-250 words. Analyse her plan: which parts of the content suit a lecture and which suit a discussion? Then redesign the two periods so that each uses a chunked lecture and at least one discussion, and justify your choices with ideas from this topic.
