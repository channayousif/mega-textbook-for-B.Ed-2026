# Topic 3.4 - Project and field-trip methods

## A real classroom situation

Madam Kulsoom teaches Grade 6 science in Badin. Last year she set a project, "Make a chart on sources of energy", due in two weeks. Most charts arrived neatly coloured, and several were clearly made by older brothers or bought from a stationery shop. When she asked the pupils about their charts, few could explain them.

This year her chapter is again "Energy". A farmer near the school has put solar panels on his tube well, and the head teacher has agreed to a short visit. Madam Kulsoom wonders how to make this year's project and visit teach real science. This topic shows how.

## Explanation

### Project method

**What it is.** In the *project method*, pupils carry out a task with a real purpose, usually over several days or weeks, and produce something: a model, a device, a survey report or an exhibition. The idea was set out by William Heard Kilpatrick, a student of John Dewey, in 1918. He defined a project as a "wholehearted purposeful act" carried on in a social setting: pupils learn best when they truly want to reach a goal and work on it with others (Kilpatrick, 1918). Kilpatrick suggested four steps: purposing, planning, executing and judging. Today we usually add presenting, so that pupils explain their work to others. Figure fig-U3-7 shows the stages.

**The teacher's role.** Kilpatrick was clear that pupils should take each step themselves as far as possible, but that "total failure" may hurt more than help, so the teacher must steer (Kilpatrick, 1918). Modern writers describe a range of projects by how much freedom pupils have: in a *task project* the teacher sets the project and the method; in a *discipline project* the teacher sets the subject area and pupils design their own approach; in a *problem project* pupils choose almost everything (Prince & Felder, 2006). Grade 4 to 8 pupils usually start with task projects and move towards more freedom as their skills grow.

**Worked example: a solar oven project.** Pakistan's science curriculum asks Grade 6 pupils to "design a solar oven" to turn solar energy into heat (National Curriculum Council, 2023). Madam Kulsoom runs it as a project:

1. **Purposing.** She shows a chocolate bar and asks, "Can we melt this using only sunlight?" The class sets the goal.
2. **Planning.** Groups of five list materials (a cardboard box, foil, black paper, a clear plastic sheet), share roles and plan a test.
3. **Executing.** Groups build their ovens in class, then test them in the school yard, recording the temperature inside every ten minutes.
4. **Presenting.** Each group shows its oven and explains why the foil reflects sunlight in and why the black paper absorbs heat.
5. **Judging.** The class compares results against the goal. Which oven melted the chocolate fastest? What would each group change?

Because the work happens in class, the teacher sees who did it, and every pupil can explain the science.

> **Common misconception:** "A project is something pupils make at home and submit." A chart copied from a book, made by an elder brother or bought from a shop is not the project method. It has no pupil purpose, no planning, no testing and no judging, so it teaches little and rewards families who can pay. The project method lives in the process, not the final product. Keep most project work in school time, where you can guide it and see it.

**Limits.** Projects take time, and some pupils may leave the work to others. Pupils may also miss content that a project does not touch (Prince & Felder, 2006). Plan clear roles, check each group's progress, and link the project to the curriculum outcomes you must cover.

### Field-trip method

**What it is.** In the *field-trip method*, the teacher takes pupils out of the classroom to learn from a real place: a farm, a canal bank, a factory, a museum, a weather station or the school garden. Pupils observe things in their real setting, use all their senses and connect classroom ideas to the world. Behrendt and Franklin (2014), reviewing research on school field trips, found that well-planned trips increase pupils' interest, knowledge and motivation, and that "the teacher's role in preplanning, implementation, and reflection" largely decides how much pupils gain.

**Before the trip.** The review gives clear advice (Behrendt & Franklin, 2014):

- Visit the place yourself first, learn its layout and check that it suits every pupil.
- Connect the trip to the curriculum. It should not be a stand-alone outing.
- Prepare pupils: describe the place, explain the purpose and set behaviour rules. A new place can distract pupils who rarely leave the school, so preparation reduces that distraction.
- Plan groups, and brief the adults who will help.

In Pakistan, add the practical steps: the head teacher's approval, written permission from parents, transport, drinking water, and enough adults to supervise safely.

**During the trip.** Stay involved: guide pupils, ask questions, and help them connect what they see to what they learned. The review found that a task sheet works best when one sheet is shared by a small group and asks pupils to look and discuss, rather than having each pupil fill in blanks alone (Behrendt & Franklin, 2014).

**After the trip.** This is the step most often skipped. In one study cited by the review, teachers at neither elementary nor secondary level ran good follow-up activities (Behrendt & Franklin, 2014). Pupils need discussion, presentations and reflection back in class to turn a pleasant day into lasting learning, and the teacher should refer back to the trip in later lessons.

**Worked example: the solar tube well.** Before the visit, Madam Kulsoom visits the farm, gets parents' permission and prepares groups with three questions: "Where does the energy come from? What does the pump do? What happens on a cloudy day?" During the visit, each group shares one sheet, sketches the panels and the pump, and asks the farmer about cloudy days. After the visit, groups present their answers and link them to their solar ovens. Figure fig-U3-8 sets out her plan.

**Limits.** Trips need time, permission and money, and safety is the teacher's responsibility. When a trip is not possible, the school garden, the playground or a short walk round the school can serve as a field site.

## Activity: Plan a trip in our town

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. List three places within walking or short travelling distance of a school you know that could support a Grade 4 to 8 science topic, for example a canal bank, a flour mill, a vegetable market, a health centre or a brick kiln.
2. Choose one place and match it to a curriculum topic.
3. Complete a field-trip plan with the six parts in Figure fig-U3-8: purpose, permissions, safety, before, during and after.
4. Write the three questions each pupil group will answer during the visit.
5. Exchange plans with another group. Check: is the trip connected to the curriculum? Is there a follow-up?

## Summary

The project method builds learning around a purposeful task that pupils plan, carry out, present and judge, with the teacher steering throughout. Its value lies in the process, so most project work should happen where the teacher can guide and see it. The field-trip method takes pupils to learn from real places; its success depends on the teacher's planning before, involvement during and follow-up after the visit. Both methods connect school science to real life, and both need careful links to the curriculum.

## Summative task

A head teacher in rural Sindh says: "Science projects and field trips waste time. The pupils only enjoy themselves, the parents make the projects, and the results do not show in exams."

Write 200-250 words in reply. Analyse which parts of the head teacher's concern are fair and which are not, using the ideas in this topic. Then propose one project and one field trip for a Grade 6 class that answer the concerns, showing how each would be planned and how learning would be checked.
