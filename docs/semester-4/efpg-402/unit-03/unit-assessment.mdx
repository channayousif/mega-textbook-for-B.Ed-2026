# Unit 3 Assessment: Methods and Approaches of Teaching Science

## Unit summary

The lecture method gives a whole class the same knowledge quickly and lets the teacher model scientific thinking, but a long, unbroken lecture leaves pupils passive and hides their wrong ideas; short chunks with check questions keep its strengths. The discussion method brings pupils' ideas into the open, but it needs an open question, some prior knowledge and a guiding teacher (Topic 3.1). In the laboratory method pupils handle materials and gather evidence themselves; it works best with a question and prediction before and a discussion after, and it does not need a laboratory room. In the demonstration method the teacher performs while pupils predict, watch and explain; it suits dangerous, costly or single-set apparatus and is a strong way to open a lesson with a surprise (Topic 3.2).

The scientific method teaches pupils to ask questions, test hypotheses fairly and conclude from evidence, but its steps are a flexible guide, not a fixed recipe. The problem-solving method, which goes back to Dewey, starts from a difficulty pupils feel and works towards a solution that works (Topic 3.3). The project method builds learning around a purposeful task that pupils plan, carry out, present and judge, and its value lies in the process, not a product made at home. The field-trip method takes pupils to learn from real places, and its success depends on planning before, involvement during and follow-up after (Topic 3.4).

The inductive method moves from examples to a rule; the deductive method moves from a rule to examples. Science uses both directions of reasoning, so good teaching combines them (Topic 3.5). Inquiry-based learning has pupils investigate questions with evidence at four levels, from confirmation to open, and the 5E model organises such lessons. Discovery learning works only when the teacher guides it (Topic 3.6). Finally, experiential learning follows Kolb's cycle of experience, reflection, conceptualisation and experimentation; activity must be minds-on as well as hands-on. Choosing a method depends on the learning goal, the pupils, the resources and the class size, and most good lessons combine several methods (Topic 3.7).

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. Which description fits the lecture method? *(Remember)*
   - A) Pupils handle apparatus in groups to test a question.
   - B) The teacher explains a topic orally while pupils listen and take notes.
   - C) Pupils plan and carry out a task over several weeks.
   - D) Pupils find a rule by testing many examples.
2. A Grade 7 teacher with 70 pupils has 15 minutes to tell the story of how vaccines were discovered. Which approach suits this goal best? *(Apply)*
   - A) An open inquiry in which pupils design their own investigation
   - B) A field trip to a hospital
   - C) A short lecture broken by one or two check questions
   - D) A month-long group project
3. When is the demonstration method a better choice than the laboratory method? *(Understand)*
   - A) When every group has its own set of safe, cheap materials
   - B) When the main aim is for every pupil to practise handling apparatus
   - C) When there is only one set of delicate or dangerous apparatus
   - D) When pupils are planning their own investigations
4. In a Grade 8 lesson, groups of pupils add drops of lemon juice, soap solution and vinegar to turmeric water and record the colours. Which method is the teacher using? *(Apply)*
   - A) Laboratory method
   - B) Demonstration method
   - C) Lecture method
   - D) Field-trip method
5. Pupils test whether light affects how many gram seeds sprout. To make the test fair, what should differ between their two plates? *(Apply)*
   - A) The type of seed
   - B) The amount of water
   - C) Whether the plate is kept in light or in the dark
   - D) The number of seeds
6. Which sequence gives Kilpatrick's steps of the project method? *(Remember)*
   - A) Planning, purposing, judging, executing
   - B) Purposing, planning, executing, judging
   - C) Executing, planning, purposing, judging
   - D) Judging, purposing, planning, executing
7. According to the research reviewed in Topic 3.4, which stage of a field trip do teachers most often neglect? *(Understand)*
   - A) Getting permission
   - B) Choosing the place
   - C) Following up in class after the visit
   - D) Arranging transport
8. A teacher gives pupils ten objects and a magnet. Pupils test each object, look for a pattern and then state a rule. Which method is this? *(Apply)*
   - A) Deductive method
   - B) Inductive method
   - C) Lecture method
   - D) Demonstration method
9. A teacher gives pupils the question "How does salt affect whether an egg floats?" and lets each group plan its own procedure. Which level of inquiry is this? *(Understand)*
   - A) Confirmation inquiry
   - B) Structured inquiry
   - C) Guided inquiry
   - D) Open inquiry
10. In Kolb's experiential learning cycle, which stage comes straight after concrete experience? *(Remember)*
    - A) Active experimentation
    - B) Abstract conceptualisation
    - C) Reflective observation
    - D) Planning

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

1. Explain two limits of a 35-minute unbroken lecture in a Grade 7 science class, and explain how breaking it into chunks reduces each limit. *(Understand)*
2. Write one open discussion question on the Grade 6 topic "Water pollution" that a class could discuss for ten minutes, and give two ground rules for the discussion. Explain in one sentence why your question is open. *(Apply)*
3. A teacher says, "Our school has no laboratory room, so I cannot use the laboratory method." Analyse this claim in three to four sentences, giving one example of practical work the teacher could still run. *(Analyze)*
4. List, in order, the steps for planning and showing a safe classroom demonstration, using the example of a candle going out under an upturned glass. *(Apply)*
5. Distinguish the scientific method from the problem-solving method by their aims, and give one Grade 4 to 8 question that suits each. *(Understand)*
6. A pupil tests whether fertiliser helps plants grow. He puts plant A, with fertiliser, in a large pot by the window, and plant B, without fertiliser, in a small pot in a corner. Analyse why this test is not fair and say how to make it fair. *(Analyze)*
7. Give three things a teacher should do before taking a Grade 5 class on a field trip to a canal bank, with a reason for each. *(Understand)*
8. A Grade 6 teacher writes "Light travels in straight lines" on the board, explains it, and asks pupils to copy two examples. Identify the method, analyse one weakness of the lesson, and suggest one step that would add the other direction of reasoning. *(Analyze)*
9. Classify each activity by its level of inquiry and justify each choice in one sentence: (a) pupils follow given steps to show that a plant in the dark turns pale, already knowing the result; (b) pupils choose their own question about plant growth and plan how to test it. *(Analyze)*
10. Explain why "activity-based learning means no teacher input" is a misconception, and name two things the teacher must do during an activity. *(Understand)*

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. A government school has no laboratory room and 50 pupils in Grade 6. The chapter "Separating mixtures" includes separating sand from salt, separating iron filings from sand, and boiling salt water until the salt is left behind. Design a plan for teaching these three activities with low-cost materials. Decide which parts you would run by the laboratory method and which by the demonstration method, and evaluate your decisions in terms of safety, materials and learning. *(Evaluate)*
2. Mosquitoes breed in standing water around a Grade 7 school in your district. Design a problem-solving project in which pupils study and tackle this problem. Set out the stages from purposing to judging, show where pupils would use a fair test or a careful observation, and explain how you would check each pupil's learning. *(Create)*
3. Plan two short lessons on the Grade 6 concept "light travels in straight lines", one inductive and one deductive. Then analyse which version suits a Grade 6 class meeting the concept for the first time, and explain how each version could bring in the other direction of reasoning. *(Analyze)*
4. A teacher gives Grade 6 pupils magnets and a tray of objects and says, "Discover the rule." She gives no further help. Half the class concludes that "magnets attract all metals". Evaluate this lesson using the research on discovery learning, then redesign it as a guided inquiry using the five phases of the 5E model. *(Evaluate)*
5. You teach in a school with no laboratory and 65 pupils per class. You must teach three topics: (a) "Seeds need water to sprout" in Grade 5, (b) "Burning needs air" in Grade 7, (c) "Should our town ban plastic bags?" in Grade 8. For each topic, choose the method or combination of methods from this unit that you judge best and defend your choice, taking the large class and the lack of a laboratory into account. Then identify which of your three choices carries the greatest risk and how you would reduce it. *(Evaluate)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### MCQ answer key

1. B - the lecture method is oral explanation by the teacher; A is the laboratory method, C the project method and D the inductive method.
2. C - telling a story to a large class in a short time suits a short lecture, and check questions keep pupils thinking; the other options take far more time than the goal needs.
3. C - a demonstration suits single-set, delicate or dangerous apparatus; A, B and D describe conditions that favour pupils' own practical work.
4. A - pupils handle the materials in groups and record evidence, which is the laboratory method.
5. C - in a fair test only the variable being tested (light) changes; seed type, water and seed number are kept the same.
6. B - Kilpatrick (1918) named purposing, planning, executing and judging.
7. C - Behrendt and Franklin (2014) report that follow-up after the visit is the step teachers most often skip.
8. B - moving from examples to a rule is inductive; the deductive method states the rule first.
9. C - the teacher gives only the question and pupils plan the procedure, which is guided inquiry; in structured inquiry the procedure is also given.
10. C - Kolb's order is concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualisation, active experimentation.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. Model: First, pupils are passive and attention drifts in long talk; a check question every eight to ten minutes makes every pupil think and respond. Second, the teacher cannot see pupils' wrong ideas; short checks and pair talk bring those ideas out during the lesson, not after the test. **Marks (4):** 1 per limit (2); 1 per matching explanation of how chunking helps (2).
2. Model: "Our town's canal water is used for washing and for crops. Who is responsible for keeping it clean, and what could our school do?" Rules: one speaker at a time; give a reason for every idea. The question is open because several reasonable answers can be argued with reasons. **Marks (4):** 2 - a question with more than one reasonable answer, linked to water pollution; 1 - two sensible ground rules; 1 - explanation of openness.
3. Model: The claim confuses a method with a room. The laboratory method means pupils handle materials and gather evidence themselves, which can happen on ordinary desks. For example, groups can test household liquids with turmeric water, or sprout gram seeds on wet cloth. The real limits are materials and supervision, which low-cost materials and group work can address. **Marks (4):** 1 - identifies the confusion of method and room; 1 - defines the laboratory method by what pupils do; 1 - a realistic low-cost example; 1 - names a real limit and a response to it.
4. Model: State the aim (pupils see that burning needs air); collect apparatus and check safety (candle, glass jar, sand tray, matches kept by the teacher); do a trial run and check visibility from the back; ask pupils to predict what will happen when the glass covers the candle; show slowly; ask what they observed and why; conclude together and pupils record. **Marks (4):** 1 - aim and safety; 1 - trial run; 1 - prediction before showing; 1 - questioning and conclusion after showing.
5. Model: The scientific method aims to explain how nature works; its product is an explanation tested by evidence, for example "Why does iron rust?" The problem-solving method aims to solve a practical difficulty; its product is a working solution, for example "How can we stop the school gate rusting?" **Marks (4):** 1 per aim (2); 1 per suitable question (2).
6. Model: The test changes three things at once: fertiliser, pot size and light. If plant A grows taller, we cannot tell whether the fertiliser, the bigger pot or the sunlight caused it. To make it fair, use the same pot size, the same place and the same water for both plants, and change only the fertiliser. **Marks (4):** 1 - identifies that more than one variable changes; 1 - names at least two uncontrolled variables; 1 - explains why the result cannot be interpreted; 1 - a fair redesign changing only fertiliser.
7. Model: Visit the canal bank first, to check safety and plan where pupils will work. Get the head teacher's approval and parents' written permission, so the trip is authorised and families know. Prepare pupils with the purpose, rules and group questions, so they know what to look for and are less distracted by the new place. Also accepted: arrange enough adults, link to the curriculum. **Marks (3):** 1 per action with a fitting reason.
8. Model: The lesson is deductive. Its weakness is that pupils copy a rule without seeing evidence for it, so they may memorise it without understanding. To add the other direction, the teacher could have pupils look at a torch beam through three cards with holes in a line, then move one card and observe that the light no longer passes. **Marks (4):** 1 - names deductive; 1 - analyses a real weakness; 2 - a practical step in which pupils observe or test.
9. Model: (a) Confirmation inquiry, because the question, procedure and result are all given and pupils only confirm. (b) Open inquiry, because pupils choose the question and plan the procedure themselves. **Marks (4):** 1 per correct level (2); 1 per justification that names what is given and what is not (2).
10. Model: Without the teacher, pupils may be busy without reflecting, so wrong ideas survive, as when pupils who made clay models still thought the sun goes round the earth. The teacher must ask questions that make pupils observe and reflect, and must introduce the scientific explanation and check understanding. **Marks (3):** 1 - explains why activity alone may not teach; 1 per teacher role (2).

### ERQ rubrics

1. Expected judgement: separating sand and salt by dissolving and filtering and separating iron filings with a magnet suit group practicals with low-cost materials; boiling salt water to dryness suits a teacher demonstration, because of the heat source and hot liquid. Analytic rubric: *Plan and materials* (0-3: realistic low-cost materials and grouping for 50 pupils) · *Method decisions* (0-3: a sensible method for each activity) · *Evaluation of decisions* (0-4, **Evaluate**: each decision is weighed against safety, materials and what pupils will learn, with at least one trade-off acknowledged).
2. Analytic rubric: *Stages of the project* (0-3: purposing, planning, executing, presenting and judging, with the teacher's role) · *Scientific enquiry inside the project* (0-3: a fair test or a systematic observation, such as counting larvae in covered and uncovered containers) · *Feasibility and safety* (0-2: suited to Grade 7 and safe) · *Checking each pupil's learning* (0-2, **Create**: individual evidence, such as a short written explanation or a role-based report, not only a group product).
3. Analytic rubric: *Inductive plan* (0-3: pupils observe examples first, such as a torch beam through holes in cards, and state the rule) · *Deductive plan* (0-2: the rule is stated, explained and applied) · *Analysis of suitability* (0-3, **Analyze**: argues which suits first meeting of the concept, with reasons about understanding, time and pupils) · *Combining directions* (0-2: a specific step in each plan that brings in the other direction).
4. Analytic rubric: *Evaluation of the lesson* (0-4, **Evaluate**: uses the research on unguided discovery, such as the studies in Bybee et al. (2006) and Prince and Felder (2006), to explain why half the class formed a wrong rule) · *5E redesign* (0-4: all five phases, with a guiding question, tricky examples such as aluminium foil and a coin, a direct explanation phase and a check) · *Accuracy* (0-2: correct science about which materials magnets attract).
5. Expected range (other well-defended choices accepted): (a) a group practical with gram seeds on wet cloth, set up as a fair test, possibly as structured inquiry; (b) a teacher demonstration with predict-observe-explain, because of the flame, followed by discussion; (c) a discussion, possibly with a short project or survey of plastic use in the town. Analytic rubric: *Choices fit the topics* (0-3) · *Defence of choices* (0-4, **Evaluate**: each choice is defended with reasons about the goal, pupils, resources, safety and the class of 65) · *Risk and remedy* (0-3: identifies the riskiest choice, such as managing 65 pupils in group practicals or a dominated discussion, and proposes a practical remedy).
