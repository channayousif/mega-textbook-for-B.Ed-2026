# Topic 3.3 - Scientific and problem-solving methods

## A real classroom situation

It is June in Khairpur and the temperature is above 45 °C. In Madam Saima's Grade 7 class, the pupils complain that the water in their plastic bottles is hot by the second period. One girl, Mehwish, says, "At home my grandmother keeps water in a clay pot and it stays cool. Why?" Another asks, "Can we make our bottles stay cool too?"

Madam Saima notices that the class has asked two different kinds of question. Mehwish's "Why?" asks for an explanation of nature. The second question asks for a solution to a practical problem. This topic shows how the scientific method and the problem-solving method use questions like these, and how they differ.

## Explanation

### Scientific method

**What it is.** In the *scientific method* as a teaching method, pupils find out about the natural world by working through the steps of a scientific enquiry: observing, asking a question, suggesting a *hypothesis* (a possible answer that can be tested), planning and carrying out a test, recording results, drawing a conclusion and sharing it. The teacher's aim is that pupils learn both the science content and how scientists find things out.

**A fair test.** Many school enquiries are fair tests. A *variable* is anything that can change in an experiment, such as the amount of water or light. In a fair test, pupils change only one variable, measure its effect, and keep everything else the same. Pakistan's curriculum names the fair test as one of five main types of scientific enquiry pupils should know by the end of Grade 5, alongside observing over time, identifying and classifying, comparing and contrasting, and research. By Grade 8, pupils should be able to judge "whether a given hypothesis is testable" and make predictions (National Curriculum Council, 2023).

**Worked example: do seeds need light to sprout?** A Grade 5 teacher follows the steps:

1. **Observe:** seeds sprout in the school garden in sunlight.
2. **Question:** do seeds need light to sprout?
3. **Hypothesis:** most pupils say, "Seeds need light, so seeds in the dark will not sprout."
4. **Fair test:** two plates of wet cloth with ten gram seeds each. One plate goes in a cupboard; one stays by the window. Same seeds, same water, same room.
5. **Record:** pupils count sprouted seeds each day for four days.
6. **Conclude:** seeds sprout in both plates. The hypothesis is not supported. Light is not needed for sprouting, although the young plants in the dark later turn pale and weak.
7. **Communicate:** groups present their tables and the new question that arises: "What do young plants need light for?"

The surprise in step 6 is valuable. It shows pupils that a hypothesis is a guess to be tested, not a fact to be proved.

> **Common misconception:** "The scientific method is one fixed recipe of five steps." Textbooks often present it this way, and studies show that many pupils and teachers believe it (Hepburn & Andersen, 2015/2026). Real scientists move back and forth between steps: a strange result sends them back to a new question, and some enquiries, such as observing the moon over a month or classifying leaves, have no experiment at all. Teach the steps as a useful guide, and let pupils see that science is more flexible than any list.

### Problem-solving method

**What it is.** In the *problem-solving method*, pupils work through a real problem, one they actually feel, using what they know and reasoning carefully to reach a solution. The method goes back to John Dewey, who began his career as a science teacher. Dewey's steps of reflective thinking begin with "a felt difficulty" and end with further observation or experiment "leading to its acceptance or rejection" (Hepburn & Andersen, 2015/2026). A teaching version of his steps runs (Bybee et al., 2006):

1. **Sense the problem.** Pupils meet a situation that puzzles or bothers them.
2. **Define the problem.** The teacher helps them state it clearly.
3. **Suggest possible solutions.** Pupils propose ideas, linking them to past experience.
4. **Reason about the solutions.** Pupils think through which idea is likely to work and how to test it.
5. **Test.** Pupils try the solutions and check the results.
6. **Conclude and act.** Pupils state the best solution and what to do next.

**Worked example: the hot water bottles.** Figure fig-U3-5 shows Madam Saima's class working through the steps. The felt difficulty is real: hot water by the second period. Defining the problem turns a complaint into a question that can be answered. Pupils suggest three solutions from home experience and plan a fair test. When they measure, the bottle wrapped in a wet cloth stays coolest. The class then learns the science behind it: as water evaporates from the cloth, it takes heat away from the bottle. That answers Mehwish's question about the clay pot too, because water seeps through the pot's walls and evaporates from its surface.

**Strengths and limits.** Problem-solving makes science useful and motivating, because pupils see that knowledge solves real problems. It builds reasoning and teamwork. But it takes time, and the problem must be one that pupils at that level can actually solve. A teacher must choose problems carefully and be ready to guide when pupils are stuck.

**How the two methods relate.** The two methods overlap: the hot-bottle problem used a fair test, which is a scientific-method step. The difference is in the aim. The scientific method asks "Why does this happen?" and its product is an explanation of nature. The problem-solving method asks "How can we fix this?" and its product is a working solution, which often uses scientific enquiry along the way. Figure fig-U3-6 compares them.

## Activity: Question sort

Work in groups of three. About 20 minutes.

1. Each group receives eight questions written on slips, for example: "Why does iron rust?", "How can we stop the school gate rusting?", "Does salt water boil at a higher temperature?", "How can we stop ants reaching the food in our lunch boxes?", "Why do leaves fall in winter?", "How can we make the classroom cooler without a fan?", "Do earthworms prefer wet or dry soil?", "How can we save water at the school tap?"
2. Sort the slips into two piles: questions for the scientific method (explaining nature) and questions for the problem-solving method (fixing a practical problem). Some may fit both; say why.
3. Choose one question from each pile. For the scientific question, write a testable hypothesis and the one variable you would change. For the problem, write the first three problem-solving steps.
4. Present one of your plans to another group, who checks that the test is fair.

## Check your understanding

1. List the steps of the problem-solving method in order. *(Remember)*
2. Explain the difference in aim between the scientific method and the problem-solving method. *(Understand)*
3. A pupil wants to find out whether plants grow taller with more water. Name the one variable to change and two things to keep the same. *(Apply)*
4. A textbook says, "The scientific method always has five fixed steps." Rewrite the sentence so that it is accurate, and give one example of an enquiry that does not follow the five steps. *(Apply)*

## Summary

The scientific method teaches pupils to find out about nature by asking questions, testing hypotheses fairly and drawing conclusions from evidence. Its steps are a helpful guide, not a fixed recipe: real enquiry loops back and takes many forms. The problem-solving method starts from a difficulty pupils actually feel and works through defining it, suggesting and testing solutions and acting on the result. The two overlap, but the first aims at an explanation and the second at a working solution.

## Summative task

A Grade 7 textbook activity says: "Step 1: Take two plants. Step 2: Put one in the dark. Step 3: After a week, see that the plant in the dark is pale. Conclusion: plants need light."

Write 200-250 words. Analyse what this activity teaches and what it misses about the scientific method; consider the hypothesis, the fairness of the test and the conclusion. Then rewrite it so that pupils form their own hypothesis, plan a fair test and decide whether the evidence supports their hypothesis.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1) | Secure (2) | Strong (3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Analysis of the activity *(Analyze)* | Describes the activity without judging it | Identifies one thing the activity misses, such as the hypothesis or fairness | Explains that the conclusion is given in advance, that no hypothesis is formed and how the test could be unfair, with reasons |
| Rewritten activity | Steps unchanged or unclear | Includes a hypothesis and a fair test | Includes a pupil hypothesis, one changed variable, controlled variables, a record and a conclusion judged against evidence |
