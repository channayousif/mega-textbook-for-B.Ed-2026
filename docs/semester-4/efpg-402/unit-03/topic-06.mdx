# Topic 3.6 - Inquiry-based and discovery learning

## A real classroom situation

Madam Nasreen teaches Grade 6 in Dadu. She has read that pupils learn best when they discover things for themselves. For her lesson on floating and sinking, she gives each group a bowl of water, salt, an egg and some spoons, and says, "Discover what makes things float." Then she sits at her desk.

After twenty minutes, one group has made a mess, one has eaten the salt, and two groups announce, "Heavy things sink." Only one group noticed that the egg floats when enough salt is added. Madam Nasreen is disappointed: "Discovery learning does not work with my pupils." Was the problem the method, or the way she used it? This topic answers that question.

## Explanation

### Inquiry-based learning

**What it is.** In *inquiry-based learning*, pupils learn science by investigating: they start from a question, a problem or some puzzling observations, collect evidence, form explanations and share them. Prince and Felder (2006) describe inquiry learning as beginning "when students are presented with questions to be answered, problems to be solved, or a set of observations to be explained". Pupils learn the content and, at the same time, the skills of scientific enquiry.

**Four levels of inquiry.** Inquiry is not all-or-nothing. Banchi and Bell (2008) describe four levels, depending on how much the teacher gives the pupils (Figure fig-U3-11):

1. **Confirmation inquiry.** The teacher gives the question and the procedure, and pupils already know the result. Pupils confirm it and practise skills such as recording data.
2. **Structured inquiry.** The teacher gives the question and the procedure; pupils find the answer and explain it from their evidence.
3. **Guided inquiry.** The teacher gives only the question; pupils plan the procedure, collect data and explain.
4. **Open inquiry.** Pupils ask their own question, plan and carry out the test, and communicate the result.

Banchi and Bell (2008) stress that pupils need much practice at the lower levels before open inquiry succeeds, and that even when pupils plan their own procedures the teacher's role is not passive: pupils still need guidance on whether their plans make sense.

**The 5E model.** A widely used way to organise an inquiry lesson is the BSCS 5E instructional model, with five phases (Bybee et al., 2006):

- **Engage:** a question or surprising event catches pupils' interest and brings out their existing ideas.
- **Explore:** pupils work with materials and gather shared, concrete experience; the teacher acts as a coach.
- **Explain:** pupils give their explanations first; then the teacher introduces the scientific explanation and terms clearly and directly.
- **Elaborate:** pupils apply the idea to a new situation, often through group discussion.
- **Evaluate:** pupils and teacher check understanding.

Notice that the "explain" phase includes the teacher teaching directly. Inquiry does not mean the teacher never explains.

**Worked example: a guided inquiry on floating.** Here is how Madam Nasreen could reteach her lesson at the guided level, inside the 5E phases.

1. **Engage (5 minutes).** She drops an egg into a glass of tap water. It sinks. "Is there any way to make this egg float without touching it?" Pupils guess.
2. **Explore (15 minutes).** She gives the question, "How does adding salt affect whether an egg floats?" Groups plan their own test: how much salt, how to add it, what to record. She visits each group and asks, "What will you change? What will you keep the same?"
3. **Explain (10 minutes).** Groups report: the egg floats once enough salt is dissolved. She then introduces the idea that salty water is denser than tap water, so it pushes up harder on the egg.
4. **Elaborate (5 minutes).** "Why is it easier to float in the sea than in a canal?"
5. **Evaluate (5 minutes).** Each pupil writes two sentences explaining the egg result in their own words.

### Discovery learning

**What it is.** In *discovery learning*, pupils work largely on their own to find a concept or rule the teacher has in mind, by exploring materials, examples or problems. In its purest form, the teacher sets the task and gives feedback but does not direct pupils' efforts (Prince & Felder, 2006). The idea is attractive: knowledge pupils discover themselves should be better understood and remembered.

> **Common misconception:** "Discovery learning works without guidance." Research does not support this. In well-known studies reviewed by Bybee et al. (2006), children aged 7 to 10 learned how to design a fair experiment better when the teacher gave examples and explanations than when they explored the same materials with no guiding questions and no feedback. Prince and Felder (2006) report that solid evidence for pure discovery learning does not exist, and that teachers more often use *guided discovery*, in which the teacher provides some guidance throughout. Madam Nasreen's lesson failed because it was unguided, not because pupils cannot discover.

**Guided discovery.** In guided discovery, the teacher plans the materials so that the pattern can be found, asks leading questions ("What did you change? What happened to the egg?"), gives feedback, and names the concept once pupils are close. Bybee et al. (2006) describe how the science learning cycle grew out of an early model of "guided discovery" for young children, and how the 5E model builds direct teaching into one of its phases. Good discovery is guided.

**Inquiry and discovery compared.** The two overlap, and with guidance they become very similar (Prince & Felder, 2006). The main difference is in the target. In discovery learning, the teacher has a particular concept in mind and pupils are led towards it. In inquiry-based learning, the emphasis is on investigating a question with evidence; at the open level, the answer may not be known in advance. ## Activity: Level up the inquiry

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. Each group receives a Grade 4 to 8 textbook activity written as a recipe, for example "Put a plant in sunlight and one in the dark; after a week, see which is healthier", or "Rub a balloon on your hair and hold it near small bits of paper; see what happens."
2. Decide which of the four levels of inquiry the activity is at now. Give reasons.
3. Rewrite it one level higher. Write the exact question you would give pupils, and say what you would no longer give them.
4. Add one guiding question the teacher could ask each group while they work.
5. Present your rewrite to another group, who checks that the new level is realistic for the grade.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the four levels of inquiry, from the lowest to the highest. *(Remember)*
2. Explain the difference between structured inquiry and guided inquiry. *(Understand)*
3. Explain, using evidence from this topic, why pure discovery often fails with Grade 4 to 8 pupils. *(Understand)*
4. Write two leading questions that would turn Madam Nasreen's unguided lesson into guided discovery. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Inquiry-based learning has pupils investigate questions with evidence; it runs at four levels, from confirmation to open, and pupils climb the levels with practice. The 5E model organises an inquiry lesson into engage, explore, explain, elaborate and evaluate, and includes the teacher's direct explanation. Discovery learning has pupils find a concept the teacher has in mind; research shows it works when guided, not when pupils are left alone. In both, the teacher designs, questions, coaches and explains.

## Summative task

A teacher says: "Inquiry and discovery are for rich private schools with laboratories. In my class of 60 with no equipment, I can only teach from the book."

Write 200-250 words. Evaluate this claim using the four levels of inquiry and the idea of guided discovery. Then plan one guided-inquiry lesson for a Grade 6 topic of your choice that would work in a class of 60 with low-cost materials, setting out the 5E phases briefly.
