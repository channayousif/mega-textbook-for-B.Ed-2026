# Topic 6.5 - Laboratory safety rules and first aid

## A real classroom situation

Sir Naveed teaches Grade 8 science at a government high school in Larkana. Today, pupils are performing an experiment to test the electrical conductivity of various household solutions using a torch bulb, wires, and beakers containing dilute hydrochloric acid, salt water, and vinegar.

Because it is a physics and electricity unit, Sir Naveed does not require pupils to wear protective safety spectacles, assuming that goggles are only necessary for dedicated chemistry lessons. At Bench 3, two boys begin playfully splashing water with their plastic droppers. One boy accidentally bumps a beaker of dilute acid, splashing droplets into the face and left eye of a nearby classmate.

The injured pupil screams in pain, clutching his eye. A student attendant rushes forward with a bottle of rosewater, while another student suggests putting butter or cooking oil on the pupil's face. Sir Naveed immediately intervenes: he brushes away the folk remedies, rushes the injured boy to the perimeter wash sink, holds the pupil's eyelids wide apart, and flushes the eye continuously with clean, running cold water for fifteen full minutes while sending another student to summon the school headmaster and local clinic.

Sir Naveed's decisive immediate intervention prevented permanent corneal burns. However, the incident forcefully demonstrated that safety rules and first-aid readiness must never be compromised in any science lesson.

## Explanation

### Laboratory safety rules

Safety in a school science laboratory is not an accidental outcome; it is the result of continuous, proactive instructional management and established behavioral contracts (CPSC & NIOSH, 2006). The science teacher holds legal, professional, and ethical duty of care for every pupil in the room.

Core laboratory safety regulations must be taught explicitly, displayed prominently on wall posters, and enforced consistently across all science disciplines (National Curriculum Council, 2023):

1. **Personal protective equipment (PPE):** Safety spectacles or goggles with side shields must be worn by all pupils and teachers whenever heating materials, handling glassware under pressure, or manipulating chemical solutions. Sturdy closed-toe footwear is mandatory; sandals, slippers, and loose dangling clothing (such as flowing dupattas or scarves) must be secured or tied back.
2. **Behavioral discipline and zero horseplay:** Running, pushing, practical jokes, eating, drinking, or chewing gum are strictly forbidden inside the laboratory. Food and drinks can easily absorb toxic chemical vapours or be contaminated by contaminated bench surfaces.
3. **Apparatus and heating safety:** Never point the open mouth of a heated test tube toward oneself or any classmate. Always heat liquids gently using a wire gauze over a tripod, and never seal a container that is being heated. Extinguish spirit burners only by covering them with their fitted metal cap; never blow across the flame.
4. **Chemical handling protocols:** Never taste, inhale, or directly smell any chemical substance. Teach pupils the proper "wafting" technique: gently fanning the air above a container toward the nose with an open hand from a safe distance. Never pipette liquids by mouth; always use rubber suction bulbs or plastic syringes.
5. **Immediate accident reporting:** Pupils must be trained to report every spill, breakage, splash, or minor burn immediately to the teacher, regardless of how minor it appears.

### First-aid measures

When accidents occur, immediate and correct first-aid action taken during the first sixty seconds determines whether an injury heals smoothly or results in permanent physical impairment (CPSC & NIOSH, 2006). Every science laboratory must maintain a fully stocked, accessible first-aid kit containing sterile gauze pads, adhesive bandages, antiseptic solution, burn dressing gel, saline eyewash bottles, blunt tweezers, and a triangular sling.

Figure fig-U6-9 details the mandatory first-aid protocols for common laboratory accidents:

- **Chemical splash in eyes:** Hold the eyelids wide open with clean fingers and flush the affected eye continuously with cold, clean water or sterile saline for at least fifteen minutes. Never use chemical neutralizing agents or home remedies in the eye; only continuous dilution and mechanical flushing protects the cornea. Cover loosely with sterile gauze and seek urgent medical evaluation.
- **Chemical splash on skin:** Immediately strip off contaminated clothing and flood the affected skin area with copious running cold water for fifteen minutes. Do not apply grease, butter, or ointments, as they trap residual heat and chemical reagents against the skin.
- **Thermal burns from hot glassware or burners:** Immediately submerge the burnt skin in clean, cold water for ten to fifteen minutes until the throbbing pain subsides. Cover the burn loosely with clean dry sterile gauze. Never burst blisters, as the intact skin blister serves as a sterile barrier against bacterial infection.
- **Glass cuts and lacerations:** Apply direct, steady pressure to the wound using a clean sterile gauze pad to control bleeding. Elevate the wounded limb above the heart. Check gently for loose surface glass splinters, but never probe deep inside an open wound.
- **Toxic fume inhalation:** Move the victim into fresh outdoor air immediately. Loosen tight collars or belts, keep the pupil calm and warm, and monitor respiration closely while summoning medical assistance.

Hazard pictograms (fig-U6-10) must be taught so pupils recognize the intrinsic dangers of chemicals before opening any bottle: corrosive symbols warn against skin and eye contact; flame symbols mandate isolation from spirit burners; skull symbols indicate lethal toxicity; and oxidizer symbols indicate severe fire acceleration risks.

#### Worked example: Managing an acid splash during a class practical

Sir Naveed manages the accidental acid splash in the Grade 8 classroom:

1. *Immediate action (0 to 30 seconds):* He leads the pupil to the perimeter wash basin, turns on the cold tap, and instructs the boy to tilt his head so the injured eye is downward, preventing contaminated water from flowing into the uninjured eye.
2. *Flushing (Minutes 1 to 15):* Using his thumbs and forefingers, Sir Naveed holds the boy's upper and lower eyelids open, ensuring the stream of cool water continuously washes across the eyeball. He times the flushing with his wristwatch for a full fifteen minutes.
3. *Class management:* He calmly instructs the rest of the class to switch off burners, step away from their benches, and remain seated.
4. *Post-flush care:* After fifteen minutes, he gently places a clean, dry sterile eye pad over the closed eye, secures it loosely with surgical tape, and arranges immediate transport to the nearest hospital ophthalmologist for clinical evaluation.

> **Common misconception:** "Safety rules and goggles are only required for chemistry practicals." Many teachers believe that physics and biology practicals are completely benign. In reality, physics practicals involve hazardous coiled springs under tension, heated glass, and mains electricity; biology practicals utilize scalpel blades, sharp dissection pins, and glass microscope slides. Protective safety rules apply equally across all scientific disciplines.

## Activity: Simulating laboratory emergency responses

Work in groups of three. About 25 minutes.

1. Role-play one of the following emergency scenarios:
   - Scenario A: A pupil's sleeve catches fire from an unattended spirit burner flame.
   - Scenario B: A test tube of boiling liquid bumps, splashing hot caustic sodium hydroxide on a pupil's forearm.
   - Scenario C: A pupil drops a 500 mL glass flask, causing a deep bleeding cut on the palm.
2. For your chosen scenario, demonstrate:
   - The immediate command the teacher gives to the rest of the class.
   - The step-by-step physical first-aid intervention executed within the first two minutes.
   - The appropriate follow-up communication to school administration and medical personnel.
3. Observe other group simulations and evaluate whether their responses followed established scientific first-aid protocols.

3. During a Grade 7 practical on heating solids, a pupil places a cork stopper tightly into a test tube before heating it over a spirit burner flame. Apply laboratory safety rules to analyze two severe risks of this action and state the exact corrective instruction you would give. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Proactive laboratory safety requires clear behavioral expectations, compulsory personal protective equipment, strict heating protocols, and thorough pupil familiarity with hazard pictograms. In the event of an accident, rapid and correct first-aid execution is paramount: chemical splashes require immediate, continuous water flushing for at least fifteen minutes; thermal burns demand immersion in cold water without greasy ointments; and bleeding cuts require direct sterile pressure and limb elevation. Safety contracts and emergency readiness transform the laboratory into a protected, productive learning environment.

## Summative task

During a school observation visit, you observe a Grade 8 practical lesson where pupils are heating test tubes without goggles, two spirit burners have frayed leaking wicks, and the teacher keeps the single first-aid kit locked in an administrative cupboard in another building. Write a 250-300 word critical laboratory safety audit for the headteacher. In your audit, analyze the immediate physical and legal liabilities of these practices, detail the exact first-aid failure risks created by locking away emergency medical gear, and prescribe an actionable safety reform plan including compulsory PPE, equipment maintenance, and emergency response training.
