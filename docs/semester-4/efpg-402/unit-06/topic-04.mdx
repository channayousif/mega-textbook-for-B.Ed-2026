# Topic 6.4 - Labelling, inventory and stock registers

## A real classroom situation

Madam Shahida takes over as head of science at a government comprehensive high school in Hyderabad. When preparing for the annual practical examinations, she finds the laboratory preparation room in administrative disarray.

Dozens of brown glass reagent bottles sit on shelves with their paper labels either completely missing, torn, or faded into illegibility by acidic fumes. Several bottles contain clear liquids that could be distilled water, dilute hydrochloric acid, or toxic ammonia solution. When the school auditor asks to see the official stock registers to account for government equipment grants received over the previous five years, Madam Shahida discovers only a few loose, water-damaged sheets of paper with incomplete notes like "10 flasks received" and "some beakers broken."

Without proper container labels, every bottle is a potential chemical hazard. Without formal stock registers, the school cannot verify equipment quantities, track breakages, prevent theft, or justify budget requisitions for new apparatus. Madam Shahida immediately establishes an inventory audit and relabelling campaign.

## Explanation

### Proper labelling

Every chemical container, reagent bottle, specimen jar, and prepared stock solution in a science laboratory must carry a clear, durable, and informative label (CPSC & NIOSH, 2006). An unlabelled chemical container is an immediate hazard that should never be used, smelled, or tasted.

Figure fig-U6-8 illustrates the mandatory elements of a modern Globally Harmonized System (GHS) compliant laboratory label:

1. **Chemical identity and concentration:** The full formal chemical name (e.g., "Hydrochloric Acid, Dilute"), chemical formula (`HCl`), and exact molarity or percentage concentration (e.g., "1.0 M aqueous"). Never use obscure commercial brand names or ambiguous shorthand.
2. **Signal word:** A prominent alert word indicating severity: "DANGER" for severe hazards (such as concentrated acids or poisons) or "WARNING" for moderate hazards.
3. **Hazard pictograms and statements:** Standardized diamond symbols indicating whether the substance is corrosive, flammable, toxic, or an oxidizer, accompanied by concise risk statements (e.g., "Causes severe skin burns and serious eye damage").
4. **Precautionary instructions:** Specific protective handling instructions (e.g., "Wear eye protection and gloves; use in well-ventilated space").
5. **Administrative metadata:** Date received, date opened or prepared, lot/batch number, and the name or initials of the teacher who prepared the solution.

Labels must be written with permanent, waterproof, chemical-resistant ink on adhesive vinyl or coated paper, covered with transparent chemical-resistant tape to protect against corrosive drips.

### Inventory and stock registers

Maintaining accurate stock registers is both an administrative statutory obligation and an educational necessity in Pakistani government and private schools (Kochhar). Efficient inventory management ensures that apparatus is available when needed for practicals, deters Pilferage, records accidental breakages, and provides an audited paper trail for government education departments.

Secondary school science laboratories must maintain three distinct official registers:

- **Permanent stock register:** Records durable, non-expendable assets that have a useful lifespan of multiple years, such as compound microscopes, analytical balances, retort stands, prisms, magnets, and large glassware like desiccators. Each item is assigned an accession number that matches the physical marking engraved or painted on the apparatus.
- **Consumable stock register:** Records materials that are consumed, exhausted, or transformed during experiments, such as chemical salts, acids, indicators, filter papers, glass tubing, delivery tubes, dry battery cells, and wooden splints.
- **Breakage and condemnation register:** Logs apparatus that is accidentally broken, worn out, or damaged beyond repair. Entries record the date, item description, cause of breakage, the name of the pupil or teacher involved, and whether replacement cost was recovered. Once per year, a formal board of survey condemns written-off items to adjust the permanent ledger balances.

Figure fig-U6-7 demonstrates a standard stock ledger layout, featuring mandatory tracking columns: Date of transaction, Item description and specifications, Voucher or requisition number, Quantity received, Quantity issued, Net balance on hand, and Teacher's initials.

#### Worked example: Logging transactions in the laboratory stock register

Madam Shahida receives a consignment from the District Education Office containing 24 borosilicate 250 mL beakers (Voucher VR-101) and 2.5 litres of 1.0 M hydrochloric acid (Voucher VR-104). Later in the week, she issues 6 beakers to Grade 8 pupils for an inquiry on heating solutions, during which one beaker suffers a thermal crack. She records these transactions systematically:

1. *Permanent register entry:* On 12 October, she logs the 24 beakers under "Permanent Stock", noting voucher VR-101, adding 24 to the previous zero balance, and storing them in Cupboard A.
2. *Consumable register entry:* On 15 October, she logs the 2.5 L of dilute acid in the "Consumable Stock" register under voucher VR-104.
3. *Issue logging:* On 22 October, she records 6 beakers issued under loan slip IS-008, reducing the active stock balance to 18 beakers.
4. *Breakage recording:* On 10 November, when one beaker is cracked by thermal shock, she records the broken item in the "Breakage Register" (BR-002), notes "Accidental thermal shock during heating; no negligence", signs the entry, and adjusts the active permanent balance to 17.

> **Common misconception:** "Labels can be postponed until the end of the term when there is more free time." When teachers prepare stock solutions and place unlabelled flasks on shelves planning to label them later, they frequently forget the exact identity or concentration within days. An unlabelled bottle becomes unusable and dangerous, requiring costly chemical disposal or risking severe laboratory poisoning.

## Activity: Conducting a simulated stock and labelling audit

Work in pairs. About 25 minutes.

1. Examine the following four laboratory storage items:
   - Container A: A plain amber glass bottle containing a clear liquid with a handwritten slip saying "Acid".
   - Container B: A cardboard box holding three glass prisms, two of which have chipped corners.
   - Container C: A plastic jar containing 500 g of white powder labelled "Copper Sulfate".
   - Container D: A glass bottle of 100 mL ethanol with no date or hazard symbol.
2. Identify the specific labelling or inventory defect in each item.
3. Draft a complete, GHS-compliant label for Container A, assuming it contains 0.5 M sulfuric acid prepared by Sir Tariq on 15 November 2026.
4. Create a sample stock register page on lined paper and enter transactions for receiving 10 new prisms and condemning the two chipped prisms.
5. Exchange your work with another pair and evaluate compliance with standard inventory formats.

## Summary

Accurate container labelling and systematic stock registers form the administrative backbone of science laboratory safety and accountability. Reagent labels must follow GHS standards, detailing full chemical identity, concentration, signal words, hazard pictograms, safety precautions, preparation dates, and the preparer's name. Laboratory inventory control requires three formal ledgers: permanent registers for durable apparatus, consumable registers for depleting supplies, and breakage registers for recording damaged items. Systematic inventory records ensure material readiness, prevent hazardous confusion, and maintain rigorous financial transparency.

Ask to review the school's permanent and consumable stock registers, noting when they were last updated and how breakages are documented. ## Summative task

You are appointed as the laboratory coordinator at a secondary school where no stock registers have been maintained for three years and numerous reagent containers lack labels. Write a 250-300 word operational rehabilitation strategy for the school principal. In your strategy, analyze the legal and safety liabilities of unlabelled reagents and undocumented inventory, propose a phased plan to audit and classify existing stock, detail the mandatory formats for permanent and consumable ledgers, and establish a strict protocol for labelling newly prepared solutions.
