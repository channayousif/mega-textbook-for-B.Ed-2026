# Topic 5.3 - Writing instructional objectives

## A real classroom situation

Madam Bilquis teaches Grade 6 General Science at a Government Girls High School in Larkana. While preparing her weekly lesson plan on "Photosynthesis and Plant Respiration," she writes the following three learning targets in her plan book:

1. "To understand the process of photosynthesis."
2. "To know the importance of plants for life on Earth."
3. "To teach pupils about the role of chlorophyll and sunlight."

During a routine professional review, the school headmistress, who holds a Master's degree in education, reads the entries and smiles gently. "Madam Bilquis, these statements express noble intentions, but they are not functional instructional objectives," she explains.

"Look at the third statement: 'to teach pupils' describes what *you* intend to do as an instructor, not what the *pupils* will learn. And consider the first two: 'to understand' and 'to know' describe hidden mental states. If a pupil in Class 6 nods her head and says 'Madam, I understand', how will you objectively verify whether she truly grasps the chemical conversion of water and carbon dioxide into glucose, or if she has simply memorized a slogan? How will you write an examination question that measures 'knowing' without demanding an observable action?"

Madam Bilquis realizes that until a learning target is phrased as an observable, measurable student performance, neither the teacher nor the learner can be sure when success has been achieved. This topic teaches future science teachers how to formulate clear, rigorous, and measurable Student Learning Outcomes (SLOs).

## Explanation

### Writing instructional objectives

In educational planning, a vital distinction exists between *educational aims*, *general goals*, and *instructional objectives*:

- **Educational aims:** Broad, long-range societal aspirations (for example, "to develop scientific literacy and rational thinking in future Pakistani citizens").
- **Curriculum goals:** Subject-level statements of intent covering a whole term or year (for example, "pupils will explore the diversity and interactions of living systems").
- **Instructional objectives (Student Learning Outcomes - SLOs):** Specific, operational statements describing what the learner will be able to perform, demonstrate, or produce by the end of a single instructional lesson (Mager, 1962; National Curriculum Council, 2023).

#### The components of an operational objective (The ABCD framework)

Robert Mager (1962) established that an objective is useful to the extent that it communicates an unambiguous picture of the intended learning outcome. A complete instructional objective answers four questions:

1. **Audience (A):** Who is the learner? (e.g., "Grade 6 pupils").
2. **Behaviour (B):** What observable action verb describes what the learner will do? The verb must describe an overt, inspectable performance (e.g., *identify*, *calculate*, *classify*, *predict*, *draw*), rather than an internal, invisible mental state (e.g., *know*, *understand*, *appreciate*).
3. **Condition (C):** Under what circumstances, materials, or restrictions will the behaviour take place? (e.g., "Given an unlabelled diagram of a plant leaf", "Using a beam balance and a set of standard masses", "Without referring to the textbook").
4. **Degree or Criterion (D):** What standard of performance indicates acceptable mastery? (e.g., "accurately identifying all four inputs and outputs", "to within 0.1 grams", "providing at least two valid scientific justifications").

#### Measurable action verbs across Bloom's cognitive levels

To prevent science lessons from dwelling exclusively on rote memorization, teachers formulate objectives across the revised Bloom's taxonomy:

- **Remembering:** *State, name, list, identify, label, define, recall.*
  - *Example:* "Pupils will identify the symbols of five common laboratory circuit components from flashcards."
- **Understanding:** *Describe, explain, paraphrase, summarize, distinguish, classify.*
  - *Example:* "Pupils will explain why stomata close during drought conditions in their own words."
- **Applying:** *Demonstrate, calculate, construct, operate, solve, use.*
  - *Example:* "Given a glass beaker, thermometer, and ice bath, pupils will determine the melting point of ice to within one degree Celsius."
- **Analyzing:** *Differentiate, compare, contrast, dissect, deduce, categorize.*
  - *Example:* "Given experimental data on plant growth under four light colors, pupils will deduce which wavelength yields the fastest rate of growth."
- **Evaluating:** *Justify, critique, appraise, defend, evaluate, judge.*
  - *Example:* "Pupils will evaluate two methods of purifying flood water and justify which method is safest for a rural village."
- **Creating:** *Design, formulate, plan, devise, construct, hypothesize.*
  - *Example:* "Using discarded plastic bottles and gravel, pupils will design a functional three-stage water filtration column."

**Worked example: Repairing vague science objectives.**
- *Vague:* "Pupils will understand electric circuits."
  - *Flaws:* 'Understand' cannot be observed directly; no conditions or criteria are specified.
  - *Measurable version:* "Given three wires, a 1.5V dry cell, a miniature bulb, and a switch, Grade 7 pupils will assemble a functional closed circuit that illuminates the bulb within five minutes."
- *Vague:* "The teacher will explain the water cycle."
  - *Flaws:* Describes teacher activity, not student performance.
  - *Measurable version:* "Grade 6 pupils will draw and correctly label the four main processes of the water cycle (evaporation, condensation, precipitation, transpiration) from memory."
- *Vague:* "Pupils will appreciate the danger of plastic waste."
  - *Flaws:* 'Appreciate' is an affective, unmeasurable verb.
  - *Measurable version:* "Pupils will write three evidence-based reasons explaining how single-use plastics harm marine life in the Arabian Sea."

> **Common misconception:** "Using the word 'understand' is fine as long as the teacher knows what it means." Many teachers defend vague objectives by saying, "I know what understanding looks like when I talk to the pupils." However, unwritten intuitions lead to unreliable assessment. Two teachers using the same objective, "understand friction", may test entirely different things: one might ask for the dictionary definition (mere recall), while the other asks pupils to calculate a coefficient of friction (analysis). Operational objectives guarantee that instruction, classroom learning activities, and examination questions remain tightly aligned.

## Activity: Rewriting science instructional objectives

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

1. Read the following four poorly drafted objectives taken from trainee teacher lesson plans:
   - Objective A: "Pupils will learn about acids and bases."
   - Objective B: "To show students how sound travels through solids."
   - Objective C: "Pupils will understand the structure of an animal cell."
   - Objective D: "To develop appreciation for biodiversity in Sindh's forests."
2. For each objective:
   - Identify the specific flaw (teacher-centred, unmeasurable verb, missing condition, or missing criterion).
   - Rewrite the objective as a fully formed SLO using the Audience, Condition, Behaviour, and Criterion (ABCD) model.
3. Exchange your rewritten objectives with another pair. Verify that every verb in your peer's sheet can be directly observed and scored on a rubric.

## Check your understanding

1. State the three essential components of an operational instructional objective according to Robert Mager's framework. *(Remember)*
2. Distinguish between an educational aim and a classroom instructional objective (SLO), providing one science example of each. *(Understand)*
3. A mentor teacher asks you to critique the following objective: "By the end of the period, pupils will thoroughly understand the respiratory system." Write a constructive critique and propose a revised objective at the *Analyze* level of Bloom's taxonomy. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Instructional objectives (SLOs) are the foundation of effective lesson planning and classroom assessment. Unlike broad curriculum aims, operational objectives specify what the student will be able to perform in observable, measurable terms. By applying Mager's criteria - defining the specific audience, observable action verb, learning condition, and degree of acceptable performance - teachers eliminate pedagogical ambiguity. Aligning action verbs with Bloom's cognitive taxonomy ensures that science lessons balance basic factual recall with higher-order inquiry, analysis, and problem-solving.

## Summative task

You are planning a 40-minute Grade 7 science lesson on "Thermal Energy and Heat Transfer (Conduction, Convection, Radiation)" aligned with the National Curriculum of Pakistan 2022-23. Formulate three distinct, complete instructional objectives for this lesson: one at the *Understand* level, one at the *Apply* level, and one at the *Analyze* level. For each objective, explicitly label its Condition, Observable Behaviour, and Criterion of Mastery, and explain how a teacher would evaluate student success during class.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1) | Secure (2) | Strong (3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Formulation of objectives | Uses vague or non-measurable verbs; components missing | Formulates three objectives with observable verbs and clear components | Formulates three flawless, highly operational objectives with explicitly labelled Condition, Behaviour, and Criterion |
| Cognitive alignment *(Analyze)* | All three objectives remain at the low recall or recognition level | Successfully distinguishes between Understand, Apply, and Analyze levels | Demonstrates sophisticated cognitive progression from conceptual understanding to real-world application and systemic analysis |
| Assessment coherence | Does not explain how objectives will be assessed in the classroom | Explains a general assessment check for each objective | Details concrete, feasible formative assessment techniques directly tied to each objective's criterion of mastery |
