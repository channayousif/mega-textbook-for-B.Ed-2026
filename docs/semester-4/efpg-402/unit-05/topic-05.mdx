# Topic 5.5 - Classroom management in science teaching

## A real classroom situation

Madam Tahira teaches Grade 8 General Science at a Government Girls High School in Badin. She plans an engaging hands-on investigation on "Plant Transpiration and Water Transport in Stems using Celery and Food Dye."

Her classroom is crowded: forty-six girls sit on narrow wooden benches designed for two pupils but holding four. At the start of the period, Madam Tahira places four glass beakers of red dye, three bottles of water, five scalpels, and several fresh celery stalks on her front demonstration table. She announces enthusiastically: "Girls, come to my table and take what you need for your groups!"

Instantly, disaster strikes. Twenty girls rush forward at the same moment, jostling in the narrow aisles between benches. In the scramble, a glass beaker of concentrated red dye is knocked over, shattering on the cement floor and splattering ink across three school bags and several uniforms. Two girls begin arguing loudly over who grabbed the sharp scissors first.

Madam Tahira is forced to shout: "Silence! Sit down immediately! Put everything down!" She spends the next eighteen minutes sweeping up broken glass, wiping dye with rags, and calming distressed pupils. When the dismissal bell rings, the celery stalks remain untouched on the table, no science was learned, and Madam Tahira sighs with deep despair: "Hands-on practical work is impossible in government schools. With forty-five pupils in a small room, it only causes dangerous chaos."

Yet, across Sindh, skilled science teachers successfully conduct active practicals in equally large, resource-constrained classrooms. What distinguishes a calm, productive science lesson from dangerous chaos is not the size of the room, but the presence of explicit classroom management routines.

## Explanation

### Classroom management in science teaching

Classroom management in science is fundamentally different from management in lecture-based subjects like history or languages. In a conventional classroom, management often means keeping pupils sitting quietly in rows, listening to the teacher, and writing in notebooks.

In science, learning requires activity: pupils must handle equipment, pour liquids, light burners, measure specimens, discuss observations in small groups, and move around the room (Wellington & Ireson, 2012; Yadav, 2002). Therefore, an effective science teacher does not aim for dead silence; rather, the goal is *purposeful, orderly activity*. Achieving this requires establishing clear routines long before equipment is unpacked.

#### The four pillars of science classroom management

1. **Cooperative group organization and assigned roles:**
   When pupils work in groups without assigned responsibilities, one dominant student does all the work, two socialize, and one wanders around causing disruptions. For every practical investigation, pupils should be organized into stable groups of four or five, with explicit, rotating responsibilities:
   - *Materials Manager:* The only pupil authorized to leave the group's desk to collect and return apparatus.
   - *Principal Investigator (Facilitator):* Reads experimental instructions, keeps the group focused on the task, and ensures everyone participates.
   - *Data Recorder (Scribe):* Accurately records measurements, observations, and tables in the group log.
   - *Safety and Clean-up Officer:* Ensures safety rules are obeyed during the trial and inspects the desk before dismissal.
2. **Material distribution and retrieval protocols (The Tray System):**
   Never invite the entire class to the front desk to fetch apparatus. Instead, use pre-assembled *investigation trays* (repurposed plastic washbasins, cardboard cartons, or shoe boxes). Before the period begins, the teacher places all necessary items for each group onto a single numbered tray. At the designated moment, only the designated *Materials Manager* from each group walks calmly to a side table to retrieve their tray. At the end of the lesson, the same manager returns the complete tray for inventory inspection.
3. **Auditory and non-verbal attention signals:**
   When forty pupils are clinking glassware, measuring temperatures, and conferring in groups, shouting "Quiet!" strains the teacher's voice and is rarely heard. Experienced science teachers institute an unmistakable, non-verbal signal:
   - *The Raised-Hand Countdown:* The teacher raises a hand with five fingers spread. Pupils who see it immediately raise their hand, stop talking, cease touching equipment, and turn toward the teacher. Within five seconds, the whole room falls silent without the teacher shouting a single word.
   - *The Chime or Double-Clap:* A small brass bell, triangle, or rhythmic double-clap that signals an immediate transition.
4. **Safety agreements and reserved clean-up buffers:**
   Practical science lessons must never run up against the dismissal bell. If a period is 40 minutes long:
   - 0–5 min: Hook, safety briefing, and task orientation.
   - 5–25 min: Hands-on exploration and data collection.
   - 25–33 min: Group reporting, concept explanation, and synthesis.
   - 33–40 min: Dedicated clean-up, material count, and desk inspection.
   The class is dismissed only after the teacher verifies that every scalpel, beaker, and lens is accounted for and all work areas are wiped dry.

#### Optimizing spatial layout in crowded classrooms

In many Pakistani secondary schools, fixed long wooden desks and benches restrict physical movement. As shown in figure fig-U5-10, teachers can optimize layout by pairing adjacent benches so that four pupils face each other across a shared working space, leaving the central aisle completely clear of school bags for safe passage.

**Worked example: Redesigning Madam Tahira's lesson.**
To prevent the chaos that occurred in Class 8, Madam Tahira implements three management adjustments:
1. *Tray prep:* During recess, she prepares eight plastic food containers, each holding one beaker, water, five drops of red dye, two celery stalks, and one plastic scalpel.
2. *Assigned roles:* She numbers the pupils in each bench cluster 1 to 4: Number 1 is Materials Manager; Number 2 is Investigator; Number 3 is Recorder; Number 4 is Clean-up Officer.
3. *Controlled movement:* She instructs: "Number 1s from Tables 1 to 4, please collect your group trays from the side shelf. Number 1s from Tables 5 to 8, wait until they return."
The materials reach all desks in two quiet minutes with zero spills or broken glass.

> **Common misconception:** "An orderly classroom is a completely silent classroom." Many school heads and inspectors mistakenly evaluate teacher competence by whether a classroom is quiet enough to hear a pin drop. In genuine scientific inquiry, total silence indicates passive, disengaged students who are listening to a lecture or copying from a board. When children are formulating hypotheses, debating observations, and analyzing anomalous data, there is a natural hum of productive cognitive dialogue. Order is demonstrated not by silence, but by purposeful focus, adherence to safety rules, and prompt compliance with teacher signals.

## Activity: Designing a practical lesson routine protocol

Work in small groups of three or four. About 25 minutes.

1. You are assigned to teach a Grade 7 science practical: "Investigating the Electrical Conductivity of Five Common Household Materials" (copper wire, plastic spoon, pencil graphite, iron nail, rubber eraser) in a crowded class of 45 pupils.
2. Develop a comprehensive one-page *Management and Safety Protocol*:
   - Specify the four group roles and write a one-sentence job description for each.
   - Describe your exact procedure for distributing the batteries, bulbs, and test objects in under three minutes without aisle congestion.
   - State the specific auditory or visual attention signal you will use to pause the class when changing tasks.
   - Outline your 5-minute end-of-period clean-up routine.
3. Present your protocol to another group. Critique each other's procedures for safety vulnerabilities and time efficiency.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the four essential group roles in cooperative science practical work and state the primary duty of each. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why the "pre-assembled tray system" is superior to inviting pupils to fetch materials individually from the teacher's demonstration desk. *(Understand)*
3. A science teacher frequently shouts over pupils during laboratory work, but the class becomes increasingly noisy and disobedient. Diagnose the underlying management failure and recommend two specific alternative techniques. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Classroom management in science is an active, structured discipline designed to channel student curiosity into safe, collaborative empirical investigations. In large, resource-constrained secondary classrooms, effective practical work depends on four foundational routines: dividing pupils into small cooperative teams with defined rotating roles; using pre-assembled tray systems and designated stations for equipment distribution; implementing clear non-verbal attention signals; and strictly reserving the final minutes of class for clean-up and apparatus inventory. By replacing aimless free-for-alls with explicit procedural expectations, science teachers transform potential classroom chaos into an orderly environment for authentic discovery.

## Try this at your practicum school

Observe a science practical or demonstration at your practicum school.
- Count how many minutes elapsed between the teacher announcing the practical and every pupil beginning active work.
- Was there a traffic bottleneck at the front desk?
- Were school bags placed in aisles where pupils could trip over them?
- Did pupils clean their working stations before leaving?
Share your observations in your practicum reflective journal and outline three procedural rules you will establish when you lead your first practical lesson.

## Summative task

A fellow trainee teacher approaches you in frustration: "I tried conducting a group practical on seed germination and soil drainage with my Grade 6 class of forty-four students, but they spilled water everywhere, fought over the measuring cylinders, and we ran out of time before anyone recorded data. Practical science is simply unmanageable in our schools!" Write a 250-300 word constructive action plan for your peer. Your response must systematically address room arrangement, role allocation, apparatus distribution, and time structuring, explaining how these specific management interventions will prevent future failure.
