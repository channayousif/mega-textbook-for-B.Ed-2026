# Topic 5.4 - Questioning and explanation skills

## A real classroom situation

Sir Niaz teaches Grade 7 General Science at a Government Comprehensive High School in Sukkur. During a lesson on "Evaporation and Cooling," he stands before forty-eight boys. Wanting to engage his pupils, he begins with a question:

"Boys, what is evaporation?"

Before anyone can raise a hand - barely one second after speaking - Sir Niaz answers his own question: "It is the change of a liquid into a gas at any temperature below its boiling point! Remember that!"

He then turns to the chalkboard and launches into an uninterrupted fifteen-minute monologue. He writes molecular definitions, draws arrows representing escaping water molecules, and recites factors affecting evaporation rate. At the end of his talk, he turns to the class and asks: "Do you all understand?" The boys respond in unison: "Yes, sir!"

Satisfied, Sir Niaz decides to test understanding. He calls on Danish, a boy sitting in the third row: "Danish, why does a puddle of rainwater on the school ground dry up much faster on a windy day than on a calm day?"

Danish stands up, stares at his desk, and remains completely silent. Sir Niaz sighs: "Danish, you were daydreaming while I was explaining! Sit down and pay attention."

In reality, Danish was not daydreaming. Sir Niaz's teaching suffered from two classic instructional flaws: he allowed zero wait-time for pupils to process thoughts, and his explanation was a disjointed delivery of abstract definitions without relatable analogies or intermediate comprehension checks. This topic examines how science teachers develop effective questioning techniques and structured explanations.

## Explanation

### Questioning skills in science

Questions are the primary engine of intellectual engagement in a science classroom. Skillful teachers use questions not simply to test whether pupils memorized textbook paragraphs, but to diagnose misconceptions, guide observations, stimulate causal reasoning, and encourage self-reflection (Yadav, 2002).

#### Levels of questions in science

1. **Lower-order (convergent / recall) questions:** These require retrieval of previously learned facts, names, or formulas. They generally have a single expected response.
   - *Example:* "What is the standard unit of electrical resistance?"
   - *Pedagogical role:* Useful during the opening minutes of a lesson to activate prior knowledge or establish common definitions.
2. **Higher-order (divergent / process / evaluative) questions:** These require pupils to analyze data, identify patterns, predict outcomes, or evaluate evidence. They invite multiple reasoned answers.
   - *Example:* "How would the rate of transpiration change if a farmer planted trees in an arid desert versus near a river bank, and why?"
   - *Pedagogical role:* Stimulates deep cognitive processing, builds scientific inquiry habits, and reveals the conceptual models pupils hold.

#### The power of wait-time (Rowe, 1974)

Educational researcher Mary Budd Rowe discovered that in typical science classrooms, teachers wait an average of less than one second after asking a question before calling on a pupil, repeating the question, or answering it themselves. Rowe (1974) identified two distinct wait-times that fundamentally transform classroom dialogue:

- **Wait-time 1 (Pause after the question):** The teacher poses a question to the whole class, and deliberately pauses for 3 to 5 seconds before selecting a pupil to answer. This quiet pause allows every child to mentally process the question and formulate a response.
- **Wait-time 2 (Pause after the pupil responds):** When a pupil finishes answering, the teacher deliberately pauses for another 3 to 5 seconds before speaking, praising, or calling on someone else. This second pause gives the speaker time to elaborate, self-correct, or provide evidence, and encourages classmates to respond thoughtfully.

*Documented effects of 3–5 seconds wait-time:* Research shows that increasing wait-time dramatically improves pupil responses: answer lengths increase threefold, unsolicited but appropriate pupil contributions rise, speculative guessing drops, weaker and shy pupils participate more frequently, and student-to-student interaction increases (Rowe, 1974).

#### Effective questioning techniques

- **Pose-Pause-Pounce-Bounce:** Pose the question to the entire class; pause for 3 to 5 seconds (Wait-time 1); call on a specific pupil ("pounce"); then invite a second pupil to build upon or critique the answer ("bounce").
- **Handling incorrect or incomplete answers:** Avoid blunt rejection ("Wrong, sit down"). Instead, use probing and scaffolding: "What part of your observation made you think that?", or "That is a common observation; let us look at the temperature reading again together."

### Explanation skills in science

Scientific concepts often involve invisible entities (atoms, magnetic fields, microbes) and abstract processes (photosynthesis, convection currents). An effective scientific explanation bridges the gap between everyday perceptual experience and formal scientific models.

#### Five stages of a structured science explanation

1. **Orient and link to prior knowledge:** Begin with a concrete hook or familiar phenomenon that pupils encounter daily in Sindh (e.g., how an earthen water pitcher, or *matka*, keeps water cool in summer).
2. **Introduce concept with an analogy:** Use an accessible physical analogy to give pupils a mental picture (e.g., comparing heat transfer in a solid to students in a crowded line passing a bucket from hand to hand without moving their feet).
3. **Present logical steps sequentially:** Unfold the scientific mechanism one step at a time, highlighting cause-and-effect relationships and writing key scientific terms on the board.
4. **Formative check for understanding:** Pause to check comprehension using a quick probe, concept question, or mini-whiteboard response before moving forward.
5. **Synthesize and summarize:** Revisit the original everyday phenomenon and summarize the scientific rule or principle in clear, concise language.

**Worked example: Repairing Sir Niaz's evaporation explanation.**
- *Ineffective monologue:* Sir Niaz simply shouts: "Evaporation causes cooling! High wind takes away vapor!"
- *Structured explanation:*
  1. *Orient:* "Put a drop of surgical spirit or hand sanitizer on your wrist. What do you feel?" (Pupils: "It feels cold!").
  2. *Analogy:* "Imagine water molecules in a puddle are like energetic children in a playground. The fastest, most energetic runners leap over the playground boundary first. What happens to the average energy of the children left behind?"
  3. *Logical steps:* The most energetic liquid molecules break free into the air as vapour; the liquid left behind has lower average kinetic energy; lower kinetic energy means a lower temperature.
  4. *Check:* "If wind blows away the vapour above the puddle immediately, does it make it easier or harder for more energetic molecules to leap out?" (Pupils pause, think, and explain why wind speeds up evaporation).
  5. *Synthesize:* Evaporation is a cooling process dependent on surface area, temperature, and air movement.

> **Common misconception:** "Good science questions always have one quick, right answer." Many teachers believe that an efficient science class is one where the teacher fires rapid questions and pupils shout instant one-word answers (e.g., "What is the speed of light?", "300,000 km per second!"). While rapid drill has limited value for memorizing constants, genuine scientific inquiry requires deliberative questions that demand reasoning, interpretation, and justification. If questions can always be answered in one second, pupils are only being trained in rote recall, not scientific inquiry.

## Activity: Micro-teaching questioning with wait-time

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

1. Design a 3-minute mini-lesson on one of the following secondary science concepts:
   - Why do iron nails sink in water, while large iron ships float?
   - Why does breathing quicken when running?
   - Why does a solar oven need a black interior and reflective flaps?
2. Partner A acts as the science teacher; Partner B acts as a Grade 7 pupil.
3. Partner A conducts the mini-lesson, deliberately implementing:
   - One higher-order divergent question.
   - At least 4 seconds of Wait-time 1 before calling on Partner B.
   - At least 3 seconds of Wait-time 2 after Partner B speaks before responding.
   - A probing follow-up question ("What evidence supports that?").
4. Switch roles. After both partners complete the exercise, write a brief self-critique: Did pausing feel uncomfortable at first? How did the pause change the quality of the student's answer?

## Summary

Questioning and explanation are two core pedagogical skills that define the quality of science instruction. Moving beyond low-level factual recall, effective science teachers pose higher-order divergent and evaluative questions that cultivate scientific reasoning. By implementing Mary Budd Rowe's wait-time (pausing 3 to 5 seconds after asking a question and again after a student responds), teachers dramatically enhance pupil cognitive participation, response depth, and equity. Scientific explanations succeed when structured logically: connecting to prior knowledge, employing culturally grounded analogies, unfolding step-by-step, checking for understanding formatively, and synthesizing conclusions.
