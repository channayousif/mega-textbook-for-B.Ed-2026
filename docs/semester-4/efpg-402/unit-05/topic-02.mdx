# Topic 5.2 - Types of lesson plans

## A real classroom situation

Sir Jameel is a newly appointed science teacher at a Government Boys High School in Hyderabad. Preparing to teach Grade 8 General Science on chemical reactions, specifically combination and decomposition reactions, he seeks guidance from two senior colleagues.

Master Abdul Ghaffar, who has taught for twenty-five years, hands him an old notebook containing a classic Herbartian plan. "Follow the five steps," he advises. "Prepare the boys with three questions, present the reaction equations on the chalkboard, compare them, state the rule, and give them two textbook questions to solve."

Meanwhile, Madam Shaista, the school's academic coordinator, points him toward the contemporary constructivist framework promoted by the National Curriculum of Pakistan 2022-23: the BSCS 5E instructional model. "If you simply write equations on the board, the boys will merely memorize formulas without understanding the molecular changes," she explains. "Let them observe vinegar reacting with baking soda, explain what gas is produced, and then introduce the chemical terms."

Sir Jameel realizes that choosing a lesson plan format is not a matter of visual layout; it reflects how a teacher believes children construct scientific knowledge. This topic examines the three primary lesson planning models used in science education: Herbartian, Bloom's objective-based, and the 5E inquiry cycle.

## Explanation

### Herbartian lesson plan

The Herbartian lesson planning approach derives from the educational philosophy of Johann Friedrich Herbart (1776–1841) and his followers. Herbart posited that learning occurs through *apperception*, a psychological process where new sensory experiences are assimilated into existing mental representations (Yadav, 2002). In South Asian teacher-education traditions, this approach was formalized into five distinct instructional steps:

1. **Preparation (or Introduction):** The teacher arouses student interest and tests previous knowledge through three to four introductory questions. The step concludes with the *Statement of Aim*, where the teacher formally announces the day's topic and writes it on the board.
2. **Presentation:** The teacher presents the new scientific content systematically. The teacher demonstrates phenomena, draws diagrams, explains terms, and delivers information in small, sequential segments.
3. **Comparison and Association:** The teacher guides pupils to compare the new facts with previously learned ideas, identifying points of similarity, difference, and mutual connection.
4. **Generalization:** Through targeted questioning, the teacher helps pupils synthesize the observed facts into a general scientific principle, law, or rule (for example, deriving that "all metals expand when heated").
5. **Application:** Pupils apply the newly established generalization to solve unfamiliar problems, answer practical questions, or complete exercises. A brief *Recapitulation* checks retention before assigning homework.

*Pedagogical assessment:* The Herbartian plan provides logical order and clarity, making it comfortable for novice teachers and effective for transmitting structured factual content. However, its primary drawback is its teacher-centred transmission orientation: pupils remain largely passive recipients of teacher demonstrations rather than active investigators.

### Bloom's objective-based lesson plan

In the mid-twentieth century, educational theorists shifted focus from teacher inputs to measurable student outcomes, inspired by Benjamin Bloom's *Taxonomy of Educational Objectives*. Bloom's objective-based lesson planning framework is organized around three interdependent components:

- **Input (Instructional Objectives):** Clear behavioural objectives stated in terms of observable pupil performance across cognitive domains (Remembering, Understanding, Applying, Analyzing, Evaluating, Creating).
- **Process (Learning Experiences):** Differentiated learning activities, teacher questions, and student tasks purposefully selected to evoke the specific cognitive level demanded by each objective.
- **Output (Evaluation):** Criterion-referenced assessment items that directly measure whether each stated behavioural objective was achieved during the period.

*Pedagogical assessment:* Bloom's framework transformed lesson planning by making learning outcomes explicit and measurable. It ensures that science lessons do not remain trapped at low-level memorization of terms, but intentionally demand higher-order analysis and problem-solving.

### The 5E model lesson plan

The 5E instructional model, developed by the Biological Sciences Curriculum Study (BSCS) team led by Rodger Bybee, is an inquiry-based, constructivist learning cycle grounded in cognitive psychology (Bybee et al., 2006). Rather than transmitting scientific laws from the teacher to the student, the 5E model guides pupils to build, refine, and restructure their own mental models through five sequential phases:

1. **Engage:** The teacher captures pupils' interest, accesses prior knowledge, and exposes existing misconceptions through a perplexing demonstration, question, or short real-world scenario. No formal definitions are given at this stage.
2. **Explore:** Working in cooperative groups with hands-on materials, pupils investigate phenomena, manipulate apparatus, record observations, and test predictions. The teacher acts as an inquiry coach, circulating and asking probing questions, but does not provide direct answers.
3. **Explain:** First, pupils articulate their observations and construct preliminary explanations in their own words based on their exploratory data. Next, the teacher formally introduces scientific vocabulary, models, and formal theories to clarify and connect the students' explanations.
4. **Elaborate:** Pupils apply and extend the newly acquired concept to a fresh, unfamiliar situation or community problem. This deepens conceptual understanding and prevents compartmentalized memorization.
5. **Evaluate:** Both the teacher and pupils assess understanding and mastery of scientific inquiry skills. This includes ongoing formative self-assessment during investigations as well as summative performance tasks.

**Worked example: Teaching "Acids, Bases, and Indicators" across models.**
- *Herbartian plan:* Sir Jameel defines acid and base on the board. He demonstrates dipping red and blue litmus into lemon juice and soap water. He writes the rule: "Acids turn blue litmus red; bases turn red litmus blue." Pupils copy the rule into their notebooks and solve three workbook questions.
- *5E plan:* Sir Jameel places slices of lemon, bitter gourd, soap solution, and vinegar on group trays without labels. In the *Engage* phase, pupils taste small safe samples (lemon vs water) or observe color changes with turmeric paste. In the *Explore* phase, groups test each mystery substance with homemade turmeric paper and strips of red and blue litmus paper, recording color changes in a matrix. In the *Explain* phase, pupils report that turmeric turns reddish-brown in soap but stays yellow in lemon; Sir Jameel introduces the terms *acidic*, *alkaline (basic)*, and *pH indicator*. In the *Elaborate* phase, pupils test canal water and soil runoff from a nearby farm. In the *Evaluate* phase, pupils justify whether wood ash can neutralize an acidic soil spill.

> **Common misconception:** "In the 5E model, the teacher never explains anything and leaves pupils to discover all science on their own." Some teachers mistakenly believe that inquiry means total unguided discovery, where the teacher remains silent. This is incorrect. In the 5E model, the teacher's explanation is essential, but its *timing* is changed. In traditional teaching, explanation comes first before pupils have any sensory experience. In the 5E cycle, the explanation comes in Phase 3 (*Explain*), *after* pupils have explored the physical evidence and wrestled with their observations. The teacher then provides precision, scientific terminology, and theoretical synthesis.

## Activity: Analyzing lesson planning models

Work in small groups of three. About 25 minutes.

1. Select one Grade 6 science topic from the National Curriculum of Pakistan (e.g., "Separation of Insoluble Solids from Liquids").
2. Assign one member to sketch a brief outline of the lesson using the Herbartian steps, one using Bloom's objective-based framework, and one using the 5E inquiry cycle.
3. Compare the three outlines:
   - At what exact minute does the pupil handle materials or actively think?
   - How does each model handle student errors and misconceptions?
   - Which model is best suited for resource-constrained schools where low-cost materials must be utilized?
4. Synthesize your findings into a one-paragraph recommendation for your placement school's science department.

## Summary

Different lesson planning frameworks reflect contrasting views of human learning. The classical Herbartian five-step model (Preparation, Presentation, Comparison, Generalization, Application) organizes instructional delivery systematically around the teacher's exposition. Bloom's objective-based framework ties classroom learning experiences directly to observable, measurable cognitive behaviours. The modern BSCS 5E model (Engage, Explore, Explain, Elaborate, Evaluate) embeds constructivist inquiry into science teaching, allowing pupils to investigate physical phenomena before formal scientific terminology is introduced. Aligning with the National Curriculum of Pakistan 2022-23, science teachers must master the 5E model to nurture active scientific inquiry and critical thinking.
