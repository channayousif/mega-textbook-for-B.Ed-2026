# Topic 1.2 - Science as process, product and attitude

## A real classroom situation

Sir Asif's Grade 7 class in Mirpurkhas is studying seed germination. On Monday each group wrapped ten gram seeds in a wet cloth and kept them in a warm corner. By Thursday most groups count eight or nine sprouted seeds. Hira's group counts only three. Hira quietly asks Sir Asif, "Can we write eight? Everyone else got eight, and three looks wrong."

Sir Asif sees three kinds of science in this one moment. The groups have been *doing* science: observing, counting, recording. They are learning a piece of scientific *knowledge*: seeds need water, warmth and air to germinate. And Hira's question is about scientific *attitude*: will she report what she saw, or what she expected? How Sir Asif answers decides which kind of science his pupils will remember.

## Explanation

### Science as a process (process skills)

Science as a **process** means the ways scientists find things out. The separate abilities used in finding out are called **process skills**. Pakistan's National Curriculum for Science, Grades 4-8, calls this strand "Scientific Enquiry" and expects pupils to build these skills step by step (National Curriculum Council [NCC], 2023):

- By the end of Grade 5, pupils ask questions, use equipment, take and record measurements, and draw a conclusion "informed by reasoning".
- By the end of Grade 8, they also check whether a hypothesis is testable, make predictions, describe trends and patterns in results, and suggest improvements to an experiment.

The curriculum also names five types of enquiry that pupils should know: observing over time, identifying and classifying, comparing and contrasting, fair testing, and research by finding information (NCC, 2023). A **fair test** is an experiment in which only one thing is changed and everything else is kept the same. Two pots of seedlings, one in sunlight and one in shade, with the same soil and the same water, make a fair test of the effect of light.

A common way to show the process is the loop in Figure fig-U1-3: observe, ask a question, form a hypothesis, test, conclude and communicate. Use it as a guide, not as a recipe. Textbooks often present "the scientific method" as a fixed four or five step procedure, but real scientists move back and forth, and there is no single method that all of science follows (Hepburn & Andersen, 2015/2026). A pupil who skips back from "conclude" to "form a new hypothesis" is doing science well, not breaking a rule.

**Worked example: the process skills in Sir Asif's lesson.** Wrapping the seeds is *planning a test*. Counting sprouted seeds is *observing and measuring*. Writing "8 out of 10" in a table is *recording*. Asking why Hira's group got only three is *questioning*. Guessing "maybe their cloth dried out" is *forming a hypothesis*. Re-doing the test with a cloth kept wet is *testing* that hypothesis. One short lesson can exercise six process skills if the teacher lets pupils do the thinking.

### Science as a product (facts, concepts, laws, theories)

Science as a **product** means the knowledge that the process produces. It has four main kinds, from the most specific to the most general.

1. **Fact.** A direct observation that anyone can repeat and check. "Gram seeds kept wet and warm sprout within a few days." "Pure water boils at about 100 °C at sea level."
2. **Concept.** An idea that groups many facts under one name. "Germination", "evaporation" and "mammal" are concepts. A concept lets pupils think about many cases at once.
3. **Law.** A statement of a pattern that holds every time, often written as a rule or a formula. It says *what* happens. For example, the law of reflection: light bounces off a mirror at the same angle at which it arrives.
4. **Theory.** A well-tested explanation of *why* things happen, which ties many facts, concepts and laws together. The particle theory of matter, taught in Grade 6, explains why solids keep their shape, why liquids flow and why gases spread (NCC, 2023).

Laws and theories are words with very specific meanings in science (Halai, 2003). A law describes a pattern; a theory explains it. A theory does not "grow up" into a law with more evidence; they do different jobs.

> **Common misconception:** "A scientific theory is just a guess." In everyday talk "theory" can mean a hunch: "My theory is that the bus is late because of rain." In science the hunch is a *hypothesis*, a possible answer still to be tested. A *theory* is the opposite of a guess: an explanation that has survived many tests by many scientists. The germ theory of disease is a theory, and it is the reason we boil water and vaccinate children. Teach pupils to say "hypothesis" for a testable guess and keep "theory" for a tested explanation.

Teaching only the product, a habit Schwab called a "rhetoric of conclusions" (Halai, 2003), leaves pupils with answers but no idea how anyone found them.

### Science as an attitude (curiosity, open-mindedness, honesty)

Science as an **attitude** means the habits of mind that scientists bring to their work. A **scientific attitude** is a settled way of thinking and behaving that respects evidence. Three attitudes are central.

- **Curiosity**: the wish to know why and how. A pupil who asks "Why did our seeds not sprout?" instead of hiding the result is curious.
- **Open-mindedness**: willingness to change your view when the evidence shows you were wrong, and to listen to other people's ideas. A group that changes its conclusion after hearing another group's results is open-minded.
- **Honesty**: reporting what you actually observed, including results that look "wrong". Science depends on honest records, because other people build on them.

Other scientific attitudes include *objectivity* (not letting your wishes change your judgement), *healthy scepticism* (asking for evidence before believing), and *perseverance* (trying again after a failed experiment). The values of objectivity and reproducibility sit behind every account of how science works (Hepburn & Andersen, 2015/2026).

Back to Hira. If Sir Asif lets her write "8", he teaches that results should match expectations. If he says, "Write three. That is your evidence. Now let's find out why," he teaches honesty and curiosity, and the class gets a better experiment.

**How the three faces fit together.** Figure fig-U1-4 shows process, product and attitude as three faces of one thing. Each needs the others. Without honest attitudes, the process gives false products. Without the process, the product is just something to believe because the book says so.

## Activity: Three faces of one lesson

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. Choose one Grade 4 to 8 topic from the National Curriculum, for example "Balanced diet" (Grade 6) or "Magnetism" (Grade 6).
2. Divide your group into three roles: Process, Product and Attitude. The fourth member is the recorder.
3. Each role-holder proposes two things a teacher could do in one 40-minute lesson to build their face of science. The Process holder names the process skills; the Product holder names the fact, concept, law or theory; the Attitude holder names the attitude and the moment it would be needed.
4. The recorder combines the ideas into a single lesson outline. Check together: does the lesson give time to all three faces? Swap outlines with another group and label each step of their outline P (process), K (product) or A (attitude).

## Check your understanding

1. List four process skills from the National Curriculum's Scientific Enquiry strand. *(Remember)*
2. Explain the difference between a law and a theory, with one example of each. *(Understand)*
3. A pupil says, "The germ theory is only a theory, so it is just a guess." Explain, in two sentences, what is wrong with this use of the word "theory". *(Understand)*
4. Classify each statement as process, product or attitude: (a) "Pupils measured the length of the shadow every hour"; (b) "Gases spread to fill their container"; (c) "Ali admitted his group had forgotten to water one pot". *(Apply)*

## Summary

Science has three faces. As a process, it is a set of enquiry skills such as observing, measuring, hypothesising and fair testing, used flexibly rather than as a fixed recipe. As a product, it is knowledge in the form of facts, concepts, laws and theories, where a theory is a tested explanation, not a guess. As an attitude, it is curiosity, open-mindedness and honesty. A science lesson that builds only one face gives pupils a broken picture of science.

## Try this at your practicum school

Observe one science lesson. Divide a page into three columns: Process, Product, Attitude. Every five minutes, note what the pupils are doing and put a tick in the column it belongs to. At the end, count the ticks. Discuss with your cooperating teacher one small change that would add time to the weakest column.

## Summative task

Read this account of a Grade 6 lesson on "Matter as particles".

> The teacher writes the definition of a particle on the board. Pupils copy it. She then puts a drop of ink into a glass of still water, and the pupils watch the colour spread. She asks, "Why did the ink spread?" One pupil says, "Because the water particles were moving." Another says, "Because the ink is lighter." The teacher writes "particles move all the time" and moves on to the next topic.

Write 200-250 words. Identify which parts of the lesson build science as process, as product and as attitude. Analyse which face is weakest and why. Then propose two changes to the lesson that would strengthen that face.
