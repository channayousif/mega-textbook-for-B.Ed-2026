# Topic 1.5 - Role of science in modern society

## A real classroom situation

It is polio campaign week in a village near Thatta. A vaccination team goes from door to door with drops for every child under five. That afternoon Zainab, a Grade 8 pupil, tells her science teacher, Sir Ghulam Mustafa, that two families in her street sent the team away. "My neighbour says the drops are not needed because her children look healthy," Zainab says. "Who is right?"

Sir Ghulam Mustafa realises that the question is not only about polio. A vaccine is the result of centuries of science, yet whether it protects a child in Thatta depends on what one mother understands about science. That is the role of science in modern society, and it is why the science teacher matters.

## Explanation

### Role of science in modern society

Science shapes almost everything a Pakistani family does in a day: the clean water they drink, the seed the farmer plants, the electricity that runs the fan and the phone in their pocket. Pakistan's National Curriculum asks pupils to "recognise the importance of science and technology to solve everyday problems" and to describe how science is used in their local area (National Curriculum Council [NCC], 2023, pp. 6, 35). Figure fig-U1-9 shows four areas of daily life where this is easy to see.

**Health.** Knowing that germs cause disease and that the body can learn to fight them led to vaccines. Pakistan's Expanded Programme on Immunization was launched in 1978 to protect children against tuberculosis, polio, diphtheria, whooping cough, tetanus and measles (Federal Directorate of Immunization, n.d.). New vaccines have been added since, including typhoid conjugate vaccine, introduced first in Sindh in 2019. Immunizing children with these vaccines may prevent up to 17% of child deaths in Pakistan (Federal Directorate of Immunization, n.d.). The National Curriculum links directly to this: Grade 5 pupils learn how to prevent the spread of diseases such as polio and COVID-19, and Grade 7 pupils learn how immunity develops over time (NCC, 2023).

**Agriculture.** Plant biology and soil chemistry give farmers better seed and fertiliser. Biotechnology, the use of living cells to make products, can produce crops with more nutrition. The National Curriculum asks Grade 8 pupils to discuss uses of biotechnology "in the Pakistani context" and gives the example of raising the iron content of rice (NCC, 2023, p. 32).

**Energy.** Solar panels now power homes, shops and tube wells across Pakistan. Grade 6 pupils compare renewable and non-renewable energy sources and build a small solar oven or use a solar panel to run a fan (NCC, 2023).

**Communication.** Mobile phones, radio and the internet are built on science about electricity and waves. They carry useful information, such as weather warnings for farmers, and also false rumours.

**Science brings harm as well as benefit.** The same science that gives us fertiliser and plastic also gives us polluted canals and choked drains. The National Curriculum asks pupils to discuss how science and technology "can have positive and negative environmental effects locally and globally" (NCC, 2023, p. 6). Science itself does not decide how it is used. People do, and they decide better when they understand the science.

**Science and the citizen.** This is the deepest role of science in modern society. Citizens make decisions that need scientific understanding: whether to vaccinate a child, which rumour to believe, how to use water in a drought. Zainab's neighbour judged by appearance ("my children look healthy"). A scientifically literate citizen knows that a healthy-looking child can still catch polio, and that vaccines work by preparing the body *before* the disease arrives.

**Worked example: tracing one advance from idea to daily life.** Use four questions to trace any scientific advance into daily life. Here they are applied to vaccination.

1. *What is the scientific idea?* The body's defence system can learn to recognise a germ and fight it faster the next time.
2. *What technology came from it?* Vaccines, and the cold boxes that keep them safe on the way to villages.
3. *How did it reach daily life in Pakistan?* Through the national immunization programme from 1978, adding new vaccines over the years (Figure fig-U1-10).
4. *What decision does it ask of citizens?* Whether to vaccinate, which depends on understanding the idea in step 1.

Use the same four questions with pupils for solar panels, mobile phones or improved seed.

> **Common misconception:** "Science is something only scientists need." Pupils often think science matters only for those who become doctors or engineers. In fact, every adult makes science-based decisions about health, food, energy and the environment. In Sindh, only 61.1% of children under two had received the full course of vaccinations in the survey the immunization programme reports, against 76.1% nationally (Federal Directorate of Immunization, n.d.). Behind each missed dose is a family decision. This is why the aim of school science is scientific literacy for *all* pupils (Topic 1.3).

## Activity: From idea to daily life

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. Each group takes one advance: solar panels, mobile phones, oral rehydration salts (ORS) or improved wheat seed.
2. Answer the four questions from the worked example together on chart paper. Each member is responsible for one question, but the group must agree on every answer.
3. Add a fifth box: "One harm or risk, and how science can help reduce it."
4. Display the charts. Walk round and add one sticky note to another group's chart: a question a Grade 8 pupil might ask about that advance.

## Check your understanding

1. Name four areas of daily life in Pakistan that science shapes, with one example each. *(Remember)*
2. Explain, in two or three sentences, why a technology can bring both benefit and harm. *(Understand)*
3. Apply the four tracing questions to the electric fan in your classroom. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Science shapes health, agriculture, energy and communication in Pakistan, from childhood vaccines to solar-powered tube wells. Its uses can bring harm as well as benefit, and people, not science, decide how it is used. Because every citizen makes decisions that need scientific understanding, the role of science in modern society is also the reason school science must reach every pupil, not only future scientists.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can describe how science shapes four areas of daily life in Pakistan.
- [ ] I can trace a scientific advance from idea to daily use with the four questions.
- [ ] I can weigh the benefits and harms of a technology using evidence.
- [ ] I can explain why every citizen, not only scientists, needs to understand science.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask a Grade 6 to 8 class to list, in five minutes, everything they used since waking up that depends on science. Group their answers into health, agriculture, energy, communication and other. Note which area pupils mention most and which they miss, and record one surprising answer.

## Summative task

A Grade 8 pupil writes in her notebook: "Science has made life better, so all new technology is good."

Write 200-250 words in reply, as her teacher. Use one example from health and one from agriculture or energy in Pakistan. Analyse the benefits and the harms of each example, and explain what a scientifically literate citizen would need to know to make a good decision about it.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1) | Secure (2) | Strong (3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Use of examples | Examples are general or not from Pakistan | Two relevant Pakistani examples | Two well-chosen Pakistani examples described accurately |
| Benefits and harms weighed *(Analyze/Evaluate)* | Lists only benefits or only harms | Gives a benefit and a harm for each example | Weighs benefits against harms for each example and reaches a reasoned conclusion about the pupil's claim |
| Citizen's understanding | Not addressed | Names something a citizen should know | Explains clearly which scientific idea a citizen needs and how it changes the decision |
