# Unit 1 Assessment: Nature and Importance of Science

## Unit summary

Science is both a body of knowledge about the natural world and the way that knowledge is built and checked. Scientific knowledge is empirical, testable, tentative yet reliable, cumulative and self-correcting, and it is made by creative people all over the world. Judging a claim means asking what evidence supports it and whether it could be tested, which is what separates science from untested common sense and superstition (Topic 1.1).

Science has three faces. As a process it is a set of enquiry skills, such as observing, measuring, hypothesising and fair testing, used flexibly rather than as a fixed recipe. As a product it is knowledge in the form of facts, concepts, laws and theories, where a theory is a well-tested explanation and not a guess. As an attitude it is curiosity, open-mindedness and honesty (Topic 1.2).

Teaching science has long-term aims and short-term objectives. Pakistan's National Curriculum 2022-23 for Science, Grades 4-8, points to six aims: understanding concepts, enquiry skills, scientific attitudes, applying science to daily life, understanding science in society and the environment, and preparing for further study and work. Together they build scientific literacy. Objectives turn these aims into measurable lesson outcomes (Topic 1.3).

Science education builds intellectual, moral and social values. Science and values are linked, because science depends on honesty and objectivity and its uses raise questions of right and wrong (Topic 1.4). Finally, science shapes health, agriculture, energy and communication in Pakistan, for harm as well as benefit. Because every citizen makes decisions that need scientific understanding, school science must reach every pupil, not only future scientists (Topic 1.5).

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. Which characteristic of science is shown when scientists replace an old explanation after new experiments go against it? *(Remember)*
   - A) Empirical
   - B) Cumulative
   - C) Self-correcting
   - D) Creative
2. A pupil claims, "Rain comes when frogs croak loudly." What makes this a claim that *could* become scientific? *(Understand)*
   - A) Many elders in the village believe it.
   - B) It can be checked by recording croaking and rainfall over several weeks.
   - C) It is written in an old book.
   - D) It sounds like something a scientist would say.
3. Which of these is a *theory* in the scientific sense? *(Understand)*
   - A) Pure water boils at about 100 °C at sea level.
   - B) "My idea is that the plants died because of the cold."
   - C) The particle model, which explains why solids, liquids and gases behave differently.
   - D) Light bounces off a mirror at the same angle at which it arrives.
4. In a Grade 7 lesson, pupils count how many of ten seeds sprouted and write the numbers in a table. Which face of science are they mainly practising? *(Apply)*
   - A) Science as a process
   - B) Science as a product
   - C) Science as an attitude
   - D) Science as a belief
5. A pupil's group gets a result different from every other group's. The pupil reports it exactly as observed. Which scientific attitude does this show most clearly? *(Apply)*
   - A) Curiosity
   - B) Honesty
   - C) Perseverance
   - D) Scepticism
6. Which statement is an *objective* rather than an *aim*? *(Understand)*
   - A) Pupils will develop scientific literacy.
   - B) Pupils will appreciate the role of science in society.
   - C) Pupils will label five organs of the digestive system on a diagram.
   - D) Pupils will develop curiosity about nature.
7. The National Curriculum of Pakistan 2022-23 says it aims to shift classroom instruction from rote learning to what? *(Remember)*
   - A) Exam-based learning
   - B) Concept-based learning
   - C) Textbook-based learning
   - D) Lecture-based learning
8. Pupils test which of three soaps removes the most chalk dust from their hands and decide from their own results. Which kind of value does deciding from the results mainly build? *(Apply)*
   - A) Intellectual
   - B) Moral
   - C) Social
   - D) Aesthetic
9. In what year was Pakistan's Expanded Programme on Immunization launched? *(Remember)*
   - A) 1947
   - B) 1965
   - C) 1978
   - D) 2002
10. A village debates whether to install solar panels or keep a diesel generator for its tube well. Which role of science in society does this decision show most directly? *(Apply)*
    - A) Science gives citizens understanding they need for everyday decisions.
    - B) Science is useful only to professional scientists.
    - C) Science settles questions of faith.
    - D) Science removes the need for any decision.

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

1. In three or four sentences, explain the meaning of science as both a body of knowledge and a way of finding out. *(Understand)*
2. A colleague says, "If scientific knowledge can change, it cannot be trusted." In three sentences, explain why scientific knowledge is both tentative and reliable. *(Understand)*
3. Give one Grade 4 to 8 classroom example each of a fact, a concept, a law and a theory. *(Understand)*
4. A Grade 6 pupil says, "Our teacher told us the germ theory is just a theory, so it might be a guess." In three or four sentences, explain how you would correct the pupil, using the words hypothesis and theory. *(Analyze)*
5. Name three process skills in the National Curriculum's Scientific Enquiry strand and give a classroom activity that practises each. *(Understand)*
6. Explain, in three or four sentences, why the aims of teaching science apply to Grades 1 to 8 even though the course guide speaks of "secondary level". *(Understand)*
7. Rewrite the statement "To teach pupils about magnets" as two measurable objectives for a Grade 6 lesson, and mark the observable verb in each. *(Apply)*
8. A teacher says, "Values are taught in Islamiat and Social Studies, not in science." Give two reasons, with an example each, why this is a misconception. *(Analyze)*
9. Choose one technology used in Pakistan. In four or five sentences, analyse one benefit and one harm it brings and say who decides how it is used. *(Analyze)*
10. In four or five sentences, analyse why a family's decision about vaccinating a child depends on scientific understanding, using one idea from the unit. *(Analyze)*

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. Read these ten statements about one Grade 6 lesson on "Matter as particles". Classify each as science as process, science as product or science as attitude, and justify your classification of any three. *(Analyze)*
   (a) Pupils watched a drop of ink spread in still water. (b) "All matter is made of tiny particles." (c) A pupil asked why the ink spread faster in warm water. (d) Groups timed how long the ink took to spread in warm and cold water. (e) "Particles in a gas move faster than particles in a solid." (f) Bilal's group admitted that they had stirred their glass by mistake. (g) Pupils predicted what would happen in ice-cold water. (h) "Diffusion is the spreading of particles from where there are many to where there are few." (i) Sana changed her idea after seeing another group's results. (j) Pupils drew a bar chart of their times.
2. Two Grade 5 claims reach your class: "Boiling water makes it safe to drink" and "Wearing a black thread protects a child from illness." Evaluate each claim against the characteristics of science, and plan how you would use the two claims to teach your pupils what science is. *(Evaluate)*
3. A school's science department writes one aim for the year: "Pupils will pass the board examination with good marks." Evaluate this aim against the aims in the National Curriculum of Pakistan 2022-23, and write three better aims with one lesson objective for each. *(Evaluate)*
4. Design a 40-minute Grade 7 lesson on preventing dengue in your community that deliberately builds intellectual, moral and social values and shows the role of science in society. Justify each step. *(Create)*
5. A parent tells you: "Science is just facts my child must memorise for the exam. It has nothing to do with real life or good character." Write a reasoned reply that draws on all five topics of this unit: the meaning and characteristics of science, its three faces, its aims, its values and its role in society. *(Evaluate)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### MCQ answer key

1. C - self-correction is the replacing or fixing of ideas when new evidence goes against them; "cumulative" is about building on earlier work, not replacing it.
2. B - testability makes a claim open to science; who believes it (A, C) or how it sounds (D) is not evidence.
3. C - a theory explains why; A is a fact, D is a law (a pattern), and B is a hypothesis or hunch.
4. A - counting and recording are process skills; the product would be the conclusion about what seeds need.
5. B - reporting an unexpected result exactly as observed is honesty.
6. C - it names one observable outcome of one lesson; the others are broad, long-term aims.
7. B - the National Curriculum's foreword states the shift "from rote learning to concept-based learning".
8. A - reasoning from evidence to a conclusion is an intellectual value; honesty in reporting would be moral.
9. C - the programme began in 1978; 2002 is when hepatitis B vaccine was added.
10. A - choosing an energy source is a citizen decision that needs scientific understanding; B, C and D contradict Topic 1.5.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. Model: Science is a body of knowledge about the natural world, such as facts and theories about plants or matter. It is also the way this knowledge is built and checked, through observation, experiment, reasoning and testing hypotheses. Both meanings matter, because knowledge without the way of finding out is only something to believe. **Marks (3):** 1 - knowledge meaning; 1 - way-of-finding-out meaning with at least one activity; 1 - links the two.
2. Model: Scientific knowledge is tentative because it can change if strong new evidence appears. It is reliable because it has survived repeated testing by many people, so we can safely use it to make medicines and build bridges. Change is a strength: it is how science corrects its errors. **Marks (3):** 1 - tentative explained; 1 - reliable explained with the role of testing; 1 - change presented as self-correction.
3. Model: Fact: pure water boils at about 100 °C at sea level. Concept: evaporation. Law: the law of reflection. Theory: the particle theory of matter. **Marks (4):** 1 per correct, grade-appropriate example of each kind.
4. Model: In everyday talk "theory" can mean a guess, but in science a guess to be tested is called a hypothesis. A scientific theory is an explanation that has passed many tests by many scientists. The germ theory is so well tested that we boil water and vaccinate children because of it. **Marks (4):** 1 - names the everyday meaning; 1 - defines hypothesis; 1 - defines theory as tested explanation; 1 - uses the germ-theory example to show why the pupil's conclusion does not follow.
5. Model: Asking questions (pupils list questions about why leaves fall); taking and recording measurements (measuring a shadow every hour); fair testing (two pots with and without sunlight, all else the same). Others accepted: classifying, predicting, drawing conclusions. **Marks (3):** 1 per skill with a matching activity.
6. Model: The programme prepares teachers for Grades 1 to 8, and the aims, such as understanding concepts, enquiry skills and scientific attitudes, start in the early grades. The National Curriculum builds the same skills from Grade 4 to Grade 8 with increasing depth. What changes is how far pupils go, not the aims. **Marks (3):** 1 - names the elementary scope; 1 - gives an aim that applies across grades; 1 - explains progression in depth.
7. Model: "By the end of the lesson, pupils will be able to **sort** ten objects into magnetic and non-magnetic" and "**show** how two magnets attract and repel by naming the poles". **Marks (4):** 1 per measurable objective; 1 per correctly identified observable verb.
8. Model: First, science itself needs values: honesty in recording results, as when a group reports seeds that did not sprout. Second, the uses of science raise questions of right and wrong: a pesticide protects cotton but can poison a canal. **Marks (4):** 1 per reason; 1 per fitting example.
9. Model (solar panels): Solar panels give villages cheap electricity for fans and tube wells without fuel costs. A harm is that old panels and batteries become waste that can pollute if thrown away carelessly. Science does not decide how panels are used; families, communities and governments do, and they decide better if they understand the science. **Marks (4):** 1 - names a real technology; 1 - benefit; 1 - harm; 1 - states that people decide, with a reason.
10. Model: A healthy-looking child can still catch polio, because protection depends on the body being prepared before the disease arrives. A family that understands how vaccines train the immune system is more likely to vaccinate; one that judges by appearance may refuse. So the decision depends on scientific literacy, which is why school science is for all pupils. **Marks (4):** 1 - states the scientific idea; 1 - contrasts judging by appearance with judging by understanding; 1 - links to a real decision; 1 - links to scientific literacy as an aim.

### ERQ rubrics

1. Expected classification: process - (a), (d), (g), (j); product - (b), (e), (h); attitude - (c) curiosity, (f) honesty, (i) open-mindedness. Accept (c) as process (questioning) if justified. Analytic rubric: *Accuracy of classification* (0-4: 4 for 9-10 correct, 3 for 7-8, 2 for 5-6, 1 for 3-4) · *Justification of three statements* (0-4, **Analyze**: each justification names the defining feature of the face, for example "(f) is attitude because it shows honesty about an error, not a skill or a piece of knowledge") · *Handling of borderline cases* (0-2: recognises that a statement such as (c) can be argued two ways and defends a choice).
2. Analytic rubric: *Evaluation against characteristics* (0-4: applies at least three characteristics, such as evidence, testability and openness to change, to each claim) · *Sensitivity* (0-2: treats the second claim as untested belief without mocking families) · *Teaching plan* (0-4, **Evaluate/Create**: a workable plan in which pupils test or discuss evidence themselves, for example boiling and observing water, and draw out what science is).
3. Analytic rubric: *Critique of the exam aim* (0-3: explains that an exam checks progress but is not itself an aim, and that this aim misses most curriculum aims) · *Use of the National Curriculum* (0-3: names at least three curriculum aims or strands accurately) · *Three better aims* (0-2) · *Three matching objectives* (0-2, measurable verbs, grade-appropriate). Higher marks require the critique to weigh what the exam aim would lose, not only to list other aims (**Evaluate**).
4. Analytic rubric: *Lesson design* (0-3: clear, realistic steps fitting 40 minutes and Grade 7, for example finding standing water around the school) · *Values built on purpose* (0-3: each of the three values is built through activity or evidence, not only by telling) · *Role of science in society* (0-2: links mosquito biology to a community action) · *Justification* (0-2, **Analyze**: each step is explained in terms of what it builds and why).
5. Analytic rubric: *Coverage of the five topics* (0-5: one mark for each topic used accurately) · *Quality of argument* (0-3, **Evaluate**: answers the parent's two claims directly, with evidence and examples, and acknowledges that facts and exams do matter) · *Tone and audience* (0-2: respectful, clear, free of jargon a parent would not know).
