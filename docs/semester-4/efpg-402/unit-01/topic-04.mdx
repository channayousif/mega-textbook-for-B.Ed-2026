# Topic 1.4 - Values of science education

## A real classroom situation

Madam Nusrat's Grade 5 class in Larkana is learning how germs spread. She rubs a little cooking oil and chalk powder on her hands to stand for germs, shakes hands with one pupil, who shakes hands with the next, and soon the "germs" have reached six pupils. Then two pupils wash with plain water and two with soap, and the class compares their hands.

At the end she asks, "So what will you do before eating?" A boy, Faraz, says, "Madam, this is science class. Telling us what we should do is for the Islamiat or Social Studies teacher." Madam Nusrat smiles. Faraz has raised a real question: does science have anything to do with values?

## Explanation

### Values of science education: intellectual, moral, social

A **value** here means a lasting benefit that learning science brings to a person and to society, beyond the facts themselves. The course guide names three kinds: intellectual, moral and social. Yadav (2002), a methods book on the course reading list, is one place to read further. Figure fig-U1-7 summarises them.

**Intellectual values** are benefits to the mind. Science trains pupils to:

- reason clearly from evidence to a conclusion;
- ask "How do you know?" before accepting a claim;
- solve problems step by step;
- think for themselves rather than repeat what they are told.

*Pakistani example.* A message on a family WhatsApp group says that drinking hot water kills the dengue virus. A pupil who has learned to ask for evidence will want to know who tested this and how. That habit is an intellectual value of science education, and it lasts long after the facts about dengue are forgotten.

**Moral values** are benefits to character. Science, done properly, requires:

- **honesty** in recording and reporting results, even unwelcome ones (recall Hira in Topic 1.2);
- **humility**: accepting that you were wrong when the evidence says so;
- **responsibility** for safe and careful work;
- **respect for life**: handling plants and animals gently and returning them unharmed.

*Pakistani example.* When a Grade 7 group catches insects for a classification activity in Sukkur, the teacher insists that the insects are released afterwards. Pupils learn that knowledge does not give them the right to harm living things.

**Social values** are benefits to the community. Science education builds:

- **cooperation**, because most investigations are group work;
- respect for other people's ideas and evidence;
- care for **community health**, such as hygiene, clean water and vaccination;
- care for the **environment**;
- informed **citizenship**, the ability to take part in decisions that need scientific understanding.

*Pakistani example.* Pakistan's National Curriculum expects Grade 7 pupils to suggest ways in which communities can protect themselves from the spread of infectious diseases (National Curriculum Council [NCC], 2023, SLO S-07-A-28). This is a social value written directly into the science curriculum. The curriculum as a whole is also meant to foster the "core, nation-building values of inclusion, harmony, and peace" (NCC, 2023, p. 4).

**Worked example: finding the values in Madam Nusrat's lesson.** Take each part of the lesson and ask which value it builds.

| Part of the lesson | Value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Comparing soap with plain water and deciding from what they see | Intellectual | Pupils reason from evidence to a conclusion |
| Reporting honestly whose hands still had chalk | Moral | Honesty in recording results |
| "What will you do before eating?" and telling families at home | Social | Care for community health |

So the answer to Faraz is: yes, the science teacher is teaching values, but through evidence. Pupils wash their hands because they saw the result, not only because they were told to.

> **Common misconception:** "Science and values have nothing to do with each other." This idea is wrong in two ways. First, science itself rests on values: honesty, objectivity and the willingness to let others repeat your work are what make scientific knowledge trustworthy (Hepburn & Andersen, 2015/2026). Second, the *uses* of science always raise questions of right and wrong. The National Curriculum asks pupils to discuss how science and technology can have positive and negative effects on the environment (NCC, 2023). A pesticide can protect a cotton crop and also poison a canal. Deciding how to use it is a question of values that needs scientific understanding.

Figure fig-U1-8 maps the values a single science lesson can build. A teacher who plans for them, rather than hoping they appear, builds them more often.

## Activity: Values hunt

Work in groups of three. About 20 minutes.

1. Each group takes one Grade 4 to 8 topic: "Balanced diet", "Renewable and non-renewable energy", "Microorganisms and diseases" or "Ecosystems".
2. Plan one short activity for that topic. Each member then takes one value (intellectual, moral or social) and adds one step to the activity that would build that value on purpose.
3. Write your activity as a three-row table like the worked example: part of the activity, value, why.
4. Pass your table to the next group. They must find one step where the value is claimed but not really built, and suggest a fix.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the three kinds of value in science education named in the course guide. *(Remember)*
2. Give one example of a moral value that is needed to do science properly, and explain why science needs it. *(Understand)*
3. In a Grade 6 lesson, pupils test three local brands of soap for how much lather they make and share the results with the school canteen. Name one intellectual, one moral and one social value this lesson could build. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Science education builds intellectual values (clear reasoning, judging evidence, solving problems), moral values (honesty, humility, responsibility, respect for life) and social values (cooperation, community health, care for the environment, informed citizenship). Science and values are linked: science depends on honesty and objectivity, and its uses raise questions of right and wrong. A good science teacher builds these values on purpose, through evidence rather than through lectures.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can explain the intellectual, moral and social values of science education with an example of each.
- [ ] I can identify which values a given science activity builds.
- [ ] I can explain to a pupil why science and values are connected.
- [ ] I can add a step to a lesson plan to build a chosen value on purpose.

## Try this at your practicum school

During one science lesson, listen for moments when a value is being built or could have been built: a pupil reporting a "wrong" result, groups sharing equipment, a link to health at home. Note two such moments. For one of them, write what the teacher did and what else could have been done.

## Summative task

Plan a 40-minute Grade 6 lesson on "Balanced diet" (National Curriculum, Grade 6) that deliberately builds one intellectual, one moral and one social value. Write 200-250 words: describe the three main steps of the lesson, name the value each step builds, and explain how you would know, at the end of the lesson, that each value had been exercised.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1) | Secure (2) | Strong (3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lesson steps | Steps are unclear or do not fit the topic | Three clear steps that fit the topic and grade | Three clear, realistic steps that use evidence or activity, not only telling |
| Values linked to steps *(Analyze)* | Values are named but not linked to steps | Each step is linked to a correct value | Each link is explained, showing exactly how the step builds the value |
| Evidence of success *(Evaluate)* | No way of checking | A general way of checking | A specific, observable sign for each value, such as honest recording in pupils' tables |
