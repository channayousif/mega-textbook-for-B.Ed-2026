# Topic 1.1 - Meaning and characteristics of science

## A real classroom situation

Madam Rukhsana teaches Grade 5 General Science at a government girls' school in Tando Allahyar. One July morning the sky is cloudy and the frogs behind the school are loud. A pupil, Sana, raises her hand: "Madam, my grandfather says rain always comes when the frogs croak like this. Is that science?" Another pupil adds, "My aunt says a black cat crossing the road brings bad luck. Is that science too?"

Madam Rukhsana pauses. She knows the textbook definition of science by heart, but it does not help her answer Sana. What makes one claim scientific and another not? This topic gives you the tools to answer Sana's question, and to teach your own pupils to ask it.

## Explanation

### Meaning and characteristics of science

**What science means.** Science is two things at once. It is a body of knowledge about the natural world, and it is the way that knowledge is built and checked. The activities most often named as typical of science are careful observation and experiment, reasoning from examples to rules and from rules to examples, making models, and forming and testing hypotheses and theories (Hepburn & Andersen, 2015/2026). A *hypothesis* is a possible answer to a question that can be tested. You will meet theories, laws and hypotheses again in Topic 1.2.

So "science" is not a list of things in a textbook. A pupil who weighs two seeds, waters one and not the other, and records what happens is doing science, even if she never learns the word "germination".

**Five characteristics of science.** No single feature marks off science from everything else, and philosophers still debate where the line falls. But five characteristics are widely used to recognise scientific knowledge (Halai, 2003; Hepburn & Andersen, 2015/2026). Figure fig-U1-1 shows them together.

1. **Empirical.** Scientific knowledge rests on evidence from the senses: observation, measurement and experiment. "Empirical" means "based on experience and evidence". An idea that cannot be connected to any observation is not a scientific idea.
2. **Testable.** A scientific claim can be checked, and it could turn out to be wrong. "Plants grow faster with more sunlight" is testable: you can grow two sets of plants and compare. "Invisible spirits make plants grow" cannot be tested, because no observation could show it wrong.
3. **Tentative, yet reliable.** Scientific knowledge is the best explanation we have *for now*. It is reliable enough to build bridges and make medicines, but it stays open to change if strong new evidence appears (Halai, 2003). However much evidence supports an idea, scientists cannot be completely certain it is true (Hepburn & Andersen, 2015/2026).
4. **Cumulative.** New knowledge builds on what came before. Our understanding of how vaccines work rests on earlier work on germs and on the body's defences. Each generation of scientists starts from where the last one stopped.
5. **Self-correcting.** Because claims are tested again and again, by different people in different places, mistakes are found and corrected. Old ideas are replaced or extended by newer ones when the evidence demands it (Halai, 2003).

Two more points complete the picture. Science is **creative**: it takes imagination to think of a new explanation or a clever experiment. And science is **human and worldwide**: people from every part of the world have contributed to it (Halai, 2003). Both points matter in a Pakistani classroom, where pupils sometimes see science as something done only by foreigners in white coats.

**Worked example: testing Sana's two claims.** Here is how a teacher can use the characteristics to judge a claim. Read the steps before you try the activity.

*Claim A: "Frogs croak more loudly before rain."*

- Empirical? Yes. It comes from people's observations over many monsoons.
- Testable? Yes. A class could record the loudness of croaking each evening for a month and note which days it rained.
- Tentative? It should be. Perhaps frogs croak because the air is already damp, not because rain is coming. The claim may need changing after testing.
- Verdict: this is a **common-sense** claim that *can become* scientific once it is tested carefully. It is a good starting question for a class investigation.

*Claim B: "A black cat crossing the road brings bad luck."*

- Empirical? Not really. People remember the times bad luck followed a black cat and forget the times it did not.
- Testable? Hard to test, because "bad luck" has no clear meaning. Anything unpleasant can be counted.
- Open to change? People who hold this belief rarely change it when it fails.
- Verdict: this is a **superstition**, a belief held without testing. Science cannot support it.

Notice the move: you do not ask "Who said it?" or "Does it sound clever?" You ask "What evidence is there, and can it be tested?" Figure fig-U1-2 sets out the contrast.

<Figure id="fig-U1-2" src="/img/figures/efpg-402/unit-01/fig-U1-2.svg" alt="Table comparing science, common sense and superstition on where each comes from, how it is checked and what happens when evidence goes against it, with one classroom example each." />

A note of care: science answers questions about how the natural world works, using evidence. It does not try to answer every question people ask. Questions of faith and of right and wrong are answered in other ways, and a good science teacher respects that. The contrast above is with *superstition* and untested habit, not with a family's faith.

> **Common misconception:** "Science is a collection of facts to memorise." Many pupils, and many teachers, think of science as a list of definitions to learn for the exam. Halai (2003) found that science teachers in Pakistan often teach science "as a collection of facts", and that pupils taught this way grow up to teach the same way, so the cycle continues. The facts are only the *product* of science. Leave out the process of asking, testing and checking, and you have taught pupils about science's results, not about science. Breaking this cycle starts with you.

## Activity: Science, common sense or belief?

Work in groups of three. About 20 minutes.

1. Each group receives six claims written on slips. For example: "Boiling water makes it safe to drink"; "Eating fish and then drinking milk causes skin disease"; "The moon changes shape because the earth's shadow falls on it"; "Onion juice protects you from dengue"; "Heavy objects fall faster than light ones"; "Neem leaves keep insects away from stored grain".
2. For each claim, one member argues that it is scientific, one argues that it is not, and the third member decides, using the five characteristics. Rotate the roles for each claim.
3. Sort the slips into three piles: science, common sense, belief or superstition. For any common-sense claim, write one sentence saying how a Grade 6 class could test it.
4. Compare piles with another group. Where you disagree, say which characteristic caused the disagreement.

## Check your understanding

1. Give a two-part meaning of science in your own words. *(Remember)*
2. Explain, in two or three sentences, why scientific knowledge can be both reliable and tentative. *(Understand)*
3. A pupil says, "My uncle's lucky stone makes him pass every exam." Use two characteristics of science to explain why this claim is not scientific. *(Apply)*
4. A colleague teaches the chapter on magnets by asking pupils to copy and memorise eight definitions. Name the misconception behind this practice and suggest one change. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Science is both a body of knowledge about the natural world and the way that knowledge is built. Scientific knowledge is empirical, testable, tentative yet reliable, cumulative and self-correcting, and it is made by creative people all over the world. To judge a claim, ask what evidence supports it and whether it could be tested. Teaching science only as facts to memorise hides the very thing that makes it science.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can state what science means in a way a Grade 5 pupil would understand.
- [ ] I can name the five characteristics of science and give an example of each.
- [ ] I can use the characteristics to decide whether an everyday claim is scientific.
- [ ] I can explain why "science is a collection of facts" is a misconception.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask three pupils in a Grade 5 to 8 class, one at a time, "What is science?" Write down their exact words. Then look at the class's science notebooks for one week of lessons. Note how much of the written work is copied definitions and how much records something pupils observed or tested themselves. Bring your notes to the next seminar.

## Summative task

A Grade 6 textbook chapter on plants contains these three sentences: (a) "Plants make their food in their leaves." (b) "Do this activity: cover one leaf of a potted plant with black paper for three days and compare it with an uncovered leaf." (c) "Scientists once believed plants got all their food from the soil, but experiments showed this was wrong."

Write 200-250 words. For each sentence, say which characteristic(s) of science it shows or could show. Then judge whether the chapter as a whole presents science as a list of facts or as a way of finding out, and suggest one change that would show the characteristics more clearly.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1) | Secure (2) | Strong (3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Characteristics identified | Names characteristics without linking them to the sentences | Links a correct characteristic to each sentence | Links each sentence to one or more characteristics and explains the link |
| Judgement of the chapter *(Analyze/Evaluate)* | Gives an opinion with no reasons | Judges the chapter with one reason from the sentences | Weighs the evidence from all three sentences and reaches a reasoned judgement |
| Suggested change | Vague or unrelated change | A relevant change | A specific change that shows a named characteristic in action |
