# Topic 4.2 - Use of laboratory equipment

## A real classroom situation

Madam Tahira teaches Grade 8 General Science in a government girls high school in Khairpur. Today she is conducting an experiment on the separation of mixtures using filtration. She has set out retort stands, glass funnels, filter papers, and beakers on the demonstration table.

She notices that several pupils handle the glassware nervously. One pupil begins to pour muddy water directly into a funnel without inserting a filter paper. Another pupil attempts to heat a graduated measuring cylinder over a spirit burner flame to dissolve sugar faster.

Madam Tahira intervenes immediately. She recognizes that before pupils can investigate scientific phenomena successfully, they must learn how each piece of equipment operates, how to read scales accurately, and what safety rules prevent breakages and injuries.

## Explanation

### Use of laboratory equipment

The school science laboratory is a dedicated learning environment where pupils test hypotheses, make precise measurements, and handle physical apparatus (National Curriculum Council, 2023). Skillful manipulation of laboratory equipment is a central Student Learning Outcome across Grades 4-8.

#### Core apparatus and safe handling protocols

Science teachers must master the standard apparatus used in middle and lower secondary classrooms (fig-U4-3; Vaidya):

1. **Test tubes and boiling tubes:** Thin cylindrical glassware used for heating, mixing, and observing chemical reactions on a small scale. Always hold test tubes with a wooden test-tube holder. Never fill a test tube more than one-third full when heating. Point the open mouth away from oneself and other learners. Heat gently along the side of the tube while swirling the liquid to prevent sudden boiling eruption (bumping).
2. **Beakers and conical flasks:** Flat-bottomed vessels designed for holding, mixing, and roughly estimating liquid volumes. Beakers must be heated on a wire gauze placed over a tripod stand, ensuring even heat distribution and preventing thermal shock cracking.
3. **Graduated measuring cylinders:** Narrow calibrated vessels designed for determining exact liquid volumes in cubic centimetres (cm³) or millilitres (mL). When taking a reading, place the cylinder on a level horizontal surface, position eyes level with the liquid surface, and record the reading at the bottom of the curved meniscus for aqueous solutions. *Never apply heat to a measuring cylinder*, as heat distorts glass calibration and causes rapid fracture.
4. **Spirit burners:** Safe, low-temperature thermal sources fueled by methylated spirits or denatured alcohol. Never leave a lit burner unattended. Never blow with breath to extinguish the flame; always place the metal or ceramic extinguisher cap firmly over the wick.
5. **Spring balances and weights:** Instruments for measuring gravitational force and friction in Newtons ($N$). Always verify that the indicator aligns with zero before attaching any load.

#### Assembling a standard apparatus set-up: Gravity filtration

Figure fig-U4-4 illustrates the correct assembly for gravity filtration, a common Grade 6-8 practical requirement:

- **Retort stand and ring clamp:** Provide stable, weighted mechanical support. The clamp must be tightened sufficiently to hold the funnel securely without crushing the glass stem.
- **Glass funnel and filter paper:** The circular filter paper must be folded into quarters, opened into a conical pocket, and seated snugly inside the glass funnel. Moisten the paper with a few drops of clean water so it adheres to the glass walls without air gaps.
- **Delivery and collection:** Position the stem tip of the funnel against the inside wall of the receiver beaker. This prevents liquid drops from splashing. When pouring the cloudy mixture, pour down a glass stirring rod held against the beaker lip to control the flow rate.

#### Worked example: Reading volume in a graduated cylinder

A Grade 7 pupil measures 45 mL of copper sulfate solution. Looking down from above the cylinder lip, she reads 47 mL due to parallax error. The teacher instructs her:
1. Place the cylinder on the flat laboratory bench.
2. Bend down until her eye is level with the fluid surface.
3. Observe the curved meniscus.
4. Align her eye with the horizontal calibration mark at the bottom of the curve.
The true reading is 45.0 mL. Parallax error occurs whenever the line of sight is not perpendicular to the instrument scale.

> **Common misconception:** "Glassware with measurement markings (like beakers) gives accurate scientific volume measurements." Many pupils assume printed beaker scales are precise. In fact, beakers are manufactured with up to $\pm 10\%$ tolerance and are suitable only for rough estimates. Precise volume measurements require calibrated graduated cylinders, burettes, or volumetric pipettes.

## Activity: Equipment identification and safety audit

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

1. Examine a tray containing five laboratory items: a test tube, a graduated cylinder, a wire gauze, a wooden clamp, and a spring balance.
2. For each item, write:
   - Its standard scientific name.
   - Its primary experimental purpose.
   - One dangerous misuse that could cause injury or equipment damage.
3. Swap lists with another pair and verify whether the safety rules match standard laboratory precautions.

## Check your understanding

1. Explain how to read the volume of water correctly in a graduated measuring cylinder. *(Understand)*
2. Name two safety precautions a teacher must enforce when pupils heat liquids in a test tube. *(Remember)*
3. A Grade 6 pupil places a glass beaker directly on the flame of a spirit burner without a wire gauze. Predict what will happen to the beaker and justify the correct procedure. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Competence in handling laboratory equipment is fundamental to experimental science. Safe practice requires systematic protocols: using clamps when heating, inserting wire gauze under heated flat glassware, checking zero readings on balances, and reading liquid scales at the bottom of the meniscus at eye level. Proper teacher demonstration and vigilant supervision prevent laboratory accidents and build pupil confidence.

## Try this at your practicum school

Visit the science laboratory or science resource storage cupboard at your practicum school. Inspect the condition of basic apparatus: check if measuring cylinders have chipped lips, whether spring balances zero correctly, and if spirit burners have functional snuffer caps. Create an inventory log of five items noting their operational condition.

## Summative task

During a Grade 7 practical lesson on solubility, you observe three groups of pupils: Group 1 is heating a sealed, stoppered test tube over a flame; Group 2 is using a 250 mL beaker to measure exactly 15 mL of acid; Group 3 is blowing across a spirit burner wick to put it out. Write a 200-250 word laboratory safety intervention. For each group, analyze the safety hazard, explain the scientific risk, and describe the immediate corrective action you would instruct pupils to take.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1) | Secure (2) | Strong (3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hazard identification | Identifies only 1-2 hazards accurately | Correctly identifies all three hazards | Identifies all three hazards and explains the underlying mechanical or chemical risks |
| Safety analysis *(Analyze)* | Gives vague reasons why the actions are unsafe | Explains the physical danger (pressure buildup, inaccuracy, vapor ignition) | Thoroughly analyzes the scientific principles of thermal expansion, precision limits, and combustion safety |
| Corrective guidance | Suggests unrealistic or incomplete corrections | Provides clear, safe operational corrections for all three groups | Articulates precise, standard laboratory corrective instructions that reinforce safe habits |
