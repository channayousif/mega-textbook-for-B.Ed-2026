# Topic 4.1 - Importance of teaching aids in science

## A real classroom situation

Sir Rashid teaches Grade 7 General Science at a government high school in Larkana. Today he is teaching the human circulatory system. For forty minutes, he lectures from the front of the classroom, reading definitions of the heart, arteries, veins, and capillaries aloud from the textbook and drawing small chalk diagrams on the blackboard.

When he asks his pupils to explain how blood flows through the heart chambers, silence fills the room. A few pupils repeat memorised sentences from the textbook, but none can trace the pathway or explain what happens when the heart contracts. In the back row, several pupils look distracted and confused.

Sir Rashid realizes that hearing spoken words and copying static textbook sketches does not help children picture a dynamic, pumping three-dimensional organ. He wonders: how can a science teacher bring invisible or complex biological mechanisms into the sensory grasp of learners?

## Explanation

### Importance of teaching aids in science

Science is an empirical, enquiry-based discipline. Pupils do not learn science merely by listening to verbal explanations or memorising textbook passages; they learn by observing, interacting, manipulating, and investigating (National Curriculum Council, 2023). A *teaching aid* is any instructional material, object, model, graphic, or digital device that a teacher uses to make learning meaningful, active, and concrete.

Teaching aids serve four vital instructional purposes in the science classroom (Vaidya; fig-U4-1):

1. **Arousing and sustaining attention:** Concrete materials capture natural curiosity and reduce classroom passivity. An actual working model or real plant specimen immediately focuses pupils' visual and cognitive attention.
2. **Developing conceptual clarity:** Many scientific concepts - such as atomic structures, planetary orbits, cellular respiration, or electromagnetic forces - cannot be perceived directly by the unaided senses. Teaching aids bridge the gap between abstract symbolic language and physical reality.
3. **Enhancing long-term retention:** Cognitive psychological research confirms that information received through multiple sensory channels (seeing, touching, hearing, manipulating) creates richer neural connections than purely verbal transmission.
4. **Promoting inclusive, equitable learning:** In multilingual classrooms across Pakistan, pupils possess varied linguistic proficiencies in Urdu and English. Visual and tangible aids ensure that learners who struggle with complex textbook prose can grasp core scientific ideas.

#### Dale's cone of experience as an instructional guide

To select appropriate teaching aids, science educators rely on Edgar Dale's (1969) *cone of experience*. Dale organised instructional media along a continuum from direct, concrete sensory experiences at the base to highly abstract, symbolic representations at the pinnacle (fig-U4-2):

- **Direct purposeful experiences (Base):** Pupils engage directly with reality by touching, measuring, and testing real materials - such as dissecting a flower, measuring boiling water, or testing circuits.
- **Contrived experiences and models:** When direct reality is too dangerous, huge, or inaccessible, the teacher uses working models, cutaways, or improvised simulators - such as a plastic bottle lung model.
- **Dramatisation and demonstrations:** Live teacher experiments or pupil role-play (such as pupils acting as electrons flowing through a resistor).
- **Visual and auditory media:** Charts, wall diagrams, recorded video clips, animations, and photographs that visually represent processes.
- **Verbal and text symbols (Apex):** Abstract terminology, formulas ($F = ma$), and textbook prose.

Dale emphasized that effective teaching does not abandon verbal symbols; rather, it *grounds* abstract concepts in concrete physical experiences before asking pupils to manipulate formulas and definitions.

#### Worked example: Teaching the mammalian heart

Consider Sir Rashid's challenge with Grade 7 circulation. If he teaches solely at the apex of Dale's cone (verbal symbols: lecture and definitions), understanding remains superficial. Instead, he can sequence aids purposefully:

1. *Direct experience:* Bring fresh sheep or goat hearts obtained from the local butcher for pupils to examine muscular chambers and elastic blood vessels.
2. *Contrived model:* Use a transparent plastic squeezy-bottle pump with one-way valves to demonstrate mechanical pumping and valve action.
3. *Visual media:* Project or display a clear coloured chart showing oxygenated and deoxygenated pathways.
4. *Verbal consolidation:* Pupils read and write the formal terminology and trace the circulatory cycle in their notebooks.

> **Common misconception:** "Good teaching aids must be expensive commercial kits." Many student-teachers assume that effective aids require imported plastic kits or digital projectors. In fact, simple, locally available items often teach foundational concepts more effectively because pupils can handle them without fear of breakage, as explored in Topic 4.3.

## Summary

Science teaching aids transform abstract verbal concepts into perceptible, observable experiences. They stimulate pupil interest, clarify difficult processes, boost retention, and provide equitable access for diverse learners. Edgar Dale's cone of experience provides a systematic framework: sound pedagogy begins with direct, purposeful manipulation or tangible models before transitioning to visual media and formal verbal terminology.
