# Topic 4.3 - Preparation and use of low-cost teaching aids

## A real classroom situation

Sir Bilal teaches General Science at a rural government middle school in Badin district. His school has no dedicated science laboratory and no budget for commercial instructional kits. When teaching Grade 5 respiration, the curriculum expects pupils to understand how the diaphragm muscle moves downward to draw air into the lungs and relaxes upward during exhalation.

Instead of merely reading the textbook description, Sir Bilal collects discarded plastic water bottles from the school canteen, several colourful party balloons from the local bazaar, plastic drinking straws, and rubber bands. During the weekend, he cuts the bottom off a plastic bottle, inserts a balloon fixed to a Y-shaped straw through the neck, and seals the cut base with a stretched rubber sheet cut from an old bicycle inner tube.

On Monday, when he pulls the rubber sheet downward, the internal balloon inflates instantly; when he pushes it upward, the balloon collapses. The pupils gasp with excitement. For the first time, every pupil understands how volume change creates the pressure difference that drives human breathing.

## Explanation

### Preparation and use of low-cost teaching aids

In many primary, middle, and secondary schools across Pakistan, teachers face resource constraints. Science education cannot stop because a school lacks expensive commercial equipment. *Low-cost teaching aids* (also termed improvised apparatus or no-cost learning materials) are instructional devices constructed by teachers and pupils using readily available local, recycled, or inexpensive everyday items (Khan et al., 2019; National Curriculum Council, 2023).

Empirical educational research conducted in Pakistani secondary schools confirms that pupils taught with low-cost improvised materials achieve significantly higher conceptual comprehension, greater curiosity, and superior retention compared to pupils taught solely through lecture methods (Khan et al., 2019). Low-cost materials demystify science, transforming it from an abstract subject into an activity rooted in daily life.

#### The five-step design cycle for low-cost aids

Creating an effective teaching aid is an intentional pedagogical process, not random assembly (fig-U4-5):

1. **Identify the target Student Learning Outcome (SLO):** Clarify the exact scientific principle or mechanism pupils must understand. An aid must serve a specific learning objective, not just look attractive.
2. **Source safe, local materials:** Collect discarded, recycled, or low-cost everyday materials (plastic bottles, cardboard boxes, wire scraps, thread, dry cells, paper clips, rubber bands, sand). Never use toxic substances, sharp glass shards, or rusted metal edges.
3. **Construct a working prototype:** Build the model ensuring mechanical stability and durability. The moving parts must operate smoothly and withstand repeated pupil handling.
4. **Test and calibrate:** Test the model before taking it to class. Verify that the demonstration works reliably and that changes are visible from the back bench of a crowded classroom.
5. **Teach, involve, and reflect:** Integrate the aid into the lesson. Allow pupils to handle, manipulate, and test the model in groups. Afterwards, reflect on whether the model accurately conveyed the concept without introducing new misconceptions.

#### Five proven low-cost aids for Grades 4-8 science

Figure fig-U4-6 presents five high-impact low-cost aids using household items:

1. **Working lung model:** Uses a plastic bottle, balloons, and straw to demonstrate how thoracic volume and diaphragm movement control pulmonary pressure changes in Grade 5-7 biology.
2. **Simple circuit tester:** Built with a $1.5\text{ V}$ torch dry cell, small bulb, paper clips, and wire scraps mounted on cardboard. Pupils test whether everyday objects (nails, erasers, coins, wood, water) conduct electricity.
3. **Pinhole camera:** Made from a shoebox with a pinhole at one end and translucent tracing paper at the other. Demonstrates that light travels in straight lines (rectilinear propagation) and forms inverted real images.
4. **Layered water purification filter:** Constructed in an inverted cut bottle with layers of cotton cloth, fine sand, coarse gravel, and crushed charcoal. Teaches mechanical sedimentation, physical filtration, and chemical adsorption.
5. **Liquid density column:** Uses a clear glass jar containing honey, coloured water, and cooking oil. Teaches that immiscible liquids form layers based on relative density, and demonstrates buoyancy when small objects float or sink.

#### Worked example: Building and testing a simple electric circuit tester

A teacher in Badin prepares a circuit tester for Grade 6 physical science:
- *Materials:* One fresh $1.5\text{ V}$ dry cell, one $1.5\text{ V}$ miniature torch bulb in a salvaged socket, two pieces of copper wire stripped at the ends, two metal paper clips, and adhesive tape.
- *Assembly:* Tape one wire from the cell's positive terminal to the bulb socket. Connect a second wire from the negative terminal to a paper clip (Probe A). Connect a third wire from the bulb socket to a second paper clip (Probe B).
- *Testing:* Touch Probe A and Probe B together. The bulb lights brightly, confirming a closed circuit.
- *Classroom application:* Pupils touch Probes A and B to different materials around the room. When touching a steel ruler, the bulb glows (conductor); when touching a plastic pen, the bulb remains dark (insulator).

> **Common misconception:** "An improvised low-cost teaching aid is only a poor substitute for real laboratory equipment." In reality, improvised aids often hold superior pedagogical value. When pupils see a lung model built from a water bottle or a circuit assembled from paper clips, they realize that science principles govern their own everyday environment, encouraging creative problem-solving and self-directed experimentation.

## Activity: Designing an improvised teaching aid

Work in groups of three. About 25 minutes.

1. Select one of the following Grade 6-7 General Science concepts:
   - Concept 1: Reflection of light using planar surfaces.
   - Concept 2: Filtration and separation of solid mixtures.
   - Concept 3: Sound transmission through solids, liquids, and gases.
2. Sketch a design for a low-cost teaching aid using only materials found at home or in school waste.
3. List the materials required, write step-by-step assembly instructions, and describe what pupils will observe during operation.
4. Present your sketch to another group and receive feedback on safety, durability, and pedagogical clarity.

## Summary

Low-cost teaching aids empower science teachers in resource-constrained schools to deliver rich, hands-on instructional experiences. Following a deliberate design cycle - from learning outcome identification to sourcing, prototyping, calibration, and reflective use - ensures safety, durability, and alignment. Improvised resources show pupils that science is accessible, creative, and directly connected to their daily lives.
