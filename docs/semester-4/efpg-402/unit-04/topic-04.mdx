# Topic 4.4 - Use of ICT in science teaching

## A real classroom situation

Madam Nasreen teaches Grade 8 General Science in Dadu district. She wants to teach atomic structure and chemical bonding. In previous years, pupils struggled to understand why atoms share or transfer electrons because chalk sketches cannot show dynamic electron shells or molecular motion.

This year, the school acquired a shared laptop and battery-powered LED projector. Madam Nasreen loads an offline interactive science simulation (PhET) that allows pupils to drag protons, neutrons, and electrons into an atom model and see whether the atom becomes stable or radioactive, neutral or an ion.

The pupils interact eagerly with the simulation. However, the head teacher observes the class and asks: "If pupils can do chemistry on a laptop screen, why do we need test tubes and beakers at all? Can we not replace all practical work with computers?"

Madam Nasreen realizes she must explain the distinct roles of digital tools and physical materials in science teaching.

## Explanation

### Use of ICT in science teaching

Information and Communication Technology (ICT) encompasses digital hardware, software, multimedia resources, and connectivity that support teaching and learning (GICT-300; National Curriculum Council, 2023). In science education, ICT tools expand the horizons of the classroom by allowing learners to visualize, manipulate, and explore phenomena that are otherwise impossible to observe directly.

#### High-impact digital ICT tools in science

Science teachers use several major categories of digital tools (Khan Academy; fig-U4-7):

1. **Interactive digital simulations (e.g. PhET Interactive Simulations):** Open educational software that lets learners manipulate variables in virtual environments. Pupils can alter electric voltage to observe current flow, change gravitational mass to trace planetary orbits, or heat gas particles inside a container to visualize pressure changes.
2. **Video demonstrations and animations (e.g. Khan Academy Science):** Curated video resources that present rare, hazardous, extreme, or microscopic processes - such as explosive alkali metal reactions with water, cellular mitosis, volcanic eruptions, or space exploration missions.
3. **Digital sensors and data loggers:** Electronic probes connected to microcontrollers or computers that record temperature, light intensity, sound levels, or pH continuously, plotting real-time graphs automatically.
4. **Mobile science applications and databases:** Smartphone tools that enable pupils to identify local plant or bird species, calculate geographic coordinates, or record field data.

#### Low-resource offline alternatives for Pakistani schools

Many schools in Sindh and across Pakistan experience frequent electrical load-shedding and lack reliable internet connectivity. A professional science teacher plans resilient instructional strategies that do not collapse when technology fails (fig-U4-7):

- *Offline simulation storage:* Download simulations and open educational videos onto USB flash drives or memory cards during periods of internet access so they run offline without live web connections.
- *Kinesthetic role-play models:* When power fails, replace particle simulations with physical role-play where pupils act as vibrating particles in solids, liquids, and gases.
- *Chalkboard coordinate tracking:* When data loggers are unavailable, pairs of pupils use simple stopwatches and rulers, recording readings on chart paper and plotting line graphs by hand.
- *Printed classification keys:* Replace mobile species apps with printed dichotomous plant and insect charts mounted on classroom display boards.

#### When to use digital ICT versus real physical objects

The decision framework in figure fig-U4-8 establishes clear criteria for choosing between digital tools and physical materials:

- **Use digital ICT when phenomena are:**
  - *Too small or microscopic:* Subatomic particles, cellular organelles, DNA replication.
  - *Too vast in scale:* Planetary orbits, galaxies, plate tectonics.
  - *Too dangerous or hazardous:* Radioactive decay, toxic gas releases, explosive chemical reactions.
  - *Too fast or too slow:* Speed of light, geological fossil formation over millions of years.
- **Use real physical materials when learning requires:**
  - *Psychomotor and manipulative skill:* Pouring liquids, folding filter paper, focusing a real optical microscope, lighting a spirit burner.
  - *Direct sensory evidence:* Feeling the temperature drop in an endothermic reaction, smelling the characteristic pungent odour of ammonia gas, observing authentic precipitate texture.
  - *Authentic measurement reality:* Experiencing friction, parallax errors, and genuine experimental uncertainty.
  - *Collaborative physical interaction:* Sharing apparatus and cooperating on bench tasks.

#### Worked example: Balancing ICT and hands-on circuits

A Grade 7 teacher plans a unit on electrical circuits:
1. *Hands-on session:* Pupils assemble physical torch bulbs, cells, and copper wires. They experience the tactile frustration of loose connections, dead batteries, and burnt-out filaments. This builds authentic motor skills and troubleshooting instincts.
2. *Digital simulation session:* Pupils use an interactive PhET circuit simulator. In the simulation, they can see blue moving dots representing electron flow inside the wires, which cannot be seen in the physical classroom.
The digital tool deepens conceptual theory, while the physical apparatus builds manual laboratory competence. Both are essential.

> **Common misconception:** "ICT can replace hands-on laboratory practical work." Some administrators view computer simulations as a cheaper alternative to laboratories. However, simulations present perfect mathematical models without friction, glassware breakage, or chemical unpredictability. A student who only clicks a mouse on a screen never develops true laboratory skills, sensory observation habits, or manual dexterity.

## Activity: Evaluating an ICT tool against physical apparatus

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

1. Consider the Grade 6 topic: "Changes of state: melting and boiling of water."
2. Imagine you have access to both:
   - Tool A: A PhET digital simulation showing water molecules vibrating and separating as temperature rises.
   - Tool B: A physical beaker of melting ice on a tripod stand with a mercury thermometer and spirit burner.
3. In a two-column chart, list two things pupils learn better from Tool A, and two things pupils learn better from Tool B.
4. Conclude with a three-sentence recommendation for how a teacher should integrate both tools into a two-day lesson sequence.

## Summary

ICT tools such as simulations, multimedia demonstrations, and digital sensors expand science learning by making invisible, dangerous, or extreme phenomena visible and interactive. However, digital simulations must complement, not replace, physical hands-on experimentation. Resilient teachers pre-load offline digital resources while ensuring that pupils develop manual dexterity, sensory observation, and authentic problem-solving skills with physical materials.

## Summative task

A secondary school receives four tablets for its science department. The Grade 8 teacher proposes using the tablets for 100% of the lessons in a unit on "Acids, Alkalis, and Indicators", arguing that virtual pH simulations are faster, cleaner, and eliminate the risk of spills. Write a 200-250 word pedagogical critique of this proposal. Analyze the limitations of virtual-only learning for this specific chemistry topic and propose a balanced blended plan that combines tablet simulations with hands-on litmus/universal indicator testing.
