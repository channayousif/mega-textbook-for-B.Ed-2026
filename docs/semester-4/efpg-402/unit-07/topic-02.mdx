# Topic 7.2 - Formative and summative assessment

## A real classroom situation

Sir Jameel is teaching Grade 8 General Science at a government high school in Sukkur. His topic is the law of conservation of mass in chemical reactions. He spends twenty-five minutes conducting a demonstration at the front table: burning a magnesium ribbon in a crucible and heating sugar until it chars black.

To check student understanding, Sir Jameel gives a surprise five-minute written quiz containing three recall questions, collects the slips of paper, and places them in his bag to mark over the weekend. He then announces: "That was your formative assessment for today. Tomorrow we begin our next unit on acids and bases."

When he marks the papers two days later, he is dismayed to find that more than half the class wrote that mass disappears when a substance burns because "smoke has no weight." By Monday morning, however, the class schedule has moved on to acids and bases, and the papers are returned with red checkmarks and numerical scores but no corrective feedback. Sir Jameel realizes that giving a brief quiz does not automatically make assessment formative if the information comes too late to shape teaching or redirect student thinking.

## Explanation

### Formative and summative assessment

Educational theorists and science curriculum planners classify classroom assessment into two major operational paradigms: *formative assessment* (assessment for learning) and *summative assessment* (assessment of learning) (National Curriculum Council, 2023; BSCS, 2006). Understanding the distinct pedagogical purpose of each is essential for every science educator.

#### Formative assessment: assessment for learning

Formative assessment occurs continuously during the instructional process. Its primary aim is to monitor student learning, identify misconceptions while they are actively forming, and provide immediate, descriptive feedback that enables both the student and the teacher to adjust their actions (fig-U7-3).

In a science classroom, formative assessment is not a single testing event; it is an ongoing, responsive instructional dialogue. When embedded within inquiry models like the BSCS 5E instructional model (Bybee et al., 2006), evaluation is not confined to the final phase. Formative checks take place throughout:
- During **Engage**, probing prior knowledge with concept cartoons or diagnostic questions.
- During **Explore**, observing laboratory manipulation, safety habits, and team collaboration.
- During **Explain**, assessing whether students can connect experimental evidence to formal scientific terminology.
- During **Elaborate**, verifying whether learners can transfer core concepts to novel real-world situations.

#### Summative assessment: assessment of learning

Summative assessment takes place at the end of an instructional period, such as the end of a topic, unit, school term, or academic year. Its primary purpose is to evaluate cumulative student achievement against designated Student Learning Outcomes (SLOs), assign grades, certify competence, and provide accountability data to parents and school authorities. Examples include end-of-chapter achievement tests, terminal board examinations (such as BISE matriculation exams), and final practical examinations.

#### Correcting a common misconception

A common misconception among teachers in Pakistan is that *any short quiz or weekly test constitutes formative assessment*. Teachers often believe that giving frequent tests satisfies the requirement for formative practice. However, if a short quiz is marked purely with scores, recorded in a grade book, and returned days later without providing actionable guidance or altering instructional plans, it is merely a frequent summative test. Assessment is formative only if the evidence generated directly informs immediate pedagogical adjustments and guides student revision.

#### The formative assessment feedback loop

An effective formative cycle in science teaching consists of four interconnected stages (fig-U7-4):

1. **Clarify learning intentions and success criteria:** Students must know what scientific understanding or skill they are striving to achieve.
2. **Elicit observable evidence of understanding:** Using quick techniques such as hinge-point questions, mini-whiteboards, exit tickets, or peer explanation prompts.
3. **Provide descriptive, non-judgmental feedback:** Moving beyond praise or numerical marks ("Good job", "7/10") to specific guidance ("Your circuit diagram shows the battery and switch, but you forgot to indicate the direction of conventional current; where should the arrow point?").
4. **Adjust teaching and learning:** Modifying subsequent lesson steps, re-teaching confusing concepts through an alternative representation, or organizing peer support.

#### Worked example: implementing a formative hinge question

- **Topic:** Grade 7 Science, Photosynthesis and Plant Nutrition.
- **Goal:** Assess whether pupils understand the source of plant biomass before introducing cellular respiration.
- **Hinge-point question:** "A tiny seedling weighing 5 grams grows in a tub of soil over five years into a small tree weighing 50 kilograms. Where did most of the dry matter of the tree come from? (A) Minerals absorbed from the soil; (B) Water absorbed by the roots; (C) Carbon dioxide absorbed from the air; (D) Sunlight absorbed by the leaves."
- **Formative observation:** 28 out of 35 pupils choose (A) or (B), revealing the common misconception that plants obtain their mass directly from soil nutrients.
- **Instructional adjustment:** Rather than lecturing on formulas, the teacher conducts a five-minute discussion examining van Helmont's classic willow tree experiment, showing that soil mass barely changes, which prepares the class to understand carbon fixation.

## Activity: Redesigning a summative test into a formative loop

Work in small groups of three. (Time box: 20 minutes)

Examine the following traditional testing practice:
"At the end of a two-week unit on acids, bases, and salts, a teacher gives a 50-mark written test on Friday. Over the weekend, the teacher marks the papers with red pens, calculating percentages. On Monday, the test papers are handed back, students compare scores, and the teacher begins the next chapter on metals."

In your group:
1. Identify three structural shortcomings of this practice from a formative assessment perspective.
2. Redesign this instructional unit to incorporate at least three formative assessment techniques (such as diagnostic entry tickets, mid-unit hinge questions, or practical peer checkpoints).
3. Outline the specific feedback mechanism that will help struggling students correct their chemical misconceptions before taking the final summative examination.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Define formative assessment and state its primary difference from summative assessment in science teaching.
2. *(Understand)* Explain how the BSCS 5E instructional model incorporates assessment throughout its phases rather than restricting it to the final Evaluate phase.
3. *(Apply)* A secondary science teacher in Hyderabad notices during a lab activity that three student groups cannot read the meniscus of a graduated cylinder correctly. Describe how the teacher should respond formatively in that exact moment rather than waiting to deduct marks on the lab report.

## Summary

Formative assessment and summative assessment serve complementary but distinct functions in science education. Formative assessment operates as an ongoing diagnostic feedback mechanism that empowers teachers to adjust instruction and helps learners close gaps in conceptual understanding in real time. Summative assessment provides necessary certification and accountability at the conclusion of instruction. When science teachers master the formative feedback loop, classroom inquiries become adaptive, equitable, and effective.

## Try this at your practicum school

Design a simple five-minute "Exit Ticket" with two conceptual questions on a topic you are teaching during your practicum (for example, floating and sinking or states of matter). Distribute it at the end of class, sort the student responses into three piles (solid understanding, partial grasp, severe misconception), and use that data to plan your warm-up activity for the very next lesson.
