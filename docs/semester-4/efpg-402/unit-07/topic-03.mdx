# Topic 7.3 - Tools and techniques of assessment

## A real classroom situation

Madam Saima teaches Grade 7 science at a government girls secondary school in Hyderabad. Her students have just spent two class periods working in the laboratory on separation techniques: dissolving impure rock salt, filtering out insoluble sand with filter paper and a funnel, and evaporating the filtrate over a spirit burner to recover clean salt crystals.

At the end of the week, Madam Saima announces a practical assessment. Instead of watching students manipulate apparatus, however, she writes three questions on the blackboard: "1. Define filtration. 2. Name two pieces of equipment used in evaporation. 3. Write two differences between a solute and a solvent."

A pupil raises her hand and asks: "Madam, will you not see how we fold the filter paper or check whether we boil the solution safely?" Madam Saima sighs and replies: "I have 55 students in this room and only one assistant. How can anyone grade practical work objectively without arguing over favoritism? Writing definitions in your notebook is the only fair way." Madam Saima's dilemma reflects a widespread crisis in science education: treating practical skills as mere textbook recall because teachers lack practical assessment tools and rubrics.

## Explanation

### Tools and techniques of assessment

Science is both a body of established knowledge and a process of active inquiry. Assessing science learning therefore requires an array of *techniques* (the procedures or methods used to gather evidence) and *tools* (the specific instruments or recording devices employed) that capture cognitive, manipulative, and affective dimensions of learning (National Curriculum Council, 2023; Directorate of Curriculum, Assessment and Research, Sindh).

#### Distinguishing techniques from tools

- **Assessment techniques:** The overarching strategies and procedures teachers use to observe, elicit, or sample student performance. Major techniques in science education include written examinations, practical performance tasks, direct classroom observation, oral questioning, student interviews, peer evaluation, and portfolio reviews.
- **Assessment tools:** The physical or digital instruments used to collect, record, and score data systematically. Common tools include objective test papers, structured observation schedules, checklists, rating scales, anecdotal records, and analytic rubrics (fig-U7-5).

#### Authentic assessment of practical and laboratory performance

Assessing practical work in school science cannot be accomplished through written recall tests alone. Practical work encompasses distinct learning domains:
1. **Manipulative skills:** Handling glassware, operating burners, reading meniscuses on cylinders, focusing microscopes, and connecting electrical circuits.
2. **Process skills:** Formulating testable hypotheses, controlling variables, recording accurate observations in tables, plotting graphs, and drawing logical inferences from data.
3. **Scientific attitudes and safety habits:** Adhering to laboratory safety protocols, disposing of chemicals responsibly, collaborating with peers, and demonstrating intellectual honesty when reporting unexpected findings.

#### Correcting a common misconception

A pervasive misconception in Pakistani schools is that *practical science skills cannot be assessed fairly or objectively*. Teachers often assume that assessing laboratory work is inherently subjective, prone to teacher bias, or impossible in large, crowded classrooms. Consequently, practical marks in secondary schools are often awarded based on student attendance, completed neat practical notebooks, or written viva questions.

In reality, practical performance can be assessed with high objectivity and reliability when teachers employ **analytic rubrics** and **structured observation checklists** (fig-U7-6). An analytic rubric breaks down a complex laboratory task into discrete, observable criteria with explicit performance level descriptors (such as Exemplary, Proficient, Developing, Beginning).

#### Worked example: designing a practical rubric for filtration

When assessing Grade 7 students on separating mixtures:
- **Criterion 1: Apparatus setup and safety:**
  - *Proficient (3 marks):* Correctly folds filter paper without tearing, seats it snugly in the funnel with water drops, clamps funnel over beaker, and wears eye protection.
  - *Developing (1-2 marks):* Folds paper clumsily or allows liquid to overflow above the filter paper rim; requires safety reminders.
- **Criterion 2: Measurement and pouring technique:**
  - *Proficient (3 marks):* Uses a glass rod to guide the liquid stream smoothly without splashing; pours slowly in increments.
  - *Developing (1-2 marks):* Pours rapidly directly from beaker, spilling mixture or disturbing the sediment.
- **Criterion 3: Data recording and deduction:**
  - *Proficient (4 marks):* Records clear descriptions of residue and filtrate; correctly concludes that dissolved salt passes through pores while sand is retained.
  - *Developing (1-3 marks):* Vague description; fails to distinguish dissolved solute from suspended particulate.

By using this 10-mark rubric, Madam Saima can observe student pairs during their regular laboratory periods and record defensible, objective marks in minutes.

## Activity: Constructing an observation checklist for a science inquiry

Work in pairs. (Time box: 20 minutes)

Select one of the following practical inquiry topics from the National Curriculum of Pakistan for Grades 6 to 8:
1. Investigating electrical conductivity of various household liquids (lemon juice, salt water, oil, distilled water).
2. Measuring pulse rates before and after two minutes of exercise.
3. Testing the effect of light on plant growth using seedlings.

In your pair:
- Identify four specific observable student behaviors that demonstrate authentic inquiry competence.
- Draft a five-point observation checklist or analytic mini-rubric for the selected task.
- Ensure that at least one criterion explicitly assesses safety habits and one assesses data recording precision.

## Summary

Assessing the full spectrum of science education demands a balanced repertoire of tools and techniques. While written tests measure theoretical concepts and factual recall, authentic practical assessment evaluates manipulative dexterity, scientific inquiry processes, and safety attitudes. By replacing informal impressions with structured observation checklists, rating scales, and analytic rubrics, science teachers ensure that practical laboratory performance is evaluated fairly, transparently, and rigorously.

## Summative task

Design a comprehensive laboratory assessment package for a Grade 8 science experiment on "Testing the Factors Affecting the Rate of Dissolving (Temperature, Stirring, and Particle Size)." Your package must include:
1. A specification of the Student Learning Outcomes (SLOs) assessed.
2. A six-item observational checklist for live teacher evaluation during the experiment.
3. A detailed analytic rubric (4 criteria, 3 performance levels each) assessing apparatus manipulation, controlled variable testing, data recording, and scientific conclusion drawing.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing | Proficient | Exemplary (Analyze/Evaluate) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alignment with inquiry SLOs | Assesses basic definitions; ignores experimental processes. | Connects assessment tasks to relevant curriculum outcomes. | Perfectly aligns assessment criteria with higher-order science process skills and manipulative standards. |
| Observational checklist quality | Vague, subjective statements ("worked hard", "behaved well"). | Lists observable technical behaviors with a binary yes/no scale. | Formulates clear, highly specific observable indicators of scientific technique, variable control, and lab safety. |
| Analytic rubric precision and depth | Uses circular language ("good", "average", "poor"). | Provides distinct levels with measurable descriptions of student performance. | Constructs rigorous, unambiguous performance descriptors across all levels, demanding critical evaluation of data validity and error sources. |
