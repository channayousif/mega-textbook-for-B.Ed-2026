# Topic 7.5 - Diagnostic and remedial teaching

## A real classroom situation

Madam Hina teaches Grade 6 General Science in a government school in Larkana. After teaching a three-week unit on "Living Organisms and Respiration," she conducts a diagnostic test. To her surprise, 24 out of 30 pupils write that "plants breathe in carbon dioxide and breathe out oxygen during respiration, while animals breathe in oxygen and breathe out carbon dioxide."

Distressed by these results, Madam Hina schedules a remedial session after school. She gathers the struggling pupils together, opens the textbook to chapter 3, and reads the exact same pages aloud again, speaking more slowly and repeatedly raising her voice: "Listen carefully, children! All living things respire! Plants use oxygen too! Memorize this sentence ten times in your notebooks!"

The following week, on the monthly test, the same students revert to their original answer, writing once again that plants do not need oxygen to respire. Madam Hina feels exhausted and despairs: "I explained it twice, I spoke louder and slower, yet they make the exact same mistake!" She does not realize that repeating the original instruction does not dismantle an ingrained cognitive misconception.

## Explanation

### Diagnostic testing in science

In medical practice, a physician does not prescribe medicine merely because a patient feels unwell; they conduct specific diagnostic blood tests to pinpoint the underlying pathogen. Similarly, in science education, a teacher cannot remedy learning difficulties simply by noting that a student scored 35 percent on an achievement test. The teacher must perform **diagnostic testing** to uncover the precise cognitive bottleneck, reasoning fallacy, or alternative conception obstructing student learning (Aggarwal; National Curriculum Council, 2023).

#### Characteristics of diagnostic tests

Unlike general achievement tests that sample broad content to rank or grade students, diagnostic tests have unique operational characteristics:
1. **Fine-grained, criterion-referenced focus:** Diagnostic tests do not award overall grades. They evaluate specific, narrow sub-skills or conceptual links (for example, isolating whether a student cannot balance chemical equations because they misunderstand subscripts versus coefficients).
2. **Analysis of error patterns:** Distractors in diagnostic multiple-choice items are deliberately engineered to represent documented scientific misconceptions (such as confusing boiling with chemical decomposition). When a student selects a specific distractor, it reveals their exact cognitive misstep.
3. **Identification of underlying causes:** Diagnostic assessment probes whether the failure stems from missing prerequisite knowledge, flawed mathematical manipulation, language comprehension barriers, or intuitive common-sense beliefs that contradict formal scientific principles.

### Remedial teaching strategies

Once diagnostic testing pinpoints the exact nature of the misconception, the teacher implements **remedial teaching**. Remedial teaching is a deliberate, individualized or small-group pedagogical intervention designed to rectify specific learning gaps and dismantle erroneous cognitive structures (Aggarwal; National Curriculum Council, 2023).

#### Correcting a common misconception

A widespread misconception among educators is that *remedial teaching simply means repeating the original classroom lesson more slowly, more loudly, or through repeated drill homework*. If an initial pedagogical explanation failed to connect with the student's cognitive schema, repeating that identical explanation will produce identical failure. Remedial teaching is not re-teaching; it requires adopting a **fundamentally different instructional modality** (fig-U7-9).

#### Effective remedial strategies in science

Science pedagogy provides several proven approaches for remedial intervention:

1. **Concrete multi-sensory modeling:** Moving from abstract textbook descriptions to hands-on manipulation. For example, if students confuse physical mixtures with chemical compounds, have them physically mix sulfur powder with iron filings, separate them with a magnet, and then heat the mixture in a test tube to observe an irreversible chemical bond.
2. **Cognitive conflict demonstrations (Discrepant events):** Creating a situation where the student's intuitive misconception leads to a prediction that is directly contradicted by an observable physical experiment. For example, demonstrating that a crumpled sheet of paper falls faster than an unfolded sheet of the same paper in air, but they hit the ground together when air resistance is eliminated.
3. **Concept cartoons and structured dialogue:** Presenting visual scenarios depicting four cartoon characters offering different explanations for an everyday science event. Students debate the merits of each claim in pairs, identifying flaws in non-scientific reasoning without fear of personal embarrassment.
4. **Targeted peer tutoring:** Pairing students who hold the misconception with peers who recently mastered the concept and can explain it using accessible peer vocabulary and analogies.
5. **Post-remedial evaluation:** Administering a parallel diagnostic probe after the remedial intervention to confirm that conceptual restructuring has truly occurred before resuming regular curriculum pacing.

#### Worked example: remediating the plant respiration misconception

- **Diagnosed misconception:** "Plants only respire at night, while photosynthesis replaces respiration during the day."
- **Why simple repetition fails:** Students intuitively associate "breathing" with gas exchange in lungs, and since plants take in carbon dioxide by day, students deduce that plants do not breathe oxygen until darkness falls.
- **Targeted remedial activity (fig-U7-10):**
  - *Step 1 (Cognitive conflict):* The teacher places germinating soaked mung bean seeds (which have no green leaves and cannot photosynthesize) in a closed thermos flask connected to a delivery tube leading into limewater.
  - *Step 2 (Observable evidence):* Students observe the limewater turning milky in broad daylight and detect a temperature increase inside the flask with a thermometer.
  - *Step 3 (Concept restructuring):* The teacher guides students to deduce: "The seeds are alive and growing rapidly in daytime, producing carbon dioxide and heat. Where does their energy come from? Cellular respiration."
  - *Step 4 (Generalization):* Students conclude that cellular respiration is the continuous life-sustaining energy process of all living cells, day and night, whereas photosynthesis is a specialized food-making process operating only in light.

## Activity: Designing a diagnostic item and remedial plan

Work in pairs. (Time box: 20 minutes)

Select one of the following classic science misconceptions frequently encountered in Sindh secondary schools:
1. "Heavier objects always fall faster than lighter objects regardless of air resistance."
2. "Electric current is 'used up' as it flows through a light bulb, so less current returns to the battery."
3. "Heat and temperature are the exact same physical quantity."

In your pair:
- Draft a two-tier diagnostic multiple-choice question: Tier 1 asks for the factual prediction; Tier 2 asks for the scientific reason behind the choice.
- Outline a 15-minute hands-on remedial activity that creates cognitive conflict and helps students abandon the misconception.
- Specify how you will verify that the misconception has been successfully dismantled.

## Summary

Diagnostic testing and remedial teaching constitute the corrective core of science pedagogy. While diagnostic testing identifies the exact root causes and cognitive patterns of student learning barriers, remedial teaching systematically disassembles flawed alternative frameworks through alternative representations, discrepant events, and concrete inquiry. When teachers view errors not as moral deficits but as diagnostic windows into student thinking, secondary science classrooms become genuine environments for conceptual transformation.
