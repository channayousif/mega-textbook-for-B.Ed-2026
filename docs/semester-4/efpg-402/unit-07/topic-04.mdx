# Topic 7.4 - Achievement tests in science

## A real classroom situation

Sir Tariq teaches Grade 7 General Science in Nawabshah. On the night before the mid-term examination papers are due at the principal's office, he opens the science textbook and quickly writes ten questions directly onto a sheet of paper.

Looking through the chapters, he chooses questions that are quick to phrase: "1. Define respiration. 2. List four organs of the digestive system. 3. Write the chemical formula for carbon dioxide. 4. State Ohm's law..." When the examination results are tabulated, 85 percent of the marks are clustered around simple rote recall. Furthermore, half the questions come from the first chapter because Sir Tariq spent more weeks teaching it, while the final two units on environmental science and simple machines are barely mentioned.

When students receive their graded papers, several high-achieving pupils who spent hours building circuits and studying ecosystem dynamics express frustration: "Sir, none of the experiments we performed or problems we solved were on the exam. It was all just memorizing definitions." Sir Tariq realizes that writing a test without a systematic plan leads to skewed, unrepresentative, and invalid assessment.

## Explanation

### Achievement tests in science

An *achievement test* is a formal systematic instrument designed to measure the knowledge, conceptual understanding, and cognitive skills that students have acquired as a result of specific instructional experiences (Aggarwal; National Curriculum Council, 2023). Unlike aptitude tests that predict future potential, achievement tests evaluate terminal learning against predetermined curriculum standards.

In secondary science, teacher-made achievement tests form the backbone of terminal grading and progress reporting. Constructing a valid, reliable, and fair achievement test requires adhering to a rigorous six-stage development cycle (fig-U7-7):

1. **Planning the test:** Defining the test purpose, target student population, total time allowance, total marks, and format distribution (objective vs constructed-response).
2. **Developing the Table of Specifications (ToS):** Creating a two-dimensional blueprint that ensures proportional sampling across content domains and cognitive levels.
3. **Drafting test items and scoring schemes:** Writing unambiguous questions aligned to specific Student Learning Outcomes (SLOs), accompanied by model answers and scoring rubrics.
4. **Reviewing and editing items:** Screening questions for scientific accuracy, linguistic clarity, readability, and freedom from unintended clues or bias.
5. **Administering the test:** Conducting the test under standardized, equitable conditions.
6. **Evaluating the test through item analysis:** Reviewing item difficulty (facility index) and discrimination power to refine future testing.

#### The role of the Table of Specifications (ToS)

The single most critical step in building an achievement test is preparing a **Table of Specifications (ToS)**, often called a *test blueprint* (fig-U7-8). A ToS is a grid with two intersecting dimensions:
- **Horizontal dimension:** Content areas or curriculum units being assessed.
- **Vertical dimension:** Cognitive domains targeted (typically based on Bloom's revised taxonomy: Remember, Understand, Apply, and Analyze/higher).

Each cell in the grid specifies the number of items and marks allocated. By determining these allocations in advance based on instructional time and curriculum priority, the teacher prevents two chronic defects:
- *Content under-representation:* Omitting vital topics that were taught.
- *Cognitive imbalance:* Overloading the examination with low-level factual recall while neglecting scientific reasoning, data analysis, and application.

#### Correcting a common misconception

A widespread misconception among school teachers in Pakistan is that *a test is valid as long as every question is taken directly from the textbook chapter end exercises*. This practice encourages rote memorization (cramming), because students simply memorize the answers to the standard textbook exercises without mastering the underlying concepts. A valid achievement test samples the curriculum systematically using newly framed, contextualized stems that demand active cognitive processing.

#### Guidelines for writing science test items

A balanced science achievement test typically combines three item formats:

- **Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs):** Consisting of a clear stem and four options (one intended key option and three plausible distractors based on common student errors). MCQs provide high content sampling and objective scoring.
- **Restricted-Response Questions (RRQs):** Short-answer questions that constrain the student to a specific answer (such as explaining a mechanism in 3 sentences, balancing an equation, or labelling a diagram). RRQs test deeper understanding with minimal writing burden.
- **Extended-Response Questions (ERQs):** Essay-type questions that require students to select, organize, analyze, and synthesize information (such as designing an experiment to test water purity or evaluating environmental trade-offs). ERQs must always be paired with an analytic rubric.

#### Worked example: calculating cell weights in a Table of Specifications

Suppose Sir Tariq is planning a 50-mark, 60-minute achievement test for Grade 7 General Science covering three units:
- Unit 1: Living Systems (15 instructional periods = 30 percent weight = 15 marks)
- Unit 2: Chemical Substances (20 instructional periods = 40 percent weight = 20 marks)
- Unit 3: Light and Waves (15 instructional periods = 30 percent weight = 15 marks)

Cognitive target distribution established by the science department:
- Remember (Recall): 30 percent (15 marks)
- Understand (Explanation): 40 percent (20 marks)
- Apply and Analyze (Problem solving and inquiry): 30 percent (15 marks)

Using this framework, the cell for "Chemical Substances - Apply and Analyze" receives 30 percent of 20 marks = 6 marks (for example, two 3-mark questions asking students to predict the products of an unknown chemical reaction from reactant properties).

## Activity: Drafting a Table of Specifications

Work in pairs. (Time box: 20 minutes)

You are tasked with creating a 25-mark mid-term science assessment for Grade 6 General Science covering two topics:
- Topic A: Plant and Animal Cells (10 teaching periods)
- Topic B: Sense Organs and Human Body Systems (15 teaching periods)

In your pair:
1. Calculate the percentage weight for each topic based on teaching periods.
2. Distribute the 25 marks across three cognitive levels: Remember (30%), Understand (40%), and Apply/Analyze (30%).
3. Fill out a six-cell Table of Specifications grid.
4. Draft one sample test item (with its scoring criteria) for the cell "Topic A - Apply/Analyze".

## Summary

Constructing a valid achievement test in science is a deliberate engineering process. Rather than writing subjective questions on the eve of an exam, teachers must formulate clear objectives, construct a balanced Table of Specifications, and draft rigorous items across objective, restricted-response, and extended-response formats. When tests reflect both content breadth and cognitive depth, achievement testing becomes a fair, motivating, and accurate measure of scientific competence.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing | Proficient | Exemplary (Analyze/Evaluate) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Table of Specifications structure | Incomplete grid; arithmetic totals do not match total marks. | Complete grid with reasonable content and cognitive weighting. | Methodically constructed blueprint perfectly balancing instructional time, national SLOs, and Bloom cognitive domains. |
| Item construction quality | Questions contain technical flaws, ambiguities, or giveaway clues. | Questions are scientifically sound and follow standard formatting rules. | Exquisitely written test items featuring robust scenario-based stems, highly plausible distractors, and clear cognitive differentiation. |
| Scoring guide and rubric rigor | Omits scoring guidelines or provides one-word solution notes. | Supplies basic scoring criteria and point allocations for each item. | Provides comprehensive model solutions, explicit point allocation criteria, and a nuanced analytic rubric for the ERQ demanding critical analysis. |
