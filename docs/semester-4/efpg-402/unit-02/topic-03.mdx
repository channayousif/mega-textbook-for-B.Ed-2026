# Topic 2.3 - Organisation and sequencing of content

## A real classroom situation

Madam Sadia teaches Grade 7 General Science in Nawabshah. This week's chapter is "The Human Digestive System." She remembers that her Grade 5 pupils also studied digestion - but only as a list of organs to label. Now in Grade 7 the textbook suddenly introduces enzymes, chemical digestion and absorption without reminding pupils what they learned before. Some pupils cannot even recall the names of the organs. Madam Sadia wonders: should the textbook not have built on what pupils already knew, adding depth each time instead of starting from scratch?

This topic looks at three ways of organising content in a science curriculum - topical, concentric and spiral - and shows why the spiral approach, used in Pakistan's NCP 2022-23, helps pupils build lasting understanding.

## Explanation

### Organisation and sequencing of content

When curriculum designers decide what to teach in each grade, they choose an approach to *organisation* (how topics are grouped) and *sequencing* (in what order topics appear). Three approaches are widely discussed in science education (Kochhar; Aggarwal).

**1. Topical (or logical) organisation.** Each topic is taught once, completely, at one grade level. For example, the whole of "Light" might be taught in Grade 6, and the whole of "Sound" in Grade 7. The advantage is completeness: every fact about light sits in one place. The disadvantage is that pupils may forget light by the time they reach Grade 8, because they never return to it.

**2. Concentric organisation.** A set of topics is taught every year, but each year the circle widens: more detail is added. This is an improvement on topical organisation because pupils revisit ideas. However, without a clear plan for *what depth to add each time*, teachers may simply repeat the same content in a larger circle without building real understanding.

**3. Spiral organisation.** The spiral approach, proposed by the psychologist Jerome Bruner (1960), goes further. The same core concepts return at each grade, but each return adds *new depth*, *new connections* and *new applications* - not just more detail. Figure fig-U2-5 shows how the concept of "matter" spirals from Grade 4 to Grade 8.

Bruner's key idea was that "any subject can be taught in some intellectually honest form to any child at any stage of development" (Bruner, 1960). A Grade 4 pupil can learn that ice, water and steam are the same substance in different forms. A Grade 8 pupil can learn *why* they change, using the particle model. The concept is the same; the depth grows.

<Figure id="fig-U2-6" src="/img/figures/efpg-402/unit-02/fig-U2-6.svg" alt="Table comparing topical, concentric and spiral organisation of curriculum content on five features, showing that the spiral approach reduces forgetting and matches the NCP 2022-23 progression grid." />

**How the NCP 2022-23 uses the spiral approach.** The National Curriculum's progression grid lists the same domains (life, physical, earth and space sciences) for every grade from 4 to 8. Within each domain, the same concepts appear again and again, each time with deeper SLOs. For example, the concept "properties of matter" appears at every grade, moving from sorting solids and liquids (Grade 4) to explaining changes of state with the particle model (Grade 8) (National Curriculum Council, 2023). This is a spiral in action.

**Worked example: tracing "the human body" through the spiral.** Open the NCP progression grid for life sciences. In Grade 4, pupils "identify the main parts of the human body and their basic functions." In Grade 6, they "describe the functions of the main organs of the digestive system." In Grade 8, they "explain how the circulatory and respiratory systems work together." The concept is "the human body," but each return asks for a deeper kind of understanding: naming parts, then describing functions, then explaining how systems interact.

**Why Madam Sadia's problem happens.** When a textbook does not follow the spiral well, pupils meet a topic at one grade with no connection to what they learned before. The jump from labelling organs (Grade 5) to enzymes and chemical digestion (Grade 7) is too large because the intervening steps are missing. The spiral approach prevents this by planning what depth to add at each grade.

> **Common misconception:** "Science should be taught on its own, apart from other subjects." This misconception is about organisation at a broader level. Subjects do not live in separate boxes in the real world: a pupil measuring rainfall uses science and mathematics together. The spiral approach makes it easier to plan these connections, because the same concept returns at each grade and can be linked to what pupils are learning in mathematics or language that year. Topic 2.5 explores this idea further.

## Activity: Tracing a concept through the spiral

Work in groups of three. About 25 minutes.

1. Your teacher gives each group a copy of the NCP 2022-23 progression grid (or the relevant pages). Each group receives a different concept: "plants and their needs," "forces and motion," or "the water cycle."
2. Find your concept at Grade 4, Grade 6 and Grade 8. For each grade, write one sentence saying what depth the SLO asks for.
3. Draw a simple spiral on paper with three loops (Grade 4, 6, 8) and write your one-sentence depth at each loop.
4. Present your spiral to another group. Together, decide: does the depth grow smoothly, or is there a jump that might cause trouble for pupils?

## Check your understanding

1. Name the three approaches to organising curriculum content and give a one-sentence meaning for each. *(Remember)*
2. Explain, in three or four sentences, how a spiral curriculum differs from a concentric one. *(Understand)*
3. A school teaches the whole of "electricity" in Grade 6 and never returns to it. Name the approach and explain one risk it carries for pupils in Grade 8. *(Apply)*
4. Look at Madam Sadia's problem. Suggest how a spiral approach would have prevented the gap between Grade 5 and Grade 7 in the topic of digestion. *(Apply)*

## Summary

A science curriculum can be organised topically (each topic once), concentrically (topics revisited with more detail) or in a spiral (core concepts revisited with planned new depth, connections and applications). The NCP 2022-23 uses a spiral approach through its progression grid, so that concepts like matter, the human body and forces grow deeper from Grade 4 to Grade 8. When a textbook does not follow the spiral well, pupils face sudden jumps in difficulty.

## Try this at your practicum school

Choose one science topic that appears in both Grade 5 and Grade 7 at your practicum school (for example, "plants" or "matter"). Read the relevant textbook chapters for both grades. Write two sentences: one about what depth Grade 5 reaches and one about what depth Grade 7 adds. Note whether the jump in depth is smooth or sudden. Bring your notes to the next seminar.

## Summative task

A provincial curriculum committee asks you to explain why a spiral approach is better than a topical approach for the concept of "forces" in Grades 4 to 8. Write 200-250 words. Compare the two approaches for this concept, give a concrete example of what pupils would learn at three different grades under each approach, and explain one risk of the topical approach and how the spiral approach avoids it.
