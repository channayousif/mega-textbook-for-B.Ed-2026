# Unit 2 Assessment: Science Curriculum and Textbook

## Unit summary

A science curriculum is a plan for what pupils should learn, in what order and how. It is not the same as a textbook: the curriculum comes first, and the textbook is one tool for carrying it out. Good curriculum construction follows five principles: it is child-centred, activity-based, relevant, flexible and integrated. Pakistan's National Curriculum 2022-23 for Science, Grades 4-8, demonstrates all five (Topic 2.1).

The national curriculum aims to build scientific literacy through six directions: understanding concepts across three domains (life, physical, earth and space sciences), developing enquiry skills through the Scientific Enquiry strand, building scientific attitudes, applying science to daily life, understanding science in society and the environment, and preparing for further study and work. These broad aims are translated into measurable Student Learning Outcomes for each grade. The curriculum flows from the national level through provincial adoption (DCAR Sindh) to textbooks and classrooms (Topic 2.2).

Content in the curriculum is organised using a spiral approach: core concepts return at each grade with planned new depth, connections and applications, so that pupils build on what they already know rather than starting from scratch. This approach, based on Bruner's (1960) idea that any concept can be taught at any age in an honest form, is better than topical (once-and-done) or concentric (more detail without planned depth) organisation (Topic 2.3).

A science textbook should be evaluated against six criteria: accuracy, curriculum alignment, language, activities, illustrations and inclusivity. A teacher who can evaluate a textbook can fill its gaps and advise on better resources (Topic 2.4).

Finally, science does not exist in a subject box. Correlation - planning connections between subjects - strengthens learning. Science connects naturally with mathematics (data and measurement), languages (vocabulary and writing), social studies (real-world issues) and Islamiat (ethics and stewardship). When teachers coordinate, pupils see that knowledge is connected (Topic 2.5).

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. Which principle of curriculum construction is shown when a Grade 4 science lesson uses objects pupils can hold, while a Grade 8 lesson on the same topic uses the particle model? *(Remember)*
   - A) Relevance
   - B) Flexibility
   - C) Child-centredness
   - D) Integration

2. A science curriculum includes a Scientific Enquiry strand alongside the three content domains. Which principle does this strand mainly serve? *(Understand)*
   - A) Flexibility
   - B) Activity basis
   - C) Integration
   - D) Child-centredness

3. How many content domains does the NCP 2022-23 for Science, Grades 4-8, have? *(Remember)*
   - A) Two
   - B) Three
   - C) Four
   - D) Five

4. Which of these is an *objective* rather than an *aim*? *(Understand)*
   - A) Develop scientific literacy in every pupil.
   - B) Build scientific attitudes such as curiosity and honesty.
   - C) Pupils will label five parts of a flower on a diagram.
   - D) Prepare pupils for further study and work.

5. A school teaches the whole of "Light" in Grade 6 and never returns to it. What approach to content organisation is this? *(Remember)*
   - A) Spiral
   - B) Concentric
   - C) Topical
   - D) Integrated

6. In a spiral curriculum, what happens each time a concept returns at a higher grade? *(Understand)*
   - A) The same content is repeated exactly.
   - B) More detail is added without new depth.
   - C) New depth, connections and applications are added.
   - D) The concept is replaced by a different one.

7. A Grade 5 textbook chapter on magnets asks pupils to use a spectrophotometer. Which textbook-evaluation criterion is weakest? *(Apply)*
   - A) Accuracy
   - B) Inclusivity
   - C) Activities (use of available materials)
   - D) Language

8. A teacher evaluates a textbook chapter and finds that it covers the NCP SLOs but has no activities. Which criterion should she score as "weak"? *(Apply)*
   - A) Curriculum alignment
   - B) Accuracy
   - C) Activities
   - D) Language

9. A science teacher asks pupils to draw a bar chart of plant growth data. Which subject is science most directly correlated with in this task? *(Apply)*
   - A) Social studies
   - B) Islamiat
   - C) Urdu
   - D) Mathematics

10. A teacher moves her water-cycle lesson to the week after the mathematics teacher teaches line graphs. This is an example of what? *(Apply)*
    - A) Textbook evaluation
    - B) Spiral organisation
    - C) Curriculum correlation
    - D) Concentric organisation

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

1. In three or four sentences, explain the difference between a curriculum and a textbook, using an example from science teaching. *(Understand)*
2. Name the five principles of science curriculum construction and give one example of how the NCP 2022-23 for Science shows each. *(Understand)*
3. Explain, in three or four sentences, how aims differ from objectives and why both are needed in a science curriculum. *(Understand)*
4. A teacher says, "I teach whatever is in the textbook." Analyse this statement using two of the five principles of curriculum construction. *(Analyze)*
5. Compare topical and spiral organisation of content in three or four sentences, naming one advantage and one disadvantage of each. *(Analyze)*
6. Explain why the spiral approach to content organisation reduces the risk of pupils forgetting earlier learning. Give one example from the NCP progression grid. *(Understand)*
7. A textbook chapter on water has no activities and uses only city-based examples. Name the two weakest criteria from the six-criterion checklist and explain why each matters. *(Analyze)*
8. A head teacher says, "Our science results are good, so the textbook must be fine." Use two criteria from the checklist to explain why good exam results do not prove a textbook is good. *(Analyze)*
9. Describe how science and mathematics are correlated and give one Grade 6-8 classroom example. *(Understand)*
10. A science teacher and an Islamiat teacher have never planned together. Suggest one connection they could make for a Grade 7 topic on the environment, and explain why it would help pupils. *(Apply)*

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. A provincial curriculum committee is reviewing the science curriculum for Grades 4-8. They ask you to evaluate the curriculum against the five principles of curriculum construction. Write a response that names each principle, gives evidence of how the NCP 2022-23 meets or fails to meet it, and suggests one improvement for the weakest principle. *(Evaluate)*

2. Two Grade 5 teachers in the same school take different approaches. Teacher A teaches each science topic once and moves on. Teacher B revisits the same concepts each term, adding new depth and applications. Evaluate which approach is likely to produce better understanding and explain why, using the ideas of spiral and topical organisation. *(Evaluate)*

3. You receive a new Grade 6 General Science textbook. Evaluate one chapter using all six criteria of the textbook-evaluation checklist. For each criterion, explain what you would look for, give a possible finding, and suggest what a teacher should do if the criterion is weak. *(Evaluate)*

4. Design a one-week correlated plan for a Grade 7 science topic on "The Water Cycle" that connects science, mathematics, Urdu and social studies. For each subject, state one activity, explain the connection to the science topic and justify why the sequence matters. *(Create)*

5. A parent tells you: "The textbook is the curriculum. If my child finishes the textbook, she has learned everything she needs." Write a reasoned reply that draws on all five topics of this unit: the principles of curriculum construction, the aims of the national science curriculum, how content is organised, how to evaluate a textbook, and how science connects with other subjects. *(Evaluate)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### MCQ answer key

1. C - child-centredness matches content to the pupil's age and thinking level; Grade 4 uses concrete objects, Grade 8 uses abstract models.
2. B - the Scientific Enquiry strand makes science active (observing, testing, concluding), which is the activity-basis principle.
3. B - the NCP 2022-23 has three content domains: life, physical, and earth and space sciences.
4. C - it names one observable, measurable outcome of one lesson; the others are broad, long-term aims.
5. C - topical organisation teaches each topic once at one grade level.
6. C - in a spiral curriculum each return adds new depth, connections and applications, not just more detail.
7. C - a spectrophotometer is not available in most schools, so the activities criterion is weakest.
8. C - the chapter aligns with the SLOs (so curriculum alignment is adequate) but has no activities.
9. D - drawing a bar chart of data is a mathematics skill used in a science context.
10. C - coordinating the timing of lessons in two subjects is curriculum correlation.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. Model: A curriculum is a plan for what pupils should learn, in what order and how; it is set by the national or provincial authority. A textbook is a resource that turns the curriculum into chapters, diagrams and exercises. For example, the NCP 2022-23 says Grade 6 pupils should "classify materials as conductors or insulators," and a textbook provides a chapter on this with definitions and a circuit activity. The textbook may cover the SLO well or poorly, but the SLO comes from the curriculum. **Marks (3):** 1 - defines curriculum; 1 - defines textbook as a resource; 1 - gives a science example showing the difference.

2. Model: (1) Child-centredness: different SLOs for each grade. (2) Activity basis: Scientific Enquiry strand. (3) Relevance: Science in Context strand links science to everyday problems. (4) Flexibility: national framework adapted by provinces (DCAR Sindh). (5) Integration: data-handling links to mathematics. **Marks (5):** 1 per principle with a correct NCP example.

3. Model: An aim is a broad, long-term direction, such as "develop scientific literacy." An objective is a specific, measurable outcome for one lesson, such as "pupils will label five parts of a flower." Both are needed because the aim gives the teacher a purpose, while the objective tells the teacher exactly what to look for in pupils' work. Without aims, teaching has no direction; without objectives, the teacher cannot check whether pupils have learned. **Marks (3):** 1 - defines aim with example; 1 - defines objective with example; 1 - explains why both are needed.

4. Model: First, this violates the principle of relevance: the textbook may not include examples from the pupils' community, and the teacher who follows it blindly misses the chance to connect science to local life. Second, it violates child-centredness: the textbook may not match the specific needs or thinking level of the pupils in the class. The curriculum - not the textbook - is the plan; the textbook is one resource for carrying it out. **Marks (4):** 1 per principle named correctly; 1 per analysis of how "teaching whatever is in the textbook" violates it.

5. Model: In topical organisation, each topic is taught once and not revisited. The advantage is completeness: everything about a topic sits in one place. The disadvantage is that pupils may forget by the next year. In spiral organisation, the same concepts return at each grade with new depth. The advantage is that revisiting reduces forgetting and builds deeper understanding. The disadvantage is that it needs careful planning to avoid mere repetition. **Marks (4):** 1 per advantage; 1 per disadvantage.

6. Model: The spiral approach reduces forgetting because pupils meet the same concept several times, each time connecting it to what they learned before. This revisiting strengthens memory. For example, the NCP treats "properties of matter" at every grade from 4 to 8: Grade 4 pupils sort solids and liquids; Grade 6 pupils use the particle model; Grade 8 pupils explain changes of state with the particle model. Each return builds on the last. **Marks (3):** 1 - explains why revisiting reduces forgetting; 1 - gives a valid NCP example; 1 - shows progression across grades.

7. Model: Activities is weakest because a chapter with no experiments or investigations means pupils learn only by reading, which contradicts the principle that science is learned by doing. Relevance (or inclusivity via "illustrations") is also weak: city-only examples ignore pupils in rural areas and disconnect science from their daily life. Activities matter because process skills develop through practice, not reading. Relevance matters because pupils who do not see their own world in the textbook lose motivation. **Marks (4):** 1 per criterion named; 1 per explanation of why it matters.

8. Model: First, curriculum alignment: the textbook may cover only the SLOs that appear on the exam and miss others, such as enquiry skills or science-in-society topics. Good results on a narrow exam do not prove the full curriculum is covered. Second, activities: a textbook with no experiments can still produce good exam results if the exam only tests recall, but pupils miss the process skills the curriculum aims to build. **Marks (4):** 1 per criterion named; 1 per analysis linking the criterion to the limitation of exam results as evidence.

9. Model: Science uses mathematics for measurement, data recording, calculation and graphing. When pupils measure and record data in a science investigation, they practise the mathematics they have learned. For example, in a Grade 7 lesson on plant growth, pupils measure stem height each week and plot the results as a line graph. The science gives the data a real context, and the mathematics gives science the tools to analyse the data. **Marks (3):** 1 - names the connection; 1 - gives a classroom example; 1 - explains mutual benefit.

10. Model: In a Grade 7 unit on ecosystems and the environment, the science teacher teaches how deforestation harms ecosystems. The Islamiat teacher, in a unit on stewardship (*khalifah*), teaches that human beings are responsible for caring for the earth. If the two teachers time these lessons in the same week, pupils see that the scientific evidence for protecting mangroves or canals connects to the ethical duty they learn in Islamiat. This helps pupils understand that protecting the environment is both a scientific and a moral responsibility. **Marks (3):** 1 - names a valid connection; 1 - explains what each teacher does; 1 - explains why the coordination helps pupils.

### ERQ rubrics

1. Analytic rubric: *Coverage of all five principles* (0-5: 1 per principle named and evaluated with evidence from the NCP) · *Quality of evaluation* (0-3, **Evaluate**: weighs evidence for and against, not only listing) · *Improvement suggestion* (0-2: specific, tied to the weakest principle, and realistic for a national curriculum).

2. Analytic rubric: *Comparison of approaches* (0-3: names both approaches accurately, with a concrete example for each) · *Quality of evaluation* (0-4, **Evaluate**: explains *why* one approach produces better understanding, using the idea of revisiting and depth, not only stating a preference) · *Use of evidence* (0-3: cites a specific concept or grade-level example to support the argument).

3. Analytic rubric: *Application of all six criteria* (0-6: 1 per criterion scored with evidence) · *Judgement quality* (0-2, **Evaluate**: reaches an overall judgement that weighs across criteria) · *Practical suggestions* (0-2: realistic improvements tied to specific weak criteria).

4. Analytic rubric: *Design of correlated plan* (0-4: one activity per subject, clearly connected to the science topic) · *Justification of sequence* (0-3, **Analyze**: explains why the order matters for pupils' learning, not just listing activities) · *Realism* (0-3: activities are feasible in a typical Sindh school, with available materials and realistic timing).

5. Analytic rubric: *Coverage of all five topics* (0-5: 1 per topic used accurately in the reply) · *Quality of argument* (0-3, **Evaluate**: answers the parent's claim directly with evidence from each topic, acknowledging that the textbook does matter but is not the whole picture) · *Tone and audience* (0-2: respectful, clear, free of jargon a parent would not know).
