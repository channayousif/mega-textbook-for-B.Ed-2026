# Topic 2.5 - Correlation of science with other subjects

## A real classroom situation

Sir Imran teaches Grade 7 General Science in Mirpurkhas. He asks his pupils to measure the temperature of water every five minutes as it cools down. The pupils record the numbers, but they cannot draw a line graph because they have not yet learned line graphs in mathematics class. Sir Imran sends a message to the mathematics teacher, who replies: "Line graphs are in the third term. I haven't taught them yet." The lesson stalls.

Meanwhile, in the Urdu class next door, pupils are writing paragraphs about "the importance of trees" - the same topic Sir Imran's class studied last week in the chapter on ecosystems. Neither teacher knows what the other is doing.

This topic explores *correlation* - the idea that subjects are connected - and shows how planning these connections makes both science teaching and other subjects stronger.

## Explanation

### Correlation of science with other subjects

*Correlation* in curriculum design means planning deliberate connections between subjects so that what pupils learn in one class supports what they learn in another. It is not about merging subjects into one lesson every day, but about making sure the timing and content of different subjects reinforce each other (Aggarwal; Kochhar).

**Why correlation matters.** In the real world, problems do not come in subject boxes. A farmer deciding how much fertiliser to use needs science (what plants need), mathematics (how much per hectare), language (reading the label) and social studies (the effect on the canal downstream). When schools teach each subject in isolation, pupils struggle to transfer what they learn from one context to another.

**How science connects with four other subjects.** Figure fig-U2-9 shows an example with ecosystems. Here are the main connections:

**1. Science and mathematics.** Science uses mathematics for measurement, data recording, calculation and graphing. When a Grade 6 pupil measures the shadow of a stick every hour to study the sun's apparent movement, she uses a ruler (measurement), a table (data recording) and a line graph (mathematics). If the mathematics teacher has not yet taught line graphs, the science lesson stalls - as happened to Sir Imran. Coordination between the two teachers can prevent this.

**2. Science and languages (Urdu and English).** Science builds vocabulary, reading comprehension and writing. Pupils learn new terms (photosynthesis, evaporation, circuit), read informational text, write observations and reports, and present arguments. In bilingual classrooms, defining science terms in both English and Urdu helps pupils who think in Urdu understand the concept, not just memorise an English word.

**3. Science and social studies.** Many science topics connect to society: health (clean water, vaccination), environment (deforestation, flooding), technology (mobile phones, solar energy) and history (how scientific discoveries changed daily life). A science lesson on water pollution connects naturally to a social studies lesson on the rights of communities living near the canal.

**4. Science and Islamiat.** Science and faith are not in conflict, but they answer different kinds of questions (Topic 1.1). The concept of stewardship (*khalifah*) in Islamiat teaches that human beings are responsible for caring for the earth - which connects to science topics on the environment and conservation. The value of honesty, emphasised in both science and Islamiat, is another natural link.

**Worked example: coordinating a lesson on water.** Sir Imran wants to teach a Grade 7 lesson on the water cycle. He checks the mathematics teacher's plan and finds that bar charts are taught in the second week of term. He moves his water-cycle lesson to the third week so that pupils already know how to draw a bar chart. He asks the Urdu teacher to assign a paragraph on "how rain forms" the same week. And he asks the social studies teacher to cover flooding in Sindh in the following week, so pupils can link the science of the water cycle to its social effects. The result: four subjects reinforce one another, and pupils see that the water cycle is not just a diagram in a science textbook.

> **Common misconception:** "Science should be taught on its own, apart from other subjects." This misconception leads to the kind of isolation Sir Imran experienced. When science is taught in a box, pupils miss the connections that make it meaningful. Correlation does not mean every science lesson must include mathematics, Urdu and social studies at once. It means that teachers plan together so that what happens in one class supports what happens in another.

## Activity: Planning a correlated week

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. Each group member takes the role of one teacher: science, mathematics, Urdu/English, social studies.
2. The science teacher picks a Grade 6 topic from the NCP progression grid (for example, "properties of matter" or "plants and their needs").
3. Each teacher writes one activity for their subject that connects to the science topic.
4. Together, the group arranges the four activities into a weekly timetable, deciding which comes first so that each builds on the one before.
5. Present your correlated week to the class and explain one connection you planned.

## Check your understanding

1. Define the word *correlation* as used in curriculum design and give one example. *(Remember)*
2. Explain, in three sentences, why teaching subjects in isolation can cause problems. Use Sir Imran's situation as an example. *(Understand)*
3. A Grade 7 science lesson asks pupils to calculate the percentage of starch in different foods. The mathematics teacher has not yet taught percentages. Suggest two steps the science teacher could take. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Correlation means planning connections between subjects so that learning in one class supports learning in another. Science connects naturally with mathematics (through data and measurement), with languages (through vocabulary and writing), with social studies (through real-world issues) and with Islamiat (through ethics and stewardship). When teachers coordinate, pupils see that knowledge is connected. When subjects are taught in isolation, pupils miss these connections and struggle to transfer learning.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask one science teacher and one teacher of another subject (mathematics, Urdu or social studies) whether they ever plan lessons together. Write down their answers. If they do, note one example of a connection they planned. If they do not, suggest one connection they could make and note their response. Bring your notes to the next seminar.

## Summative task

A school's science and mathematics teachers have never planned together. The science teacher wants to teach a Grade 7 unit on "Energy" that includes measuring temperature, drawing graphs and calculating energy use. The mathematics teacher covers graphs in the same term but two weeks later. Write 200-250 words proposing a plan for coordinating the two subjects for this unit. Explain what each teacher would do, when they would do it and why the coordination would help pupils.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Developing (1) | Secure (2) | Strong (3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coordination plan | Names the problem without a plan | Proposes a plan with a role for each teacher | Proposes a plan with specific timing, roles and reasons for each step |
| Justification *(Analyze)* | States that coordination helps without explaining why | Explains why coordination helps with one reason | Explains why coordination helps with reasons linked to how pupils learn |
| Practical feasibility | Plan ignores the school context | Plan is realistic for a typical Sindh school | Plan is realistic and names one obstacle and how to handle it |
