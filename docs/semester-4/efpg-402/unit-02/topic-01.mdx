# Topic 2.1 - Principles of science curriculum construction

## A real classroom situation

Sir Tariq teaches Grade 6 General Science at a government school in Sukkur. This year the school received a new set of textbooks. Sir Tariq notices that the first chapter asks pupils to "observe the behaviour of iron filings near a magnet", but the school has no magnets and no iron filings. The next chapter discusses water purification with a diagram of a modern treatment plant that none of his pupils have ever seen. Meanwhile, the pupils are curious about why the canal behind the school turns green every summer - but the textbook says nothing about algae until Grade 8.

Sir Tariq wonders: who decided what goes into this textbook, and what rules did they follow? This topic introduces the principles that curriculum designers use - and that you, as a future teacher, can use to judge whether a curriculum serves your pupils well.

## Explanation

### Principles of science curriculum construction

A *curriculum* is a plan for what pupils should learn, in what order and how. It is not the same as a textbook: the curriculum comes first, and the textbook is one tool for carrying it out. When curriculum designers build a science curriculum, they follow a set of principles - guiding ideas that shape every decision about content, activities and assessment. Five principles appear again and again in the literature on science curriculum construction (Kochhar; Aggarwal).

**1. Child-centredness.** The curriculum is built around the learner, not around the subject alone. Content, language and activities match the age and thinking level of the pupils. A Grade 4 lesson on matter lets pupils sort solid, liquid and gas objects they can hold; a Grade 8 lesson on the same topic introduces the particle model. The National Curriculum of Pakistan 2022-23 for Science, Grades 4-8, shows this principle: it sets different Student Learning Outcomes (SLOs) for each grade, with concepts growing deeper as the child grows older (National Curriculum Council, 2023).

**2. Activity basis.** Science is learned best by doing. A good science curriculum includes observations, experiments, model-building and fieldwork, not only reading and listening. The NCP 2022-23 places a Scientific Enquiry strand alongside the three content domains (life, physical, earth and space sciences), so that process skills are taught in every unit, not in a separate chapter (National Curriculum Council, 2023).

**3. Relevance.** What pupils learn should connect to their daily life and to the problems of their community. In Sindh, relevant science topics include canal water quality, monsoon flooding, dengue prevention, solar energy for tube wells and cotton pest management. A curriculum that uses only foreign examples loses this connection.

**4. Flexibility.** A rigid curriculum becomes outdated quickly and cannot serve different regions equally. Flexibility means that the national framework sets the core standards, but provinces and schools can add local content. The Directorate of Curriculum, Assessment and Research (DCAR) Sindh adapts the national curriculum for the province, and a teacher in a coastal town can add an example about mangroves even if the textbook does not mention them (DCAR Sindh).

**5. Integration.** Science does not exist in a box. A good curriculum links science with mathematics (for measurement and data), with language (for reading and writing about investigations), with social studies (for understanding science in society) and with Islamic studies (for the ethics of using science). You will explore this further in Topic 2.5.

**Worked example: judging Sir Tariq's textbook.** Look at Sir Tariq's situation again. The textbook asks for magnets the school does not have (not flexible), uses a foreign water-treatment-plant diagram (not relevant) and postpones algae to Grade 8 even though the pupils see it every summer (not child-centred, because it ignores what they are curious about now). Using the five principles, you can name exactly what is wrong - and suggest what a better curriculum would do.

> **Common misconception:** "The textbook is the curriculum." Many teachers and parents believe the textbook *is* what pupils must learn. In fact, the curriculum is the plan; the textbook is one resource for carrying it out. A teacher who follows the curriculum's aims may sometimes go beyond the textbook - for example, by investigating the canal behind the school - and still be teaching the right content. This misconception matters because it stops teachers from adapting their teaching to their pupils' needs.

## Activity: Principles in your own schooling

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

1. Each partner thinks of one science lesson from their own school days (Matric or intermediate) that they remember clearly.
2. For each remembered lesson, decide together which of the five principles it followed well and which it missed. Write your judgement in a two-column table: "Principle followed" and "Principle missed", with one sentence of evidence for each.
3. Then answer: if you could redesign that lesson, which one missing principle would you add first, and how? Share your redesign with another pair.

## Check your understanding

1. Define the word *curriculum* in one sentence and explain how it differs from a textbook. *(Remember)*
2. Name all five principles of science curriculum construction and give a one-line meaning for each. *(Remember)*
3. A school's Grade 5 science syllabus has no experiments and no activities - only reading passages and end-of-chapter questions. Which two principles does it violate, and why? *(Apply)*

## Summary

A science curriculum is a plan for learning, not a textbook. Good curriculum construction follows five principles: it is child-centred, activity-based, relevant, flexible and integrated. Pakistan's National Curriculum 2022-23 for Science shows all five. A teacher who understands these principles can judge whether a curriculum or textbook serves pupils well - and can fill the gaps when it does not.

## Try this at your practicum school

Look at the science syllabus or yearly plan the school uses for one grade. For each of the five principles, note one place where the plan follows the principle and one place where it does not. Bring your notes to the next seminar.

## Summative task

A district education officer asks you to review the first unit of a Grade 5 General Science syllabus. The unit lists twelve definitions for pupils to memorise, includes one diagram of a laboratory the school does not have, and uses examples only from large cities. No activities or experiments are included. Write 200-250 words evaluating this unit against the five principles of curriculum construction, and suggest two specific changes that would improve it.
