# Market Research and Business Models - Notes for Teachers

The guide names lectures, interactive discussions, question-answer sessions, demonstration and case-study analysis as teaching strategies, and hands-on computer exercises, group assignments, internet research tasks and presentations as practical work. These notes show one way to use them across the two weeks.

## Sequencing the two weeks

- **Week 5 (Topic 3.1).** Open with: "Hina's friends all loved her idea. Was that market research?" Take a show of hands, teach the definition, then return to the vote. Use a short lecture for data-collection methods, a demonstration for the questionnaire (show a bad question and ask the class to repair it), and a case study for the competitor analysis. The school's own locked science kit box is a good local case.
- **Week 6 (Topic 3.2).** Demonstrate first: fill the nine blocks of the canvas on the board for one venture the class knows, with suggestions from the room. Then run the "Draw the model, then pitch the value" activity, and end with the three-suggestions discussion from ERQ 5, so trainees connect the model to the research evidence of Week 5.

## Likely misconceptions and how to handle them

- **"My friends liking my idea is market research."** Ask: "How many people did you ask, and how many would actually pay?"
- **"Having no competitors means a great opportunity."** Ask the class to name a need nobody serves in their town, then ask why. Competitors are evidence of demand.
- **"A business model is the same as a business plan."** Ask one trainee to fill the nine blocks in two minutes, and another to say what a bank would want in a plan.

**One good discussion prompt.** "Is a school that charges a small fee for a Saturday club running a business, and should it be?" Expect a split. Use it to show that the same model and revenue questions apply to a school venture and a shop.

## Practical work: a small market-research exercise

1. In groups of three, trainees choose one small venture that could serve their practicum school. They may use the internet only for secondary data, such as school enrolment figures or what a local shop sells.
2. Each group writes one research question, names the target customer, and picks two methods, one primary and one secondary. If the placement allows, they ask five to ten target customers a short question in person.
3. Each group lists two competitors, one direct and one indirect, and says how the venture would be different from each.
4. Each group fills the nine blocks of the canvas, writes a one-sentence value proposition and picks two revenue streams.
5. Groups give a five-minute presentation, and the class asks one challenging question per group.

Follow your institution's consent rules. Do not record anyone without permission, and leave out names if the person prefers. Judge whether each group's claims rest on evidence it actually gathered, not on whether the idea sounds good.
