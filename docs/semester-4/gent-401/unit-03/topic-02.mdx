## A real classroom situation

Bilal is a B.Ed trainee on practicum at a government boys' middle school in Sukkur. The science teacher has been on leave for two months, and the head teacher asks Bilal to run a Saturday science club for Class 8. Bilal agrees. Then the questions begin.

His friend Imran says, "Charge 200 rupees a month per pupil and keep the money." His friend Sajid says, "Make a simple kit and charge the school 50 rupees a week to lend it to pupils." Bilal's uncle, who runs a pharmacy, says, "First write a business plan." So Bilal writes twenty pages about his idea, the school, and his qualifications. But the uncle reads it and asks three questions Bilal cannot answer: "Who exactly will pay you? What do they get that they cannot get free? And where does the money come from?" By the end of this topic you will be able to answer all three.

## Explanation

Topic 3.1 asked whether customers exist. This topic asks how the venture works once you believe they do. It answers three questions. What is a business model, and what shapes it? What value do you promise the customer? And where does the money come from?

### Types of business models

A **<Glossary term="Business model">business model</Glossary>** is the plan of how a venture creates value for customers and captures some of that value back as income. Scott (2017, p. 53) calls it "a workable business strategy or the way an organization does business". The OpenStax textbook quotes the definition by Osterwalder and Pigneur: a business model "describes the rationale of how an organization creates, delivers and captures value" (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 11.2). A model covers who the customer is, what is offered, how it reaches them, and how money flows back.

Models differ in what they optimise. Scott (2017, pp. 53-57) sets out the choices as pairs of opposites, and his point is that none of them is right by default:

- **Sales volume or profit margin.** A supermarket sells food at a thin mark-up and wins on volume; a pharmacy sells it at a fat mark-up and wins on each sale. Scott (2017, p. 54) recalls the economist Bernardo Trujillo's advice: percentages do not go into a bank, only money does.
- **High price or low price.** Higher prices can signal higher value, but a high price needs a product good enough to carry it.
- **Market share or profitability.** Scott (2017, p. 55) tells of the South African retailer Pick 'n Pay, whose chairman put profit per store ahead of the number of stores, and grew faster than a rival chasing market share.
- **Less choice or more choice.** A travel agency cut its international tours from twelve to three and they filled up; too much choice baffled its customers (Scott, 2017, p. 55).
- **Competition or cooperation.** Rivals can share purchases, training or distribution and cut costs (Scott, 2017, p. 55).
- **Selling a product or selling a service.** A chemical maker switched to selling the benefit: it applies the chemical, cleans up, and takes it back for reuse (Scott, 2017, pp. 56-57).

OpenStax names three broad revenue architectures that follow from these choices: the **direct model**, where users pay you; the **multisided model**, where one group uses the service free and a second group pays to reach them; and the **marketplace model**, where you join buyers and sellers and take a share (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 11.1).

A useful tool for laying a model out is the **Business Model Canvas**, nine blocks on one page: key partners, key activities, key resources, value propositions, customer relationships, channels, customer segments, cost structure and revenue streams (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 11.2). Spriggs (2016) describes it as a tool that students and teachers can use to describe, organise and design a model, and as a way to test an idea with customer feedback rather than writing the whole plan first. The diagram at the start of this topic fills the nine blocks for Bilal's Saturday science club.

**Correcting a common misconception: "A business model is the same as a business plan."** A business model is a working picture of how the venture creates, delivers and captures value, and it is meant to be redrawn as you learn. A business plan is a longer document that sets out the venture's goals, operations, market and finances for a reader such as a bank. OpenStax stresses that a model is "outlining an approach but changing that approach" when your assumptions turn out wrong (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 11.1), and Scott (2017, p. 58) adds that reworking a plan is not weakness if the plan is proving futile. Bilal's twenty pages were not the problem; the model inside them was never made clear.

**A worked example: filling the nine blocks.** Bilal fills the canvas for the Saturday science club.

| Block | Bilal's answer |
|---|---|
| Key partners | The school (room, pupils, head teacher), a local science teacher who can help |
| Key activities | Plan experiments, run Saturday sessions, check pupils' work |
| Key resources | A kit box, printed worksheets, a classroom, Bilal's own time |
| Value propositions | Pupils who miss school catch up; parents see progress (see below) |
| Customer relationships | Weekly face-to-face sessions, a notice to parents |
| Channels | The school gate, the head teacher's announcement, a class WhatsApp group |
| Customer segments | Class 8 pupils who need science support, and their parents who pay |
| Cost structure | Printing, kit materials, transport, Bilal's time |
| Revenue streams | A monthly fee from parents, plus a small fee for the worksheets |

Notice that the last block is where his uncle's third question gets its answer.

### Value proposition

A **<Glossary term="Value proposition">value proposition</Glossary>** is a short statement of the benefit a customer can expect: what problem is solved, what gain is offered, and why this offering is better than the alternatives. OpenStax defines it as a summary describing the benefits, or value, customers can expect (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 11.1). The same section reports Clayton Christensen's idea of a job to be done: a customer "hires" a product to get a job done. A value proposition works when it names that job clearly.

OpenStax also describes a **Value Proposition Canvas**, which sets the customer's pains and gains against the venture's pain relievers, gain creators and products (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 11.2). The point is to start from the customer's life, not from your product.

**A worked example: drafting a value proposition.** Spriggs (2016) gives candidate value propositions for a driving school that teaches manual gearboxes: "Helping you learn to drive a manual transmission safely"; "Learn to drive a manual transmission without harming the car or your friendships". Each names a job and a gain. Bilal tries three for the science club.

| Draft | What it names |
|---|---|
| "Saturday science practice for Class 8 pupils who missed school" | The job (catch up) and the customer |
| "A pupil who can solve physics numericals without a teacher at home" | The gain, in the pupil's own words |
| "Cheap science tuition at the school gate" | The price, but no gain beyond cheapness |

The first two work. The third is weak because price alone is easy to copy, and a rival with a lower price can beat it tomorrow. A strong value proposition says what the customer gets that they cannot get elsewhere.

**Correcting a common misconception: "A value proposition is a slogan."** A slogan is made to be remembered; a value proposition is made to be tested. It should be specific enough that a customer could say no to it, and it should point to evidence. Bilal's first draft can be tested: ask twenty Class 8 pupils whether they would come on Saturdays for catch-up practice.

### Revenue streams

A **<Glossary term="Revenue stream">revenue stream</Glossary>** is one distinct way a venture brings in money. A venture may have one or several. Scott (2017, p. 144) makes a sharp point: price is the only part of the marketing mix that produces revenue; everything else in marketing is a cost. So the revenue question is really two questions: what do customers pay for, and on what basis?

OpenStax names three revenue architectures to think about (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 11.1). In a **direct** model the customer pays you: a pupil buys a worksheet. In a **multisided** model one group gets the service free and another group pays to reach them: pupils attend free and a publisher pays to put its guide in their hands. In a **marketplace** model you join buyers and sellers and take a share of each deal: a school fair where you collect a fee from each stall.

<Figure id="fig-U3-4" src="/img/figures/gent-401/unit-03/fig-U3-4.svg" alt="Table of five common revenue streams - sale, subscription, fee, advertising and licensing - each with a short note on how it works and one example from a school-based venture." />

**A worked example: two revenue streams for the science club.** Bilal picks two from the table.

| Stream | How Bilal would use it | What it depends on |
|---|---|---|
| Subscription | Parents pay 200 rupees a month for four Saturday sessions | Pupils must keep coming, so the sessions must be worth it |
| Sale of materials | Pupils buy the printed worksheet pack for 50 rupees a term | The worksheets must be useful enough to buy, not just free handouts |

He drops advertising, because he has no audience to sell. He drops licensing, because he owns no name or design yet. Two streams suit a small club better than five.

**Correcting a common misconception: "More revenue streams are always better."** Scott (2017, p. 58) warns businesses not to try to be all things to all people, because they end up confused and unfocused. A venture with five half-built streams spends its energy on all five and serves none well. Start with one or two streams that fit the customer's job, then add another only when the first is steady.

## Activity: Draw the model, then pitch the value

Work in groups of four. About 30 minutes.

1. Each member names one small venture that could serve your practicum school: a tuition centre, a stationery supply, a canteen item, a repair service, a learning-materials seller.
2. Choose one venture. Together, fill the nine blocks of the business-model canvas on a large sheet. Write no more than six words in each block.
3. Write a one-sentence value proposition for it, naming the job the customer hires it to do.
4. Pick two revenue streams from the table and say what each depends on.
5. Pass your sheet to another group. They must cross out one block they think is empty or copied, and one revenue stream they think does not fit.

Explain your choices in two minutes. The other group's challenge must be answered with a reason, not a defence.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Without looking back, write one sentence defining a business model and one sentence defining a revenue stream.
2. *(Remember)* Name the nine blocks of the business-model canvas.
3. *(Understand)* In three sentences, explain why a business model is not the same as a business plan, using Bilal's case.
4. *(Understand)* A classmate says, "I will charge the lowest price in the market, so my value proposition is price." Give two reasons, from this topic, why that is weak.
5. *(Apply)* A trainee in Hyderabad wants to run an evening mathematics club for Class 9 pupils in a government school. Fill in four blocks of the canvas for that club and draft one sentence of value proposition.

## Summary

A business model is the plan of how a venture creates value for customers and captures some of it back as income. Models differ in what they optimise: volume or margin, high price or low, market share or profit, few choices or many, competition or cooperation, product or service. The Business Model Canvas lays the model out in nine blocks so it can be redrawn as evidence arrives. A value proposition is a short, testable statement of the benefit a customer can expect, built from the customer's job to be done. Revenue streams are the distinct ways money comes in, and a small venture is usually better with one or two that fit than with many half-built ones.

- [ ] I can define a business model and explain how it differs from a business plan.
- [ ] I can fill in the nine blocks of a business-model canvas for a described venture.
- [ ] I can write a one-sentence value proposition that names the customer's job and gain.
- [ ] I can choose two revenue streams for a described venture and say what each depends on.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher or the head teacher about one income the school already earns or could earn: a canteen rent, a uniform supplier's commission, or a fee for using the hall. Write five lines that fill four blocks of the canvas (customer segments, value proposition, revenue streams, cost structure) for that income, and name its revenue stream from the table.

## Summative task

Write 250-300 words about one small venture you could start for families around your practicum school.

1. Fill in six blocks of the business-model canvas for it, and state what your model optimises (volume, margin, market share, profit, choice or service).
2. Write a one-sentence value proposition that names the customer's job and the gain.
3. Choose two revenue streams and explain, for each, what it depends on and why it fits this customer.
4. Analyse one way your model could fail, and say what evidence from Topic 3.1 would warn you before you spent money.

*(Analyze)*
