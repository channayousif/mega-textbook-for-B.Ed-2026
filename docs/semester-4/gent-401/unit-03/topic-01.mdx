## A real classroom situation

Hina is a B.Ed trainee on practicum at a government girls' high school in Larkana. In the physics room she notices a locked steel box labelled "science kit", untouched for two years because nobody trained the staff to use it. Hina has an idea: she will buy low-cost kits with magnets, lenses and simple circuits, and sell them to pupils for board exam practice.

She tells her two roommates and her cousin. All three say it is a wonderful idea. Hina is ready to spend her savings on a hundred kits. Her cooperating teacher stops her with one question: "Have you asked the pupils what they need, and what they would pay?"

So Hina asks. She talks to forty pupils in two Class 9 sections and to two science teachers. She learns that pupils worry most about physics numericals and want cheap practice sheets, not kits. She visits two shops in the town that sell science materials and notes what they stock and what they charge. By the end of this topic you will be able to do what Hina did, and to explain why her friends' approval was not enough.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. Who are your customers and how big is your market? How do you collect data you can trust? And who else is already serving these customers? The cycle above is the spine of all three. Start with a question you can answer with evidence, choose a method, collect the data, analyse it, and then decide: go on, change the idea, or stop.

### Understanding customers and markets

**<Glossary term="Market research">Market research</Glossary>** is the collection and analysis of information about the people who might buy from a business. The OpenStax textbook defines it as the collection and analysis of data related to a business's target market, and says its purpose is to build an understanding of customer needs and wants (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 8.2). A **market** is simply all the buyers, or potential buyers, of a particular product or service (Scott, 2017, p. 34). A **target market** is the part of that market a venture decides to serve. A **target customer** is one group inside the target market, described clearly enough that you could recognise them: their age, where they live, what they earn, what they do each day.

Research should begin with the everyday life of the ideal customer. OpenStax suggests describing the customer's demographics (age, gender, income, education, location), psychographics (activities, interests, lifestyle) and behaviour (how often they use a product, and how they feel about it) (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 8.2). A market with two very different groups forces a choice: change the product, or serve only one group.

The OpenStax authors describe a three-step discipline often shortened to STP (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 8.2):

1. **Segmenting.** Divide the population into groups of people with similar tastes, backgrounds, incomes or needs.
2. **Targeting.** Choose the group or groups that can and will buy, and that you can reach.
3. **Positioning.** Decide how you want those customers to see your offering compared with the alternatives.

A **market opportunity** is an unmet need within a target group that a product or service could meet (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 8.2). Noticing the need is not enough. **Validation** is the act of checking that the need is real, usually by talking to potential customers or asking them to respond to a short survey (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 8.2).

**Correcting a common misconception: "My friends liking my idea is market research."** Friends are a tiny and friendly sample. They know you, they do not want to hurt your feelings, and they are not the people who will pay. Hina's three friends all approved of her kit idea, and all three were wrong for her purpose. Real market research talks to the target customers, asks questions that do not push them towards the answer you want, and then checks the answer another way. A friend's approval is encouragement, not evidence.

**A worked example: building a customer profile.** Hina writes down what she learned about the pupils she wants to serve.

| Question | What Hina found |
|---|---|
| Who are they? | Girls in Class 9 and 10 at a government school in Larkana |
| What do they worry about? | Physics numericals in the board exam |
| What do they do now? | Copy solved examples from the board; share one tattered guidebook |
| What can they pay? | About 30 to 50 rupees for something they will use weekly |
| Where do they look? | The school gate, the one stationery shop in the bazaar |

Her profile tells her what to sell: cheap practice sheets in Sindhi and Urdu, not kits. One conversation with the right people changed the whole venture.

### Data-collection methods

Data comes in two kinds. **Primary data** is information you collect yourself, first-hand, for your own purpose. **Secondary data** is information someone else already collected, usually for another purpose (Scott, 2017, pp. 35-37). Secondary data is cheaper and faster to get, because it already exists. Primary data is slower and costs more, but it answers your exact question.

OpenStax sets out six steps for primary research (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 8.2): define the goal, choose the design, choose a sample, collect the data, analyse it, and act on the results. Scott (2017, pp. 35-37) describes the same movement as four stages: define the question, choose the tools, collect the information, analyse it.

<Figure id="fig-U3-2" src="/img/figures/gent-401/unit-03/fig-U3-2.svg" alt="Table of four data-collection methods - survey, interview, observation and secondary data - with a rough cost and what each one reveals, and a note that no single method is enough on its own." />

The table shows the four methods Hina could use, what each costs, and what each is good at revealing. Notice what the table cannot tell you: how to ask. Scott (2017, pp. 37-38) gives a warning worth remembering. A questionnaire should be simple, clear and short. It should not ask biased questions, use words the public does not know, ask about money or other private matters, or go on so long that people give up. He shows a bad question: "Would you be interested in a new restaurant if it opened in this area?" Almost everyone says yes, and the answer proves nothing. A better question is: "On average, how many times a month do you eat out?"

One more rule matters more than any other. Scott (2017, p. 39) urges researchers to use more than one source and method to confirm a result, a process he calls **triangulation**. Ask a question in a survey, then watch what people actually do, then read what the government or a trade body has published. If all three point the same way, you can decide with confidence.

**A worked example: a short questionnaire and a count.** Hina asks pupils which subject is hardest, how they practise numericals, what they would pay, and where they buy materials. She also counts how many customers stop at the science shelf in each of the two bazaar shops. The survey tells her what pupils say; the count tells her what the shops actually sell.

**Correcting a common misconception: "One good survey is all the research you need."** People say they will buy and then do not, and a survey of one class may not represent the whole school. Triangulation is the answer: combine at least one primary method with one secondary method before you commit money (Scott, 2017, p. 39).

### Competitor analysis

A **competitor** is any business that serves the same customer need, whether or not it sells the same thing. A **competitor analysis** is the work of finding out who else serves your target customers, what they offer, how they price it, and where they are weak. Scott (2017, pp. 41-42) tells entrepreneurs to size up the competition and consider how the new business can fit in among it. OpenStax is blunter: the more you know about your competition, the easier it will be to work out how your offering is different (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 8.2).

Competitors come in two kinds. A **direct competitor** sells much the same thing to much the same people: the second stationery shop in the same bazaar. An **indirect competitor** meets the same need in a different way: a YouTube channel that teaches physics numericals for free. Hina has both: the kit shop two streets away is direct, and the free videos pupils watch on a borrowed phone are indirect.

A useful way to lay out what you find is a **SWOT analysis**: Scott (2017, pp. 43-44) describes a chart of four boxes, strengths and weaknesses inside your own control and opportunities and threats outside it.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Having no competitors means a great opportunity."** It usually means the opposite. Competitors are evidence that customers exist and that money changes hands. If nobody serves a need you have spotted, ask why. Perhaps nobody can make it pay. Perhaps the need is smaller than it looked. A market with three small rivals is often a better bet than a market with none, because the rivals have already proved that people will pay.

**A worked example: Hina's competitor map.** She writes one row per rival and fills in what she observed.

| Rival | What they offer | Price | What they are weak at |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stationery shop A, bazaar | Guides and past papers, photocopied | 20 to 40 rupees a sheet set | No Sindhi or Urdu practice sheets; owner will not take orders |
| Stationery shop B, near the college | Printed guides, ready-made | 60 to 120 rupees | Too dear for Class 9 pupils; no practice questions |
| Free physics videos, online | Video lessons on numericals | Free | No practice questions to solve; needs a phone and data |
| The school's own locked kit box | Kits, unused | Free if staffed | Locked; nobody trained to use it |

The map shows Hina a gap: cheap, printed practice in Sindhi and Urdu, sold at the school gate and ordered in advance. It also shows a threat: the online videos are free, so her sheets must offer something a video cannot, such as questions to solve with a teacher's check.

## Activity: Test an idea before you spend on it

Work in groups of four. About 30 minutes.

1. Each member names one small venture that could serve your practicum school or its neighbourhood: a stationery shop, a tuition centre, a canteen item, a repair service, a learning-materials seller.
2. Choose one venture. Write one research question it needs answered, and name the target customer in a single sentence.
3. For that venture, pick two methods from the table and say what each would reveal. One must be primary and one secondary.
4. Each member writes two good survey questions and one bad one. The group swaps sets and marks the bad ones, saying why they fail.
5. As a group, list two possible competitors, one direct and one indirect, and say how the venture would be different from each.

Present your research plan to another group. They must challenge one of your two methods and name a better one.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Without looking back, write one sentence defining market research and one sentence saying why Hina's friends' approval was not enough.
2. *(Remember)* List the four data-collection methods in the table and state one thing each is good at revealing.
3. *(Understand)* A classmate says, "I will email a questionnaire to 2,000 people, so my research will be solid." Give two reasons, from this topic, why that may still mislead.
4. *(Understand)* In three sentences, explain the difference between a direct and an indirect competitor, with one school-based example of each.
5. *(Apply)* A trainee in Sukkur wants to sell low-cost mathematics practice sheets to Class 8 pupils in government schools. Build a short customer profile for that venture, using four of the five questions in the worked example.

## Summary

Market research is the collection and analysis of data about the people a venture wants to serve. It starts with a clear question, uses primary data you collect and secondary data that already exists, and confirms its findings by triangulation. A competitor analysis turns the same habit outward: it names who else serves the customer, what they charge and where the gap is. Friends' approval, a single survey and an empty market are weak evidence; real customers, checked in more than one way, are strong evidence.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can define market research, a target market and a customer segment, and explain why a venture needs them.
- [ ] I can tell primary data from secondary data and name what each method reveals.
- [ ] I can write a short questionnaire that avoids biased, unclear or overly personal questions.
- [ ] I can carry out a simple competitor analysis and use it to decide how a venture is different.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find one small business near your practicum school: a canteen, a stationery shop, a photocopy shop or a tuition centre. With the owner's permission, ask three questions: who are your main customers, what do they buy most often, and what do they complain about? Then name one direct and one indirect competitor you think the business has. Write five lines: what you learned, and one thing the owner could do differently. Do not record anyone without permission, and leave out names if the person prefers.

## Summative task

Write 250-300 words about a small venture you could start for the families around your practicum school.

1. Name the target customer in one sentence and describe their everyday need.
2. Plan the research: one research question, two data-collection methods (one primary, one secondary), and one way you will triangulate the answer.
3. Analyse two competitors, one direct and one indirect, and explain how the venture would be different from each.
4. Judge whether the evidence you describe would be strong enough to spend money on, and say why.

*(Analyze)*

**Mini-rubric (10 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Target customer and need (2 marks) | Customer named and specific, with a real everyday need (2) | Customer vague or need loosely stated (1) | Absent |
| Research plan (3 marks) | Question clear; two suitable methods; one triangulation step (3) | Two elements present, or methods unsuited (1-2) | None |
| Competitor analysis (3 marks) | Two rivals analysed, one direct and one indirect, with a realistic difference (3) | One rival, or rivals without a clear difference (1-2) | None |

The judgement criterion asks you to analyse. Saying "the research is good" without naming why does not earn full marks.
