# Developing a Business Plan - Notes for Teachers

The course guide specifies lectures, interactive discussions, question-answer sessions, demonstrations, and case-study analysis as instructional strategies, alongside computer exercises, group assignments, internet research, and presentations as practical work. These notes guide teacher educators in facilitating Unit 4 across Weeks 7 and 9.

## Sequencing the two weeks

- **Week 7 (Topic 4.1 - The plan's frame: components, summary, vision and mission).** Begin with the classroom scenario: ask trainees whether an executive summary can be drafted before the plan is constructed. Conduct a brief interactive lecture walking through the nine standard business plan sections. Use the four-part formula on the board to demonstrate drafting a mission statement live, contrasting it with a 10-year vision statement. Have student pairs critique each other's vision and mission drafts using the provided tests.
- **Week 9 (Topic 4.2 - Marketing, operations and organisation).** Open with the case of Mehran Reader to illustrate the dangers of operational chaos in small teams. Guide students through mapping an end-to-end operational flowchart, emphasizing the placement and criteria of quality control checkpoints. Facilitate a group activity where students design organizational charts for a 5-person educational enterprise, ensuring every core business function has assigned accountability.

## Likely misconceptions and how to handle them

- **"A business plan is written once and never changed."** Remind trainees that market realities always differ from initial assumptions. Ask: "What should a founder do when a timber supplier doubles their prices two weeks after launch?" Guide them to see that a business plan is an adaptable living tool used for continuous learning.
- **"The executive summary should be written first because it appears on page one."** Ask trainees: "Can you summarize a book before you have read or written the chapters?" Clarify that while the summary introduces the plan to external readers, it can only be synthesized accurately once operational, market, and financial analyses are finalized.
- **"A small business does not need an organizational structure."** Present a scenario where a school's urgent order is lost because nobody was assigned to log delivery receipts. Emphasize that role clarity prevents duplicate effort, eliminates blame-shifting, and protects customer trust even in two-person or three-person teams.

**One good discussion prompt.** "If a private school offers your non-profit educational venture a lucrative contract that distracts from your core mission of serving rural children, should you accept it?" Use this prompt to discuss mission drift and illustrate how clear vision and mission statements function as operational decision-making filters.

## Practical work: business plan workshop and pitch exercise

The course guide sets aside Week 16 for business plan submissions and presentations. Unit 4 provides the structural core for this course project. Facilitate a two-part classroom workshop:

1. **Collaborative Planning Sessions:** In teams of three or four, trainees select a viable educational venture idea for their district (such as low-cost science kits, local-language remedial reading packs, or a mobile preschool resource library). Teams use library or computer lab access to research local supplier costs and draft the company description, vision, mission, 4Ps marketing mix, operational workflow, and 5-person organizational chart.
2. **Peer Review Gallery Walk:** Each team displays their business plan framework on butcher paper or slides. Peer groups review the plans using the unit's rubrics, specifically testing whether the operational workflow has an explicit quality gate and whether the mission statement clearly delineates target customers.
3. **Five-Minute Mini-Pitch:** Each team delivers a concise 5-minute presentation summarizing their plan to the class acting as a school board or microfinance committee, answering questions regarding feasibility and risk management.

Ensure that all student projects adhere to local institutional safety guidelines, avoid promoting hazardous materials, and respect copyright when referencing published educational resources.
