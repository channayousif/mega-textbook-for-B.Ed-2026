## A real classroom situation

Nadia is a B.Ed trainee on practicum at a government girls' high school in Sukkur. Outside the school gate there are four small shops. Three of them sell the same pens, registers and geometry boxes at the same prices. Last month, a fourth shopkeeper, Rafiq, opened a copy of the first three.

A few streets away, a retired teacher named Sana Memon noticed something different. Pupils in Class 6 to 8 had no cheap practice sheets in Sindhi for mathematics. She wrote simple worksheet packs, tested them with her neighbour's children, and now prints them in small batches for three schools. Last year she hired two young women to help her.

In a Class 9 lesson on "work and earning", a pupil asks Nadia: "Miss, Rafiq and Sana Madam both run a business. Are they both entrepreneurs?" Nadia is not sure. By the end of this topic, you will be able to answer the pupil and explain why.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What is entrepreneurship? Why does it matter for a country's economy? And what do successful entrepreneurs have in common?

### Entrepreneurship: meaning and concept

The word "entrepreneur" comes from the French word *entreprendre*, which means "to undertake" or "to do something". Early writers used it for a person who took a money risk in running a business. Later writers added the job of managing the business. Today, economists mainly see entrepreneurs as people who bring new things into the world, notice opportunities others miss, and take risks when the future is uncertain (Butler, 2020).

The OpenStax textbook gives a clear working definition. An **entrepreneur** is someone who identifies and acts on an idea or a problem that nobody else has identified or acted on (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 1.1). **Entrepreneurship** is the process of doing this: spotting an opportunity to bring something new, acting on it, and accepting the risk that comes with it. The new thing can be a product, a service, a better method, or an old product taken to people who did not have it before.

An **entrepreneurial venture** is any new business, organisation or project that carries some risk because it acts on an opportunity that was not already established (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 1.1). A venture does not have to make a profit for its owner. Some ventures aim at a social need, such as teaching out-of-school children. You will meet these social enterprises in Unit 2.

**Correcting a common misconception: "An entrepreneur is anyone who owns a shop."** Owning a business and being an entrepreneur overlap, but they are not the same. A **small business owner** runs a business that follows a model that already exists, such as one more stationery shop beside three others. An entrepreneur creates something new (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 1.1). The two are not opposites. A shop owner who brings a product to an area where nobody sold it before is acting entrepreneurially. What matters is the new idea and the action, not the shop.

**A worked example: is this person acting as an entrepreneur?** Use three questions.

1. Did the person spot a problem or an opportunity that others had not acted on?
2. Did the person act on it, by making or offering something?
3. Did the person accept a real risk of losing time or money?

| Person | New problem or opportunity? | Acted on it? | Accepted risk? | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rafiq opens a fourth stationery shop with the same goods and prices | No, he copied an existing model | Yes | Yes | Small business owner, not acting entrepreneurially |
| Sana writes and sells low-cost Sindhi worksheet packs | Yes, nobody was meeting this need | Yes | Yes, her savings and time | Entrepreneur |
| A tutor in Larkana starts the town's first evening classes for girls who left school early | Yes | Yes | Yes | Entrepreneur, with a social aim |
| A teacher talks every day about opening a coaching centre but never starts | Yes | No | No | Not yet an entrepreneur: an idea alone is not enough |

The last row matters. Entrepreneurship needs action. An idea that is never tried is not yet entrepreneurship.

### Importance of entrepreneurship in economic development

**Economic development** means a lasting rise in the living standards of a country's people: more and better jobs, higher incomes, and better access to goods and services. Entrepreneurship is one of its engines.

Butler (2020) calls entrepreneurship the "unseen factor of production". Land, labour and money (capital) produce nothing on their own. Someone has to see how to combine them to make something people value. A field, a few workers and a loan only become a dairy business when a person decides to start it and takes the risk.

The concept map at the start of this topic shows five ways entrepreneurship supports development.

1. **Jobs.** A new venture needs workers. Sana's worksheet business started with one person and now pays two helpers.
2. **<Glossary term="Innovation">Innovation</Glossary>.** Entrepreneurs bring new products and better ways of working. Butler (2020) argues that the steady pressure to find cheaper, better and more useful products explains most of the long-run rise in living standards.
3. **Income.** Wages go to workers, earnings go to the owner, and taxes go to the state, which can spend them on schools and roads.
4. **Competition.** When a new venture offers something better, older businesses must improve their quality or prices to keep customers. Butler (2020) describes competition as an ongoing process in which entrepreneurs play a central part.
5. **Community welfare.** Many ventures meet local needs directly: cheaper learning materials, a clinic, clean drinking water or a bus service.

These effects feed each other. When workers earn more, they buy more, and new needs appear. New needs are new opportunities for the next entrepreneur.

**A worked example: tracing one venture.** A young woman in Hala starts making ajrak-patterned school bags from local cloth, sold through two shops and online.

| Effect | What happens in this case |
|---|---|
| Jobs | She employs three block-printers and a tailor from her village |
| Innovation | A traditional craft is used for a new, everyday product |
| Income | Workers earn wages; she earns profit; the shops earn a margin |
| Competition | Bag sellers in the town start offering better-made local bags |
| Community welfare | A local craft gains new buyers, and the skill is passed to younger workers |

One small venture touches all five effects. This is why governments and universities try to encourage entrepreneurship.

### Characteristics of successful entrepreneurs

What do successful entrepreneurs have in common? The OpenStax textbook describes an entrepreneurial spirit that is passionate, purposeful, positive, bold, curious and persistent. It adds creativity, problem-solving skill, readiness to innovate and open-mindedness (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 1.3). Butler (2020) lists similar qualities: creativity, alertness to opportunities, commitment, confidence, experience and willingness to take risks.

The table below sets out seven characteristics that appear in both sources. Each is linked to a behaviour you can see, because a characteristic is only useful if you can recognise it in action.

<Figure id="fig-U1-2" src="/img/figures/gent-401/unit-01/fig-U1-2.svg" alt="Table of seven characteristics of successful entrepreneurs, each paired with an everyday behaviour that shows it, from noticing a need others walk past to changing a product after listening to customers' complaints." />

**Correcting a common misconception: "Entrepreneurs are born, not made."** Many people believe you either have a "business brain" from birth or you do not. The evidence points the other way.

- Not every successful entrepreneur has all these characteristics, and those who have them do not have them in equal measure (Butler, 2020). There is no single "entrepreneur personality".
- Butler (2020) notes that experience can count for more than formal education. Experience is gained, not inherited.
- The OpenStax authors argue that because anyone can learn to notice opportunities instead of following old patterns, anyone can become an entrepreneur (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 1.3).
- A study of 805 university students in Pakistan found that entrepreneurship education and practical support from the university raised students' intentions to start a business (Saeed et al., 2014).

So these characteristics are habits that grow with practice. Mellor (2009) built a whole textbook on this idea, titled *Entrepreneurship for everyone*. For you as a future teacher, this matters: if entrepreneurship can be learned, it can be taught in school.

**A worked example: finding evidence of characteristics.** Read Sana's story again and match her actions to the table.

| What Sana did | Characteristic it shows |
|---|---|
| Noticed that pupils had no Sindhi practice sheets | Curiosity and alertness |
| Wrote her own worksheet packs | Creativity |
| Tested them with neighbours' children before printing many | Willingness to take calculated risks |
| Rewrote two packs after children found them confusing | Openness to learning and persistence |

Notice how each claim is backed by an action from the story. When you analyse a case, always point to the evidence.

## Activity: Entrepreneurs in our town

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. Each member names one person they know or have heard of in their own town or village who started something new: a business, a service, a school project or a social effort.
2. For each person, the group applies the three-question test from this topic. Record the result in a table.
3. For two of the people the group agrees are entrepreneurs, find evidence of at least two characteristics. Write the action next to the characteristic, as in the worked example.
4. Together, decide which of the five effects on economic development each of the two ventures has had on your town.

Share your table with another group. The other group must challenge one of your decisions, and you must defend it with evidence.

## Summary

An entrepreneur identifies and acts on a problem or an opportunity that others have not acted on, and accepts the risk that comes with it. Owning a business is not enough; what makes someone entrepreneurial is the new idea put into action. Entrepreneurship supports economic development through jobs, innovation, income, competition and community welfare, and these effects create new opportunities in turn. Successful entrepreneurs share characteristics such as curiosity, creativity, persistence and willingness to take calculated risks, but these are habits that can be learned, not gifts given only at birth.

## Summative task

Write 250-300 words about one entrepreneur from your own district. This can be a person you know or one reported in a local newspaper.

1. Describe what the person started and the problem or opportunity they acted on.
2. Apply the three-question test and show that the person is acting as an entrepreneur.
3. Analyse the venture's effects on economic development in your district, using at least three of the five effects.
4. Identify two characteristics the person shows, with evidence from their actions.
