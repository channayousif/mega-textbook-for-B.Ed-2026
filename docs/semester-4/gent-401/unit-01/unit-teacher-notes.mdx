# Entrepreneurship and the Entrepreneurial Mindset - Notes for Teachers

The course guide names lectures, interactive discussions, question-answer sessions, demonstration and case-study analysis as teaching strategies, and lists individual and group assignments, internet research tasks and presentations as practical work. These notes show one way to use them across the two weeks.

## Sequencing the two weeks

- **Week 1 (Topic 1.1).** Open with the question from the unit: "Are the pen seller and the worksheet maker both entrepreneurs?" Take a quick show of hands before teaching the definition, then return to the vote after the three-question test. Use a short lecture for the economic effects, and a case study for the characteristics.
- **Week 2 (Topic 1.2).** Start with a demonstration: take one school problem through the five steps on the board, live, with the class suggesting ideas. Then run the "Turn a school problem into an innovation" activity. End the week with the role-play comparing an entrepreneur and an administrator.

## Likely misconceptions and how to handle them

- **"An entrepreneur is anyone who owns a shop."** Ask: "What is new about a fourth identical shop?" Let trainees apply the three-question test to it themselves.
- **"Entrepreneurs are born, not made."** A likely wrong answer is, "Only people from business families succeed." Ask trainees to name one local entrepreneur whose family was not in business, and to say what that person learned along the way.
- **"Entrepreneurship needs a lot of money."** Ask the class to plan a test of an idea that costs less than 1,000 rupees. Most groups can, and the point lands without a lecture.

**One good discussion prompt.** "Is a government school teacher who starts a free reading club for her pupils an entrepreneur?" Expect a split. Use it to show that the three-question test can apply outside business, and that profit is not part of the definition.

## Practical work: a local entrepreneur case study

The guide lists group assignments, internet research tasks and presentations. This case study combines them.

1. In groups of three, trainees choose one entrepreneur from their district. Where possible, they interview the person, with consent; otherwise they use newspaper reports or the venture's own pages online.
2. Each group applies the three-question test, traces the venture's effects on economic development, and finds evidence of at least three characteristics.
3. Groups add one finding about how the person took calculated risks or solved a problem after launch.
4. Each group gives a five-minute presentation, and the class asks one challenging question per group.

Follow your institution's consent rules. Do not record anyone without permission, and leave out names if the person prefers. The case study is not marked against a fixed model answer; judge whether trainees use evidence from the case to support each claim.
