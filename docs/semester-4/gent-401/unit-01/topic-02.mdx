## A real classroom situation

Imran is a B.Ed trainee on practicum at a government boys' middle school in Mirpurkhas. Many pupils arrive without breakfast and buy fried snacks from a cart at the gate. By the third period, several of them are tired and cannot concentrate.

The school canteen is run by a contractor's manager, Mr Bashir. He says, "My job is to sell what the contract lists, keep costs down and close the accounts each month. Changing the menu is not my job." Imran's friend Kiran, who finished her B.Ed last year, hears about the problem. She says, "I could make a cheap egg-and-vegetable roll at home, sell it at the gate before school, and see if pupils buy it." Her mother warns her: "You have no money. Business is for rich people."

Imran wonders why Mr Bashir and Kiran see the same problem so differently, and whether Kiran's mother is right. By the end of this topic, you will be able to explain both.

## Explanation

This topic is about the way entrepreneurs think. It answers three questions. How do new ideas become useful innovations? How do entrepreneurs take risks without gambling? And how does entrepreneurial thinking differ from the thinking of a manager?

### Creativity and innovation

The OpenStax textbook separates three ideas that people often mix up (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 4.2).

- **Creativity** is the ability to develop something original, especially an idea.
- **<Glossary term="Innovation">Innovation</Glossary>** is creativity put to use: a change that adds value to an existing product, service or way of working.
- **Invention** is a bigger leap: a truly new product, service or process that did not exist before.

So creativity happens in the mind, while innovation happens in the world. Kiran's idea of a healthy breakfast roll is creative. It becomes an innovation only when pupils actually buy and eat the rolls and arrive better fed. She has not invented anything, because rolls already exist. Most entrepreneurs are innovators, not inventors. They improve, combine or re-use existing things to meet a need.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Creativity is a gift that only some people have."** The OpenStax authors state that creativity, innovation and inventiveness can all be developed (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 4.2). Creativity grows when you follow a process and practise it. One simple tool is to turn a complaint into a question that starts with "How might we...?" (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 4.1). "Pupils are hungry" becomes "How might we give pupils a cheap, healthy breakfast before school?" A question invites ideas; a complaint does not.

The flowchart at the start of this topic shows five steps from a problem to an innovation. It is a simplified form of the design-thinking process described in OpenStax, which moves from understanding people's needs, to defining the problem, to generating ideas, to building a small version and testing it (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 4.1).

**A worked example: from problem to innovation.** Follow Kiran through the steps.

| Step | What Kiran does |
|---|---|
| Notice a problem | Pupils at the Mirpurkhas school arrive hungry and buy fried snacks |
| Ask questions | She talks to six pupils: most leave home early, have about 30 rupees, and like eggs |
| Generate ideas | Egg roll, boiled chana cup, banana and paratha, a breakfast club run by parents |
| Test a small version | She sells 20 egg-and-vegetable rolls at the gate on two mornings |
| Decide | Rolls sell out, but pupils say they are too spicy; she changes the recipe and tests again |

After two rounds, the roll is cheap, popular and filling. Kiran's idea has become an innovation, because it now adds real value for real pupils. Notice the loop: the first test did not fully work, and she learned from it.

### Risk-taking and problem-solving

Every new venture can fail. Butler (2020) explains that entrepreneurs must commit time, effort and money while facing deep uncertainty about what customers will want in the future. Nobody can know in advance whether a new product will sell. So risk-taking is part of entrepreneurship.

But entrepreneurs do not gamble. The OpenStax textbook separates risk from **calculated risk**. The difference is careful checking: doing the research needed to make an informed decision that keeps the risk small (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 1.1). Many entrepreneurs reduce their risk before they launch, for example by keeping their job while they test a venture part-time (Laverty & Littel, 2020, sections 1.1 and 1.3).

**Correcting a common misconception: "Entrepreneurship needs a lot of money to start."** Kiran's mother believes this, and many people do. Some ventures, such as a factory, do need large sums. But many start small. Kiran's first test cost the price of 20 rolls. A tutor can start with one room and five pupils. Starting small is itself a way of taking a calculated risk: you spend a little, learn a lot, and only then decide whether to spend more. You will study ways of funding a venture in Unit 5.

**Problem-solving** is the other side of risk-taking. The OpenStax authors describe entrepreneurs as problem solvers who turn a problem into an opportunity (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 1.1). Problems do not stop after launch: a supplier is late, a price rises, a customer complains. An entrepreneur treats each problem as information and looks for a fix, instead of giving up.

**A worked example: a simple risk check.** Before she sells every day, Kiran asks four questions.

| Question | Kiran's answer |
|---|---|
| What could go wrong? | Pupils stop buying; eggs become expensive; the head teacher stops sales at the gate |
| How likely is each, and how serious? | Price rise: likely, but small effect. Ban at the gate: less likely, but serious |
| How can I make each risk smaller? | Keep the price fixed by changing the filling; ask the head teacher's permission in writing |
| How much can I afford to lose? | One week's ingredients, about 2,000 rupees, without borrowing |

Kiran has not removed the risk. She has understood it and made it smaller. That is what a calculated risk looks like.

### Entrepreneurial versus managerial thinking

Mr Bashir and Kiran see the same hungry pupils, but they think differently. Their roles explain much of the difference.

A **manager** runs an existing organisation so that it works well. A manager's job is to plan, organise, control costs and meet agreed targets with resources that the organisation already has. Entrepreneurial thinking starts from an opportunity and asks how to gather the resources to act on it. Early economists saw both jobs as belonging to one person, who took both the risk and the management of a business (Butler, 2020). But Butler (2020) points out that the two do not always come together: there are brilliant innovators who are poor managers, and excellent managers who misread what customers want.

<Figure id="fig-U1-4" src="/img/figures/gent-401/unit-01/fig-U1-4.svg" alt="Table comparing entrepreneurial and managerial thinking on goals, risk, resources and decisions, with a note that a growing venture needs both kinds of thinking." />

**Correcting a common misconception: "Entrepreneurs are good and managers are boring."** The table is not a contest. A venture that only has entrepreneurial thinking may start well and then collapse because nobody keeps the accounts or the stock in order. A business that only has managerial thinking may run smoothly until customers change and it does not. When Kiran's roll business grows to three schools, she will need to plan, keep records and control costs, which is managerial thinking. The skill is to know which kind of thinking a situation needs.

**A worked example: one problem, two responses.** A school-uniform supplier in Hyderabad finds that sales have fallen by a third because parents are buying second-hand uniforms.

| Step | A salaried manager might... | An entrepreneur might... |
|---|---|---|
| How the problem is seen | A threat to this year's sales target | A sign of an unmet need: parents want cheaper uniforms |
| First response | Cut costs, reduce stock, offer a small discount | Talk to parents about what they would pay for |
| Idea | Keep the same products and sell harder | Start a buy-back and resale service for good used uniforms |
| Attitude to risk | Avoid anything untested | Test the resale idea at one school for a month |

Both responses are reasonable. The manager protects what exists. The entrepreneur looks for a new opportunity in the same problem. A strong business does both: it protects current sales while it tests the new idea.

## Activity: Turn a school problem into an innovation

Work in groups of four. About 30 minutes.

1. As a group, list three real problems you have seen in schools in your area, such as broken furniture, no drinking water, or pupils without books.
2. Choose one problem. Rewrite it as a "How might we...?" question.
3. Each member writes two possible solutions on separate slips without discussion. Put all the slips together. Do not judge them yet.
4. Choose the most promising idea. Use the flowchart steps to plan a small, cheap test that could be done in one week.
5. Carry out the four-question risk check for your test.
6. Finally, two members play a salaried school administrator and two play an entrepreneur. Each pair explains, in two minutes, how they would respond to the problem.

Present your test plan and risk check to another group. They must name one risk you missed.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Without looking back, define creativity, innovation and invention in one sentence each.
2. *(Remember)* List the five steps from a problem to an innovation shown in the flowchart.
3. *(Understand)* In three sentences, explain the difference between taking a calculated risk and gambling.
4. *(Understand)* A friend says, "You need lakhs of rupees to become an entrepreneur." Give two reasons, with examples, why this is not always true.
5. *(Apply)* The library in your practicum school is locked most of the day because there is no librarian. Rewrite this problem as a "How might we...?" question, and suggest one small test that an entrepreneurial teacher could run in a week.

## Summary

Creativity is having original ideas; innovation is putting an idea to use so that it adds value; invention is a leap to something truly new. Creativity can be developed by following a process: notice a problem, ask questions, generate ideas, test a small version, and improve. Entrepreneurs take risks, but calculated ones: they check carefully, start small and only risk what they can afford to lose, so a venture does not always need a lot of money. Entrepreneurial thinking looks for new opportunities and finds resources to act on them, while managerial thinking runs existing systems well. A growing venture needs both.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher or the head teacher about one change they introduced at the school, such as a new timetable, a reading corner or a parents' meeting. Ask: How did the idea start? Did you test it first? What went wrong, and how did you solve it? Write five lines that link their answers to the steps from problem to innovation, and say whether their thinking was mainly entrepreneurial, managerial or both.

## Summative task

Write 250-300 words. Choose one real problem faced by pupils or parents in your town, such as unsafe transport to school, costly textbooks or no place to study in the evening.

1. Rewrite the problem as a "How might we...?" question.
2. Propose one innovation that could address it, and show how you would test a small version first.
3. Carry out a risk check for your test and explain how it keeps the risk calculated.
4. Analyse how a salaried manager of an existing organisation, such as a school or a transport company, would respond to the same problem differently, and judge which response would serve pupils better in this case.

*(Analyze)*

**Mini-rubric (10 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing (0) |

| Problem and question (2 marks) | Real, local problem turned into a clear "How might we...?" question (2) | Problem stated but question weak (1) | Absent |
| Innovation and small test (3 marks) | Innovation adds value; test is small, cheap and realistic (3) | Idea present but test missing or unrealistic (1-2) | None |
