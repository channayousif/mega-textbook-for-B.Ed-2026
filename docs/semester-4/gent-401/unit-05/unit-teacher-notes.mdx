# Financial Planning and Funding - Notes for Teachers

## How to teach this unit

The guide lists lectures, interactive discussions, question-answer sessions, demonstration and case-study analysis. This unit suits a worked-example lecture followed by a case. Teach Topic 5.1 as one worked example carried through all three sub-topics: Imran's tuition centre. Start with the start-up budget, then calculate break-even from the same numbers, then read the first-month income statement. Students see one set of figures change meaning in three statements. Teach Topic 5.2 as a comparison: put the five funding sources on the board before the details, then use the flowchart to decide which fits a scenario.

Sequence the topics in order. Break-even needs the vocabulary of fixed and variable costs, so it must come after the budget. The funding choices in Topic 5.2 make more sense once students can read a simple income statement.

## One good discussion prompt

Give the class a scenario: a former pupil wants to open a small printing unit and can either borrow Rs 100,000 from a bank at 15 percent interest or take Rs 100,000 from a relative for 30 percent ownership. Ask: "Which would you choose, and what would change your mind?" The useful answers weigh control against repayment and ask what the venture can realistically earn.

## One likely wrong answer

Students often say, "If the income statement shows a profit, the business has cash." Correct it with a concrete case: the canteen shows a profit of Rs 12,000 but cannot pay Rs 8,000 for supplies because two teachers have not yet paid for the month. Then show how the cash-flow statement reveals the gap. The same correction works for "break-even means the business is successful" and "investors give money for nothing".

## Likely misconceptions and how to handle them

- "Profit and cash are the same thing." Use the income statement and the cash-flow statement side by side, and show a sale that has been taught but not yet paid for.
- "Break-even means the business is successful." Emphasise that break-even is the floor. Ask what happens in the first month if only half the break-even number of pupils join.
- "A budget is just a guess." Show how a budget is checked against actual income at the end of the month, and how the difference is used.
- "Investors give money without expecting anything back." Ask students what a lender or an investor would want in return, and write the answers on the board.

## Practical work

The guide's practical work includes hands-on computer exercises, group assignments and internet research tasks. Two activities fit this unit:

1. **Build a budget spreadsheet.** In pairs, students build Imran's start-up budget and first-month income statement in a simple spreadsheet. They change the number of pupils from 10 to 20 and watch the net income change. About 30 minutes.
2. **Interview a local business owner.** Students ask one local shopkeeper or canteen operator how they raised the money to start, what they gave up, and what they wish they had known. They classify the source as bootstrapping, a loan, an angel investor, venture capital or crowdfunding, and report back in five minutes.
