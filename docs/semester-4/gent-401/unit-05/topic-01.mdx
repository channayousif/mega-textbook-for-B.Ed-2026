# Topic 5.1 - Costs, budgets, break-even and statements

## A real classroom situation

Nadia is a B.Ed trainee on practicum at a government secondary school in Larkana. Her friend Imran has rented a small room to start an evening tuition centre for Class 9 mathematics. He has saved Rs 50,000. He tells Nadia: "I think 15 pupils will join. If each pays Rs 2,000 a month, I will take Rs 30,000. That is a good income."

Nadia notices what Imran has left out. He has not counted the rent, the assistant's pay, the electricity or the printing of worksheets. She asks him: "How many pupils do you need just to cover your costs?" Imran is not sure. He knows 15 would be nice, but he cannot say what 15 is worth. By the end of this topic, you will be able to answer his question and read the statements that tell him how the centre is really doing.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What does the venture need to spend before it opens? How much must it sell to cover its costs? And what do the basic financial statements tell the owner?

### Start-up costs and budgeting

A <Glossary term="Start-up cost" /> is money spent once to open the business, before the first customer arrives. For Imran these are the rent deposit and the first month's rent, furniture and a whiteboard, books and printed worksheets, a fan and fittings, and registration. They are one-time costs, not monthly bills.

A <Glossary term="Budget" /> is a plan for money before it arrives. It lists the income the venture expects and the spending it plans, so the owner can see whether the plan is possible. A budget is not a guess and it is not a record of the past. It is a forward plan that the owner can check at the end of the month.

A <Glossary term="Fixed cost" /> is a cost that stays about the same each month, whatever the sales. For Imran these are the room rent of Rs 8,000, the assistant's pay of Rs 10,000, and electricity and water of Rs 2,000. His fixed costs are Rs 20,000 a month. A <Glossary term="Variable cost" /> is a cost that rises and falls with the number of pupils or units sold. For Imran the main variable cost is printing worksheets and practice material, about Rs 400 per pupil.

**A worked example: Imran's start-up budget.** Before he opens, Imran lists what he must buy or pay once.

| Start-up cost | Amount (Rs) |
|---|---|
| Rent deposit and first month's rent | 16,000 |
| Furniture and a whiteboard | 20,000 |
| Books and printed worksheets | 8,000 |
| Fan and fittings | 4,000 |
| Registration | 2,000 |
| **Total start-up costs** | **50,000** |

His savings of Rs 50,000 cover these exactly. The budget tells him two useful things. First, he has enough to open. Second, he has no money left for a month in which fewer pupils join. A budget turns his plan into numbers he can check.

**Correcting a common misconception: "A budget is just a guess, so it does not matter."** A budget is a plan, not a prediction of the future. A good budget uses careful estimates and then compares them with what actually happens. Scott (2017, chapter 13) lists five ways that attention to money keeps a business solvent: it measures success, points out weaknesses, shows where to improve, helps anticipate a crisis, and shows whether good months can carry slow ones. A budget is the simplest form of that attention.

### Break-even analysis

<Glossary term="Break-even point" /> means the level of sales at which total revenue exactly equals total costs. At that point the venture makes no profit and no loss. It is a planning tool: it tells the owner the least the business must sell to survive.

To calculate it, you need three numbers: the fixed costs, the selling price per unit, and the variable cost per unit. The difference between the selling price and the variable cost per unit is called the <Glossary term="Contribution margin" />. Each unit sold contributes that amount towards paying the fixed costs.

The formula is:

> Break-even quantity = fixed costs / contribution margin per unit

**A worked example: Imran's break-even.** Imran's fixed costs are Rs 20,000 a month. Each pupil pays Rs 2,000 and costs Rs 400 in variable costs, so the contribution margin is Rs 1,600 per pupil.

> Break-even quantity = 20,000 / 1,600 = 12.5, so 13 pupils.

Imran needs 13 pupils each month just to cover his costs. At 15 pupils he is above break-even and makes a small profit. At 10 pupils he loses money. The chart at the start of this topic shows the same result: the total cost line and the revenue line cross at 13 pupils.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Break-even means the business is successful."** Break-even only means costs are covered. It is the floor, not the target. A business at break-even has no profit to put back into the venture, no buffer for a slow month, and no reward for the owner's time. A realistic plan aims above break-even and asks how long it will take to get there. Sintha (2020) describes break-even analysis as a financial tool that helps decide how many products or services a business must sell to cover its costs, especially its fixed costs, and notes that many small businesses fail because they never do this calculation at all.

### Basic financial statements

Once the venture is running, three statements answer three different questions.

An <Glossary term="Income statement" /> shows what the business earned and spent over a period, usually a month or a year. Revenue minus expenses equals net income. Imran's first-month income statement is in the table below.

A <Glossary term="Balance sheet" /> shows the position of the business on one day. It lists assets (what the business owns), liabilities (what it owes) and the owner's equity (the owner's claim on what is left). The accounting equation must always balance: assets = liabilities + equity. If the two sides do not match, a record is wrong.

<Glossary term="Cash flow" /> is the money actually moving in and out of the business. A cash-flow statement records where cash came from and where it went. It matters because the income statement records revenue when the work is done, not when the cash arrives. A pupil may owe fees for a month; the income statement counts the fee as soon as the class is taught, but the cash is not yet in the bank.

<Figure id="fig-U5-2" src="/img/figures/gent-401/unit-05/fig-U5-2.svg" alt="Two tables for one tuition centre: a start-up budget totalling 50,000 rupees, and a first-month income statement showing revenue of 30,000, variable costs of 6,000, fixed costs of 20,000 and net income of 4,000 rupees." />

**A worked example: reading Imran's first month.** With 15 pupils, revenue is 15 x Rs 2,000 = Rs 30,000. Variable costs are 15 x Rs 400 = Rs 6,000. Fixed costs are Rs 20,000. Net income is 30,000 - 6,000 - 20,000 = Rs 4,000. So the centre is profitable on paper. But suppose five pupils have not yet paid their fees. The income statement still shows Rs 30,000 of revenue, while the bank holds only Rs 20,000. The cash-flow statement shows the gap.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Profit and cash are the same thing."** They are not. Profit is a result calculated over a period; cash is a balance at a moment. A business can be profitable and run out of cash because customers pay late, because stock is bought before it is sold, or because a loan must be repaid. Scott (2017, chapter 10) names cash-flow problems among the main reasons new businesses fail. The OpenStax textbook makes the same point: the income statement records revenue when work is completed, not when cash arrives, which is why a separate statement of cash flows is needed (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 9.4).

## Activity: Will 15 pupils be enough?

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. Each group receives one venture card: a tuition centre, a school canteen, a printing unit or a phone repair shop. The card gives the monthly fixed costs, the selling price per unit and the variable cost per unit.
2. Each member calculates the break-even quantity for the venture, showing the formula and the working.
3. The group decides whether a realistic plan would sell more or fewer units than the break-even quantity, and names one reason for its judgement.
4. One member presents the calculation to the class. The class must ask one question about the group's assumptions.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* List three one-time start-up costs for a small venture and three monthly fixed costs.
2. *(Understand)* In two sentences, explain why a budget is useful before the business opens.
3. *(Apply)* A friend plans a canteen with fixed costs of Rs 30,000 a month, a selling price of Rs 150 per plate and variable costs of Rs 90 per plate. Calculate the break-even number of plates and say what the answer means.
4. *(Apply)* A venture shows a profit of Rs 10,000 on its income statement but has no cash in the bank. Explain how this can happen, using the cash-flow statement.

## Summary

A start-up budget lists the one-time costs of opening and the monthly income and spending expected, so the owner can see whether the plan is possible. A break-even calculation uses fixed costs, selling price and variable cost to find the least the business must sell to cover its costs; it is a floor, not a promise of success. The income statement shows profit over a period, the balance sheet shows assets, liabilities and equity on one day, and the cash-flow statement shows the money actually moving in and out. Profit and cash are not the same.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask the head teacher, or the owner of a small shop near the school, about one cost they pay every month and one cost they paid only once to start. With their permission, estimate a break-even quantity for a small school canteen: the monthly rent and wages, the price of one lunch plate, and the cost of the food for one plate. Write five lines on what you found and whether the canteen would need many customers to break even.

## Summative task

Imran's tuition centre has fixed costs of Rs 20,000 a month, charges Rs 2,000 per pupil and has variable costs of Rs 400 per pupil. He hopes for 15 pupils in the first month.

Write 250-300 words analysing Imran's plan.

1. Calculate the break-even quantity and show your working.
2. Judge whether 15 pupils is a realistic target for the first month, giving two reasons.
3. Explain one way the plan could look profitable on paper and still leave Imran short of cash.
