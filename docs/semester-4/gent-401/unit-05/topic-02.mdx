# Topic 5.2 - Where the money comes from

## A real classroom situation

Imran's tuition centre needs Rs 50,000 to open. He has saved Rs 20,000. He sits in the school staffroom in Larkana with three pieces of advice. His uncle says, "I will lend you Rs 30,000, but you must pay it back with interest in two years." A friend says, "My aunt is wealthy. She might give you Rs 30,000 for a share of the centre." His sister says, "You do not need one big donor. Ask a hundred former pupils to help with Rs 300 each, and promise them a free worksheet pack."

Imran is confused. One offer is a loan, one is an investor and one is a crowd. They are not the same, and they carry different costs and different control. By the end of this topic, you will be able to compare them and advise him.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What are the main ways to raise money for a new venture? How do angel investors and venture capitalists differ? And how does crowdfunding work?

### Funding sources: bootstrapping, investors, loans

<Glossary term="Bootstrapping" /> means funding the venture yourself, from personal savings and the venture's own early income, instead of taking outside money. The OpenStax textbook defines it as self-funding a company and making the best use of personal funds (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 9.2). Bootstrapping has real advantages: the founder keeps full control, gives up no ownership and avoids debt. It also has costs: it is slow to start, and the founder must make personal sacrifices. Scott (2017, chapter 1) records the view of many practitioners that starting with little money teaches frugality and efficiency, because a large pile of cash often gets spent on things the venture does not need.

A <Glossary term="Bank loan" /> is debt financing: borrowing money that must be repaid with interest. Banks are cautious. Scott (2017, chapter 12) notes that banks look for proven cash revenues or collateral, which is something of value pledged to secure the loan, and that they want firm facts and figures rather than a hopeful story. A loan does not give the lender ownership, but it creates a regular cash outflow that the venture must meet even in a slow month. The OpenStax textbook summarises the trade-off: debt means the lender has no ownership but requires early and regular cash outflow; equity gives the lender an ownership stake but no immediate cash outflow (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 9.1).

<Glossary term="Equity financing" /> means raising money by selling part ownership in the business. The investor becomes a part owner and shares in the success of the venture. The money does not have to be repaid like a loan, but the founder now answers to the investor and may have to accept the investor's views on how the venture should grow. A study of startups in Poland found that founders' capital, bootstrapping and support from friends and family are the classical sources, while crowdfunding, business angels and venture capital are the alternative sources, and that most Polish startups are financed mainly by the founders' own capital (Cegielska & Zawadzka, 2018).

**A worked example: Imran's three options.** Imran has Rs 20,000 and needs Rs 50,000.

| Option | What Imran gives | What Imran must do | How much control he keeps |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bootstrapping: start with Rs 20,000 and buy less furniture | No ownership, no interest | Start smaller and grow slowly from income | Full |
| Bank loan: borrow Rs 30,000 | Interest and possibly collateral | Repay on time every month | Full, but the bank has a claim |
| Angel investor: Rs 30,000 for part ownership | Part of the centre | Share decisions and profits | Shared |

**Correcting a common misconception: "Investors give money without expecting anything back."** No outside money is free. A lender expects repayment with interest. An equity investor expects part ownership and a share of the success, and often expects the venture to grow quickly. Scott (2017, chapter 12) observes that venture capitalists in particular expect yearly growth rates of up to 40 percent or higher. Before taking money, the founder should ask what the investor expects in return.

### Venture capital and angel investors

An <Glossary term="Angel investor" /> is a wealthy private individual who invests in a new venture, usually in exchange for part ownership. Angels often invest at the earliest stage, when the venture is little more than an idea and a plan. Because they are often successful business people themselves, they may also offer advice and introductions. The OpenStax textbook defines an angel investor as a wealthy private individual seeking investment options, and places angels among the seed-stage sources alongside personal savings and friends and family (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 9.1).

<Glossary term="Venture capital" /> is financing from a person or group that specialises in funding early-stage companies. A venture capitalist expects the venture to grow fast and pay back the investment, often by selling their share later. Scott (2017, chapter 12) notes that many venture capitalists are not really interested in small businesses; they look for high-flying ideas with large profit expectations. The OpenStax textbook places venture capitalists among the early-stage sources, after angels, and notes that they specialise in early-stage companies (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 9.1).

**A worked example: which investor fits Imran?** A tuition centre grows slowly and serves a small town. It is unlikely to offer the fast, large returns a venture capitalist wants, so venture capital is a poor fit. An angel investor who knows education in Larkana might help, but Imran should expect to give up part ownership and some control. For a small, steady venture, bootstrapping or a loan is often the more realistic path.

### Crowdfunding

<Glossary term="Crowdfunding" /> means collecting small sums of money from a large number of people, usually through an online campaign. The OpenStax textbook defines it as collecting small sums from a large number of people, and calls the contributors backers (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 9.2). There are several models. A reward campaign offers a product or a thank-you gift in return for support. A donation campaign asks for a gift with nothing expected back. An equity campaign sells small shares in the business. A debt campaign lends money that is repaid.

Crowdfunding is not easy money. The OpenStax textbook warns that a campaign needs an established network and skilful use of the media, and that many projects deliver their rewards late (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 9.2). Scott (2017, chapter 12) writes that without a vibrant business network, crowdfunding can be as fickle as winning a lottery. A study of crowdfunding in several countries found that the funding target, the number of backers and the minimum sum invested all affected success, while the length of the funding period did not (Wati & Winarno, 2018). For Imran, a reward campaign among former pupils and parents could work if the goal is small and clear, the worksheet reward is easy to deliver, and he can reach a few hundred people.

## Activity: Choose the money

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. Each group receives one scenario: a tuition centre needing Rs 50,000, a printing unit needing Rs 200,000, a food stall needing Rs 25,000, or an edtech repair shop needing Rs 150,000. Each scenario states how fast the venture expects to grow and how much control the founder wants.
2. The group uses the flowchart at the start of this topic and the comparison table to choose one funding source, or a mix of two.
3. Each member writes two sentences defending the choice: one on control and one on risk or repayment.
4. Groups compare choices. Where two groups chose differently, each must explain what in the scenario made the difference.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Define bootstrapping in one sentence and name two of its advantages.
2. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between debt financing and equity financing in two sentences.
3. *(Apply)* A classmate says, "A bank loan is free money because the bank has plenty of it." Give two reasons this is wrong.
4. *(Apply)* A tuition centre needs Rs 50,000 and expects slow, steady growth. Choose the funding source that fits best and give two reasons.

## Summary

The main ways to fund a new venture are bootstrapping, debt and equity. Bootstrapping keeps control but grows slowly; a loan must be repaid with interest; equity brings outside owners who share the success and the decisions. Angel investors are wealthy individuals who often back early ventures, while venture capitalists fund high-growth ventures and expect fast returns. Crowdfunding collects small sums from many people and works best with a clear goal, a real network and a deliverable reward. No outside money is free, so the founder must weigh control, risk and repayment.

Classify the source as bootstrapping, a loan, an angel investor, venture capital or crowdfunding. Write five lines: what they raised, what they gave up, and whether the same source would work for a small venture at your practicum school.
