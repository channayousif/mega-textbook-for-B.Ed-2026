## A real classroom situation

Rizwana is a B.Ed trainee on practicum at a government secondary school in Thatta. At the morning assembly, the head teacher invites three guests to talk about their work.

Haji Ali has run a general store in the bazaar for twenty years. He sells rice, oil, soap and candles to the same families his father served. He employs one helper and earns enough to support his household.

Asif started a mobile-phone repair corner outside the bus stand five years ago. He now has three branches in three towns, a small workshop with six technicians, and sells repaired phones to wholesalers in Hyderabad.

Saima runs a centre for out-of-school girls in a rented room. The girls learn to read, sew and do simple accounts. The centre pays for itself by selling school uniforms and reusable bags to parents, with a small grant from a local welfare trust.

After the talks, a Class 9 pupil asks Rizwana: "Miss, which of these three is the real entrepreneur?" Rizwana smiles, because the answer is not one of them. By the end of this topic, you will be able to explain why.

## Explanation

This topic sorts entrepreneurship into its common forms. The three guests at the school show the three main ones. Two further forms, intrapreneurship and women's entrepreneurship, describe who is doing the entrepreneuring rather than the shape of the venture.

### Small businesses and scalable start-ups

A <Glossary term="Small business">small business</Glossary> is an independent business with few employees that serves a local market and earns income for its owner and family. A shop, workshop, clinic, tuition centre or small restaurant are typical examples. It does not aim to grow quickly or to spread to other places. Its goal is a steady livelihood.

A <Glossary term="Scalable start-up">scalable start-up</Glossary> is a new venture built to grow quickly, often by serving many customers in many places, sometimes with technology. It usually looks for outside money so that it can grow before it earns much. The OpenStax textbook gives Facebook, Airbnb and Zipline as examples of scalable ventures (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 2.1).

The difference is not size. A small business can be profitable and long-lasting, and a start-up can be small in its first year. The difference is ambition and design. A small business is designed to serve a known local market well. A start-up is designed to grow fast and to repeat its model in new markets.

The OpenStax textbook groups self-employment into one broad category that includes small-business owners, start-up founders and freelance gig workers (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 2.1). A person who drives for a ride-hailing service, or who sells freelance work online, is self-employed. They may be a small business, or they may be building a venture.

**A worked example: sort these two ventures.** Use four questions.

1. Does the venture mainly serve a local market?
2. Does it aim to grow quickly and reach new markets?
3. Does it need outside money to grow?
4. Could the same model be repeated in many towns?

| Venture | Local market? | Aims to grow fast? | Needs outside money? | Repeatable in many towns? | Form |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Haji Ali's general store | Yes | No | No | Not really, each town needs its own stock and customers | Small business |
| Asif's phone-repair business | No, it sells to three towns and to wholesalers | Yes | Yes, for new branches and equipment | Yes, the repair model repeats | Scalable start-up |

**Correcting a common misconception: "A small business is a failed start-up."** Many people assume that a business that stays small has not grown because it failed. That is not right. A small business is a deliberate choice for a stable livelihood and a known market. Many small businesses are profitable, employ local people and serve their communities for decades. Growth is one goal, not the only measure of success. Mellor (2009) writes for a wide range of entrepreneurs, not only for those who want to build large companies, and his textbook treats small and large ventures as different paths rather than as successes and failures.

### Social entrepreneurship

A <Glossary term="Social enterprise">social enterprise</Glossary> is a business whose main aim is a social or environmental good, not the largest possible profit for its owners. It sells goods or services and tries to pay its own costs from what customers pay, so it does not depend on donations.

A social entrepreneur is a person who acts like an entrepreneur but for a cause. The OpenStax textbook describes a social entrepreneur as "first and foremost an advocate or champion for a social cause", using a financially viable business as the vehicle for that cause (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 3.2). It adds that social enterprises are "typically designed to be financially self-sufficient" (section 3.2). Grameen Bank and Muhammad Yunus's micro-lending are given as an example.

A study of social entrepreneurship in Pakistan interviewed fourteen social entrepreneurs, practitioners and academicians (Qamar et al., 2020). It found that social entrepreneurship in Pakistan is growing, but that much of the discussion is still about definitions and ideas rather than about how these ventures work in practice. The authors suggest that government could raise awareness of what they call the "fourth sector", which sits beside the public sector, the private sector and traditional charities.

**A worked example: is it a charity or a social enterprise?** Ask three questions.

1. Does it sell something that customers choose to pay for?
2. Does it try to cover its own costs from that income?
3. What happens if donations stop?

| Venture | Sells something? | Covers its own costs? | If donations stop | Form |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A free food kitchen funded by donors | No, food is free | No, it runs on donations | It closes | Charity |
| A cafe that trains and employs young women and sells meals | Yes | Yes, wages and rent come from meal sales | It can continue | Social enterprise |
| Saima's girls' centre, which sells uniforms and bags and also receives a small grant | Yes | Mostly, with grant support for some costs | It could continue, with fewer activities | Social enterprise |

**Correcting a common misconception: "A social enterprise is a charity."** The two are easy to confuse because both work for a good cause. The difference is the money. A charity depends on donations, grants or funding from others, and it cannot distribute profits to its members or investors (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 3.2). A social enterprise sells something and tries to pay for itself. Some social enterprises begin as non-profit projects and later start earning, such as Embrace Innovations, which began at Stanford University and became a product company (section 3.2).

### Other forms: intrapreneurship and women's entrepreneurship

<Glossary term="Intrapreneurship">Intrapreneurship</Glossary> is entrepreneurial action by an employee inside an existing organisation, rather than by starting a separate business. The OpenStax textbook defines an intrapreneur as "an employee who acts as an entrepreneur within an organization, rather than going solo" (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 2.2). For example, a teacher who, with her head teacher's support, starts a reading programme in her school and finds local sponsors to fund books is acting as an intrapreneur. She does not leave her job or register a company, but she spots a need, acts on it and takes a risk.

A large organisation may encourage intrapreneurship because it needs new ideas. Butler (2020, p. 23) notes that there are brilliant innovators who are poor managers and inspiring managers who misread the market. An organisation that lets employees try new things gains the innovator's energy while the manager keeps the work orderly.

<Glossary term="Women entrepreneurship">Women's entrepreneurship</Glossary> means women starting and running their own ventures. The OpenStax textbook reports that woman-owned businesses in the United States grew from about four million thirty years ago to more than eleven million (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 2.1). In Pakistan, women run home-based embroidery and food businesses, tuition centres, online clothing stores, beauty services and craft enterprises. Many of these begin small and stay local, and many grow.

Women entrepreneurs in Pakistan often face barriers that men do not. They may need permission from the family to travel or to work outside the home, they may have less access to bank loans, and they may have less time because of household work. These barriers shape how a venture starts and grows. They are not a sign that women cannot be entrepreneurs.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Entrepreneurship is for men who can take big risks."** Women start ventures at every scale, from a home kitchen to a factory. The barriers are real, but they are about context and access, not about ability. A trainee teacher should notice that many of the small businesses around a school are run by women, and that a school-based venture may be one of the few options open to a woman in her home.

## Activity: Sort the ventures

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. Your teacher gives each group six short cards describing a venture in a Pakistani town. Read each card.
2. Sort the six ventures into small business, scalable start-up, social enterprise, intrapreneurship and women's entrepreneurship. A venture may fit more than one form.
3. For each card, write one sentence giving the evidence for your choice. Use the four questions from the worked example.
4. One group member swaps with another group. Each group challenges one of the other group's decisions, and the other group must defend it with evidence from the card.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* In one sentence, what is the difference between a small business and a scalable start-up?
2. *(Remember)* Name two things that make a social enterprise different from a charity.
3. *(Understand)* A classmate says, "A social enterprise is just a charity that sells things." Explain what is wrong with this statement, using the three questions from the worked example.
4. *(Apply)* A teacher in Badin starts a weekend coaching class inside her school with the head teacher's permission. She keeps her job and uses the fees to buy books for the school library. Which form of entrepreneurship is this? Give two reasons.
5. *(Apply)* A woman in Sukkur sells homemade pickles through WhatsApp. She now employs two helpers and supplies three grocery shops. Is this a small business or a scalable start-up? Give two reasons for your answer.

## Summary

Entrepreneurship takes several forms. A small business serves a local market and earns a living for its owner. A scalable start-up is designed to grow quickly and to reach many customers, often with outside money. A social enterprise puts a social or environmental cause first and tries to pay its own costs through sales. Intrapreneurship is entrepreneurial action inside an existing organisation, and women's entrepreneurship is entrepreneurship started and run by women. The forms overlap: a woman may run a small business, a social enterprise or a start-up, and an intrapreneur may work inside any of them.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find one person connected with your practicum school who has started something new: a canteen, a book shop, a uniform supply business, a club or a small teaching programme. With their permission, ask them what they started, how they pay for it, and whether they aim to grow. Write five lines: what the venture is, which form of entrepreneurship it is, and two pieces of evidence for your choice.

## Summative task

Write 250-300 words comparing a small business and a social enterprise from your district. They can be real businesses you know or two you describe.

1. Describe each venture and what it does.
2. Name the form of entrepreneurship each one is, with one piece of evidence for each.
3. Analyse which of the two has the larger effect on the community, using at least two of the effects from Unit 1.
4. State a clear judgement about which you would rather start, and justify it.
