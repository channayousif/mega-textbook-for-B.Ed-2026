# Types of Entrepreneurship and Opportunity Recognition - Notes for Teachers

The course guide names lectures, interactive discussions, question-answer sessions, demonstration and case-study analysis as teaching strategies, and lists hands-on computer exercises, individual and group assignments, internet research tasks and presentations as practical work. These notes show one way to use them across the two weeks.

## Sequencing the two weeks

- **Week 3 (Topic 2.1).** Open with the three guests at the school and ask the class to vote on which one is the real entrepreneur before teaching the forms. After the explanation, return to the vote. Use a short lecture for the three main forms, a case study for intrapreneurship, and a small-group sorting task for practice.
- **Week 4 (Topic 2.2).** Start with a demonstration: take the school's six ideas through the three screening criteria on the board, live, with the class supplying the scores. Then run the "idea fair" activity. End the week with a short presentation from each group and one challenging question from the class.

## Likely misconceptions and how to handle them

- **"A small business is a failed start-up."** Ask: "What is the owner trying to achieve?" Let trainees compare a shop that has supported a family for twenty years with a venture that never grew.
- **"A social enterprise is a charity."** Ask: "What happens if the donations stop?" Let the class trace the money in a café that employs young women and in a free food kitchen.
- **"Only new inventions count as opportunities."** Ask trainees to name a business in their town that is not an invention but meets a real need, such as the first shop to sell ready-made uniforms.
- **"A good idea is enough to succeed."** Ask the class to screen a good idea that fails the resource test, such as a school van with no driver. The point lands without a lecture.

**One good discussion prompt.** "Can a government school teacher be an entrepreneur?" Expect a split. Use it to show that intrapreneurship and social enterprise both sit inside organisations, and that the three-question test can apply outside business.

## Practical work: the idea fair

The guide lists group assignments, internet research tasks and presentations. The idea fair combines them.

1. In groups of four, trainees choose one real problem at their practicum school or in its area, such as missing books, unsafe water or no after-school study space.
2. They brainstorm ten ideas in ten minutes, following the rules: no criticism, welcome unusual ideas, build on other ideas, aim for quantity.
3. Each group scores its three strongest ideas with the weighted checklist and picks one to pitch in two minutes.
4. The class asks one challenging question per group, and the group answers with evidence.

Trainees can use the internet to check whether a similar idea already exists elsewhere, which is a simple form of secondary research. The fair is not marked against a fixed model; judge whether trainees use the criteria and evidence to support their scores and their choice.
