## A real classroom situation

Bilal is a B.Ed trainee on practicum at a government boys' high school in Khairpur. The school library has almost no books, and the head teacher wants to raise money for new ones. At a school assembly he asks the pupils: "What business should we start?"

The ideas come quickly. A tuck shop at the gate. A photocopier for the market. A plant nursery on the empty plot. A stall that sells clean drinking water. A mobile-phone repair corner. A small van to take pupils to inter-school events.

The head teacher writes all six on the board. Then he asks, "Which one should we actually try?" The hall goes quiet. Everyone has an opinion, but nobody has a way to decide. By the end of this topic, you will have one.

## Explanation

This topic is about the front of the entrepreneurial journey. First you find an opportunity. Then you generate ideas for meeting it. Then you screen the ideas so that you spend your time and money on the strongest one.

### Identifying business opportunities

A <Glossary term="Business opportunity">business opportunity</Glossary> is more than an idea. The OpenStax textbook defines it as the point where identifiable consumer demand meets the feasibility of satisfying what customers want (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 5.1). Two things must both be true. Customers must want it enough to pay. And you must be able to deliver it at a cost that leaves room for the venture to survive.

The same section lists where opportunities come from:

- **A deliberate search**, often around a new technology.
- **Chance or accident**, such as a mistake that turns out to be useful.
- **Solving a problem**, from a large one to a small everyday frustration. The textbook notes that many entrepreneurial ideas aim to solve small everyday problems.
- **Work experience**, where you notice a better way to do something. The founder of FedEx noticed this while working.
- **Customer feedback and observation**, which is also a simple form of market research.

The economist Joseph Schumpeter described five paths to new opportunities (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 5.1): open a new market for an existing product; find a new source of supply that lowers cost; use existing technology to make an old product in a new way; use existing technology to make a new product; and use new technology to make a new product. Only the last two depend on invention. The other three are open to anyone who notices an unmet need.

**A worked example: which of the school's six ideas is an opportunity?** Ask the two questions from the definition.

| Idea | Do customers want it and will they pay? | Can the school deliver it? | Opportunity? |
|---|---|---|---|
| A tuck shop at the gate | Yes, pupils buy snacks daily | Yes, a trolley and a supplier | Probably |
| A photocopier for the market | Yes, but two shops already offer it | Yes, but the machine is costly | Weak |
| A plant nursery on the empty plot | Maybe, sales are seasonal | Yes, but it needs water and care | Uncertain |
| A stall selling clean drinking water | Yes, if the supply is reliable | Yes, but the filter costs money | Probably |
| A mobile-phone repair corner | Yes | Only if someone has the skill | Weak |
| A van for inter-school events | Maybe, parents may prefer it | No, the school has no driver or vehicle | Not yet |

Notice that two ideas pass the first question but fail the second. An idea that you cannot deliver is not yet an opportunity.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Only new inventions count as opportunities."** Most opportunities are not inventions. A woman who opens the first shop in her area to sell ready-made school uniforms is not inventing anything, but she is meeting a real demand with a feasible service. Schumpeter's first three paths do not require a new technology at all. What makes an idea an opportunity is the demand and the delivery, not the novelty.

### Idea-generation techniques

Once you have a problem or a direction, you need ideas. The OpenStax textbook describes several techniques (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 6.2):

- **Brainstorming**: a group generates as many ideas as possible in a setting free of judgement. The rules matter. Do not criticise ideas while they are being produced. Welcome unusual ideas. Build on other people's ideas. Aim for quantity first.
- **Crowdsourcing**: you post a challenge to a large group, usually online, and people send in solutions, often for a prize or payment. A snack company once asked the public to invent a new flavour.
- **Storyboarding**: you draw the idea as a sequence of steps, then move the steps around to see the process better.
- **Team creativity**: a group works through the same steps together, using trust and different points of view.

The same section gives a five-step creative problem-solving process: **clarify** the real problem, **ideate** as many solutions as you can, **develop** the strongest ones, **implement** the chosen solution, and **evaluate** how well it worked (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 6.2). A fishbone diagram can help in the first step by tracing the causes of a problem. The textbook also describes design thinking, which moves through empathy, defining the problem, ideating, prototyping and testing (section 4.1).

**A worked example: a ten-minute brainstorm.** The problem is that many pupils at a school in Sukkur have no clean water to drink during the day. The group follows the brainstorming rules and produces twenty ideas in ten minutes: a filtered water stall, a cooler with cups, a scheme where pupils bring bottles, a stall selling low-cost filters, a water point at the gate, a fund to repair the school tap, and so on. Nobody is allowed to say "that will not work" during the ten minutes. Only after the list is complete does the group pick three ideas to screen.

### Screening and evaluating ideas

Ideas are easy to produce and expensive to pursue. Screening is the step that protects your time and money. The OpenStax textbook gives three criteria for deciding whether an idea is a real opportunity (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 5.2):

1. **Significant market demand**: the idea solves a real problem that the target market will pay for.
2. **Significant market structure and size**: the market can grow, the barriers to entering it are manageable, and the venture can win customers.
3. **Significant margins and resources**: the profit can justify the effort, and the money, skills and distribution needed are within reach.

The section then lists questions to ask after the three criteria: would others value it; does it solve a significant problem; is the market definable; is the timing right; what resources are needed before launch; is the advantage sustainable; and what is the timeline to the first sale and to profit (section 5.2).

**A worked example: a weighted checklist.** Weights show what matters most. Each idea is scored from 1 to 5 on every criterion, and the score is multiplied by the weight.

| Criterion | Weight | Tuck shop (score) | Plant nursery (score) | Water filter stall (score) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Demand | 30 | 4 | 3 | 5 |
| Margin | 25 | 2 | 4 | 4 |
| Low start-up cost | 20 | 4 | 3 | 2 |
| Matches our skills | 15 | 4 | 2 | 2 |
| Time to first sale | 10 | 5 | 3 | 2 |
| **Weighted total** | **100** | **360** | **310** | **340** |

The tuck shop scores highest. The head teacher may still choose the water filter stall because it also improves pupils' health. Screening does not make the decision for you. It makes the decision clear, so that you can defend it.

**Correcting a common misconception: "A good idea is enough to succeed."** An idea is the start, not the finish. The OpenStax textbook warns that ideas must be tested against demand, market and resources before money is spent (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 5.2). Many good ideas fail because nobody wanted to pay, because the cost was too high, or because the person who had the idea never tested it. This is why Unit 1's three-question test is not enough on its own: it tells you whether a person is acting as an entrepreneur, not whether the venture will work.

## Activity: The idea fair

Work in groups of four. About 30 minutes.

1. Each group chooses one real problem at your practicum school or in its area, such as late arrival, missing books, unsafe water or a lack of after-school study space.
2. In ten minutes, brainstorm as many ideas as you can to address it. Follow the rules: no criticism, welcome unusual ideas, build on other ideas, aim for quantity.
3. Pick your three strongest ideas. Score each one from 1 to 5 against the three screening criteria, using the weighted checklist.
4. Choose one idea to take forward and prepare a two-minute pitch: the problem, the idea, why it passes the criteria, and the smallest test you could run.
5. Another group asks one challenging question. Answer it with evidence, not with enthusiasm.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* In one sentence, what is a business opportunity?
2. *(Remember)* List three places where business opportunities come from.
3. *(Understand)* A classmate says, "If an idea is truly new, it is an opportunity." Explain what else must be true.
4. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between brainstorming and crowdsourcing, and give one example of each.
5. *(Apply)* A tailor in Sukkur thinks of selling school uniforms through WhatsApp. Use the three screening criteria to judge whether this is an opportunity.

## Summary

A business opportunity exists where real customer demand meets a feasible way to deliver. Opportunities come from deliberate search, chance, solving problems, work experience and customer feedback, and Schumpeter's five paths show that most of them do not require an invention. Brainstorming, crowdsourcing, storyboarding and the five-step creative problem-solving process help a group produce ideas. Screening then tests the ideas against demand, market structure and margins, using a weighted checklist so that the strongest idea is chosen for clear reasons. A good idea is only the start.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask the head teacher or a pupil council for one problem the school would like to solve, such as a lack of drinking water, a messy library or no place to buy stationery. Brainstorm ten ideas with three classmates, then score the top three with the weighted checklist. Bring your totals and your recommended idea to the head teacher, and note their response in five lines.

## Summative task

Write 250-300 words analysing one idea that failed in your town or village, or one idea you expect to fail.

1. Describe the idea and the people who tried it.
2. Apply the three screening criteria and explain which criterion the idea failed most clearly.
3. Analyse what the people could have done differently before spending money.
4. State whether you would take the idea forward, and justify your judgement.
