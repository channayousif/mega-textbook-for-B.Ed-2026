## A real classroom situation

Rukhsar teaches in a government primary school in Sukkur. Three years ago she began making Sindhi practice sheets for Class 3 mathematics in the evenings and selling them to two schools. Today twelve women in her neighbourhood prepare and pack the sheets, three shops sell them, and a second town has started ordering. Rukhsar still checks every sheet herself, still keeps the money in a steel box at home, and still delivers the orders on her motorcycle because she does not trust anyone else to do it.

At a school staff meeting, the head teacher asks her to supply the whole cluster of twelve schools. Rukhsar feels proud and frightened at once. If she takes the order she will need more hands, a proper stock register and a way to check quality without looking at every page. If she does not, another printer in the city will. By the end of this topic you will be able to decide what she should do and how.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. How does a founder lead once there are other people to lead? How should a venture grow? And what can go wrong, and how do you prepare for it?

### Leadership and team building

**Leadership** is the work of deciding where a venture is going and getting other people to move there with you. **<Glossary term="Team building">Team building</Glossary>** is the work of choosing those people, giving each a clear task, and helping them work well together. In a very small venture the two jobs sit in one person, and that is where many founders get stuck.

When a venture is new, the founder does almost everything: buying, making, selling, accounts and deliveries. This works while the work is small. It stops working when the founder becomes the bottleneck, which is exactly what happened to Rukhsar. OpenStax describes this stage as one in which "the owner usually wears many hats" and the processes are informal (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 10.5). Growth forces a change: the founder must hand over tasks, write down how they are done, and trust other people to do them.

The shift runs in two directions. The founder moves from doing tasks to arranging them, and from memory to written routines. At the same time the venture needs a team with *complementary* skills: people who are good at different things, not copies of the founder. OpenStax stresses that a new venture rarely succeeds on one person's effort, and gives the same rule this topic uses: identify the tasks, identify the skills each needs, then search for people with those skills (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 12.2).

**A worked example: what Rukhsar should hand over.** She has twelve workers, three shops and a second town. Sort her tasks by how much they need her judgement.

| Task | Who could take it | What Rukhsar must write down first |
|---|---|---|
| Check every practice sheet page | A senior worker as batch checker | What a correct page looks like, and how many pages to sample |
| Keep the cash book and pay workers | One trusted worker as recorder | How to record a sale, a cost and a payment, with a receipt |
| Buy paper and ink in the right quantity | The batch checker, with a weekly limit | How much paper one hundred packs need |
| Set the price and choose which schools to supply | Rukhsar | A simple rule, such as a minimum order size |
| Deliver orders and collect payment | A delivery person with a log | Which schools, which day, and how much to collect |

The point is not that Rukhsar stops working. It is that she spends her time on the row only she can do, and writes down enough for the others to do the rest well. OpenStax warns that growth brings the stress of "managing cash, delegating tasks to others, and ensuring the right people and systems are in place" (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 10.5). Naming that difficulty is the first step to handling it.

**Correcting a common misconception: "If the venture is mine, I must control every part of it."** Many founders equate trust with checking everything. Control of every detail feels safe, but it caps the venture at the founder's working hours. A venture that cannot grow beyond one person is a job, not a business. Delegation with a written routine gives control *and* room to grow.

### Scaling strategies

**Scaling** means growing the venture's sales and reach in a deliberate way, rather than waiting for more customers by accident. OpenStax describes growth as an increase in sales and profit, and lists a set of strategies a venture can choose from, each with its own cost and risk (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 10.5). Choosing one is a decision, not a mood.

The four strategies in the table at the start of this topic are the ones a small Pakistani venture meets first.

1. **Enter a new market.** Sell the same thing to a new town or school cluster. Nothing about the product changes, but the new area may not want it, and travel costs rise. OpenStax calls adding new markets "a riskier path to growth" than selling more to current customers (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 10.5).
2. **Add a new product.** A new subject or an English-medium version for the same schools. It uses relationships you already have, but you spend on printing and stock before you know whether it will sell.
3. **Franchise.** Let a shopkeeper in the next town sell your packs under your name for a fee. You grow fast using their shop, but your name and quality are in their hands. OpenStax defines franchising as sharing your business model with distributors for a fee, with training and support from you (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 10.5).
4. **Sell online.** Take orders by phone, message or a simple page, and deliver by post or van. It reaches schools far away, but orders, payments and deliveries must be tracked.

**A worked example: choosing one.** Rukhsar's head teacher offers her twelve schools in the cluster. That is a new market with a ready buyer, so strategy 1 is the obvious first step. But the schools spread across two towns, so she should write the delivery and collection routine before she signs, not after.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Growing faster is always better."** Growth brings cash strain as well as profit. OpenStax points out that in the growth stage "there is usually an increase in cash coming in and going out", and that a venture must watch costs and customer payments closely or run out of cash (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 10.5). A venture can grow too fast for its money and its people, and fail while sales look healthy. Growth should be chosen because the venture can carry it, not because a bigger number feels better.

### Managing risks

**Risk management** is the habit of asking what could go wrong, how likely it is, how much it would hurt, and what you can do about it. A **risk** is the chance of a loss and how serious that loss would be. OpenStax describes enterprise risk management as a process of risk identification, risk assessment and risk abatement, and groups business risks into natural, economic and human causes (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 13.7).

OpenStax groups business risks into natural causes such as flood, economic causes such as rising prices or a new competitor, and human causes such as a worker leaving or mishandling cash (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 13.7).

A **risk matrix** sorts risks by two questions: how likely each is, and how much damage it would do. The matrix below places four risks from Rukhsar's venture.

<Figure id="fig-U7-2" src="/img/figures/gent-401/unit-07/fig-U7-2.svg" alt="Risk matrix of likelihood against impact, with four example risks from a small printing venture placed in the quadrants and a note to act first on high-likelihood, high-impact risks." />

Read the matrix by quadrant:

1. **High likelihood, high impact:** act now. Rukhsar cannot stop the cheaper printer, so she must answer it, perhaps with a guaranteed delivery date.
2. **High impact, low likelihood:** prepare and insure. A flood could ruin her stock, so she moves it off the floor and keeps a reserve.
3. **Medium on both:** watch and reduce. Training two workers for each key task means no single person is irreplaceable.
4. **Low on both:** accept. A small delivery accident is a cost of doing business.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Risk can be removed completely."** It cannot. OpenStax is plain about this: not all risks can be insured against, and a bad decision to enter a market that does not work out is a risk no policy covers (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 13.7). Risk management does not make a venture safe; it makes the founder ready. The goal is to know your biggest risks, reduce the ones you can, and be able to absorb the ones you cannot.

## Activity: Grow it without breaking it

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. Each group takes one of these ventures: a school canteen that wants to supply two more schools, a tuition centre that wants a second branch, or a uniform-mending service that wants to serve a whole colony.
2. Name two scaling strategies that fit it, with one benefit and one risk each.
3. Draw a simple risk matrix and place four risks: one natural, one economic, one human and one of your own.
4. For the risk in the high-likelihood, high-impact quadrant, write two practical ways to reduce it.

Another group challenges one of your choices. Defend it or change it, and say which you did.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* In one sentence each, define leadership, team building and scaling.
2. *(Remember)* Name the three causes of business risk, with one example of each.
3. *(Understand)* A classmate says, "A founder who delegates has lost control of the business." Using Rukhsar's case, explain what is wrong with this view in three sentences.
4. *(Understand)* Explain in two or three sentences why growing fast can cause a venture to run out of cash even while sales rise.
5. *(Apply)* A women's handicraft group in Khairpur sells embroidered school bags to three shops and wants to grow. Recommend one scaling strategy with its benefit and risk, and name two risks it should place on a risk matrix, with the reason for each placement.

## Summary

Leadership decides where a venture is going and gets people to move there; team building chooses those people, gives each a clear task and helps them work together. As a venture grows, the founder must move from doing every task to arranging tasks, and from memory to written routines. Scaling is a deliberate choice among a new market, a new product, a franchise or online sales, each with its own benefit and risk, and growth should be chosen because the venture can carry it. Risk management places each risk by likelihood and impact, reduces what can be reduced, and prepares for the rest, because risk cannot be removed completely.

## Try this at your practicum school

With permission, talk to the person who runs a school-connected business you know: the canteen contractor, the uniform or book supplier, or a teacher who runs a club that raises money. Ask what they do themselves and what they hand to others, how they decided to grow, and what they worry could go wrong. Write five lines: one task they have delegated and how they check it, one growth choice and its reason, and one risk they carry and how they manage it.

## Summative task

Choose one small venture you know in your own district that wants to grow. Write 250-300 words.

1. Describe the venture and the people currently doing the work.
2. Recommend two scaling strategies, and for each give one benefit and one risk. Say which one you would choose first and why.
3. Analyse the venture's risks: place four risks on a risk matrix and explain the placement of at least two of them.
4. Suggest one way to reduce the risk you consider most serious.

*(Analyze)*

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Venture and team (2 marks) | Clear local venture with the current division of work named (2) | Vague or general (1) | Absent |
| Two scaling strategies (2 marks) | Two strategies named, each with a benefit and a risk (2) | One strategy, or benefits without risks (1) | None |

The risk criterion asks you to analyse with reasons. A list of worries without a likelihood or an impact does not earn full marks.
