# Growing a Business and Pitching It - Notes for Teachers

The course guide names lectures, interactive discussions, question-answer sessions, demonstration and case-study analysis as teaching strategies, and lists hands-on computer exercises, individual and group assignments, internet research tasks and presentations as practical work. These notes show one way to use them across the two weeks.

## Sequencing the two weeks

- **Week 14 (Topic 7.1).** Open with a question: "Can a business grow too fast?" Take a show of hands, then teach scaling strategies and founder delegation. Run the "Grow it without breaking it" activity in groups, and close with the risk matrix.
- **Week 15 (Topic 7.2).** Demonstrate one venture through the seven pitch parts on the board, with the class supplying the ask. Then run the "Two minutes that count" pair activity, and close with the class pitch competition below, which also prepares trainees for the guide's final-week presentations.

## Likely misconceptions and how to handle them

- **"A founder who delegates has lost control."** Ask: "What does the founder still check?" Let trainees list the checks Rukhsar keeps, such as the weekly total and the page sample.
- **"Growing faster is always better."** A likely wrong answer is, "More sales always mean more money." Ask the class to list what growth costs before it pays: stock, wages, delivery and the gap before customers pay.
- **"Risk can be removed completely."** Ask trainees to name a risk no insurance covers, then ask what preparation looks like instead of elimination.
- **"A good pitch is a long one with many slides."** Ask how they feel when a speaker reads slides for twenty minutes, and what they would cut.

## Practical work: a class pitch competition

The guide lists group assignments, presentations and internet research tasks. This competition combines them and matches the guide's final-week project presentations.

1. In groups of three or four, trainees choose a school-connected venture, either from their course project or a new one. They research two checkable facts: a local price for one input, and the number of possible customers in their town.
2. Each group builds a three-minute pitch with the seven parts and a deck of no more than ten slides, plus one risk and its reduction.
3. Groups deliver to a panel of two or three colleagues or a guest from a local business, who ask one question each.
4. The panel gives feedback on three things: whether the ask was specific, whether the numbers could be checked, and whether the speaker looked at and listened to the panel.

Keep the event short: eight minutes per group with questions. Do not mark against a fixed model answer; judge whether each claim is supported by a detail the group can defend. Follow your institution's consent rules for any guest or any recording, and leave out names if a group prefers.
