## A real classroom situation

A government high school in Hyderabad holds a small business-plan competition for Class 10. Each team has five minutes with a panel of three local business owners. Team 4 has a good idea: affordable reading corners for primary schools, built from local crates and filled with books in Sindhi and Urdu. Their leader, Hira, has prepared twenty slides. She plans to read them one by one, because she is afraid of forgetting something.

In the rehearsal, she finishes her twelfth slide in five minutes and never mentions how the reading corners will be paid for. The panel's first question is the one she dreaded: "How much do you need, and what will it buy?" By the end of this topic you will be able to help Hira rebuild her pitch so that it answers that question in the first minute.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What is a pitch made of? What does an investor presentation need? And which communication skills decide whether a pitch is believed?

### How to pitch a business idea

A **<Glossary term="Pitch">pitch</Glossary>** is a short, planned talk that asks someone for something: money, a partnership, a place in a competition, or a first customer. OpenStax defines it as "a formal but brief presentation" made to potential investors, and adds a simple rule: to pitch is to ask for something (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 7.3). Everything in a pitch should serve that ask.

A pitch has a shape. The flowchart above gives one that fits a three-minute talk, built on the six elements OpenStax lists for a pitch deck: the need, the market opportunity, the innovative solution, how the venture will be managed, what funding is needed, and how risks will be mitigated (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 7.3). Spend the first part on the problem and the solution, because a listener who does not feel the problem will not care about the solution.

**A worked example: Hira's three-minute reading-corner pitch.**

| Part | What Hira says | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | "In my school, Class 3 has forty pupils and nine books. I counted." | 20 seconds |
| Problem | "Primary schools in our town have no cheap, local-language reading material." | 30 seconds |
| Solution | "We build reading corners from local crates and stock them with Sindhi and Urdu books, maintained for one year." | 40 seconds |
| Market | "Twelve primary schools in our town, about 2,400 pupils. Three head teachers said they would try one." | 30 seconds |
| Model | "Each corner costs 18,000 rupees. Schools pay 12,000 and a local sponsor covers 6,000." | 30 seconds |
| Team | "Four of us build and paint; our art teacher chooses the books; my uncle's workshop cuts the crates." | 20 seconds |
| Ask | "We need 60,000 rupees to equip the first four corners. That buys crates, paint and the first set of books." | 20 seconds |
| Next step | "Visit our school and see a sample corner on Thursday." | 10 seconds |

Notice that the ask is specific: an amount, and what it buys. OpenStax stresses that a pitch is designed to be "clear, concise, and compelling", and that the ask is the specific money, assistance or outcome you are seeking (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 7.3). "Support us" is not an ask. "Sixty thousand rupees for four corners" is.

**Correcting a common misconception: "A good pitch is a long one with many slides."** The opposite is true. OpenStax suggests keeping a core deck to no more than ten slides and practising a two-minute version that covers all the key elements, because you may meet a possible supporter anywhere (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 7.3). A long pitch hides its ask; a short one shows that the speaker knows what matters.

### Investor presentations

An **investor presentation** is a pitch made to people who might put money into the venture. It is usually a stand-up talk with slides, and it is more formal than a pitch competition. OpenStax notes that investors are mainly interested in the team, the product, the value proposition and the likely return, and that a good pitch explains not only what makes the product good but what makes the market good (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 7.3).

Investors also ask about risk. OpenStax's six elements include how risks will be mitigated, so an investor presentation should name the two or three biggest risks and say what the venture does about them. A presenter who pretends there are no risks loses credibility fast.

A **pitch deck** is the slide set behind the talk. OpenStax calls it a slide presentation that gives a quick overview of the product and what you are asking for, and advises that it be visual, easy to edit, and matched to what the speaker says (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 7.3). Three practical habits make a deck work:

1. **One idea per slide.** A slide with four ideas has no idea.
2. **Slides support the speaker, not replace them.** If the audience is reading, they are not listening.
3. **Numbers that can be checked.** Say where a figure comes from: a count, a survey, a price list.

An investor presentation usually ends with a clear **ask** and a **next step**: what you want, and what the listener can do now. It is also honest about what is not yet known. OpenStax points out that investors value a team that can explain a market and admit what it still has to learn, and that feedback from an investor is useful even when the answer is no (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 7.3).

**A worked example: what to cut from Hira's deck.** Keep the eight slides that carry the eight parts above, and cut the school's history, the team photographs and the slide of thank-yous. If a slide does not move one of the eight parts forward, it is a distraction.

### Communication skills

A pitch is delivered by a person, so the delivery decides much of the result. OpenStax advises writing the pitch down to edit it, clearing away buzzwords, and practising it aloud, because an elevator speech is given in person (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 7.3). Communication has two channels, and both matter:

- **<Glossary term="Verbal Communication">Verbal communication</Glossary>** is the words: a clear opening, short sentences, numbers instead of vague claims, and no reading from the slides.
- **<Glossary term="Non-verbal Communication">Non-verbal communication</Glossary>** is everything else: standing still, facing the listeners, eye contact with the whole room, a voice that carries to the back, and hands that are still when not pointing at something.

Two further skills decide how a pitch lands with a panel:

1. **Listening.** A pitch is not a speech to be survived. When a panel asks a question, the speaker should listen to the whole question, answer it directly, and not return to the prepared script. OpenStax notes that investor feedback, including criticism, should be considered carefully (Laverty & Littel, 2020, section 7.3).
2. **Handling questions.** Answer the question asked, not the one you wished for. If you do not know, say so and say how you would find out. A honest "I do not know yet, but I would check the price with two suppliers" is stronger than a invented figure.

**A worked example: Hira's delivery.** She stops reading the slides and memorises her opening sentence. She stands where the panel can see her face, looks at each member in turn, and pauses before answering the money question. When she does not know the cost of crates in another town, she says so and offers to check with two suppliers by the next day. The panel notices the honesty more than the gap.

## Activity: Two minutes that count

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

1. Each person chooses a venture idea for a school or neighbourhood: a reading corner, a breakfast roll stall, a phone repair corner, a uniform-mending service, or one of your own.
2. Write a two-minute pitch using the seven parts in the flowchart, and say it aloud to your partner without reading.
3. The partner times it and gives two pieces of feedback, one thing that worked and one thing to change. Feedback is about the delivery, not the idea.
4. Switch roles, and deliver the second pitch with the change made.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* List the seven parts of the pitch structure in this topic, in order.
2. *(Remember)* Name the two channels of communication and give one example of each.
3. *(Understand)* Explain in two sentences why a pitch with twenty slides usually fails where a ten-slide pitch succeeds.
4. *(Understand)* A classmate says, "If an investor asks about risk, I should say there is none." Explain what is wrong with that answer in two or three sentences.
5. *(Apply)* A teammate is to pitch a breakfast-roll stall at your school. Her draft opens with the school's history and ends with "thank you". Rewrite her opening and her closing so that they carry the hook and the ask.

## Summary

A pitch is a short, planned talk that asks for something, and it has a shape: a hook, the problem, the solution, the market, the model, the team and the ask, ending in a clear next step. An investor presentation is a formal version of the same thing, and it should name the main risks and say how the venture will handle them. The deck supports the speaker rather than replacing them. Delivery decides much of the result: plain words, a voice that carries, eye contact, still hands, and honest answers to questions. A short pitch with a specific ask beats a long one with many slides.

## Try this at your practicum school

Attend a school event where someone speaks to a group: a parent-teacher meeting, a morning assembly address, or a local council or business gathering. Watch one speaker for five minutes and note three things: how they opened, whether they looked at the audience, and how they handled one question. Write five lines on what you would copy and what you would change if you were speaking.

## Summative task

Outline a three-minute pitch for your course-project venture, the one you have built across Units 2 to 7. Write 250-300 words.

1. Give the hook, the problem and the solution in one short paragraph each.
2. State the market, the model and the team, with one concrete detail for each.
3. State your ask: the amount or help you want, and what it will buy or do.
4. Name one risk and say how you would reduce it.
5. Write the first sentence of your delivery, and say one non-verbal habit you will keep.
