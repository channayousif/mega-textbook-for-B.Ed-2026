# Topic 2.4 - Professionalism, inclusion and the whole lesson

## A real classroom situation

In Week 8 Riffat watches one complete Class 5 lesson from the first minute to the last. The teaching is steady, the pupils are busy, and she is about to write "a good lesson" when she notices two things. At the gate before the lesson, the teacher speaks sharply to a parent who has come to ask about her child, and in the staffroom she says to a colleague, "That class is hopeless, I have given up on them." Inside the room, a girl who uses a wheelchair sits at the back near the door. She has the same workbook as everyone else, but in forty minutes the teacher never speaks to her, never moves towards her desk, and never checks her work. Riffat realises that the lesson she was about to praise is not the same lesson for every pupil, and that how a teacher speaks about pupils when no pupil can hear is also part of what she is there to observe.

## Explanation

### The teacher's professional conduct and interaction with colleagues and parents

Professional conduct is the behaviour a teacher shows as a member of a profession: towards pupils, colleagues, parents and the school. It is visible long before a lesson begins and long after it ends. The HEC practicum handbook expects student-teachers to work with parents and the community under the guidance of the cooperating teacher, and to observe how a teacher plans, speaks and carries responsibility (HEC, 2013). McBurney-Fry's guide to the practicum treats professional behaviour as something a trainee learns by watching practitioners, including how they handle disagreement and confidential information (McBurney-Fry, 2002).

When you observe conduct, look for:

1. **Punctuality and preparation.** Does the teacher arrive before the class, have the materials ready, and start on time?
2. **Language towards pupils.** Is the tone respectful, including when a pupil is wrong or slow? Does the teacher avoid sarcasm, nicknames and public shaming?
3. **Fairness.** Does the teacher call on the same pupils, or does opportunity move around the room? Does the teacher allow mistakes to be corrected?
4. **Collegiality.** How does the teacher speak to colleagues, especially about a class or a pupil? Confidentiality is an ethical duty, not a preference.
5. **Interaction with parents.** Does the teacher receive parents courteously, listen, and share only what is needed and appropriate?

**Worked example.** Riffat records: *"Before the lesson, at the gate, the teacher told a parent, 'Your daughter never works. Take her home if you want.' In the lesson, she praised three pupils by name and ignored the rest. In the staffroom she said, 'That class is hopeless.' In all three moments, the pupils were not addressed as learners."* She has not written that the teacher is unprofessional. She has recorded three moments that a supervisor could raise with the teacher, and she has noted that two of them happened where pupils or parents could hear.

> **Common misconception:** "Professional conduct means being strict and formal." Professional conduct is about trust. A teacher can be warm and still professional, and a teacher can be quiet and still unprofessional if pupils or colleagues are treated without respect.

### Inclusive practice in the classroom

Inclusive practice is teaching that makes sure every pupil can take part and learn. It is wider than disability. It includes girls and boys, pupils who speak another language at home, pupils who learn more slowly or more quickly, pupils who are poor, and pupils who are often absent. Teach Primary defines inclusive teaching practices as those that create a classroom where all pupils can participate, and its second edition was revised specifically to capture them better (Molina et al., 2021). Educational psychology reminds us that individual differences are normal, that they have many causes, and that a teacher's task is to respond to them rather than to label them (HEC, 2013).

Observable signs that a classroom is inclusive include:

- **Multiple ways to take part,** not only putting up a hand: pair talk, writing on a slate, a role in a group.
- **Seating and grouping that include every pupil,** with the teacher moving towards pupils who are far from the front.
- **The teacher responding to individual needs,** such as giving a pupil more time, a different task, or a material they can use.
- **No bias or stereotype in the teacher's talk,** so that no group is praised, blamed or ignored because of who they are.
- **Materials and the room accessible to every pupil,** including a pupil who uses a wheelchair or has difficulty seeing the board.

**Worked example.** In the lesson Riffat observed, the pupil in the wheelchair had a workbook but was never called on, never approached and never checked. Riffat does not write "the teacher is not inclusive." She writes: *"In 40 minutes, 0 teacher-to-pupil moves to Desk 12 (the pupil in the wheelchair). 22 moves to the front two rows. The pupil's workbook was closed at the end of the period."* The table below gives four practices and an observable sign for each, so that a record can stay factual.

> **Common misconception:** "Inclusion is only about pupils with disabilities." Disability is one part of inclusion. A classroom can include every disabled pupil and still exclude girls, slow learners, or a pupil who cannot read the board. Look for who takes part, not only for who has a diagnosis.

### Observing one complete lesson from start to end

The course guide asks you to observe one complete lesson from start to end. A single lesson is the smallest unit in which all the parts of teaching must work together: the opening must set up the practice, the practice must feed the check, and the check must feed the closure. Watching only the middle of a lesson tells you nothing about how the teacher got there or what was done with what came out.

A complete lesson record works best as a timeline. Divide the period into minutes, write the time and the event, and mark which focus area each event belongs to. Then add the other tools: a time-on-task tally, an interaction count and an inclusion note.

**Worked example.** Riffat's timeline for a Grade 4 lesson is shown in the figure above. It reads: *"0 to 5 min: entry and attendance; 5 to 7: objective on the board; 7 to 18: explanation with bundles of counters; 18 to 26: guided practice in pairs; 26 to 31: teacher questioning, 6 questions, 2 to the back rows; 31 to 34: summary on the board; 34 to 35: exit ticket, one line each."* From this she can see that the lesson had a clear shape, that the practice followed the explanation, and that the questioning reached the back rows only twice.

## Activity: The lesson through an inclusion lens

**Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.**

1. Read this whole-lesson record:
   > "The teacher stated the objective, explained for 15 minutes using the textbook, then asked questions for 8 minutes. All 9 questions went to boys in the front two rows. Pupils then copied an exercise for 12 minutes. Two girls at the back asked each other for help three times. The teacher said, 'Work silently.' At the end, the teacher collected 24 of 38 books."
2. Mark the record where a pupil could have been included and was not. Write one factual observation for each mark.
3. Draft one question you could ask the teacher that would open a conversation about inclusion without sounding like an accusation.
4. Compare your question with another pair and choose the one you would actually use.

## Check your understanding

1. Name four signs of professional conduct you can observe outside the classroom itself. *(Remember)*
2. Explain what inclusive practice means and why it is wider than disability. *(Understand)*
3. A lesson record shows that every question went to the front two rows and that a pupil in a wheelchair was never approached. Identify two observations and explain what each suggests about inclusion. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Professional conduct, inclusion and the whole lesson complete the placement picture. Conduct tells you how a teacher treats pupils, colleagues and parents when the lesson plan is not in the room. Inclusion tells you whether the lesson reaches every pupil. A minute-by-minute timeline lets you hold the whole lesson in view at once, so that the parts can be judged together rather than in isolation.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can describe professional conduct with observable examples.
- [ ] I can explain what inclusive practice means and name its observable signs.
- [ ] I can record one complete lesson as a minute-by-minute timeline.
- [ ] I can combine a timeline with time on task, interaction and inclusion notes.

## Try this at your practicum school

During your final placement week, observe one complete lesson and record it as a minute-by-minute timeline. Add one inclusion observation and one note on the teacher's professional conduct outside the room. Keep the record factual, and use it as the basis for a conversation with your mentor teacher about what you would do differently.

## Summative task

Using the whole-lesson record in the activity above, write a 200 to 250 word analysis of the lesson. Identify two features that made the lesson effective, two that limited inclusion, and for each limitation propose a specific change. Then draw a minute-by-minute timeline of the same lesson, marking the point where you would change the teaching and what you would do instead.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Below Standard (1 mark) | Meets Standard (2 marks) | Exceeds Standard (3 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Analysis of inclusion and effectiveness** *(Analyze)* | Comments on inclusion in general terms without evidence from the record. | Identifies two effective features and two limits on inclusion, each with specific evidence from the record. | Explains how the effective features and the exclusion interact, and justifies which change would matter most. |
| **Proposed changes** *(Apply)* | Proposes changes that are vague or unrelated to the record. | Proposes one specific change for each limitation. | Proposes changes that a teacher could make in the next lesson, with a way to tell they worked. |
