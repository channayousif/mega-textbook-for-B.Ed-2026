# Topic 2.2 - Observing teaching

## A real classroom situation

In Week 6 Riffat's mentor asks her to observe at least three or four lessons in different subjects. She watches a mathematics lesson that is immaculately organised: the objective is on the board, the steps are clear, the summary is neat. Almost no pupil speaks. The next day she watches a science lesson that looks chaotic: pupils are out of their seats, the room is noisy, and the teacher seems to be chasing one group after another. Yet by the end, the pupils can explain the experiment to her. Riffat is torn. She cannot tell her mentor that one lesson was "good" and the other "bad" without sounding as if she is judging personality rather than practice. She needs a way to compare the two lessons that a reader could check.

## Explanation

### Observing three to four lessons in different subjects

One lesson is a sample of one. It tells you what happened in one room on one day, with one teacher, one class and one topic. A second lesson by the same teacher can look very different, because the topic, the time of day and the pupils' mood all change. This is why the course guide asks you to observe at least three to four lessons in different subjects during the placement. Several lessons let you separate a one-off event from a pattern.

Three habits make a small sample fair:

1. **Hold the context steady where you can.** Observe the same class across subjects if your mentor allows it. Then differences in participation are more likely to be about teaching than about the pupils.
2. **Vary the subject.** A reading lesson, a mathematics lesson and a science lesson draw on different methods. A single subject can give you a misleading idea of a teacher's range.
3. **Record the same focus areas every time.** If you look at objectives, methods, aids and participation in every lesson, you can compare lesson with lesson instead of impression with impression.

The HEC practicum handbook asks student-teachers to document observations of three to four lessons using proformas, and to note lesson objectives, teaching methods and strategies, use of teaching aids, and student engagement and participation (HEC, 2013). The HEC guide on general methods of teaching supplies the vocabulary for what you will see, including teacher-centred and student-centred strategies, questioning, wait time, and the use of low-cost materials (HEC, 2013).

> **Common misconception:** "One good or bad lesson tells you whether a teacher is good." A single observation is a snapshot, not a verdict. Judge a teacher by a pattern across lessons, and describe the pattern in evidence rather than the teacher in adjectives.

### Lesson focus: objectives, methods and strategies, teaching aids, pupil participation

Four focus areas carry most of what an observer needs from a lesson.

**1. Lesson objectives.** An objective says what pupils will be able to do by the end, not what the teacher will cover. "Pupils will solve at least eight of ten two-digit addition problems" is an objective; "Adding two-digit numbers" is a topic (HEC, 2013). Note whether the objective is shared with the class, whether the activities match it, and whether the closing check tests it.

**2. Teaching methods and strategies.** Record the actual strategy, not a label. "The teacher explained place value for twelve minutes using bundles of ten sticks, then asked pairs to make numbers with their own sticks" is a record. "Good teaching" is not. Teach Primary's instruction element separates articulating the objective, explaining content in more than one form, connecting the lesson to pupils' lives, modelling by thinking aloud, checking for understanding, giving specific feedback, and asking open-ended questions (Molina et al., 2021).

**3. Teaching aids.** Note what was used and how: the textbook, the chalkboard and its organisation, concrete objects, charts, and low- and no-cost materials made from local resources (HEC, 2013). An aid that stays in the teacher's bag is not used.

**4. Pupil participation.** Count what pupils actually do. How many answer? Who are they? How long do they think before answering? How many are off task? Teach Primary measures this with short snapshots: a 1 to 10 second scan in which the observer records whether the teacher is providing a learning activity and how many pupils are on task (Molina et al., 2021).

**Worked example.** Riffat records a Class 5 mathematics lesson like this: *"Objective on board: 'Add two-digit numbers with carrying.' Teacher demonstrated with 3 bundles of 10 sticks and 7 single sticks. 6 pupils came to the front; 21 of 34 pupils practised on slate paper. Teacher asked 11 questions, 4 of them open-ended; wait time after questions was about 2 seconds. No textbook used."* Every clause is observable. Her mentor can see exactly what happened and can discuss it without feeling accused.

> **Common misconception:** "A lesson that follows the plan is a good lesson." A plan is a promise. What matters is whether the pupils learned, and that is only visible in what they do during the lesson. Record the doing, not the intending.

## Activity: Two lessons, one proforma

**Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.**

1. Read these two short descriptions of the same Class 4 lesson, recorded by two different observers:
   > **Observer A:** "The teacher was confident and the lesson was interesting. The pupils were well behaved."
   > **Observer B:** "Teacher stated the objective at 9:05. Explained the water cycle for 9 minutes using a chart. Asked 6 questions, all to boys in the front row. Pupils copied the diagram for 12 minutes. No pupil asked a question."
2. Fill in one row each of the lesson-observation proforma for Observer B's record: objectives, methods, aids, participation, questioning.
3. Explain to your partner why Observer B's record is more useful to a mentor teacher, using two specific reasons.
4. Rewrite Observer A's three sentences so that they are still positive but observable.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the four focus areas of a lesson observation and state one thing you would record under each. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why observing three to four lessons in different subjects gives a fairer picture than observing one. *(Understand)*
3. A lesson objective reads "Pupils will enjoy stories." Rewrite it as a measurable objective and say what you would look for during the lesson to know it was met. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Observing teaching well means observing several lessons, not one, and recording the same four focus areas each time. Objectives, methods, aids and participation turn a vague impression of a lesson into evidence that a mentor and a trainee can discuss. The lesson stages give the observation a shape, so that nothing important is missed between the opening and the closure.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can explain why a sample of three to four lessons is fairer than a single lesson.
- [ ] I can distinguish a lesson objective from a topic.
- [ ] I can record methods, aids, participation and questioning in low-inference language.
- [ ] I can track a lesson through its stages from opening to closure.

## Try this at your practicum school

Over the next two placement weeks, observe three lessons in different subjects using the proforma in the figure above. After each lesson, write one sentence that a mentor could act on. Bring the three proformas and your sentences to a feedback conversation with your mentor teacher, and ask which focus area they would watch if they observed you.

## Summative task

Design a six-item lesson-observation proforma for a Grade 5 science lesson. Three items must track objectives and methods, two must track aids and participation, and one must track questioning. For each item, write the observable indicator an observer would record. Then write 100 to 150 words justifying why your proforma would give a fair picture of the lesson to a mentor teacher who was not present.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Below Standard (1 mark) | Meets Standard (2 marks) | Exceeds Standard (3 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Analysis of the focus areas** *(Analyze)* | Items repeat one focus area or mix focus areas without reason. | Six items spread across objectives and methods, aids and participation, and questioning, each with a clear reason. | A deliberate, justified allocation that shows how the items combine to give a fair picture of the lesson. |
| **Observable indicators** *(Apply)* | Indicators are vague or evaluative (for example "teacher is enthusiastic"). | Indicators describe what an observer could see or hear. | Indicators are precise, countable or verbatim, with no judgement words. |
| **Justification** *(Understand)* | The justification is a general claim about observation. | The justification explains why a mentor could trust the proforma. | The justification anticipates a weakness in the proforma and addresses it. |
