# Topic 2.3 - Observing management and assessment

## A real classroom situation

In Week 7 Riffat sits in on two Class 6 lessons. In the first, the room is silent. The teacher reads from the textbook and the pupils copy. Halfway through, Riffat tallies the class and finds that fourteen of thirty pupils are not writing, not looking at the board and not responding when asked. In the second lesson, the room is noisy. Pupils are arguing about a science question in their groups, and the teacher is circling the room, stopping at each group. Later the teacher tells Riffat, "We do not test the pupils in this school. They are too young." Yet in the lesson Riffat watched, the teacher asked twenty-six questions, marked a short quiz on the board and set a homework task. Riffat is confused about what "managed" and "assessed" actually mean.

## Explanation

### Managing the class: discipline techniques, time management, pupil behaviour and engagement

Classroom management is everything a teacher does to make learning possible: setting expectations, running routines, moving between activities, and responding to behaviour. It is not the same as discipline, which is only one part of it. A well-managed class is one in which pupils know what to do, can do it, and keep doing it.

Observe these four things:

1. **Discipline techniques.** Does the teacher state expectations before an activity? Does the teacher acknowledge positive behaviour, redirect misbehaviour to the expected behaviour, and move towards a disturbance rather than raising their voice? Teach Primary's element on positive behavioural expectations separates setting clear expectations, acknowledging positive behaviour, and redirecting misbehaviour while focusing on the expected behaviour rather than the undesired one (Molina et al., 2021).
2. **Time management.** Record how long each part of the lesson takes and how long transitions take. Time on task is the share of the lesson in which pupils are actually doing the learning activity. Teach Primary measures it with three short snapshots of 1 to 10 seconds each, scanning the room and recording how many pupils are on task (Molina et al., 2021). A tally across the lesson gives a fairer picture than one snapshot.
3. **Pupil behaviour.** Note what pupils do, not what you think of them. "Six pupils talked to neighbours while the teacher was at Desk 1" is a record; "the class was undisciplined" is not.
4. **Engagement.** Behaviour alone is not engagement. A silent pupil may be copying without understanding. Look for signs that pupils are thinking: answering, asking, arguing, writing, making mistakes and correcting them. Educational psychology reminds us that behaviour must be read against the pupil's stage of development and individual differences, and that motivation is shaped by the social context of the home, school and community (HEC, 2013).

**Worked example.** Over a 35-minute lesson, Riffat tallies the class in five-minute blocks and records the counts shown in the figure above. She writes: *"Blocks 1 to 3: 32 to 34 of 38 on task. Block 4: 18 of 38, after a 7-minute transition to group work. Blocks 5 to 7: 28 to 31 of 38. Average 29 of 38. The dip follows the transition, not the topic."* She has found a specific, changeable moment rather than a general complaint.

> **Common misconception:** "A quiet class is always a well-managed class." Silence can mean concentration, but it can also mean copying, fear or disengagement. A well-managed class is better judged by what pupils do with the learning task than by the noise level. Record the task and the pupils' action on it.

### Assessment methods in use: oral questions, quizzes and homework

Assessment is any way a teacher finds out what pupils have learned and then acts on it. In Pakistani primary classrooms it takes many forms, and the HEC guide on general methods of teaching distinguishes formative assessment, which happens during teaching and changes it, from summative assessment, which reviews learning at the end of a unit or term (HEC, 2013).

The methods you are likely to see:

- **Oral questions,** from the teacher to the class or to individuals, including open and closed questions and the wait time after them.
- **Observation of work,** as the teacher moves around the room during group or independent work.
- **Short quizzes,** on the board or on paper, often unannounced.
- **Homework checks,** collecting, marking and returning written work.
- **Written tests,** the formal end-of-unit or end-of-term kind.

Record not just which method was used, but what the teacher did with the result. Feedback is what turns assessment into learning. Teach Primary's feedback element asks whether the teacher gives specific comments that clarify misunderstandings or identify successes (Molina et al., 2021). A quiz that is marked and returned with a comment is a different event from a quiz that is never looked at again.

> **Common misconception:** "Assessment happens only in tests." Oral questions, observations of group work, short quizzes and homework checks are all assessment. A teacher who never sets a test may still be assessing pupils constantly, and a teacher who tests often may not be assessing at all if the results are never used. ## Activity: Quiet or managed?

**Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.**

1. Read these two short records of the same period in two different classrooms:
   > **Class A:** "The room was silent. The teacher wrote on the board and 30 pupils copied. Fourteen pupils stopped writing for more than two minutes."
   > **Class B:** "The room was noisy. Pupils argued in four groups. The teacher moved from group to group and asked each group one question. Three groups had the wrong answer; the teacher re-taught the step."
2. Decide which class was better managed. Give two pieces of evidence for your decision, and one piece of evidence that would change your mind.
3. For the class you judged less well managed, write two factual observations the teacher could act on.
4. Compare your judgement with another pair and note where you read the same evidence differently.

## Check your understanding

1. Name three discipline techniques an observer can look for, and state what you would record for each. *(Remember)*
2. Explain what time on task means and describe one way to measure it during a lesson. *(Understand)*
3. A teacher says, "I do not believe in assessment, so I never test my class." Observe that in one lesson the teacher asks fifteen oral questions and marks a homework exercise. Explain why the teacher is assessing after all, and what the teacher does with the results. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Management and assessment are best observed as behaviour and evidence, not as atmosphere and reputation. Time on task tells you how much of a lesson pupils actually spend learning, and a record of assessment methods tells you whether the teacher uses what pupils do to change the teaching. Both are visible, both can be recorded in low-inference language, and both give a mentor something concrete to work on.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can describe discipline techniques and record them without judging the teacher.
- [ ] I can measure time on task with snapshots or a tally across a lesson.
- [ ] I can distinguish formative from summative assessment.
- [ ] I can list oral questions, quizzes and homework checks as assessment methods.

## Try this at your practicum school

During one placement week, choose two lessons and complete a time-on-task tally in five-minute blocks for each. Then, for one of the same lessons, record every assessment method you see and note what the teacher did with the result. Bring both records to your mentor teacher and ask which one they would find more useful in a feedback conversation.

## Summative task

Read this short record of a Grade 4 lesson:

> "The teacher greeted the class and wrote the objective on the board. For twelve minutes she explained the topic while 30 pupils watched. She then asked pupils to open their books and copy a exercise. Four pupils finished quickly and began talking; the teacher told them to be quiet three times. At the end of the period she collected the books and said she would mark them at home."

Analyse the management and the assessment in this lesson (200 to 250 words). Identify two strengths, two weaknesses, and for each weakness propose one specific change the teacher could make. Support every point with evidence from the record.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Below Standard (1 mark) | Meets Standard (2 marks) | Exceeds Standard (3 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Analysis of management and assessment** *(Analyze)* | Identifies problems without connecting them to evidence or to learning. | Identifies two strengths and two weaknesses, each tied to specific evidence in the record. | Explains how the management and the assessment interact, and ranks the changes by likely effect on learning. |
| **Proposed changes** *(Apply)* | Proposes vague advice such as "be stricter". | Proposes one specific, workable change for each weakness. | Proposes changes that are specific, realistic for a large class, and linked to a measurable indicator. |
| **Use of evidence** *(Understand)* | Quotes the record rarely or not at all. | Uses specific details from the record for most points. | Uses precise details consistently and distinguishes observation from inference. |
