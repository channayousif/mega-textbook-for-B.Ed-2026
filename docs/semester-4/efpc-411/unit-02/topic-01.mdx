# Topic 2.1 - Reading a school and a classroom

## A real classroom situation

In the first week of her fifteen-day placement, Riffat arrives at Government Girls Elementary School in Ghotki. The headteacher, proud of the new boundary wall and the freshly painted classrooms, takes her on a tour. Riffat notes both with care. Then she sits in on Class 4. Fifty-eight pupils share one textbook between five, and the teacher reads and explains from the front for the whole period while the pupils copy in silence. In the staffroom the headteacher tells her, "We are the best school in the union council because our building is the best." Riffat goes home unsure how to record what she saw without sounding disrespectful, and unsure which of the two impressions, the proud building or the silent class, she should trust.

## Explanation

### Observing the school environment: physical layout, resources and the discipline system

The **school environment** is the physical and material setting in which teaching and learning happen. It is the first thing an observer can record, because it is visible, factual and hard to argue with. The HEC practicum handbook asks student-teachers to complete an inventory of school resources and to record the school's discipline procedures and policy during the first placement week (Higher Education Commission [HEC], 2013). The World Bank's Teach Primary manual adds that the learning environment includes the accessibility of the physical environment, the classroom set-up and the materials available (Molina et al., 2021).

When you observe the school environment, note at least these features:

1. **The building and grounds:** the number and size of classrooms, the boundary wall, the playground, the condition of the roof and floor, and how pupils move between rooms.
2. **Water, sanitation and safety:** a working hand pump or tap, separate toilets for girls and boys, a clean drinking-water point, and a safe way in and out of the building.
3. **Learning resources:** textbooks, chalk and dusters, a chalkboard in good repair, charts, a small library or reading corner, and science or mathematics materials.
4. **Classroom display:** pupils' work on the walls, a timetable, a class rules chart, an attendance board and any assistive aids.
5. **The discipline system:** the rules that are displayed, how assembly is run, who is responsible for discipline, how good behaviour is acknowledged, and how problems are handled and recorded.

> **Common misconception:** "A tidy, well-built school is a good school." A new building is real evidence, but it is evidence about resources, not about learning. A school can be clean, walled and silent and still teach very little. Read the environment as one layer of evidence, and test it against what you see in the classrooms.

**Worked example.** Suppose you are recording the row for "Classroom resources" on a school-environment observation sheet. A weak entry is: *"The school has good resources."* A usable low-inference entry is: *"Class 4 has 58 pupils and 12 mathematics textbooks. The teacher keeps the textbooks in a locked cupboard and brings out one for the demonstration."* The second entry states what is there, how many, and what the teacher does with it. A reader can act on it; the first cannot. The completed sheet below shows five such rows.

### School rules, routines and the daily schedule

A routine is a repeated, predictable sequence of actions that the school or the class follows without having to decide it each time. Taking the register, lining up for assembly, distributing notebooks, cleaning the board and closing with a prayer are all routines. Routines matter to an observer for three reasons. They protect learning time, because pupils do not have to wonder what happens next. They are low-inference evidence, because you can record exactly what was done and when. And they reveal the school's priorities, because a school that spends twenty minutes on assembly and five on reading is telling you what it values.

The HEC practicum handbook asks student-teachers to note both the routines the cooperating teacher has established and the routines that exist in the school as a whole (HEC, 2013). The HEC guide on general methods of teaching lists establishing rules and routines as one of the first tasks of managing complexity in the classroom (HEC, 2013).

Observe the daily schedule as well as the rules on paper. Compare the printed timetable with what actually happens: when the gates open, when assembly begins and ends, how long each period really runs, when the break falls, and how attendance is recorded.

**Worked example.** At a government primary school in Dadu, the printed timetable gives forty minutes to each of six periods. On the day Riffat observes, assembly runs from 8:00 to 8:22, the first period starts at 8:31 and ends at 9:02, and the second period is cut short by a staff meeting at 9:20. Her record reads: *"Timetable shows 6 periods of 40 min. Observed: assembly 22 min, period 1 = 31 min, period 2 = 18 min (ended early)."* She has not said the school is badly run. She has recorded a gap between the plan and the reality, which is exactly what a later discussion with her mentor can use.

### Reading one classroom: interaction, seating and climate

Classroom climate is the felt quality of life in the room: whether pupils seem safe, willing to try, and willing to be wrong. Teacher-pupil interaction is the pattern of who speaks to whom, how often, and about what. Seating arrangement is how desks and pupils are placed, and it shapes who can see the board, who can talk to whom, and who the teacher can reach.

The HEC practicum handbook directs observers to look at the classroom environment, the placement of materials, the arrangement of work spaces, the traffic patterns, and classroom interactions including whole-class teaching and teacher-to-student, student-to-student and student-to-teacher-initiated interactions (HEC, 2013). Teach Primary's classroom culture element describes the same ground: a supportive learning environment in which the teacher treats pupils respectfully, uses positive language, responds to needs and shows no bias, and positive behavioural expectations that are stated, acknowledged and redirected (Molina et al., 2021).

A practical way to record interaction is a seating map with a small set of codes. Draw the room from above, mark the teacher's position and each pupil's desk, then add codes as you watch:

- **T-P:** the teacher speaks to, questions or acknowledges a pupil.
- **P-P:** pupils speak to each other, as in pair or group work.
- **P-T:** a pupil speaks to the teacher, asks a question or offers an answer.

**Worked example.** In a Class 5 Sindhi lesson, Riffat maps the room and tallies twenty-two T-P moves, three P-P moves and four P-T moves over thirty minutes. All twenty-two T-P moves go to pupils in the front two rows. She does not write "the teacher ignores the back rows." She writes: *"22 teacher-to-pupil moves in 30 min; 18 to the front two rows, 4 to the back four rows; no pupil-to-pupil talk observed."* The map in the figure above shows the same idea with arrows.

> **Common misconception:** "Row seating means poor teaching." Seating is a tool, not a verdict. Rows can support a focused written task; groups can support a discussion that never happens. Judge the seating by what it makes easy or hard, and record the interaction that actually follows from it.

## Activity: Two schools, two readings

**Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.**

1. Read these two short descriptions:
   > **School A:** A bright, two-storey building with a boundary wall, a tidy playground and a decorated reception board. In Class 4 the teacher explains from the front for the whole period and the thirty pupils copy in silence.
   > **School B:** A single-storey building with two classrooms and no playground. In Class 4 the twenty-two pupils sit in four groups, the teacher moves between them, and pupils explain their answers to one another.
2. Decide which three observations you would make first in each school to find out whether learning is happening. Give a reason for each choice.
3. Write two questions you would ask the headteacher in each school, keeping the questions factual and respectful.
4. Swap your choices with another pair. Say which observation you would add and why.

## Check your understanding

1. Define the school environment in one sentence and name four features an observer records. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why routines are useful evidence for an observer, giving two reasons. *(Understand)*
3. A friend visits a school and says, "It is clearly a good school, the building is new and the classrooms are silent." Identify two observations that would test her claim, and say what each would show. *(Apply)*

## Summary

A school's environment, rules, routines and schedule are the first and most factual layer of observation, and they can be recorded without judgement. A classroom adds the layer of interaction: who speaks to whom, where pupils sit, and what the room feels like. Together they give a trainee a grounded picture of the placement school, and they keep the trainee from mistaking appearance for quality.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can describe the school environment and record its features in low-inference language.
- [ ] I can explain why routines and the daily schedule are useful evidence.
- [ ] I can draw a classroom seating map and use T-P, P-P and P-T codes to record interaction.
- [ ] I can describe classroom climate and seating without turning either into a verdict.

## Try this at your practicum school

During your first two placement days, complete one school-environment observation sheet for the five areas in the figure above. Then choose one classroom and, in a single period, draw a seating map and tally T-P, P-P and P-T moves for ten minutes. Keep both records factual, and bring them to your next discussion with your mentor teacher.

## Summative task

Choose one of the two schools in the activity, or your own placement school. Complete the school-environment observation sheet for all five areas, then draw a classroom seating map with interaction codes for one period. Write 150 to 200 words analysing which two features of what you observed are most likely to affect learning, and justify your judgement with specific evidence from your own records.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Below Standard (1 mark) | Meets Standard (2 marks) | Exceeds Standard (3 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Analysis of environment and classroom evidence** *(Analyze)* | Describes features without connecting them to learning; evidence is vague or missing. | Connects at least two observed features to their likely effect on learning, with specific low-inference evidence from the sheet or the map. | Argues a clear, well-ordered judgement about which features matter most, supported by precise evidence and a reason for the ranking. |
| **Quality of the records** *(Apply)* | Entries are opinions, or the map codes are missing or inconsistent. | Entries are observable and factual; the map uses the codes consistently. | Entries are exemplary low-inference records with counts, times or verbatim detail. |
| **Professional tone** *(Understand)* | The writing judges the school or the teacher personally. | The writing stays descriptive and respectful. | The writing models how a trainee can raise a difficult finding respectfully. |
