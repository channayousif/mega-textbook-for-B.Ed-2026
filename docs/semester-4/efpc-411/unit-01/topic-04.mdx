# Topic 1.4 - From findings to feedback and growth

## A real classroom situation

Following a 40-minute social studies lesson on provincial geography in a Mirpurkhas primary school, two student-teachers, Naveed and Bilal, conduct their peer debriefing. Naveed begins immediately: *"Bilal, your lesson had several big problems. You spoke too quickly, you did not use the wall map properly, and you lost control when the students were copying questions from the board. You need to fix all of these before the supervisor arrives next week."* Bilal becomes defensive, crosses his arms, and retorts: *"The children were quiet and finished their work on time. You are just being overly critical because you think you are better than me."*

Their university supervisor, who observed the interaction from a nearby desk, intervenes. She models a structured feedback approach: *"Bilal, let us look at the evidence Naveed collected. During the first ten minutes, you engaged all forty students by asking four open-ended questions about their home villages, and twenty-eight students raised their hands. That was a very strong opening. At 10:18 AM, when you asked students to copy the map questions from the board, twelve students in the back could not see because the chalk was faint, which led to talking. What is one practical adjustment you could make to ensure board work is legible for everyone?"* Bilal relaxes, looks at the timestamped notes, and says: *"I could write in larger letters on the left board and read each question aloud as I write it."* Naveed sees how descriptive, evidence-based feedback leads to problem-solving, whereas evaluative criticism produces resistance.

## Explanation

### Analysing observation findings

Raw observation notes - whether tallies, transcripts, or checklists - are only raw data. To become useful, they must be analysed systematically (HEC, 2013). Data analysis involves looking across the entire lesson to identify patterns, relationships, and trends rather than reacting to isolated moments.

When analysing observation records, observers look for four specific patterns:

1. **Time distribution across lesson stages:** Observers calculate how many total minutes were devoted to teacher talk versus student active learning. In many traditional Pakistani classrooms, teachers lecture for 30 minutes of a 35-minute period, leaving only 5 minutes for hurried copying (HEC, 2013). Analysing time stamps makes this imbalance visible.
2. **Distribution of teacher attention and questioning:** Observers review seating charts and question tallies to examine equity. Did the teacher address questions to all sectors of the room, or did questioning cluster around the front benches? Were higher-order questions directed to both boys and girls?
3. **Alignment between objectives and student work:** Observers compare the stated learning objective with the actual tasks students completed. For example, if the stated objective was "students will compare the climates of Sindh and Punjab", but students merely copied textbook definitions, the analysis reveals a misalignment between planned intent and actual cognitive demand.
4. **Triggers of pupil engagement or off-task behaviour:** Observers cross-reference student inattention with specific teacher moves. Often, disengagement occurs during slow transitions, prolonged dictation, or when teacher instructions are ambiguous.

### Giving professional and constructive feedback

Providing feedback to another teacher is a delicate professional skill. Evaluative criticism damages relationships and closes minds; conversely, empty compliments ("You did a great job!") provide no direction for growth. Professional feedback is **developmental, evidence-based, and focused on specific instructional actions** (Molina et al., 2021).

Effective feedback follows a four-step collaborative protocol:

1. **Invite teacher self-reflection first:** Begin the post-observation conference by asking the teacher to reflect on their own lesson: *"How did you feel the lesson went? What part of the lesson went exactly as you had planned?"* Allowing the teacher to speak first establishes a collegial, reflective atmosphere.
2. **Ground discussion in factual evidence:** Present specific low-inference data rather than opinions: *"According to the notes, when you showed the real wheat plant at 9:15 AM, thirty-two students leaned forward and asked eight spontaneous questions."*
3. **Focus on one or two priority areas:** Avoid overwhelming the teacher with a long list of deficiencies. Select one high-leverage area that will make the largest positive difference to student learning.
4. **Agree upon actionable next steps:** Collaborate with the teacher to formulate a concrete strategy they can test in their next lesson, ending with an agreed date for follow-up observation.

> **Common Misconception:** Student-teachers frequently believe that *giving feedback means pointing out everything that went wrong in the lesson*. This misconception turns post-observation conferences into stressful interrogations. Professional feedback balances recognition of authentic instructional strengths with focused, collaborative problem-solving around one clear growth target.

### Linking observation to self-improvement: the role of reflection in teaching practice

In teacher preparation, observing experienced teachers and peers is not merely an external exercise; its ultimate purpose is **self-improvement** (HEC, 2013). When a student-teacher watches another professional manage a multi-grade classroom or explain an abstract science concept, the observer holds up a mirror to their own emerging practice.

This process relies upon structured **reflective practice**. As teacher education scholar Donald Schön emphasized, professional expertise develops through *reflection-on-action* (analysing what occurred after an event) and *reflection-in-action* (adjusting instructional decisions in real time while teaching).

Student-teachers link observation to self-improvement through a three-step cycle:

1. **Observation as a source of instructional models:** The observer identifies specific teaching strategies that succeeded in the observed class (for example, using a call-and-response routine to quiet a noisy room) and examines why they worked in that context.
2. **Critical self-comparison:** The student-teacher asks: *"How do I typically handle this instructional moment? What challenges do I face when teaching similar concepts? What would happen if I adapted this strategy for my placement classroom?"*
3. **Formulating personal action targets:** The trainee translates observational insights into measurable personal goals recorded in their professional practicum portfolio (such as: *"In my next practicum lesson, I will pause for five seconds after asking each question before calling on a student"*).

#### Worked Example: From an Observation Finding to Personal Action

- **Observed finding:** In Class 4 mathematics, the teacher wrote four multiplication problems on the board and asked students to solve them in pairs using small bundles of ten matchsticks. All forty students were actively engaged, checking each other's work.
- **Analytical insight:** Using concrete manipulatives combined with structured peer work eliminated off-task behavior and allowed struggling learners to receive immediate peer explanation.
- **Personal growth target:** "In my upcoming Grade 3 place-value lesson on Tuesday, I will prepare bundles of ten ice cream sticks for every desk pair so students can physically bundle tens and units rather than relying solely on textbook diagrams."

## Activity: Conducting a peer feedback conference

Work in pairs. Time: 20 minutes.

1. Role-play a post-observation debriefing based on the following scenario:
   > *"Student A (the observer) has low-inference observation notes on Student B's 30-minute English lesson. Student B had an energetic storytelling opening (10 mins, high pupil interest), but during the grammar worksheet stage (20 mins), twelve students in the back rows did not understand the instructions and copied answers from their neighbours while Student B assisted only the front row."*
2. Student A must use the four-step feedback protocol: (a) invite Student B to share their self-reflection, (b) cite the specific evidence from the notes, (c) identify the strong storytelling opening, and (d) guide Student B to formulate a strategy for monitoring all rows during independent work.
3. Student B should respond authentically, moving from initial defensiveness to collaborative problem-solving.
4. Switch roles after 10 minutes and reflect on how it felt to give and receive evidence-based feedback.

## Summary

Observing classrooms yields valuable professional data that must be analysed for instructional patterns, equity of participation, and curricular alignment. Transforming these findings into professional growth requires skilled, constructive feedback that begins with teacher self-reflection, relies on low-inference evidence, celebrates genuine strengths, and focuses on agreed priority targets. Ultimately, observation is a powerful catalyst for self-improvement, enabling student-teachers to reflect critically on their own instructional choices and establish concrete pedagogical goals for their placement teaching.

Observe a colleague or peer student-teacher teaching a complete 35-minute lesson. During the lesson, track one specific dimension (such as the teacher's distribution of praise or wait-time). Immediately after the lesson, conduct a 10-minute feedback conversation using the four-step protocol: ask the peer how they felt, share two concrete positive moments backed by verbatim notes, explore one area for refinement, and help them write one actionable target in their reflective journal.

## Summative task

Read the following post-observation transcript between an observer (Zubair) and a student-teacher (Noman) following a Class 5 Mathematics lesson:

> **Zubair:** *"Noman, that lesson was quite chaotic. Your classroom management was poor. The boys on the side were making noise, and you took too long explaining fractions. You need to be much stricter."*  
> **Noman:** *"You do not understand this class. These boys are always noisy, and mathematics is hard. No one could have done better."*

Complete the following requirements (175-250 words):
1. **Feedback Critique:** Analyse Zubair's feedback approach and explain why it provoked defensiveness from Noman, identifying three specific violations of constructive feedback principles. *(Analyze)*
2. **Dialogue Reconstruction:** Rewrite the dialogue from Zubair's opening sentence as an exemplary post-observation conference following the four-step feedback protocol. Assume the factual notes show: (a) twenty-five students correctly solved the first three fraction problems, and (b) at 10:15 AM, four boys on the right bench talked while waiting eight minutes for their notebooks to be stamped. *(Apply)*
3. **Reflective Action Plan:** Write a two-sentence personal self-improvement target that Noman could derive from this reconstructed conference to improve his lesson pacing. *(Create)*
