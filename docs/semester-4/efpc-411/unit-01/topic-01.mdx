# Topic 1.1 - Why and how teachers are observed

## A real classroom situation

At Government Boys Elementary School in Matiari, Tariq, a fourth-semester B.Ed student, arrives for his first observation morning. He enters Class 5 with a notebook in hand, feeling nervous. The classroom teacher, Mr Ghulam, stiffens visibly as Tariq sits at the back. Mr Ghulam stops his planned group discussion on water conservation and switches abruptly to reading aloud from the textbook, demanding complete silence from the forty-two students.

During the break, Tariq speaks with Mr Ghulam in the staffroom. Mr Ghulam admits that he felt uneasy: "Whenever someone sits in my classroom with a pen, I assume an education department officer has come to inspect my performance, find mistakes, and report me to the district office." Tariq realises that without clear communication and an understanding of developmental observation, an observer's presence triggers fear rather than professional dialogue.

## Explanation

### Meaning and importance of classroom observation

Classroom observation is the deliberate, systematic process of watching, recording, and analysing teaching practices and student learning interactions inside a live classroom (Higher Education Commission [HEC], 2013). It is not a casual drop-in visit, nor is it an exercise in finding faults. Instead, systematic observation gathers factual evidence about how curriculum objectives are translated into classroom reality.

In Pakistani teacher education, observation serves four essential purposes:

1. **Making instructional reality visible:** It allows prospective teachers to see how experienced teachers introduce concepts, manage multi-grade classes, ask questions, and handle unexpected disruptions.
2. **Connecting theory with practice:** Concepts studied in university lecture halls, such as child development or lesson pacing, become concrete when observed in real school settings.
3. **Informing professional reflection:** Observing others provides a mirror for student-teachers to examine their own instructional habits and assumptions.
4. **Providing evidence-based feedback:** Systematic observation replaces vague impressions with objective data that helps teachers refine specific instructional techniques.

> **Common Misconception:** Many student-teachers and school staff believe that *observation means judging or inspecting the teacher*. This misconception causes unnecessary anxiety and defensiveness. In professional teacher preparation, observation is developmental rather than punitive. Its primary goal is learning and improvement, focusing on the interactions between teacher, students, and learning tasks rather than evaluating an individual's personal character.

### Types of classroom observation: formal, informal, peer, and supervisor-led

Classroom observations take different forms depending on their intended purpose, the relationship between the observer and the teacher, and how the gathered data is used:

- **Formal observation:** This is a scheduled, structured visit with a predetermined focus, agreed observation tools (such as an approved rating scale or checklist), and dedicated pre- and post-observation conferences. It is typically conducted by teacher educators, headteachers, or district academic supervisors to document overall professional competencies.
- **Informal observation:** This consists of brief, periodic walkthroughs (lasting ten to fifteen minutes) to gauge general school climate, student engagement routines, or resource usage. It carries low stakes and requires minimal paperwork.
- **Peer observation:** In this collaborative model, two student-teachers or colleague teachers observe each other's classes with a shared developmental question (for example, "How evenly do I distribute questions across the room?"). Because there is no administrative power dynamic, peer observation fosters honest dialogue and mutual experimentation.
- **Supervisor-led observation:** Conducted by university practicum supervisors, this type monitors student-teachers' compliance with teacher education standards and pedagogical guidelines. It balances developmental coaching with formal verification of practicum competencies.

### Roles of supervisors, mentors, and observers

Successful practicum placements rely on distinct yet complementary roles among the three key participants in the observation process (HEC, 2013):

1. **The Practicum Supervisor (University Faculty):** The supervisor oversees academic quality and course alignment. Their role is to ensure student-teachers apply evidence-based methods, adhere to ethical standards, and meet institutional course requirements. Supervisors provide structured feedback linked directly to course learning outcomes.
2. **The School Mentor (Cooperating Teacher):** The mentor is an experienced classroom teacher in the placement school. Their role is daily guidance, cultural orientation, and supportive coaching. A good mentor models effective strategies, explains local context, answers practical questions, and provides immediate, contextualised feedback after lessons.
3. **The Student-Teacher Observer:** As an observer, the student-teacher is an active, respectful learner. Their role is to record factual descriptions of classroom events without interrupting the lesson. The observer does not intervene, criticize, or take over instructional authority unless an emergency occurs.

#### Worked Example: Differentiating Roles in Practice

Consider a Class 4 mathematics lesson on fractions at a government school in Hyderabad:
- **Observer role:** A student-teacher sits quietly at the side, using an observation proforma to tally student questions and record the concrete objects used during the explanation.
- **Mentor role:** The cooperating teacher leads the lesson, later sitting down with the student-teacher during planning time to explain why small wooden sticks were distributed to support visual understanding.
- **Supervisor role:** The university supervisor reviews the student-teacher's written observation notes, checks whether the records distinguish facts from opinions, and guides the student-teacher to link the mentor's questioning strategy to HEC teaching standards.

### Professional ethics during classroom observation

Classroom observations involve entering real educational environments where vulnerable learners and working professionals interact. Observers must strictly uphold five professional ethical standards:

1. **Institutional permission:** Never observe a class without written approval from the school head and the explicit consent of the classroom teacher.
2. **Confidentiality:** All notes, records, and reflections must remain strictly confidential. Observation records are shared only with the observed teacher and the designated university supervisor. They must never be discussed casually in public or shared on social media.
3. **Pupil anonymity:** Never record a student's full name, home address, or personal identifying information. Refer to students by neutral codes, such as "Student A" or "Learner 3". Never photograph or video-record students without official institutional and parental consent.
4. **Professional respect and non-interference:** Arrive before the lesson begins, dress in modest professional attire according to local cultural norms, greet the teacher courteously, and sit where you do not obstruct pupil view or teacher movement. Never interrupt the teacher or correct them in front of their class.
5. **Objective evidence over subjective evaluation:** Record observable, low-inference actions (what was said, how many minutes were spent, how many hands were raised) rather than personal labels or emotional reactions.

## Summary

Classroom observation is a structured, purposeful method of collecting authentic evidence about teaching practices, pupil engagement, and instructional decisions. Rather than serving as an inspection or fault-finding tool, observation in teacher education is developmental, designed to deepen pedagogical understanding and promote self-reflection. Observers must understand the distinct roles of university supervisors, school mentors, and student observers, while strictly upholding professional ethics regarding permission, confidentiality, pupil anonymity, and respectful non-interference.

## Try this at your practicum school

During your preliminary orientation visit to your assigned placement school, request permission from your cooperating teacher to spend fifteen minutes observing the start of the school day. Do not evaluate the teacher or pupils. Instead, note three factual, observable routines that take place between the morning assembly and the beginning of the first period (such as attendance taking, notebook distribution, or board preparation). Compare your factual notes with a fellow trainee to verify that both records are free from personal opinions.

## Summative task

Read the following scenario and complete the task below:

> "Kamran, a trainee teacher, is observing a Grade 4 science lesson on plants in a government primary school. In his observation notes, he writes: *'The teacher was very boring and clearly did not prepare his lesson. The students were lazy and misbehaved throughout the period. The classroom was completely out of control.'*"

Write a critique of Kamran's observation notes (150-200 words). In your critique:
1. Identify three specific flaws in Kamran's notes based on professional observation standards.
2. Rewrite each of Kamran's three statements as an objective, evidence-based, low-inference observation record.
3. Explain how Kamran's original notes violate the ethical principles of professional respect and objectivity.
