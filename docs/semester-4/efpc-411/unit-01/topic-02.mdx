# Topic 1.2 - What to look at, and with what tools

## A real classroom situation

In a crowded Government Comprehensive Girls High School in Larkana, Rabia and Bushra, two B.Ed trainees, observe the same 40-minute English lesson on narrative writing in Class 6. Rabia sits with a blank lined notepad, trying to write down every word the teacher and students say. By the fifteenth minute, her hand is cramping, she has lost track of the lesson structure, and she has missed the teacher's transition from guided writing to independent seatwork.

Bushra, sitting next to her, uses an observation tool provided in the HEC Practicum Handbook. Her form focuses specifically on two dimensions: instructional strategies and pupil time-on-task. In five-minute intervals, Bushra records the primary activity and tallies how many students are actively writing versus sitting idle. During their post-lesson discussion, Rabia has pages of disordered, incomplete transcript, whereas Bushra has precise, actionable data showing that students spent 22 minutes actively writing and 6 minutes waiting for clarification. Rabia quickly sees that observing without a focused lens and an appropriate instrument leaves the observer overwhelmed.

## Explanation

### Observation focus areas

A classroom is a complex, rapid environment where hundreds of verbal and non-verbal events occur simultaneously. Trying to watch everything at once results in cognitive overload and superficial impressions. Professional observers select specific focus areas before entering the room (Molina et al., 2021; HEC, 2013).

There are five core focus areas for classroom observation:

1. **Lesson objectives and planning:** The observer examines whether the teacher has a clear instructional goal linked to curriculum competencies (HEC, 2013). Key questions include: Are the learning objectives communicated clearly to learners? Does the lesson unfold logically from introduction to closure? Are prerequisite concepts revisited before introducing new material?
2. **Teaching strategies and methods:** This area examines how content is delivered and explored (HEC, 2013). Is the lesson predominantly teacher-centred (lecturing, chalking on the board) or student-centred (inquiry, collaborative problem-solving, structured practice)? Does the teacher use diverse questioning techniques to stimulate higher-order thinking?
3. **Classroom management and environment:** This focuses on the organisation of the physical and social space. How does the teacher establish and maintain classroom routines? How are transitions between activities handled? How does the teacher manage student movement, materials distribution, and off-task behaviour calmly and fairly?
4. **Pupil engagement and participation:** Rather than watching only the teacher, effective observers watch the learners. Are all students actively participating, or do only a few confident children in the front row answer questions? How does the teacher support struggling learners or multi-grade groups?
5. **Use of teaching aids and learning resources:** This area evaluates how instructional materials enhance conceptual understanding. Does the teacher use the chalkboard effectively and legibly? Are low-cost, locally available concrete materials (charts, flashcards, counters, everyday objects) used to make abstract ideas tangible?

> **Common Misconception:** Trainees often assume that *a complete observation requires recording every word spoken in the room*. In reality, trying to record everything produces superficial, unorganized notes. Professional observers choose one or two target dimensions per observation session, allowing them to gather deep, reliable, and evidence-based data.

### Observation tools: checklists, rating scales, anecdotal records, and reflective journals

To collect reliable evidence, observers use structured instruments suited to their observation goal. These tools range from highly structured, closed forms to flexible, open-ended narrative records.

- **Observation Checklists:** A checklist contains a list of specific, predetermined classroom behaviors, features, or instructional practices that are marked as either present or absent (Yes/No, Tick/Cross). Checklists are quick and easy to use, making them ideal for verifying routines (such as checking whether homework was reviewed or whether learning objectives were written on the board). However, checklists indicate only *whether* an action occurred, not its quality or contextual effectiveness.
- **Rating Scales:** A rating scale records the frequency or qualitative degree of observed behaviors along a continuum (for example, a 1 to 5 Likert scale ranging from "Rarely" to "Consistently", or descriptive bands from "Developing" to "Proficient"). Standardised frameworks like the World Bank's *Teach Primary* tool use descriptive rating rubrics to evaluate teacher practices such as positive framing and feedback (Molina et al., 2021). While rating scales capture nuance, they require clear behavioural descriptors to prevent subjective scoring differences between observers.
- **Anecdotal Records:** An anecdotal record is an open-ended, written description of a significant, specific classroom incident or interaction. It captures the context, the exact words spoken, the actions taken, and the subsequent outcome in chronological sequence. Anecdotal records are rich in detail and preserve the authentic narrative of the classroom, but they demand rapid, skilled writing and can be distorted if the observer records personal feelings rather than factual events.
- **Reflective Journals:** A reflective journal is maintained by the student-teacher observer after the observation session. It combines factual observation data with personal reflection, questioning, and professional synthesis. Trainees analyze what they saw, compare it with theoretical concepts, and consider how they will handle similar situations in their own future teaching.

#### Worked Example: Selecting the Right Tool for the Purpose

Consider three distinct observation purposes during a placement visit:

- *Purpose A:* Finding out whether a primary teacher uses teaching aids and writes the date and topic on the board in every lesson.  
  *Selected Tool:* **Observation Checklist**. A simple checklist allows rapid verification of physical resources and procedural steps across multiple short visits.
- *Purpose B:* Evaluating the quality of teacher feedback during a Class 7 mathematics problem-solving session.  
  *Selected Tool:* **Rubric-Based Rating Scale**. An analytical scale with defined levels (e.g., evaluative praise vs corrective guidance vs elaborated diagnostic feedforward) allows the observer to assess the pedagogical depth of feedback.
- *Purpose C:* Documenting how a teacher de-escalates a sudden emotional conflict between two students during group work.  
  *Selected Tool:* **Anecdotal Record**. A chronological narrative captures the triggering event, student words, teacher intervention moves, and the resolution.

## Activity: Selecting and drafting observation instruments

Work in groups of three. Time: 25 minutes.

1. Read the following observation objective:
   > "You are assigned to observe a Grade 5 science lesson on simple machines in a rural government school. You want to determine how effectively the teacher uses questioning to involve both boys and girls during the explanation stage."
2. Design a five-item observation tool to capture this evidence. You may choose between a structured checklist with frequency tallies or a four-point descriptive rating scale.
3. For each of your five items, ensure the behavior described is concrete, observable, and free from vague adjectives like "good" or "nice".
4. Exchange your tool with another group. Test whether the other group can unambiguously identify what evidence an observer must look for under each item.

## Summary

Observing a classroom requires a focused conceptual lens and structured instruments to manage cognitive load and ensure data accuracy. The five core observation focus areas - lesson planning, instructional strategies, classroom management, pupil engagement, and learning resources - guide observers to look at critical teaching dimensions. Observers select among checklists for presence or absence, rating scales for qualitative frequency, anecdotal records for rich episodic narratives, and reflective journals for personal synthesis. Matching the tool to the specific observation purpose is essential for gathering objective, developmental evidence.

## Try this at your practicum school

Obtain a copy of the standard lesson observation checklist used by your university or cooperating school. Select one 35-minute lesson during your initial visits. Before the lesson begins, review the checklist items and highlight three items that focus directly on student engagement (such as "Students ask clarifying questions" or "Students work collaboratively in groups"). Keep a tally of how many times you observe each of these three behaviors during the lesson. In your field notebook, record two concrete, low-inference examples that justify your tallies.

## Summative task

Read the following observation record written by an untrained observer:

> *"The teacher showed good classroom management. The students liked the story very much. The teacher used charts appropriately and the lesson was successful."*

Complete the following requirements (150-250 words):
1. **Critical Analysis:** Explain why this record fails to provide useful developmental feedback, identifying at least three specific weaknesses in the wording. *(Analyze)*
2. **Instrument Design:** Convert the vague claims in the record into a four-item structured observation checklist or rating scale with clear, observable indicators that could reliably measure these dimensions in a primary classroom. *(Apply)*
3. **Rubric Alignment:** Provide a one-sentence descriptive anchor for each level of one of your rating scale items to demonstrate how subjective guessing is eliminated. *(Create)*

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Below Standard (1 mark) | Meets Standard (2 marks) | Exceeds Standard (3 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Analysis of observation language** *(Analyze)* | Identifies that the record is brief without explaining why subjective impressions lack developmental validity. | Clearly identifies subjective words ("good", "liked", "appropriately", "successful") and explains why they lack evidentiary value. | Delivers a rigorous critique showing how high-inference claims prevent actionable teacher reflection and distort feedback. |
| **Operational instrument design** *(Apply)* | Designed items remain ambiguous or continue to rely on subjective qualifiers. | Develops four concrete, observable indicators that define specific teacher and student actions. | Creates an exemplary four-item instrument with precise behavioral markers suitable for systematic data collection. |
| **Descriptive anchor clarity** *(Create)* | Anchors are simple numerical labels or repeat generic words. | Provides clear, differentiated descriptive anchors that define distinct levels of observed classroom practice. | Formulates sophisticated, low-inference descriptive anchors that establish observable performance benchmarks. |
