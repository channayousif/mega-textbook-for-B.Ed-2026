# Topic 1.3 - Conducting an observation

## A real classroom situation

At a government primary school in Tando Allahyar, Maryam, a student-teacher, is observing a Grade 3 Urdu reading lesson. During the first twenty minutes, the teacher, Ms Shazia, repeatedly calls upon four students seated on the front right bench to read sentences from the primer. Maryam writes in her field notebook: *"Ms Shazia clearly favours these four students and ignores the children in the back rows because she does not care about struggling learners."*

Later that afternoon, Maryam attends a post-observation discussion. When Maryam politely asks about the seating arrangement, Ms Shazia explains: *"Three weeks ago, the district eye screening team visited our school. Those four students were identified with severe visual impairments, and their families are waiting for glasses from the welfare committee. I moved them to the front bench directly under the window light so they could see the blackboard clearly while we practice."* Maryam feels a wave of embarrassment. Because she had skipped a pre-observation briefing and jumped to conclusions, her notes recorded unfair, biased accusations rather than factual classroom reality.

## Explanation

### Pre-observation briefing

Observation is not an isolated event that begins when an observer walks into a classroom; it is a four-stage professional cycle (HEC, 2013). The first critical stage is the **pre-observation briefing** (or pre-conference). This is a short, collaborative meeting (lasting ten to fifteen minutes) between the observer and the classroom teacher prior to the lesson.

The pre-observation briefing accomplishes three key goals:

1. **Clarifying instructional context:** The teacher explains the lesson's learning objectives, the specific topic being taught, where the lesson fits within the broader curriculum unit, and any relevant student background factors (such as language backgrounds, attendance patterns, or specific learning needs).
2. **Agreeing upon the observation focus:** The observer and teacher establish a shared target for the visit. Rather than conducting a generic inspection, they agree on a specific focus, such as "monitoring student interaction during pair work" or "tracking teacher wait-time after questioning".
3. **Establishing logistics and seating:** The teacher indicates where the observer should sit to avoid distracting pupils, confirms the lesson timetable, and agrees on how the post-observation debriefing will be scheduled.

Conducting a pre-observation briefing builds mutual trust, reduces teacher anxiety, and ensures that the observer understands the instructional intent before recording classroom events.

### Techniques for accurate note-taking

During the observation, the observer's primary tool is disciplined note-taking. Professional observers distinguish sharply between **high-inference notes** and **low-inference notes** (Molina et al., 2021):

- **High-inference notes:** These contain subjective evaluations, summaries, emotional reactions, or premature judgements (for example: *"The teacher was disorganised"*, *"The students were bored"*, or *"Good explanation"*). High-inference notes reflect the observer's internal opinions rather than what actually happened. They provide no actionable evidence for reflection or feedback.
- **Low-inference notes:** These record concrete, observable facts, verbatim quotations, specific numerical counts, and chronological actions (for example: *"Teacher asked: 'What is the function of a root?' Paused for 4 seconds. Called on a student with hand raised."*). Low-inference notes describe classroom reality like a video camera, recording words and actions without adding interpretations.

To maintain accurate low-inference records, student-teachers should apply three established techniques:

1. **Verbatim scripts:** Write down exact phrases spoken by the teacher and students, enclosing direct quotes in quotation marks. Capture both instructions and student answers.
2. **Time stamping:** Note the clock time at regular intervals (every 3 to 5 minutes) in the margin of your notepad. This documents how instructional time is allocated across introduction, exposition, student practice, and closure.
3. **Event and frequency tallies:** Count specific observable behaviors, such as how many open-ended versus closed questions were asked, how many boys versus girls were called upon, or how many students were off-task during group work.

> **Common Misconception:** Trainees often believe that *a good observer writes down clever opinions and evaluative labels during the lesson*. In reality, writing down opinions blinds the observer to real classroom dynamics. A skilled observer records only descriptive facts during the lesson, deferring all interpretation and analysis until the evidence is reviewed after the class.

### Avoiding bias in observation

Every human observer enters a classroom with personal beliefs, prior schooling experiences, and unconscious cognitive biases. Left unchecked, these biases distort observation records, leading observers to see only what confirms their existing expectations (HEC, 2013).

Observers must recognise and actively guard against four common observation biases:

1. **The Halo / Horns Effect:** If an observer forms an initial positive impression of a teacher (for example, admiring their articulate speech or pleasant greeting), the observer tends to rate all aspects of the lesson positively, overlooking weak lesson planning or disengaged students (Halo Effect). Conversely, if an observer is irritated by a late start or strict demeanor, they view every subsequent action negatively (Horns Effect).
2. **Confirmation Bias:** An observer enters the room with a preconceived belief (such as "multi-grade teaching cannot work" or "young teachers lack discipline") and selectively records only evidence that supports that belief while ignoring contradictory evidence.
3. **Leniency or Severity Bias:** Some observers hesitate to record any shortcomings because they want to appear kind and supportive (leniency bias). Others hold unrealistic, perfectionist standards, judging a novice teacher against ideal theoretical perfection rather than practical realities (severity bias).
4. **Cultural and Contextual Bias:** An observer imposes their own urban, well-resourced standards onto a rural, under-resourced school, criticizing the teacher for not using multimedia or print worksheets without considering local material constraints.

#### Worked Example: Transforming Biased Notes into Low-Inference Evidence

- *Biased high-inference note:* "The teacher had terrible board handwriting and the blackboard was a total mess."
- *Objective low-inference revision:* "Teacher wrote the five stages of the water cycle on the right side of the chalkboard in 3-inch chalk letters. The left side retained arithmetic problems from the previous period."
- *Explanation:* The revision removes the emotional label "terrible mess" and specifies the exact location, content, and legible size of the writing, providing factual data for discussion.

### Post-observation reflection

The final phase of the observation cycle is **post-observation reflection**. Immediately following the lesson (or as soon as possible on the same day), the observer reviews their raw notes while their memory is fresh.

Post-observation reflection involves three steps:

1. **Reviewing and organising raw evidence:** The observer cleans up rough abbreviations, checks time stamps, and tallies frequency data.
2. **Separating facts from interpretation:** The observer examines their notes line by line, identifying and crossing out any subjective labels that slipped in, replacing them with factual evidence.
3. **Self-reflective questioning:** The observer asks: "What did I learn from watching this lesson about the challenges of teaching this topic? How would I teach this concept differently in my own classroom? What evidence of student learning did I see?"

## Activity: Converting high-inference judgements to factual records

Work in pairs. Time: 20 minutes.

1. Read the following five high-inference observation notes recorded by student-teachers during practicum:
   - Note A: *"The teacher lost total control of the class during the transition to group work."*
   - Note B: *"The students showed complete mastery of multi-digit multiplication."*
   - Note C: *"The teacher's explanation was confusing and boring."*
   - Note D: *"A lazy student sitting in the corner refused to participate."*
   - Note E: *"The teacher used excellent formative assessment throughout the lesson."*
2. For each note, identify the subjective or high-inference words that represent an opinion rather than a fact.
3. Rewrite each statement as a low-inference, evidence-based observation record containing observable student or teacher behaviors, verbatim speech, or quantifiable data.
4. Share your rewritten notes with the class and explain how the rewritten records provide better evidence for teacher self-reflection.

## Summary

Conducting an effective classroom observation requires disciplined adherence to the four-stage observation cycle: pre-observation briefing, systematic observation and recording, post-observation reflection, and collaborative feedback. To produce valid and developmental data, observers must avoid high-inference value judgements and personal cognitive biases, including the halo effect and confirmation bias. By recording low-inference, factual evidence - such as verbatim teacher-pupil dialogues, chronological time-stamps, and frequency tallies - observers capture an authentic picture of classroom life that supports constructive professional dialogue.

## Try this at your practicum school

Arrange a 10-minute pre-observation briefing with your cooperating teacher before an upcoming mathematics or language lesson. Use three standard questions: (1) What is the main learning outcome of this lesson? (2) What teaching strategy will you use? (3) What specific student interaction would you like me to observe? During the lesson, sit quietly and record time-stamped, low-inference notes focusing exclusively on the agreed interaction. Share your factual notes with the teacher during break time.

## Summative task

Read the following raw observation notes taken by a trainee during a 35-minute Class 5 General Science lesson in a government school in Hyderabad:

> *"Period begins at 9:00 AM. The teacher is unprepared. He writes 'Electricity' on the board and starts lecturing. The students do not care about electricity. At 9:12 AM, two boys in the third row start misbehaving because the teacher is dull. The teacher shouts angrily at them. At 9:20 AM, the teacher asks a question, but only smart kids answer. The teacher rushes through the textbook exercise and ends early. A poorly taught lesson."*

Complete the following requirements (175-250 words):
1. **Bias Identification:** Identify three high-inference, biased words or phrases in this narrative and explain the specific cognitive bias (e.g., confirmation bias, emotional projection, horn effect) they demonstrate. *(Analyze)*
2. **Low-Inference Reconstruction:** Rewrite the entire episode as a structured, low-inference observation log with chronological time-stamps, observable teacher and pupil behaviors, and neutral factual descriptions. *(Apply)*
3. **Reflective Critique:** Explain how your reconstructed log empowers the observed teacher to reflect productively on their classroom management and pacing, whereas the original notes would provoke defensiveness and conflict. *(Evaluate)*
