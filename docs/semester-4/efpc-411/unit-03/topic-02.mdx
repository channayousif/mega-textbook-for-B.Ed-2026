# Topic 3.2 - Recording teaching and learning

## A real classroom situation

Farhan, a student-teacher placed at Government Boys Primary School in Mirpurkhas, observes a Grade 5 General Science lesson on the characteristics of flowering plants. He sits quietly at the back of the classroom with a standardized observation proforma. The classroom teacher, Mr Rafiq, begins by writing five terms on the blackboard: roots, stem, leaves, flower, and seed. He reads each term aloud and instructs the forty-six students to chant them in chorus.

When Farhan reviews his notes after the lesson, he notices that his proforma contains only a chronological summary of what Mr Rafiq said: "09:05 Teacher wrote on board; 09:12 Teacher read textbook; 09:20 Students copied into notebooks." Farhan wrote almost nothing about what the children were doing, whether they understood the plant parts, or how many students were actively looking out the window. During his weekly reflection, Farhan writes: "The lesson was very good because the class was completely silent." When he shares this reflection with his placement mentor, she asks: "Was silence proof of learning, or proof of passive memorization? Did any child touch a real leaf?" Farhan realizes that recording teaching requires capturing the interaction between teacher strategies and student learning responses, followed by critical analysis rather than mere storytelling.

## Explanation

### Documenting lesson observations with proformas

During Week 10 of the course schedule (Week 2 of portfolio documentation), student-teachers must observe and document three to four complete lessons across different subjects and grade levels (HEC, 2013). Systematic lesson recording requires standardized lesson observation proformas that capture both instructional moves and student responses (McBurney-Fry, 2002).

A standard lesson observation proforma organizes data across five essential pedagogical dimensions:

1. **Context and objectives:** Subject, topic, grade, number of students present, lesson duration, and the teacher's stated Student Learning Outcomes (SLOs). The observer notes whether the SLOs were communicated clearly to students.
2. **Teaching methods and instructional staging:** The sequence of lesson stages:
   - *Opening/Hook:* Activating prior knowledge and introducing the central concept.
   - *Direct instruction:* Explanation, modeling, and demonstration by the teacher.
   - *Guided and independent practice:* Individual, pair, or group tasks where students apply the concept.
   - *Closure:* Summarizing key understandings and checking mastery.
3. **Use of learning resources and teaching aids:** How effectively the teacher uses the blackboard, charts, textbooks, low-cost or no-cost local materials, realia (actual objects), and digital media to make abstract concepts concrete.
4. **Student engagement and interaction patterns:** Active participation levels across the classroom, noting whether engagement is limited to a few students in the front rows or distributed across all learners, including girls and shy children.
5. **Teacher questioning techniques:** The balance between lower-order recall questions ("What is the capital of Sindh?") and higher-order analytical questions ("Why do desert plants have small waxy leaves?"), wait time allowed after questions, and distribution of questions across the room.

#### Worked example: Objective proforma recording versus general impressions

When completing a lesson observation proforma, observers must document observable teacher and student behaviors rather than evaluative adjectives.

*Evaluative impression:* "The teacher used bad questioning techniques and the students were uninterested."

*Objective factual record:* "During the 15-minute explanation stage, the teacher asked 14 questions. 12 questions were recall items requiring one-word answers. The teacher waited an average of 1.2 seconds before answering the questions himself or calling on the same two male students seated in Row 1. No wait time was provided for students seated in the back rows. Three students in the back row began drawing on their desks at minute 18."

### Reflective entry: analysing strategies, what worked and why

The second weekly reflective entry (Week 10 / Week 2 of portfolio documentation) requires student-teachers to move beyond narrative description to evaluate why specific strategies succeeded or struggled (HEC, 2013; Richards & Lockhart, 1994).

> **Common Misconception:** Many student-teachers believe that *reflection means writing a step-by-step descriptive summary of what happened in the lesson*. A descriptive summary merely answers the question "What happened?" Professional reflection goes much deeper by asking "Why did it happen? What did it mean for student understanding? What alternative strategies could have improved student learning?"

<Figure id="fig-U3-4" src="/img/figures/efpc-411/unit-03/fig-U3-4.svg" alt="Flowchart illustrating the four-stage reflective cycle: describe, analyse, interpret, and plan." />

To achieve analytical depth, student-teachers apply a structured four-stage reflective cycle (Richards & Lockhart, 1994; Scoupe et al., 2024):

1. **Describe (What occurred?):** A brief, factual summary of the specific lesson episode under examination (for example, a paired vocabulary matching activity).
2. **Analyse (Why did it happen?):** Examining the underlying causes of the observed outcome. For example, why did the group activity fail to engage all students? Was it due to unclear instructions, lack of individual accountability, or insufficient time?
3. **Interpret (What does it mean for learning?):** Connecting the episode to pedagogical theory and child development principles. For example, interpreting high off-task behavior through cognitive load theory or motivational dynamics.
4. **Plan (What next?):** Formulating concrete pedagogical alternatives that the student-teacher will implement in their own future teaching practice.

Evidence to attach to this weekly entry includes:
- Completed lesson observation forms for 3 to 4 lessons.
- Observation checklists focusing on questioning or resource usage.
- Authentic artifacts where permitted (for example, copies of anonymized pupil worksheets or blackboard organization sketches).

## Activity: Transforming descriptive notes into analytical reflection

In pairs, review the following descriptive excerpt from a student-teacher's placement notebook:

> *"The teacher taught Grade 4 English punctuation. She wrote five sentences without punctuation marks on the board and called five students to add commas and full stops. Most students got confused and made errors. The teacher erased the marks and gave them exercises from page 32."*

Collaborate for 35 minutes to convert this raw descriptive excerpt into a four-part reflective entry:
1. **Describe:** State the core factual episode in two clear sentences.
2. **Analyse:** Identify two instructional reasons why the students struggled at the blackboard (consider scaffolding, modeling, and prior practice).
3. **Interpret:** Explain what this indicates about the difference between testing student knowledge and scaffolding learning.
4. **Plan:** Propose two concrete instructional adjustments the teacher could use to support learners before sending them to the board.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* List the five essential dimensions documented in a standard lesson observation proforma.
2. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between lower-order recall questions and higher-order analytical questions in terms of cognitive engagement.
3. *(Apply)* Review an observation proforma noting: "Teacher explained photosynthesis for 30 minutes; students copied notes silently." Apply the four-stage reflective cycle to write an analytical interpretation and a concrete future instructional alternative.

## Summary

Documenting lesson observations requires standardized proformas that systematically record lesson objectives, instructional staging, teaching aids, student participation, and questioning patterns. Objective, low-inference recording provides the empirical foundation for meaningful reflection. Reflective entries must transcend chronological narratives by applying a four-stage reflective cycle: describe, analyse, interpret, and plan. Analysing why teaching strategies succeed or fail enables student-teachers to connect university theory with authentic school realities and build actionable pedagogical insights.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can document a complete lesson using a structured proforma covering all five instructional dimensions.
- [ ] I can record low-inference, observable data regarding teacher questioning, wait time, and student participation.
- [ ] I can distinguish between descriptive recounting and analytical reflection in a weekly portfolio entry.
- [ ] I can apply the four-stage reflective cycle (describe, analyse, interpret, plan) to an observed classroom episode.

## Try this at your practicum school

Observe two consecutive lessons taught by different teachers in the same subject (for example, English or Mathematics in Grades 4 and 5). Use a standard lesson observation proforma to track teacher questioning: tally each question asked, mark whether it was recall or analytical, record the wait time provided, and map where the answering students were seated in the room. In your evening reflective notes, compare how each teacher's questioning pattern affected pupil engagement.

## Summative task

Select one completed lesson observation proforma from your placement (or a detailed simulated classroom lesson). Write a complete 500-word Reflective Entry (Portfolio Reflection Week 2) applying the four-stage reflective cycle: Describe the focal instructional moment, Analyse why the strategy worked or struggled, Interpret the pedagogical implications using relevant learning principles, and Plan specific pedagogical modifications for your future teaching.

Evaluate your entry against the following mini-rubric:

| Criterion | Developing | Proficient | Exemplary |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Proforma Documentation** *(Understand)* | Proforma has blank sections or relies on vague, subjective judgements ("good lesson"). | Proforma is completely filled with objective data across all five instructional dimensions. | Comprehensive, highly detailed proforma with precise time stamps, verbatim questions, and clear interaction mapping. |
| **Analytical Depth** *(Analyze)* | Recounts chronological events; provides superficial praise without examining root causes. | Identifies why specific teaching moves succeeded or failed, linking strategies to student reactions. | Rigorous, critical examination of underlying pedagogical factors, teacher assumptions, and student cognitive engagement. |
| **Actionable Pedagogical Transfer** *(Apply)* | Offers vague general intentions ("I will teach better" or "I will use charts"). | Formulates two clear, practical instructional strategies for future classroom implementation. | Proposes detailed, evidence-grounded instructional adaptations with explicit operational steps and success criteria. |
