# Topic 3.1 - Fundamental rights in the Constitution of 1973

## A real classroom situation

Bushra is a B.Ed trainee completing her practicum at a government girls' elementary school near Sukkur. During her second week, a father visits the headmistress's office with his eleven-year-old daughter, Marvi. The family recently moved from an agricultural village to the town outskirts. The father explains that a local clerk told him Marvi could not be enrolled because she lacked a formal municipal birth certificate and the school was already crowded.

The headmistress politely tells the father to return next term. The father sighs, preparing to leave. He tells Bushra, "A poor labourer cannot argue with government officers. If the department has no room, ordinary people have no claim."

Bushra remembers her coursework on the Constitution of Pakistan. She knows that in 2010, the parliament passed the Eighteenth Constitutional Amendment, inserting Article 25A. This article explicitly commands that the State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children aged five to sixteen years. Basic schooling is not an administrative gift or a departmental favour; it is an enforceable fundamental right guaranteed to every child in Pakistan.

Bushra asks the headmistress if she can help the father complete a temporary admission form under provincial enrolment rules while his identity documents are processed. By the end of this topic, you will understand what fundamental rights are, how Pakistan's 1973 Constitution guarantees them, and how citizens and teachers can recognise them in daily life.

## Explanation

This topic answers three central questions. What are fundamental rights and liberties? How did Pakistan's constitutional system develop? And how does the 1973 Constitution protect citizens against arbitrary state power?

### Fundamental rights and liberties

In civics and constitutional law, **liberty** means the freedom of individuals to live, speak, associate, think, and work without unjust restriction. Standard introductory texts such as *Civics Today* explain that liberty does not mean the absence of all law; rather, true liberty exists under the rule of law, where established legal rules protect each person's freedom from interference by others or by the government (Glencoe/McGraw-Hill, n.d.).

A **legal right** is a claim recognised and protected by law. For instance, the right to recover money owed under a private business contract is a legal right created by ordinary statute. However, ordinary statutes can be changed, amended, or repealed by a simple majority vote in a legislature.

**<Glossary term="Fundamental Rights">Fundamental rights</Glossary>** are different. They are the supreme class of rights enshrined directly in a country's written constitution. Because the constitution is the supreme law of the land, fundamental rights stand above ordinary laws and administrative decisions. They serve as legal shields that protect individual citizens and minority groups from arbitrary actions by the state, whether by the police, the bureaucracy, or a temporary legislative majority.

Dean (2005) notes that civic education in Pakistan has often taught constitutional provisions as dry, memorised clauses rather than living guarantees of human dignity and democratic participation. When learners examine fundamental rights as real instruments of justice, they understand their true civic purpose:

1. **Constitutional protection above ordinary statutes.** Under Article 8 of the Constitution of Pakistan, any law, custom, or usage having the force of law that is inconsistent with or in derogation of fundamental rights is void to the extent of such inconsistency. If a provincial assembly or the federal parliament passes an ordinary act that violates a fundamental right, the superior courts have the constitutional power to strike it down.
2. **Direct judicial enforceability.** Unlike general political promises or policy aspirations, fundamental rights can be directly enforced through the judiciary. Under Article 199, an aggrieved citizen can file a <Glossary term="Writ petition">writ petition</Glossary> in the relevant provincial High Court. Under Article 184(3), the Supreme Court of Pakistan can take notice of questions of public importance involving fundamental rights. The courts can issue binding orders to public authorities to cease unlawful actions or to carry out mandatory constitutional duties.
3. **Limiting state overreach.** Governments hold immense power: they command police forces, collect taxes, manage public institutions, and administer prisons. Fundamental rights place explicit legal fences around the citizen, declaring areas that the state cannot violate without due process of law.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Rights are unlimited."** Many citizens mistakenly believe that possessing a right means having absolute freedom to do whatever they wish. No constitutional democracy offers unlimited rights. If freedom of speech meant the right to slander neighbours or incite violent riots, other citizens' safety and dignity would be destroyed. The Constitution of Pakistan carefully balances freedoms with "reasonable restrictions imposed by law." For example, Article 19 protects freedom of speech and the press, but subjects it to reasonable legal restrictions in the interest of the glory of Islam, the integrity, security or defence of Pakistan, friendly relations with foreign states, public order, decency, or in relation to contempt of court. A restriction is constitutional only when it is reasonable, enacted by a valid law, and serves a legitimate public purpose.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Rights come from the government's goodwill."** A second misconception is that citizens hold rights only because the current government chooses to grant them, meaning a minister or deputy commissioner can take them away at will. In constitutional theory, fundamental rights belong inherently to citizens as members of the political community. The government is not the author of human liberty; it is the constitutional servant created to protect it. Ahmad (2004) points out that understanding this distinction is crucial for developing a genuine democratic culture: citizens must recognise that fundamental rights are constitutional guarantees that belong to the people, not gifts distributed by officials.

**A worked example: analysing a school scenario under fundamental rights.**

Consider this situation: A district education officer issues a circular stating that children belonging to religious minorities must attend Islamic religious instruction classes during the school day or face suspension. Does this circular violate constitutional fundamental rights?

To analyse this situation, follow three systematic steps:

| Analysis step | Question to ask | Application to scenario | Constitutional conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Step 1: Identify the affected citizen interest | What freedom, interest, or protection is being restricted? | Non-Muslim students are being forced to attend religious instruction of a faith other than their own. | Relates to freedom of conscience and educational rights regarding religion. |
| Step 2: Locate the governing constitutional provision | Which specific article in Chapter 1 of Part II applies? | Article 22(1) explicitly states: "No person attending any educational institution shall be required to receive religious instruction... if such instruction... relates to a religion other than his own." | The circular directly contradicts the text of Article 22(1). |
| Step 3: Determine legal effect under Article 8 | Does the departmental circular have legal force to override the Constitution? | Under Article 8(1) and (2), any administrative order or law in derogation of fundamental rights is void. | The circular is unconstitutional, null, and void. The students have a clear legal right to exemption. |

### Constitution of Pakistan (1973)

The constitutional protections enjoyed by Pakistani citizens today are the result of a long and complex history of political struggle and debate.

- **The Government of India Act 1935 and early independence.** In August 1947, Pakistan began its independent journey using the Government of India Act 1935 (as adapted by the Pakistan Provisional Constitution Order) as its interim working constitution. In March 1949, the First Constituent Assembly passed the historic Objectives Resolution, moved by Prime Minister Liaquat Ali Khan. The resolution proclaimed that sovereignty over the entire universe belongs to Allah Almighty alone, and the authority to be exercised by the people of Pakistan through their chosen representatives is a sacred trust. It specifically guaranteed fundamental rights, including equality of status and opportunity, equality before law, and freedom of thought, expression, belief, faith, worship, and association.
- **The 1956 Constitution.** On 23 March 1956, Pakistan enacted its first homegrown constitution, declaring the country an Islamic Republic. It instituted a parliamentary system with a unicameral National Assembly and included an explicit chapter on fundamental rights. However, this constitution lasted only two and a half years: in October 1958, President Iskander Mirza declared martial law and abrogated the constitution, handing power to General Ayub Khan.
- **The 1962 Constitution.** Enacted by Field Marshal Ayub Khan on 8 June 1962, this constitution established an authoritarian presidential system with indirect elections managed by 80,000 local representatives known as Basic Democrats. Initially, it omitted an enforceable chapter of fundamental rights; public pressure and legal advocacy forced the regime to pass the First Constitutional Amendment in 1963, restoring fundamental rights and judicial review. The 1962 Constitution was abrogated in March 1969 when Ayub Khan resigned and handed power to General Yahya Khan.
- **The 1973 Constitution.** Following the tragic events of 1971 and the separation of East Pakistan, the newly elected National Assembly drafted a permanent constitution. On 10 April 1973, the assembly approved the draft unanimously across political and ideological divisions, and it came into effect on 14 August 1973. It established a federal, democratic, parliamentary system with an elected Prime Minister, a bicameral parliament (National Assembly and Senate), and an independent judiciary.

The cornerstone of the 1973 Constitution is Chapter 1 of Part II (Articles 8 to 28), which sets out twenty-four comprehensive fundamental rights. We can group these essential protections into four main categories:

1. **Life, liberty, and due process (Articles 9 to 14).**
   - *Article 9 (Security of person):* No person shall be deprived of life or liberty save in accordance with law. This is the foundational safeguard against unlawful execution, arbitrary detention, or police violence.
   - *Article 10 (Safeguards as to arrest and detention):* Any person arrested must be informed of the grounds of arrest as soon as possible, allowed to consult and be defended by a legal practitioner of choice, and produced before a magistrate within twenty-four hours.
   - *Article 10A (Right to fair trial):* Inserted by the Eighteenth Amendment in 2010, this article guarantees that for the determination of civil rights or in any criminal charge against a person, they are entitled to a fair trial and due process.
   - *Article 11 (Prohibition of slavery and forced labour):* Strictly bans slavery, human trafficking, and bonded labour. It also forbids employing children under the age of fourteen in any factory, mine, or hazardous work.
   - *Article 12 and 13:* Protect against retrospective punishment (punishing an act that was legal when committed) and double jeopardy (being tried or punished twice for the same offence), as well as self-incrimination (forcing an accused person to witness against themselves).
   - *Article 14 (Inviolability of dignity of man):* Declares that the dignity of man and, subject to law, the privacy of home, shall be inviolable. It expressly forbids torture for the purpose of extracting evidence.
2. **Freedoms of movement, assembly, and expression (Articles 15 to 19A).**
   - *Article 15 (Freedom of movement):* Every citizen has the right to enter, move freely throughout, and reside in any part of Pakistan, subject to reasonable legal restrictions in the public interest.
   - *Article 16 (Freedom of assembly):* Every citizen has the right to assemble peacefully and without arms.
   - *Article 17 (Freedom of association):* Every citizen has the right to form associations, trade unions, or political parties.
   - *Article 18 (Freedom of trade, business, or profession):* Citizens have the right to enter upon any lawful profession or trade.
   - *Article 19 (Freedom of speech):* Guarantees free expression and freedom of the press under reasonable legal restrictions.
   - *Article 19A (Right to information):* Inserted in 2010, giving citizens the right of access to information in all matters of public importance, which is vital for civic transparency and government accountability.
3. **Religious, cultural, and educational protections (Articles 20 to 22 and 28).**
   - *Article 20 (Freedom of religion):* Every citizen has the right to profess, practice, and propagate their religion, and every religious denomination has the right to establish and maintain its institutions.
   - *Article 21 and 22:* Protect citizens from taxes dedicated to religions other than their own, and safeguard students against being forced to receive instruction in a religion other than their own.
   - *Article 28 (Preservation of language, script, and culture):* Guarantees that any section of citizens having a distinct language, script, or culture has the right to preserve and promote it.
4. **Equality, non-discrimination, and the right to education (Articles 25 to 27).**
   - *Article 25 (Equality of citizens):* Declares that all citizens are equal before law and are entitled to equal protection of law. There shall be no discrimination on the basis of sex. Crucially, Article 25(3) empowers the state to make special affirmative provisions for the protection of women and children.
   - *Article 25A (Right to education):* Added by the Eighteenth Amendment in 2010, commanding that "The State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children of the age of five to sixteen years in such manner as may be determined by law."
   - *Articles 26 and 27:* Guarantee non-discrimination in access to public places (such as parks, roads, and transport) and safeguard against discrimination in public services and government appointments.

Ahmad (2004) emphasises that when teachers understand this constitutional architecture, they can connect civic education directly to the daily lives of pupils. Teaching children that they possess constitutional dignity under Article 14 and an absolute entitlement to education under Article 25A transforms school from a site of passive compliance into a practical training ground for constitutional citizenship.

## Activity: Mapping constitutional rights

**Time:** 35 minutes  
**Format:** Small groups (3 to 4 trainees)

**Scenario cards:**  
Each group receives one of the following four real-world community scenarios:

1. *Card A (Mirpurkhas):* A local municipal authority demolishes a fruit vendor's cart and seizes his goods without a written notice or legal hearing, claiming the street must be cleared for a visiting dignitary.
2. *Card B (Larkana):* A government primary school headmaster informs parents that girls will only be taught up to Class 3 because the community allegedly does not need educated female workers.
3. *Card C (Karachi):* A youth civic organisation wishes to hold a peaceful gathering in a public park to discuss environmental pollution and garbage collection, but police officers threaten to arrest them if they carry banners.
4. *Card D (Thatta):* An investigative journalist requests official budget records regarding the repair of a local canal embankment from the irrigation department, but the executive engineer refuses to share any documents.

**Instructions:**
1. Read your group's card carefully.
2. Identify which specific fundamental right (by article number and title) is being threatened or violated.
3. State whether the action could be justified under any "reasonable restriction" mentioned in the Constitution, and explain why or why not.
4. Draft a three-sentence explanation that a trainee teacher could share with local citizens explaining their constitutional protection and what legal recourse exists.
5. Appoint one spokesperson to share your group's findings with the whole class.

## Summary

Fundamental rights are constitutional guarantees that stand above ordinary laws, protecting individual liberty and human dignity against arbitrary state action. Under Article 8 of the 1973 Constitution, any law or rule inconsistent with fundamental rights is void. Pakistan's constitutional journey progressed from the interim 1935 framework and the 1956 and 1962 constitutions to the consensus parliamentary constitution of 1973. Articles 8 to 28 provide enforceable protections, including security of person (Article 9), fair trial (Article 10A), inviolable human dignity (Article 14), freedom of speech (Article 19), equality before law (Article 25), and free and compulsory education for all children aged five to sixteen (Article 25A). Constitutional rights are not absolute gifts from the government; they belong to the people and are subject only to reasonable restrictions established by law for public order and the rights of others.

## Try this at your practicum school

During your next school placement, examine the school's written rules and daily routines through the lens of fundamental rights:

1. **Article 25A check:** Inquire politely with the head teacher about admission procedures. Does the school turn away any school-age children due to poverty, lack of documentation, or disability? How does the school address barriers to universal access?
2. **Article 14 check:** Observe how discipline is maintained. Are corporal punishment or degrading verbal insults practiced? How can a teacher uphold the constitutional guarantee of human dignity in daily classroom management?
3. **Written reflection:** Write a one-page note describing one concrete step your placement school could take to align its student policies more closely with the protections of the 1973 Constitution.
