## A real classroom situation

When the coronavirus closed schools in 2020, two Class 9 pupils in the same government school in Sanghar faced the same work. Saim had a family smartphone, a broadband connection at home and a quiet corner. He joined the online class every day. His classmate Hira had one old phone shared with three brothers, and the nearest signal was two kilometres away at her uncle's shop. She missed most of the year. When the school reopened, the two pupils were no longer at the same level.

The head teacher saw the same pattern in civic life. Parents who had internet at home followed the school's notices, registered complaints and joined the parents' group. Parents who did not, mostly daily-wage workers and some mothers in remote villages, heard about meetings after they happened. By the end of this topic you will be able to explain why the digital divide is not one gap but several, and what it does to citizenship.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What is the digital divide? What forms does it take in Pakistan? And what does it do to citizenship?

### Digital divides and their impact on citizenship

The **<Glossary term="Digital divide">digital divide</Glossary>** is the gap between people who can use digital tools and the internet well, and people who cannot. It is not one gap but several, and they overlap. The guide names four: access, usage, socioeconomic and geographic.

1. **The access divide.** Some people have no device, no signal, or no electricity to charge a phone. This is the first and most basic gap. A citizen who cannot get online cannot use a citizen portal, join an online class, or read a government notice.
2. **The usage divide.** Access alone is not enough. Some people have a phone but lack the skills or confidence to use it well: they cannot judge a message, fill an online form, or protect their data. This is why <Glossary term="Digital literacy">digital literacy</Glossary> matters. Khalid and Ahmad (2026) found that digital literacy partly explained the link between social media use and civic engagement among Pakistani university students.
3. **The socioeconomic divide.** Income, education, gender and age shape who gets online and what they can do there. The cost of a handset and mobile data is a real barrier for a daily-wage family. Families may also give the one phone to a son rather than a daughter.
4. **The geographic divide.** Cities and towns have better networks and services than villages and remote areas. A pupil in Karachi can often rely on a stable connection; a pupil in rural Sindh or Balochistan may have weak or no signal for much of the day.

Shair, Waheed, Kamran and Kubra (2022) studied ICT use across Pakistan with household survey data. They found a sizeable digital divide among the people of Pakistan and reported that the leading determinants are ICT infrastructure and access to the technology itself, alongside socioeconomic, demographic, regional and supply-side factors (Shair et al., 2022). The same study places Pakistan 135th out of 144 countries for internet access, a reminder that the divide is not only a personal problem but a national one.

Iftikhar, Ahmed and Shah (2023) studied university students after the shift to online education. They found that online education widened an already existing divide between those who have and those who have not. The barriers they name are the scarcity of gadgets, internet access, family income, gender, field of study, age and location (Iftikhar et al., 2023). Notice how the barriers match the four kinds above.

**What the divide does to citizenship.** The last two topics showed that more civic life now runs through digital platforms. That makes the divide a civic problem, not only a technical one. A citizen who is offline cannot sign an e-petition, track a complaint, read a health message, join a school group, or learn about a government scheme. Digital access has become part of the door to public services, and the door is not equally open.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Everyone in Pakistan now has internet access."** It is true that mobile phones and mobile internet have spread fast. But "everyone" is wrong. The divide is structured: it follows income, education, gender, age and place. A pupil in a city school and a pupil in a village school can both be "in Pakistan" and have very different digital lives. The honest statement is: access has grown a great deal, and it is still unequal, so citizenship online is unequal too.

**A worked example: which divide is it?** Read each short case and name the main divide.

| Case | Main divide | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A boy in a village has no signal at home | Access | No network, whatever his skill or income |
| A girl has a phone but her family will not let her use it after Class 8 | Socioeconomic | Gender and family income limit her use |
| A man has a smartphone but cannot tell a true message from a false one | Usage | He has access but lacks the skill |
| A farmer in a remote valley cannot reach a government office online | Geographic | Place, not person |

The same citizen can face more than one divide at once, and they add up. Hira in Sanghar faced access and geographic divides together.

## Activity: Who is left out?

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. Each member writes down one real person they know, or know of, who has trouble getting online: a parent, a pupil, a shopkeeper, a farmer, an older relative. Do not write the person's full name.
2. For each person, name the main divide (access, usage, socioeconomic or geographic) and one barrier from the table that fits.
3. Discuss one action the school, union council or a local group could take to bring that person in, such as a printed notice, a phone-free desk, a community phone, or a short digital-literacy class.
4. Choose the action your group thinks would help the most people, and be ready to say why.

Share with another group. They must name the divides and say whether they would choose the same action.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Without looking back, define the digital divide and name its four kinds.
2. *(Remember)* According to Shair et al. (2022), what are the leading determinants of the digital divide in Pakistan?
3. *(Understand)* In three sentences, explain why the divide is a civic problem and not only a technical one.
4. *(Apply)* A daily-wage labourer in a town has a basic phone, no data package and no one to ask for help with online forms. Name the divides he faces and explain one way a union council could still reach him.

## Summary

The digital divide is the gap between those who can use digital tools and the internet well and those who cannot. It has at least four kinds: access, usage, socioeconomic and geographic. Studies in Pakistan report that ICT infrastructure and access are the leading determinants, and that online education and services widen an already existing divide. Because so much civic life now runs online, the divide decides who can take part, who can be heard and who can reach public services. Access has grown, but it is still unequal.

## Summative task

Write 250-300 words. Choose one group in your area that is often left out of digital life, such as daily-wage workers, mothers in a village, older people, or pupils in a remote school.

1. Describe the group and the divide it faces.
2. Explain two barriers from the table that affect this group.
3. Analyse one action a school, union council or local group could take to bring the group in, and one limit of that action.
