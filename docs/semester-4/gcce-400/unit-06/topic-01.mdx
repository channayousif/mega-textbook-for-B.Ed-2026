## A real classroom situation

Viqas is a B.Ed trainee on practicum at a government girls' high school in Thatta. After a week of heavy rain, part of the school's boundary wall collapses, and a stray dog walks into the courtyard during lessons. The head teacher posts a photo of the broken wall in the school's WhatsApp group and asks the parents what to do. Within two hours, thirty parents have replied. Some offer bricks and labour. Others say the school should register a complaint on the Pakistan Citizen's Portal, the government's complaint app, so the education department has to respond. One father asks why the school needs an app at all; he would rather go to the union council in person, as his father always did.

Viqas notices two things. The online group moved faster than any meeting could have. But two mothers in the group stay silent, and he later learns they have no smartphone or internet at home. By the end of this topic you will be able to explain what digital platforms add to civic life, and what they leave out.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What is digital citizenship? How do digital platforms carry civic engagement? And what do they make easier and harder?

### The use of digital platforms for civic engagement

**<Glossary term="Digital citizenship">Digital citizenship</Glossary>** is the ability to take part in digital society safely, ethically and constructively, carrying the rights and responsibilities that arrive together. It is the online side of the citizenship you met in Unit 1. **<Glossary term="Digital platform">Digital platforms</Glossary>** are the online services that carry communication and public life: social media such as WhatsApp, Facebook and X, video sites such as YouTube, government apps and portals, and petition sites. **Civic engagement**, as you know, is any action a person takes, alone or with others, to improve the life of their community or to influence public decisions. **<Glossary term="Digital civic engagement">Digital civic engagement</Glossary>** is civic engagement carried out through these platforms.

The Council of Europe's *Digital citizenship education handbook* defines a digital citizen as someone who can "actively, positively and responsibly engage in both on- and offline communities" (Richardson and Milovidov, 2019, p. 11). That definition matters for a teacher, because it puts the online and offline parts of citizenship in one piece. Posting about a broken wall is a civic act, not a separate activity from civic life.

Digital civic engagement takes at least five common forms, shown in the concept map above.

1. **E-petitions.** Citizens sign and share an online petition asking an authority to act, for example to repair a school roof or post a missing teacher.
2. **Citizen portals.** A government app or website lets a citizen register a complaint or suggestion. The Pakistan Citizen's Portal is a Government of Pakistan service where a citizen can register with a CNIC and mobile number, file a grievance, and track its progress.
3. **Online campaigns.** A group uses hashtags, posts and videos to put an issue in public view, such as a campaign for clean drinking water in a town.
4. **Community groups.** A WhatsApp or Facebook group of neighbours, parents or a mohalla discusses a shared problem and organises a response.
5. **Online volunteering and crowdfunding.** Citizens give time, skills or money online, for example raising funds for flood relief or tutoring pupils over video.

These forms are not new rights. Article 19 of the Constitution gives every citizen the right to freedom of speech and expression, and Article 17 gives the right to form associations, both subject to reasonable limits set by law (Government of Pakistan, 2012). Digital platforms are new ways to use these old rights.

**A worked example: which actions count as digital civic engagement?** Use one test. Does the action try to improve shared life or influence a public decision, and does it happen through a digital platform?

| Action | Tries to improve shared life? | Through a digital platform? | Digital civic engagement? |
|---|---|---|---|
| A school registers a complaint about a collapsed wall on the Pakistan Citizen's Portal | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| A father goes in person to the union council office | Yes | No | No, it is offline civic engagement |
| A pupil shares a meme about a teacher | No | Yes | No, it is not civic |
| A group posts a video of a blocked drain and tags the district administration | Yes | Yes | Yes |

The first and last rows count. The second is real civic engagement, but it is not digital. The third uses a platform but is not civic.

Khalid and Ahmad (2026) surveyed 384 university students in Pakistan and found that social media use significantly predicted civic engagement, and that digital literacy partly explained the link. They also found a dual effect: social media strengthened connectivity while it also helped create echo chambers and polarisation (Khalid and Ahmad, 2026). That is the honest picture. A platform can carry a real civic action and a real civic harm at the same time.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Online participation is not real civic engagement."** There is a grain of truth here. A post can be shallow: a like, a share or a signature is not the same as showing up, voting, or doing the work. But the statement is wrong when it says online action is not civic. Registering a complaint on a citizen portal creates a record the government must answer. A video of a blocked drain can move an official to act. The right conclusion is narrower: online action is real civic engagement, but it is often only the first step, and it must be judged by what it leads to.

## Activity: Two routes to the same office

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. Choose one real problem in your area, such as a broken hand pump, a street without lights, a missing teacher, or a drain that floods.
2. On paper, draw two routes to get the problem fixed: the offline route (a visit to the office, a paper application, a union council meeting) and the digital route (a citizen portal, a WhatsApp group, an email, a social media post).
3. For each route, note who can use it and where it is likely to stall. Then mark one step where the online voice can turn into a real result, such as an official response, a meeting or a repair.
4. Name one person in your community who cannot use the digital route, and say what they would need to take part.

Exchange your map with another group. They must name your two routes and the person at risk of exclusion, without looking at your notes.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Without looking back, define digital citizenship in one sentence and name three forms of digital civic engagement.
2. *(Remember)* What is the Pakistan Citizen's Portal, and what can a citizen do on it?
3. *(Understand)* A classmate says, "Posting about a problem online is not real civic engagement." In three sentences, explain what is right and what is wrong about this statement.
4. *(Apply)* A union council in Badin posts a photo of a cleaned drain and asks residents to report blocked drains through the same page. Name the forms of digital civic engagement shown, and explain one risk of relying on this channel.

## Summary

Digital citizenship is taking part in digital society safely, ethically and constructively. Digital platforms carry civic engagement in forms such as e-petitions, citizen portals, online campaigns, community groups and online volunteering. These forms use rights the Constitution already gives, such as freedom of speech and expression and the right to form associations. They make participation faster, wider and cheaper, but they also spread misinformation, harden echo chambers, expose personal data and leave out people who cannot get online. Online action is real civic engagement, but it is often only the first step.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher or the head teacher whether the school uses an online group or portal to contact parents or the education office. Write three lines: what it is used for, who is included, and one family that might be left out.

## Summative task

Write 250-300 words. Choose one real public problem in your town or village, such as a dirty water supply, a school without a boundary wall, or a lane that floods.

1. Describe the problem and who it affects.
2. Show how citizens could use two digital platforms and one offline action to respond.
3. Analyse which step is most likely to produce a real result, and why. Name one risk of the digital step.

*(Analyze)*

**Mini-rubric (10 marks)**

|---|---|---|---|
| Problem described (2 marks) | Clear, real and local, with the people affected (2) | Vague or general (1) | Absent |

The analysis criterion asks you to compare and give reasons. Listing more platforms does not meet it.
