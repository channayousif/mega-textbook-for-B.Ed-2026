## A real classroom situation

Rizwana teaches Class 7 at a government primary school in Khairpur. One Monday morning, four pupils arrive upset. A message has spread overnight in their families' WhatsApp groups: the school will stay closed all week because of a "new virus" in the district. The message has no source, only a forwarded arrow and the words "share immediately". Some parents kept their children home. One father drove to the school to close it himself.

Rizwana checks the district education office's page. There is no closure notice. The message is false. She also notices something quieter: a colleague had posted a photo of her class in the school corridor on Facebook the week before, with the pupils' faces clear and their full names in the caption. No parent had been asked. The false message and the class photo are different mistakes, but they come from the same gap: nobody paused to ask what could go wrong. By the end of this topic you will have a routine for that pause.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What is cyber ethics? Why is responsible social media use a civic duty, not just a personal habit? And what routine can you use before you share?

### Cyber ethics and responsible use of social media

**<Glossary term="Cyber ethics">Cyber ethics</Glossary>** is the set of principles that guide right and wrong conduct online. It is ordinary ethics applied to digital spaces: be honest, be fair, respect others, take responsibility for what you do, and protect what is not yours to share. The Council of Europe's handbook makes ethics and empathy one of the ten domains of digital citizenship. It describes this domain as online ethical behaviour and interaction with others, built on the ability to recognise and understand the feelings and perspectives of others (Richardson and Milovidov, 2019, p. 13). Empathy matters online because a screen makes it easy to forget that a real person reads what you write.

Responsible social media use rests on a few principles you can teach in a classroom.

1. **Honesty.** Do not invent, fake or quietly change information. If you are not sure, say so.
2. **Respect.** Do not insult, mock, threaten or shame a person or group. Disagree with an idea, not a person's dignity.
3. **Responsibility.** You are answerable for what you post and what you share. A share is not a neutral act.
4. **Respect for privacy.** Do not publish a person's name, photo, address, marks or health details without consent. <Glossary term="Personal data">Personal data</Glossary> is any information that identifies a living person, and <Glossary term="Data protection">data protection</Glossary> means treating that data as belonging to the person it describes.
5. **Self-protection.** Do not give passwords, identity numbers or meeting details to strangers, and do not open files from people you do not know.

**<Glossary term="Misinformation">Misinformation</Glossary>** is false or misleading information that spreads, whether or not anyone meant to deceive. **<Glossary term="Disinformation">Disinformation</Glossary>** is false information spread on purpose. A **digital footprint** is the trail of data a person leaves online: the active part they publish and the passive part collected about them. Every share adds to someone's footprint, sometimes your own.

The scale of the problem is real. Ali and Qazi (2023) ran a randomised experiment in urban Pakistan to test educational interventions against misinformation among people with low digital literacy. General video messages about fake news had no significant effect. But when the videos were followed by personalised feedback based on each person's past sharing, the ability to identify fake news improved by 0.14 standard deviations (Ali and Qazi, 2023). The lesson for a teacher is clear: people learn to spot false news when the help is specific and repeated, not when they are simply warned.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Anything posted online is private."** This is false, and it is the most dangerous of the three misconceptions in this unit. A post can be copied, screenshotted, forwarded and searched long after it is deleted. Privacy settings limit who sees a post, but they do not make it private, and they do not bind the people who already saw it. The class photo from Khairpur is the example: the teacher's account may have been set to "friends", but one forward carried it beyond her friends in minutes. Treat everything you post as public and permanent.

**Correcting a second misconception: "Sharing is not the same as saying it, so I am not responsible."** Sharing is an act. When you share a post, you publish it to your own network and you lend it your name. If it is false, you have spread the falsehood; if it is harmful, you have helped it reach the people it can hurt. The law and ethics both treat publication seriously. Before you share, use the five checks in the flowchart above: check the source, check the date, check the evidence, consider the harm, then decide.

**A worked example: should you share this post?** A post in a community group says a local hospital has turned away a sick child because the family could not pay. It has a photo, no source, and the date is not visible.

| Check | What you do | What you find |
|---|---|---|
| Check the source | Who made the post, and do they have reason to know? | An anonymous account with no history |
| Check the date | Is the event recent, or is this old news? | The photo is from a different year |
| Check the evidence | Does a fact-check, the hospital or a news report confirm it? | The hospital's page says no such case |
| Consider the harm | Could sharing hurt a named person or group? | It could anger people against the hospital and its staff |
| Decide | Share with a note, or pause and correct it? | Pause; do not share; post the hospital's correction |

The same routine works for the false closure message in the hook. The message fails at the first check: it has no source.

## Activity: The sharing decision

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

1. Your teacher or you write four short sample posts on slips of paper. Each is about a public issue in a Pakistani town: a rumour that a school is closing, a photo of a class, a claim that a hospital turned away a patient, and a message asking for a stranger's identity number.
2. For each post, run the five checks aloud: source, date, evidence, harm, decision. Write one line for each check.
3. Decide: share with a note, pause and correct, or do not share. Give one reason.
4. Swap your four decisions with another pair. They must tell you which check changed your decision, and whether they agree.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Without looking back, define cyber ethics and misinformation in one sentence each.
2. *(Remember)* Name the five checks you should make before sharing a post about a public issue.
3. *(Understand)* A friend says, "I only shared the message, I did not write it, so it is not my fault." In three sentences, explain why sharing is an ethical act.
4. *(Apply)* A WhatsApp message says a government school in your district is closed this week. It has no source. Using the five checks, explain what you would do and why.

## Summary

Cyber ethics is ordinary ethics applied online: honesty, respect, responsibility, respect for privacy and self-protection. Misinformation spreads fast, and experiments in Pakistan show that specific, repeated feedback helps people spot it, while general warnings do not. Nothing posted online is truly private, and sharing a false or harmful post is an act for which the sharer is responsible. A five-check routine, source, date, evidence, harm and decision, turns that responsibility into a habit.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher how the school handles messages that spread in parents' WhatsApp groups, such as rumours about closures, exams or fees. Write three lines: what happens now, which of the five checks the school could use, and one way to correct a false message without blaming the person who sent it.

## Summative task

Write 250-300 words. A false message about your school or union council is spreading in a WhatsApp group. It says a named public service has stopped, and it asks people to "share immediately".

1. Describe the message and the harm it could do.
2. Apply the five checks to it, one line each.
3. Analyse which check is most likely to stop the false message in your community, and why. Explain one limit of that check.

*(Analyze)*

**Mini-rubric (10 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Harm described (2 marks) | Clear, real harm to a named service or group (2) | General harm (1) | Absent |
| Five checks applied (3 marks) | All five checks applied to the message, one line each (3) | Three or four checks, or checks named without application (1-2) | None |
| Analysis of the most effective check (5 marks) | Chooses one check with reasons tied to the message, and names a real limit (4-5) | Chooses a check with weak reasons or no limit (2-3); only states a preference (1) | No analysis |

The analysis criterion asks you to compare the checks and give reasons. Listing the checks again does not meet it.
