## A real classroom situation

Bilal is a B.Ed trainee on practicum at a government primary school in district Larkana. In his first week he notices three things. The roof of Class 4 leaks, and the class meets in the corridor when it rains. Only one of the three posted teachers is present on most days. And a Class 4 pupil who walks with a crutch cannot climb the stairs to the classroom, so his father carries him up each morning.

Bilal raises the roof with the headteacher. The headteacher shrugs. "These are personal problems," he says. "Some families are poor, some children are weak, and the government will do what it can. You cannot fix everything." Bilal is not sure. The roof is not a personal problem, and a child who cannot reach his classroom is not weak.

This topic looks at what inequality looks like in Pakistan, how to tell a private trouble from a public injustice, and what a citizen can actually do about one.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What forms does inequality take in Pakistan? How do we tell an injustice from a private trouble? And what does a citizen do about one?

### Forms of social inequality in Pakistan

**<Glossary term="Social inequality">Social inequality</Glossary>** means differences in resources, opportunities and treatment that are systematic rather than accidental. They persist over time, they follow group lines, and they can be measured. **Injustice** is a stronger word. It is used when an inequality is avoidable and when a right is being denied.

The 2023 census gives indicators for four forms. The table above carries them.

1. **Education.** About 35.60 percent of children aged five to sixteen were out of school in 2023, and girls were out of school more often than boys (38.99 percent against 32.44 percent). Article 25A says the State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children in that age group (Government of Pakistan, 2012). The gap between the promise and the count is the inequality.
2. **Income and work.** About 38.57 percent of the population was employed during the census reference year. The figure for males was 59.68 percent and for females 16.10 percent. Work, pay and who is expected to work at all are unequally distributed.
3. **Gender.** Literacy among those aged ten and above was 68.00 percent for males and 52.84 percent for females. The gap is not the same everywhere. Khyber Pakhtunkhwa reports 27.42 percentage points, Balochistan 17.70, Sindh 14.02, Punjab 11.79 and Islamabad Capital Territory 9.10 (Pakistan Bureau of Statistics, 2023, Sections 3.9 and 4.21).
4. **Region.** Literacy in 2023 was 66.25 percent in Punjab, 57.54 percent in Sindh, 51.09 percent in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and 42.01 percent in Balochistan, with rural Sindh at 38.14 percent. A child's chances still depend heavily on where they were born.

**A worked example: indicator or impression?** Ask whether the claim can be checked against a count, and whether the difference follows a group line.

| Claim | Indicator or impression? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Girls in this district leave school earlier than boys." | Indicator | It can be checked against out-of-school counts by sex |
| "People here are poor." | Impression | It names no count and no group |
| "Balochistan has lower literacy than Punjab." | Indicator | Two provincial rates can be compared |
| "The school is not as good as it used to be." | Impression | It has no measure and no comparison group |

### Responding to injustice: from noticing to action

**Correcting a common misconception: "Injustice is a private trouble, so the citizen cannot act."** The headteacher's answer is a natural one, and it hides the problem. A private trouble touches one person. An injustice has two features: a pattern that reaches a group, and a duty that someone is failing to meet. Bilal's three observations have both. A leaking roof and an absent teacher belong to the school's public duty. A child who cannot reach his classroom is being denied the education Article 25A promises. Once a pattern and a duty are visible, the problem is public, and a citizen can act on it.

Sociologists describe collective action of this kind as a <Glossary term="Social movement">social movement</Glossary>: a purposeful, organised group working towards a common social goal. Conerly, Holmes and Tamang (2021, Section 21.2) set out the usual stages. A movement emerges when people notice a problem, coalesces when they organise to publicise it, institutionalises when it becomes an established body with staff and funding, and declines when the issue fades or the change is won. Not every response becomes a movement. A single petition or one meeting with a councillor is a civic response at the first stage, and that is often enough.

The flowchart below sets out a sequence you can use for any injustice you meet. It is not a law. It is an order of operations, and the early steps are cheap.

<Figure id="fig-U7-6" src="/img/figures/gcce-400/unit-07/fig-U7-6.svg" alt="Flowchart of a civic response to injustice, in four steps left to right. One: notice and name the problem. Two: gather evidence, such as attendance records, photographs, budget papers and census counts. Three: dialogue with the school, the union council or the education office. Four: advocacy through an association, the media, an elected representative or a peaceful gathering protected by Article 16. A final branch from dialogue leads to legal remedy through a court, under Article 25, Article 25A or Article 27." />

1. **Notice and name.** Say plainly what is wrong and who is harmed. "The Class 4 roof leaks" is a fact. "The school is not giving Class 4 proper teaching" is a claim about a duty.
2. **Gather evidence.** Facts travel further than feelings. Attendance records, dated photographs, budget papers, a written application and a census count are all evidence. Evidence also protects you: it shows you are not making a personal complaint.
3. **Dialogue.** Take the evidence to the body that can act: the headteacher, the school management committee, the union council, the district education office. Ask for a decision in writing and a date.
4. **Advocacy.** If dialogue fails, widen the audience. An association, a teachers' group, a press club, an elected representative or a peaceful gathering can put the issue in public. Article 16 protects the right to assemble peacefully and without arms, subject to reasonable restrictions set by law, and Article 19 protects freedom of speech and of the press (Government of Pakistan, 2012).
5. **Legal remedy.** Where a right is clear, a court can enforce it. Article 25 guarantees equality before law. Article 25A creates a duty to educate children aged five to sixteen. Article 27 forbids discrimination in appointment to the service of Pakistan. A citizen does not need to be a lawyer to point to an article; the point is that the duty exists and can be invoked.

**A worked example: what should happen next?** Read each situation and choose the next step.

| Situation | Next step | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A pupil has been mocked for his faith, and the teacher did not intervene | Dialogue | The school has a duty to protect the pupil; raise it with the headteacher first |
| A road has been reported to the union council three times with no reply | Advocacy | Dialogue has been tried; widen the audience to an elected representative or the press |
| A girl is refused admission though Article 25A applies to her age group | Legal remedy | A clear right is being denied, and a court can enforce it |
| A trainee notices that two of six teachers are often absent | Gather evidence | Attendance should be recorded before anything is claimed |

## Activity: Choose the next step

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. Each member writes one real problem from their own town or village on a slip: a closed school, a missing teacher, an unrepaired road, a pupil kept at home, a drained but uncovered ditch.
2. Mix the slips. For each one, the group agrees: (a) what is the pattern and who is harmed; (b) which step of the flowchart should come first; and (c) what evidence would be needed.
3. Choose one slip where your group disagreed about the next step. Use the articles in this topic to settle it, and write one sentence explaining your decision.
4. One member from each group explains to the class how a private trouble becomes a public injustice, using an example the group did not see in this topic.

## Summary

Social inequality is a systematic difference in resources, opportunities or treatment, and it can be measured. In Pakistan the 2023 census shows it in education, in work, between men and women, and between provinces and districts. An injustice is more than a private trouble: it has a pattern that reaches a group and a duty that someone is failing to meet. A citizen's response follows a sequence: notice and name the problem, gather evidence, try dialogue, escalate through advocacy, and, where a right is clear, seek a legal remedy. Movements grow through stages, but a single petition or a meeting with a councillor is often the first stage of one.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask the headteacher or a senior teacher for one public duty the school struggles to meet, such as attendance, a building repair or a missing teacher. Note what evidence the school keeps about it: registers, letters, budget papers, photographs. Then ask one teacher what the school did about it last time, and whether it worked. Write four lines: the duty, the evidence, the action, and the result.
