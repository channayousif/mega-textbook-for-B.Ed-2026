## A real classroom situation

Zainab teaches Class 6 at a government primary school on the edge of Sukkur. Her 34 pupils speak four mother tongues at home: Sindhi, Urdu, Pashto and Saraiki. One morning a boy says, "Miss, Ali speaks Pashto at home. That means he is not really Sindhi, doesn't it?" Ali goes quiet. Another pupil says, "We are all Pakistani, so why do we need all these languages? They just divide us."

Zainab sees the misunderstanding behind the question. Her pupils think that belonging to one group means not belonging to another, and that difference itself is a problem. This topic takes that idea seriously and then corrects it.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What is diversity? What forms does it take in Pakistan? And is diversity really a threat to national unity?

### Understanding diversity in society

**<Glossary term="Diversity">Diversity</Glossary>** is the range of differences among the people who make up a society. A society is rarely made up of one kind of person, and Pakistan is less so than most. The course guide asks you to look at four dimensions: ethnic, cultural, economic and political.

Sociologists separate three ideas that are often mixed together. <Glossary term="Race">Race</Glossary> refers to physical differences that a particular society treats as significant. **Ethnicity** refers to shared culture: the practices, values, language, religion and traditions of a group (Conerly, Holmes and Tamang, 2021, Section 11.1). A <Glossary term="Minority group">minority group</Glossary> is not defined by numbers. It is a group that is treated as subordinate, or that lacks power, whatever its size. Wirth's definition runs to more than fifteen words, so it is paraphrased here: a group singled out for unequal treatment because of its physical or cultural characteristics, and which comes to see itself as an object of collective discrimination.

Pakistan's diversity can be read along six lines. The concept map above shows them.

1. **Language.** The 2023 census counted 240.5 million people and asked each one their mother tongue. Punjabi was reported by about 37 percent, Pashto by 18 percent, Sindhi by 14 percent, Saraiki by 12 percent and Urdu by 9 percent. Balochi, Hindko and Brahvi follow, and the census lists many more (Pakistan Bureau of Statistics, 2023, Table 11). No single language is spoken at home by most Pakistanis.
2. **Ethnicity and culture.** Shared language, history and tradition mark ethnic groups such as Punjabis, Sindhis, Pashtuns, Baloch, Saraiki-speakers and Muhajirs. Culture also travels in smaller units: a mohalla, a caste group, a family's food and dress.
3. **Religion.** The census reports that about 96 percent of the population is Muslim, with Christian, Hindu, Ahmadi, Scheduled Caste and other communities making up the rest (Pakistan Bureau of Statistics, 2023, Table 4.13). Each community has its own festivals, places of worship and personal law.
4. **Region.** Punjab holds about 53 percent of the population, Sindh 23 percent, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa 17 percent and Balochistan 6 percent, with the Islamabad Capital Territory about 1 percent (Pakistan Bureau of Statistics, 2023, Table 4.2). Province, district and village all shape how people see themselves.
5. **Economic class.** Income, occupation, land ownership and whether a family lives in a city or a village divide life chances as sharply as language does.
6. **Political view.** Parties, opinions and interests differ, and in a federation those differences are represented rather than hidden.

**A worked example: which dimension?** Ask what kind of difference the example turns on. Is it a shared way of life, a physical marker, an income, a province, or an opinion?

| Example | Dimension | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A pupil who speaks Saraiki at home and Urdu at school | Language | The difference is a mother tongue |
| Two cousins who earn very different wages in the same city | Economic class | The difference is income and occupation |
| A family that supports different parties at election time | Political view | The difference is opinion, not identity |
| A Hindu pupil celebrating a festival the rest of the class does not | Religion | The difference is a religious community |

The same person sits at the crossing of several dimensions at once. Ali in Zainab's class is a Pashto speaker, a Pakistani, a pupil in Sindh and a member of one economic class. None of these cancels the others.

### Diversity and national unity

**Correcting a common misconception: "Diversity is a threat to national unity."** The claim sounds reasonable, and it is wrong in a specific way. It treats differences between groups as the cause of conflict. The Constitution points the other way. Article 28 says that any section of citizens with a distinct language, script or culture has the right to preserve and promote it, and may establish institutions for that purpose (Government of Pakistan, 2012). Article 33 then says the State shall discourage "parochial, racial, tribal, sectarian and provincial prejudices" among citizens. The Constitution protects diversity and names prejudice as the thing to be discouraged. Difference is not the enemy; prejudice is.

This is not a uniquely Pakistani position. Kymlicka and Norman (2000) edited a volume on citizenship in diverse societies, which asks how people who belong to different cultural groups can still share a political community and equal rights. Their answer, in outline, is that shared citizenship does not require everyone to be the same. It requires that no group be forced to give up its identity in order to belong.

Think of a school sports day. Teams may be drawn by house, by class or by neighbourhood. The competition is real, and each team wants to win. What makes the day work is that everyone accepts the rules and takes part again tomorrow. A country is not a single team. It is more like the sports day itself: many groups, one set of rules, and a reason to keep playing. Unity in this sense means agreeing on how to live together, not agreeing on everything.

Zainab's pupils were half right. The languages do differ. What they got wrong was the conclusion. Ali is not less Sindhi for speaking Pashto at home, and the country is not less united for having both.

**A worked example: difference or threat?** Read each statement and ask whether it describes a difference between groups or a harm done to a group.

| Statement | Difference or harm? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Saraiki is spoken in southern Punjab." | Difference | It names a shared language, not an injury |
| "A pupil was mocked for the way he spoke." | Harm | Prejudice was acted on, which Article 33 tells the State to discourage |
| "Two provinces want different shares of water." | Difference | Competing interests are normal in a federation |
| "A family was stopped from voting because of their faith." | Harm | A right was denied, which Article 25 protects |

## Activity: Mapping the dimensions

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. Each member writes down one real difference they can observe in their own town or village: a language, a festival, an occupation, a neighbourhood, an opinion. Do not name any person.
2. Pool the slips. For each one, the group agrees which dimension it belongs to, and whether it is a difference between groups or a harm done to a group.
3. Choose one difference and discuss what could turn it into a harm, and what could keep it a difference. Use Article 28 and Article 33 to explain the line.
4. Together, write four or five sentences answering Zainab's pupil: "We are all Pakistani, so why do we need all these languages?" Send one member to read your answer to the class.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Without looking back, define diversity in one sentence and name the four dimensions the course guide asks you to consider.
2. *(Remember)* Name three of Pakistan's main mother tongues and the province where each is mainly spoken.
3. *(Understand)* A friend says, "A minority group means a group with fewer people." In three sentences, explain why this is not the sociological meaning.
4. *(Understand)* In your own words, explain the difference between race and ethnicity, with one example of each.
5. *(Apply)* A pupil in your class says the rest of the class should stop celebrating a festival he does not share, because it "divides the class". Use Article 28 and Article 33 to explain why his request is mistaken.

## Summary

Diversity is the range of differences among the people of a society. In Pakistan those differences run along language, ethnicity, religion, region, economic class and political view, and the census shows that no single group holds a majority of the population. Race is a physical marker a society treats as significant; ethnicity is shared culture. A minority group is defined by subordination and unequal treatment, not by numbers. Diversity is not a threat to national unity. The Constitution protects the right to preserve language and culture in Article 28, and tells the State to discourage prejudice in Article 33. The threat comes from prejudice and discrimination, not from difference.

## Summative task

Write 250-300 words on the following task.

1. Choose one dimension of diversity in your district: language, religion, region or economic class.
2. Describe one real example of that difference, and one real example of a prejudice or discrimination attached to it.
3. Analyse whether the difference or the prejudice is the more serious problem for social harmony, and justify your judgement with evidence from this topic and from the Constitution.
