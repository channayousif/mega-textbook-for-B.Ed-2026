## A real classroom situation

Rizwan is the headteacher of a government high school in Hyderabad. His school gives every class the same timetable, the same textbook and the same examination paper. He is proud of this. "We treat everyone equally," he tells a visiting trainee. "No favouritism."

The trainee notices a problem. A Class 8 pupil with a visual impairment receives the same paper as everyone else, in the same print, at the same time, and cannot read it. A girl who missed two years of school because her family moved is in Class 8 with pupils two years younger. A pupil who speaks Saraiki at home is told that only Urdu may be used in the classroom. "Equal treatment" is producing unequal results.

Rizwan's school is not cruel. It is applying one word, equality, in a way that hides what his pupils actually need. This topic looks at what equal rights require, what inclusion adds, and what a school, a home and a community can do about it.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What are equal rights? Why is equal treatment not enough? And what does an inclusive school, home and community actually do?

### Inclusive citizenship and equal rights

**<Glossary term="Inclusive citizenship">Inclusive citizenship</Glossary>** means that every citizen belongs to the political community and can take part in it, whatever their language, faith, sex, class, ability or region. It is not a special status for some citizens. It is the standard that citizenship should meet for all of them.

The Constitution sets out equal rights in plain terms. Article 25 says all citizens are equal before law and entitled to equal protection of law, and that there shall be no discrimination on the basis of sex. Article 26 forbids discrimination in access to public places on the ground only of race, religion, caste, sex, residence or place of birth. Article 27 forbids discrimination in appointment to the service of Pakistan on the same grounds. Article 36 requires the State to safeguard the legitimate rights and interests of minorities, including their due representation in the Federal and Provincial services (Government of Pakistan, 2012).

**Equal treatment and equity.** <Glossary term="Equality">Equality</Glossary> means giving everyone the same treatment, resources or opportunities. <Glossary term="Equity">Equity</Glossary> means giving each person the resources and support they need to take part and succeed, which may differ from one person to another. The diagram above shows the difference. Three children of different heights stand on identical boxes. The tallest child already saw over the fence. The middle child just managed. The shortest still could not see. Giving everyone the same box was equal. Sharing the boxes according to need was equitable, and only that let all three watch the match.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Equality means treating everyone in exactly the same way."** The claim is the one Rizwan's school lives by, and it fails for a simple reason. People do not start from the same place. The same treatment given to people in different circumstances produces different results. A pupil who cannot see the paper, a pupil who has missed two years of school, and a pupil who is forbidden to use the language they think in are all being treated the same and all being held back. Equal rights are not violated by giving someone more support. They are violated when a difference in circumstances is ignored and the person is left behind.

That is why the Constitution pairs equal treatment with special provision. Article 25(3) allows the State to make special provision for the protection of women and children. Article 27 allows posts to be reserved for a class or area to secure adequate representation. Article 37(a) requires the State to promote, with special care, the educational and economic interests of backward classes or areas. A rule that treats everyone identically can be a way of preserving an advantage. Equity is the remedy the Constitution itself allows.

**Inclusion in practice.** Rights on paper become real in habits. The table below sets out practices at three levels.

<Figure id="fig-U7-8" src="/img/figures/gcce-400/unit-07/fig-U7-8.svg" alt="Table of practices that promote inclusive citizenship at three levels. At home: asking each family member what they think, sharing chores regardless of sex, and letting children speak their home language. In school: seating and grouping that mix pupils, materials in a language pupils understand, extra time or a reader for a pupil who needs it, and a classroom rule that mockery of a person's background is not allowed. In the community: inviting every group to a school event, a notice board in more than one language, and a school management committee that includes women and minority members." />

**Why this produces harmony.** The course guide's third outcome is to recognise the importance of diversity and inclusivity for societal harmony and peaceful coexistence. The link is practical. A citizen who is excluded does not stop being a citizen. They stop believing the community is theirs. Article 33 tells the State to discourage parochial, racial, tribal, sectarian and provincial prejudices, and Article 28 protects the right to preserve a distinct language, script or culture. Inclusion is therefore not a kindness added after unity is achieved. It is how unity is achieved.

**A worked example: equal or equitable?** Read each practice and ask whether it gives everyone the same thing or what they need.

| Practice | Equal or equitable? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Every pupil gets the same printed exam paper | Equal | The same treatment, though one pupil cannot read it |
| A pupil with a visual impairment gets a reader and extra time | Equitable | Support is matched to need, so the test measures the same thing |
| Only Urdu may be spoken in the classroom | Equal | It looks neutral but penalises a home language |
| A pupil new to the school is paired with a buddy for a month | Equitable | Temporary support removes a barrier |
| The school buys one textbook for each pupil | Equal | The same resource, assuming all can read it |

## Activity: Build one inclusive routine

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. Each group chooses one routine in a school day: the morning assembly, a group task, a reading lesson, a sports period, a school event, or a parents' meeting.
2. Redesign the routine so that every pupil, whatever their background, can take part. Say what changes, who it helps, and what it costs in time or materials.
3. Test your routine against the misconception. Ask a member of another group to argue that your routine is unfair because it treats pupils differently. Answer the objection in two or three sentences.
4. Write your routine on the board in five steps, and note which article of the Constitution it rests on.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Without looking back, define inclusive citizenship in one sentence and name three articles of the Constitution that protect equal rights.
2. *(Remember)* Give one practice that promotes inclusion at home, one at school and one in the community.
3. *(Understand)* In three sentences, explain the difference between equality and equity, with an example.
4. *(Understand)* Explain why Article 25(3) and Article 37(a) allow special provision rather than identical treatment.
5. *(Apply)* A school gives every pupil the same homework, in Urdu, to be completed in one evening. A pupil has no quiet place to work at home and another has been told the language of the homework is not the one they speak. Using this topic, say what the school should change and why.

## Summary

Inclusive citizenship means every citizen belongs and can take part. The Constitution guarantees equal rights in Articles 25, 26, 27 and 36, and it also allows special provision for women and children, for under-represented classes or areas, and for backward classes or areas. Equality gives everyone the same treatment; equity gives each person the support they need, which may differ. Equal treatment can hold people back when they start from different places, so inclusion is a matter of practice as well as of law. Small habits at home, in school and in the community, such as mixing groups, providing materials in a language pupils understand, and refusing mockery, are what turn a right into a fact. Harmony follows, because a citizen who is included believes the community is theirs.

## Try this at your practicum school

Choose one routine in your practicum school, such as the morning assembly, a reading lesson or a parents' meeting. Note one thing that could stop a pupil from taking part fully: the language used, the seating, the materials, the time, or an assumption about what pupils can do. Ask one teacher what the school does about it. Write four lines: the routine, the barrier, the school's response, and one change you would suggest.

## Summative task

Design one classroom routine that makes every pupil, whatever their background, feel included, and write 300-350 words justifying it.

1. Name the routine and the point in the school day where it happens.
2. Explain how it works, step by step, and which pupils it helps.
3. Justify it from this unit: use the equality and equity distinction and at least one article of the Constitution.
