# State, Government and Civil Society - Notes for Teachers

The course guide lists no teaching strategies for this unit, but it suggests practical activities for the course. These notes offer one way to plan the two weeks and to use a service-learning visit.

## Sequencing the two weeks

- **Week 4 (Topic 2.1).** Start from the pupil's question, "Is Pakistan a new country after an election?" Settle the state and government difference before teaching the tiers, because trainees who mix the two also mix up tiers. Bring printed copies of Articles 1, 140A and 142, or open the online text; reading the articles is quicker than describing them.
- **Week 5 (Topic 2.2).** Start from the Hyderabad mothers' group. Ask trainees whether it was the government, a business or an NGO before you give the definition of civil society. End the week with turnout figures and the one-vote argument.

## Likely misconceptions and how to handle them

- **"The state and the government are the same."** Ask: "Did Pakistan's borders or people change after the last election?" Let the class find the answer.
- **"Civil society means NGOs."** A likely wrong answer is, "A mohalla committee is not civil society because it is not registered." Ask what registration adds to the three features of voluntary, non-state and non-profit.
- **"One vote makes no difference."** Work the union council ward example on the board, then ask trainees to change the numbers and see when the result flips.

**One good discussion prompt.** "Should civil-society groups take part in politics, or only in welfare work?" Expect a split. Use it to show that watching government and voicing needs are part of democracy, while supporting one party is a different matter.

## Practical work: service-learning with a local organisation

The guide suggests service-learning with a local organisation as one practical activity for the course. It fits this unit's second week.

1. In small groups, trainees choose one local civil-society organisation, such as a welfare trust, a literacy programme or a community committee, and arrange a short visit with the permission of their institution.
2. They spend part of the visit helping with a simple task the organisation chooses, and part asking how it works with the local council or the provincial government.
3. Each group reports back in five minutes, linking what they saw to the two directions of the democracy and civil society relationship.

Follow your institution's consent and safety rules, and do not photograph anyone without permission. The activity is not marked against a fixed model answer; judge whether trainees link what they saw to the ideas of the unit.
