## A real classroom situation

Sana is a B.Ed trainee on practicum at a government girls' middle school in Mirpurkhas. It is the week after a general election, and a new government is being formed in Islamabad. In her Class 8 social studies lesson, a girl asks, "Miss, now that the government has changed, is Pakistan a new country?" Another pupil laughs, but a third says, "My uncle says the government and the state are the same thing."

On the same day, rain leaks through the roof of Class 6. The headteacher writes to the district education office. A pupil says, "Why not write to the Prime Minister? The government fixes everything." Another says the union council should do it, because it fixed the street lights last year.

Sana sees two questions in her classroom. What is the difference between the state and the government? And which part of government is responsible for what? This topic answers both.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What is a state? How is the government of Pakistan arranged? And what does a government do?

### Concept of state

A <Glossary term="State">**state**</Glossary> is a political community of people who live in a defined territory, under their own government, and who are free to run their own affairs. The idea is old, but a widely used modern statement is in the Montevideo Convention of 1933. It says a state should have a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states (Convention on Rights and Duties of States, 1933, Art. 1).

Civics books usually name four **elements of a state**. The diagram at the start of this topic shows them.

1. **Population.** A state needs people who live in it permanently. There is no fixed minimum number.
2. **Territory.** A state needs a defined area of land, with its waters and air space. Article 1 of the Constitution says the territories of Pakistan comprise the four provinces of Balochistan, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab and Sindh, the Islamabad Capital Territory, and any other areas that become part of Pakistan (Government of Pakistan, 2012, Art. 1).
3. **Government.** A state needs an organisation that makes laws, carries them out and settles disputes. Without it, the people cannot act together.
4. **<Glossary term="Sovereignty">Sovereignty</Glossary>.** A state has the final power inside its territory, and no outside power controls it. This is the meaning of "capacity to enter into relations with other states" in the Montevideo text: only a sovereign state can sign treaties and deal with other states as an equal.

Take away any one element and there is no state. A large group of people with no territory of their own is a nation or a community, but not a state. A province has population, territory and a government, but it is not sovereign, because it works under the Constitution of the whole country.

**Correcting a common misconception: "The state and the government are the same thing."** The government is only one of the four elements. It is the part through which the state acts. The pupil in Mirpurkhas was right to be puzzled: after an election the government changes, but Pakistan stays the same state, with the same people, territory and sovereignty. A useful picture is a school. The school stays the same school when a new headteacher arrives; only the person running it has changed.

The Constitution itself shows that the state acts through many bodies. For the purpose of fundamental rights, Article 7 says "the State" means the Federal Government, Parliament, a Provincial Government, a Provincial Assembly, and local authorities that the law allows to impose a tax (Government of Pakistan, 2012, Art. 7). So when a citizen claims a right against "the State", the claim can be made against any of these bodies, not only the government in Islamabad.

**A worked example: state or government?** Use one test. Is the statement about something lasting, which belongs to the whole political community, or about the group of people running public affairs for a period?

| Statement | Lasting, belongs to all? | Group running affairs for a period? | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Pakistan became independent in 1947." | Yes | No | The state |
| "The new cabinet announced a budget." | No | Yes | The government |
| "Pakistan's borders must be defended." | Yes | No | The state |
| "The Chief Minister of Sindh visited flood areas." | No | Yes | A (provincial) government |

### Structure of government in Pakistan

Article 1 says Pakistan "shall be a Federal Republic" (Government of Pakistan, 2012, Art. 1). A <Glossary term="Federation">**federation**</Glossary> is a system in which the Constitution divides power between a central government and governments of the units, here the provinces. Each has its own powers, and neither can simply take away the powers of the other. Under the provinces, Article 140A adds a third tier, local government. The flowchart below shows the three tiers and how a federal law is made.

<Figure id="fig-U2-2" src="/img/figures/gcce-400/unit-02/fig-U2-2.svg" alt="Flowchart in two parts. The first shows the three tiers of government in Pakistan: the federal tier with Parliament, the Federal Government and the federal courts; the provincial tier with the Provincial Assembly, the Chief Minister's government, the Governor and the High Court; and the local tier of elected councils set up by provincial law. The second shows a federal bill passing one House, then the other, then going to the President for assent within ten days, with a joint sitting of both Houses if the second House rejects it or does not pass it in 90 days." />

At each tier there is a body that makes laws or rules, and a body that carries them out.

**1. The federal tier.**

- **Parliament (Majlis-e-Shoora)** is made up of the President and two Houses, the National Assembly and the Senate (Government of Pakistan, 2012, Art. 50). The National Assembly has 336 seats, including seats reserved for women and non-Muslims (Art. 51). The Senate has 96 members (Art. 59).
- **The Federal Government** is the Prime Minister and the Federal Ministers. The Prime Minister is the chief executive and is elected by the National Assembly (Arts. 90-91).
- **The federal courts.** Since the Twenty-seventh Amendment of 2025, Article 175 names a Federal Constitutional Court and a Supreme Court, and a High Court for each province and for Islamabad.

**2. The provincial tier.**

- **The Provincial Assembly** makes laws for the province. The Provincial Assembly of Sindh has 168 seats: 130 general seats, 29 seats for women and 9 for non-Muslims (Government of Pakistan, 2012, Art. 106).
- **The Provincial Government** is the Chief Minister and Provincial Ministers. The Assembly elects the Chief Minister (Arts. 129-130).
- **The Governor** is appointed by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister (Art. 101). The executive authority of the province is exercised in the Governor's name, but by the Chief Minister's government.

**3. The local tier.** Article 140A says each province shall, by law, set up a local government system and give political, administrative and financial responsibility to elected local representatives. Elections to local governments are held by the Election Commission of Pakistan. In Sindh, the Sindh Local Government Act 2013 does this. It divides each district into urban and rural areas, each with elected councils. Urban areas become corporations or committees, such as a Metropolitan Corporation or a Municipal Committee, and the Act also provides for union councils and union committees, the smallest units and the ones closest to the people (Sindh Local Government Act, 2013).

**Who makes which laws?** Article 142 divides law-making. Parliament alone makes laws on the matters in the Federal Legislative List, such as defence, foreign affairs and currency. A Provincial Assembly makes laws on every matter not in that list, and both can make laws on criminal law, criminal procedure and evidence (Government of Pakistan, 2012, Art. 142). Before the Eighteenth Amendment of 2010, there was also a "Concurrent List" of shared subjects. The Amendment removed it, so subjects such as school education passed to the provinces.

**How a federal law is made.** A bill on a federal matter can start in either House (Art. 70). When one House passes it, it goes to the other. If the second House passes it without changes, it goes to the President. If the second House rejects it, or does not pass it within 90 days, the two Houses can meet in a joint sitting and decide by majority. A Money Bill, such as the budget, can start only in the National Assembly (Art. 73). The President must assent within ten days, or, except for a Money Bill, send it back once to be reconsidered (Art. 75).

**A worked example: which tier?** Ask: is the matter one for the whole country, for one province, or for one town or village?

| Problem | Tier | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Defending Pakistan's border | Federal | Defence is on the Federal Legislative List |
| A leaking roof in a government school in Mirpurkhas | Provincial (through the district education office) | School education is a provincial subject since 2010 |
| A broken street light in the school's lane | Local | Municipal services such as street lighting are local council work under the Sindh law |

Sana's pupils were partly right. The Prime Minister does not fix school roofs in Sindh. The headteacher wrote to the right office, and the union council was right for the street lights.

### Functions of government

A government has three main jobs. Each is done by a different branch.

1. **Making laws** (the <Glossary term="Legislature">legislature</Glossary>): Parliament and the Provincial Assemblies.
2. **Carrying out laws and running public services** (the <Glossary term="Executive">executive</Glossary>): the Federal and Provincial Governments and their departments, and local councils.
3. **Interpreting laws and settling disputes** (the <Glossary term="Judiciary">judiciary</Glossary>): the courts.

Keeping these jobs in different hands is called the <Glossary term="Separation of Powers">**separation of powers**</Glossary>. It protects citizens, because no single person or body holds all the power. A law passed by an Assembly can be challenged in a court, and a minister who acts outside the law can be stopped by a court.

Civics books also divide what governments do by purpose (Glencoe/McGraw-Hill, n.d.). Some functions are basic to any state: defending the country, keeping law and order, giving justice, and collecting taxes to pay for these. Other functions serve the people's welfare: education, health, roads, clean water and help after disasters. In Pakistan, welfare functions are also duties set by the Constitution. Article 25A, for example, says the State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children aged five to sixteen (Government of Pakistan, 2012, Art. 25A).

**A worked example: which branch and which purpose?**

| Action | Branch | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| The Provincial Assembly of Sindh passes a law on school education | Legislature | Welfare |
| The police in Hyderabad arrest a suspect | Executive | Law and order |
| A High Court decides a land dispute | Judiciary | Justice |
| A district health office runs a polio campaign | Executive | Welfare |

## Activity: Who is responsible?

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. Each member writes one real public problem from their own town or village on a slip, such as a closed health centre, a broken bridge, a missing teacher or a lane without drains.
2. Mix the slips. For each slip, the group decides: (a) which tier is responsible, federal, provincial or local; (b) which branch would act, legislature, executive or judiciary; and (c) which office a citizen should contact first.
3. Choose one slip on which your group disagreed. Use the Constitution articles in this topic to settle it, and write one sentence explaining your decision.
4. One member from each group explains to the class why the state and the government are not the same, using an example the group did not see in this topic.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Without looking back, name the four elements of a state and give the Pakistani example of each.
2. *(Remember)* Name the three tiers of government in Pakistan and the Constitution article that requires the third tier.
3. *(Understand)* A friend says, "When the government falls, the state falls too." In three sentences, explain why this is wrong.
4. *(Understand)* Describe, in order, what happens to a federal bill after the National Assembly passes it.
5. *(Apply)* A village near Sanghar has no doctor at its basic health unit, and a dispute over the land for a new unit has gone to court. Name the tier and the branch involved in each part of this problem.

## Summary

A state is a political community with four elements: population, territory, government and sovereignty. The government is only one element, the one through which the state acts; governments change, but the state continues. Pakistan is a federation with three tiers: federal, provincial and local. The Constitution divides law-making between Parliament and the Provincial Assemblies, and since 2010 every subject outside the Federal Legislative List belongs to the provinces. Governments make laws, carry them out and settle disputes through separate branches, and they serve both basic and welfare purposes.
