## A real classroom situation

Kashif is a B.Ed trainee from Hyderabad. In his neighbourhood, the government girls' primary school has had no science teacher for a year. A group of mothers starts meeting in one home to discuss it. A retired teacher helps them write an application. The local teachers' association supports them, and a reporter from the press club writes about the school. Within two months, the education department posts a teacher.

Kashif tells his cousin the story. His cousin is not impressed. "That is just NGO work," he says. "And anyway, real change needs power. I did not vote in the last election. One vote makes no difference."

Kashif is sure his cousin is wrong on both points, but he cannot yet say why. The mothers, the association and the reporter were not NGOs, and they were not the government either. What were they? And does one vote matter? This topic answers both questions.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What is civil society? How do democracy and civil society depend on each other? And why do participation and representation matter?

### Meaning of civil society

**<Glossary term="Civil society">Civil society</Glossary>** is the space where citizens join together, by their own choice, to pursue shared aims, outside the family, the market and the state. Batool, Mahmood and Ullah (2019) describe civil society in Pakistan as an umbrella term for many non-state and non-market citizen organisations, initiatives, networks and unions.

The concept map at the start of this topic shows where civil society sits. Each of the four spaces has its own way of working.

- **The family** is held together by kinship and care.
- **The market** is held together by buying, selling and profit.
- **The state** is held together by law and the power to enforce it.
- **Civil society** is held together by voluntary cooperation. People join because they choose to, and they can leave.

Civil society in Pakistan takes many forms:

1. **Associations and unions**, such as teachers' associations and trade unions.
2. **Welfare and service organisations**, such as the Edhi Foundation's ambulance service or trusts that run schools.
3. **Media and press**, such as press clubs and independent newspapers.
4. **Community groups**, such as mohalla committees and the mothers' group in Hyderabad.
5. **Professional, religious and cultural bodies**, such as bar associations, mosque committees and literary societies.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Civil society means only NGOs."** A registered non-governmental organisation (NGO) is one kind of civil-society group, but only one. Kashif's mothers' group had no office, no funding and no registration, and it was still civil society. So were the teachers' association and the press club. What makes a group part of civil society is not registration but three features: it is voluntary, it is not part of the state, and it does not exist to make a profit.

**A worked example: which space?** Ask three questions. Is it held together by kinship? Does it exist to make a profit? Is it part of the state? If the answer to all three is "no", and people join by choice, it is civil society.

| Group or body | Kinship? | Profit? | Part of the state? | Space |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A family arranging a wedding | Yes | No | No | Family |
| A shop selling school uniforms | No | Yes | No | Market |
| The union council | No | No | Yes | State (local government) |
| A mohalla committee that organises a clean-up | No | No | No | Civil society |
| A teachers' association | No | No | No | Civil society |

### Relationship between democracy and civil society

Democracy and civil society need each other. The relationship runs in both directions.

**Civil society strengthens democracy.**

1. **It watches the government.** Batool et al. (2019) argue that civil society keeps a watch on the government's activities and pushes undemocratic elements to follow democratic norms. A press report about a school without a teacher is one small example.
2. **It gives citizens a voice between elections.** An election comes about once in five years. Associations and community groups carry citizens' needs to the government on all the other days.
3. **It teaches democratic skills.** In a committee or association, people learn to discuss, vote, keep accounts and accept a decision they disagree with. These are the skills a democracy needs.
4. **It checks the fairness of elections.** The Free and Fair Election Network (FAFEN), a network of civil-society organisations, observes general elections in Pakistan and publishes reports on turnout and process (Business Recorder, 2024).

**Democracy protects civil society.** Civil society can work freely only where the law protects it. The Constitution gives every citizen the right to form associations or unions (Article 17), freedom of speech and of the press (Article 19), and the right to assemble peacefully (Article 16), each subject to reasonable limits set by law (Government of Pakistan, 2012). These rights are the ground that civil society stands on.

The relationship is not always smooth. Batool et al. (2019) note that civil society in Pakistan was weakened from the start by the way the state was built, and that it has grown stronger in recent years but is still developing. A balanced view also accepts that not every civil-society group is democratic inside, and that some groups divide people instead of joining them. Civil society strengthens democracy when its groups are open, peaceful and respect the rights of others.

**A worked example: following the two directions.** Take Kashif's story.

| Step in the story | Which direction? | How |
|---|---|---|
| The press club reporter writes about the school | Civil society strengthens democracy | Watches the government and informs the public |
| The mothers meet freely and write an application | Democracy protects civil society | Freedom of association and expression (Articles 17, 19) |
| The education department posts a teacher | Civil society strengthens democracy | Citizens' voice reaches the government between elections |

### Importance of political participation and representation

**<Glossary term="Political participation">Political participation</Glossary>** means the actions citizens take to influence who governs and what the government does. The table below compares its main forms.

<Figure id="fig-U2-4" src="/img/figures/gcce-400/unit-02/fig-U2-4.svg" alt="Table of six forms of political participation, what each asks of a citizen and a Pakistani example: voting in an election, contacting a representative such as an MPA, joining a party campaign, sending a petition to the union council, peaceful protest protected by Article 16, and standing for a union council seat." />

**<Glossary term="Representation">Representation</Glossary>** means that elected people act for the citizens who chose them. A member of the National Assembly speaks and votes for a constituency of many thousands of people, who cannot all sit in Parliament themselves.

Representation also asks whether every group has a voice. The Constitution reserves seats for groups that might otherwise be left out. Of the National Assembly's 336 seats, 60 are reserved for women and 10 for non-Muslims, and the Provincial Assembly of Sindh has 29 seats for women and 9 for non-Muslims (Government of Pakistan, 2012, Arts. 51, 106).

Participation and representation are linked. Representatives know which groups vote and which groups speak up. A group that does not take part is easier to ignore. This is one reason why turnout matters. In the general election of 8 February 2024, about 60.6 million people voted. That was more people than in 2018, but turnout fell from 52.1 percent to 47.6 percent, because the number of registered voters had grown to 128.6 million (Business Recorder, 2024). So more than half of the registered voters did not vote.

Young people are a large share of Pakistan's population. Youniss and Levine (2009) brought together research on how schools and communities can engage young people in civic life, and teachers are part of that work. In Pakistan, Ahmad (2004) argued that the social studies curriculum of his time gave little space to democratic values such as participation. Teachers can help close that gap.

**Correcting a common misconception: "One vote makes no difference, so participation does not matter."** It is true that one vote rarely decides an election alone. But the claim misses three points.

1. **Votes count together.** An election result is the sum of many single decisions. If everyone who thinks "my vote does not matter" stays at home, a large group goes unheard.
2. **Margins can be small.** In a local government election, a ward has far fewer voters than a National Assembly constituency, so a small number of votes can change the result.
3. **Participation is more than voting.** As the table shows, a citizen can contact a representative, sign a petition or join an association. Kashif's mothers' group changed a decision without any election at all.

**A worked example: does one vote matter?** Imagine a union council ward with 4,000 registered voters. Candidate A gets 1,050 votes and Candidate B gets 1,000. Only 2,050 people voted, so 1,950 stayed at home. If just 51 of those who stayed at home had voted for B, B would have won. No single voter decided the result, but the group of non-voters did, by staying silent.

## Activity: Mapping civil society in our town

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. Each member lists three groups from their own town, village or mohalla that are not family, not business and not government. Include at least one that is not a registered NGO.
2. Pool your lists. For each group, agree on its kind (association, welfare, media, community or professional) and use the three-question test to check that it belongs to civil society.
3. Pick one group. Discuss how it strengthens democracy, using at least one of the four ways in this topic, and which constitutional right it depends on.
4. Together, write a short reply of four or five sentences to Kashif's cousin, answering both of his claims with evidence from this topic.

Exchange replies with another group and check whether they answered both claims.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Without looking back, define civil society in one sentence and name the three spaces it is distinguished from.
2. *(Remember)* List four forms of political participation and say what each asks of the citizen.
3. *(Understand)* In three sentences, explain one way civil society strengthens democracy and one way democracy protects civil society.
4. *(Understand)* Explain why the Constitution reserves seats for women and non-Muslims in the assemblies.
5. *(Apply)* A group of farmers near Badin forms an association to ask the irrigation department for a fair share of canal water, and a local reporter writes about it. Classify each actor by space (family, market, state or civil society) and name the form of participation the farmers used.

## Summary

Civil society is the space where citizens join together by choice, outside the family, the market and the state. It includes associations, unions, welfare bodies, media and community groups, not only registered NGOs. Democracy and civil society depend on each other: civil society watches government, voices needs and teaches democratic skills, while democratic rights to association, speech and assembly protect it. Political participation takes many forms, and representation works best when every group takes part and has a voice. One vote alone rarely decides a result, but the votes of many single citizens always do.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find out which civil-society links your practicum school has, for example a parents' group, a welfare trust that helps pupils, a teachers' association or a local committee. Ask one teacher how that group has helped the school. Write three lines: the group, its kind, and one thing it did.

## Summative task

Write 300-350 words. Choose one civil-society group you know or can find out about in your district, such as a welfare trust, an association, a press club or a community committee.

1. Explain why the group belongs to civil society, using the three-question test.
2. Describe how it takes part in public life and which constitutional rights it relies on.
3. Evaluate how far the group strengthens democracy and representation in your area, and name one limit or risk in its work.

*(Evaluate)*

**Mini-rubric (10 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Civil society identified (2 marks) | Applies all three features accurately to the group (2) | Names the group as civil society with weak reasons (1) | Absent or wrong |
| Participation and rights (3 marks) | Clear account of what the group does, linked to the right articles (3) | Description without the rights link, or the reverse (1-2) | None |
| Evaluation of democratic contribution (5 marks) | Reaches a reasoned judgement on how far the group strengthens democracy and representation, supported by specific evidence, and names a real limit (4-5) | A judgement with general support, or no limit (2-3); only praise (1) | No evaluation |

The evaluation criterion asks you to weigh evidence. Describing the group's good work is not enough.
