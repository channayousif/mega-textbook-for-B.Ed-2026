## A real classroom situation

Imran teaches Class 3 at a government primary school in a village near Thatta. Every year in the harvest season, attendance falls by a third. In the staff room he says, "Parents here do not care about education. When I call a meeting, nobody comes."

The head teacher tries something different. She asks the school management committee to hold the next meeting on a Friday after the midday prayer, in the shade of the village otaq, and to speak in Sindhi. Two mothers from the committee visit women at home to invite them. This time forty parents come. A father explains that children help pick cotton because families are paid by weight. A mother says girls stay home when there is no female teacher in the early classes. Together, parents and teachers agree on a plan: a shorter school day during the six harvest weeks, and a rota of mothers to walk the younger girls to school.

Were the parents uninterested, or had nobody asked them in a way they could answer? This topic explains what a community is, how people engage with it, what a famous Karachi project teaches us, and why the school is itself a community institution.

## Explanation

This topic has four parts. It begins with the idea of community, then looks at how people engage with their communities, studies one successful Pakistani initiative, and ends with the school.

### Concept, nature and characteristics of community and its development

In everyday speech, a "community" can mean a village, a caste or biradari, the people of one faith, or everyone in a city. Researchers have tried to find what these uses share. MacQueen and colleagues (2001) asked many different groups of people what "community" meant to them. A common definition emerged: a group of people with diverse characteristics who are linked by social ties, share common perspectives, and take joint action in a place or setting.

That definition gives five **characteristics of a community**:

1. **Social ties.** Members know one another, help one another and feel they belong.
2. **Something shared.** A common interest, need, value, language or way of life.
3. **Joint action.** Members can and do act together, for example at a wedding, a funeral, a flood or a repair.
4. **A place or setting.** A mohalla, a goth, a school, a workplace, or even an online group.
5. **Diversity.** Members are not all the same. They differ in age, wealth, opinion and sometimes faith or language.

**Correcting a common misconception: "A community is just the people who live in one place."** Living near one another is not enough. Two hundred families in a new apartment block in Hyderabad who never meet are neighbours, but not yet a community. And a community need not share one place: the fishing families of the Indus delta, the teachers of a district, or the parents in a school's WhatsApp group can all be communities because they share something and act together.

**A worked example: is this group a community?** Test each group against the five characteristics.

| Group | Social ties | Something shared | Joint action | Setting | Community? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Families in one lane in Orangi who meet to build a sewer | Yes | Yes, a need for sanitation | Yes | The lane | Yes |
| Passengers on one bus from Hyderabad to Karachi | No | Only the journey | No | The bus | No, a crowd |
| Parents in a primary school's WhatsApp group who organise a cleaning day | Some | Yes, their children's school | Yes | Online and the school | Yes |

**The nature of community.** Communities are not fixed. They grow, shrink, split and join. People belong to several communities at once: a woman can belong to her village, her profession and a women's group. Communities also contain disagreement and unequal power. A community that seems united may leave out its poorest members, its women or its minorities. Seeing this is the first step to fair engagement.

**Community development.** <Glossary term="Community development">**Community development**</Glossary> is the process by which members of a community work together, often with government and other organisations, to improve their living conditions and to build their own ability to solve future problems. The second half of the definition matters. A road built for people improves conditions. A road planned, partly paid for and looked after by people also builds their skill and confidence.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Community development is the government's job alone."** Government has duties that communities cannot carry out alone, such as trunk sewers, big roads and teachers' salaries. But a community that waits for everything loses years, and work done without local people is often badly suited to their needs. The Orangi case below shows a better division of work: the community does what it can do well, and government does what only government can do.

### Approaches to effective community engagement and social cohesion

**Community engagement** is the process of working with people, as partners, on the issues that affect their lives. Not all engagement is equal. A widely used spectrum from the International Association for Public Participation (IAP2), explained in a guide by the United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA, 2026), sets out five levels. Each level gives the public more influence over a decision than the one before.

| Level | What it means | Example: choosing where to place a new water tank in a goth |
|---|---|---|
| Inform | Give people honest, full information. They have no say. | The council puts up a notice saying where the tank will go. |
| Consult | Ask for opinions at set points, then decide. | The council asks residents to comment on two possible sites. |
| Involve | Bring people in from the start, with many chances for input. | Residents join meetings from the first survey to the final design. |
| Collaborate | Work together to find a shared solution. | Residents and council staff work out a shared recommendation, which the council adopts. |
| Empower | People make the decision themselves. | Residents vote, and the council carries out their choice. |

No single level is always right. A safety notice only needs to inform. But a decision that changes people's daily lives, such as the site of a water tank, needs at least involvement. At Involve and Collaborate the agency still makes the final decision; collaboration works towards a shared solution, it does not hand over the decision. The EPA guide warns that informing people is not the same as persuading or managing them: at every level, the information must be honest.

**What makes engagement effective.** Drawing these ideas together with the Orangi experience below, effective community engagement usually follows the cycle in the flowchart at the start of this topic, and it keeps six practices:

1. **Start from the community's own priority**, not the outsider's. Ask before you decide.
2. **Meet people where they are**: a suitable time, place and language. The Friday meeting in the otaq is an example.
3. **Include every group affected**, especially those who are usually silent.
4. **Share information honestly**, including about money.
5. **Build local leadership**, so that the work continues when outsiders leave.
6. **Follow through and report back.** Engagement that asks and then disappears teaches people that taking part is useless.

**Social cohesion.** <Glossary term="Social cohesion">**Social cohesion**</Glossary> is the strength of the bonds that hold a society together. The OECD (2011) describes a cohesive society as one that "works towards the well-being of all its members". It fights exclusion, creates a sense of belonging, builds trust, and gives people a fair chance to improve their lives. Good engagement builds cohesion because people who plan and work together learn to trust one another across differences. Poor engagement can damage it. If a project benefits only one biradari, or a meeting is held where women cannot come, it can deepen divisions instead of healing them.

### Case studies of successful community-driven initiatives: the Orangi Pilot Project

Orangi is a large area of informal settlements, called *katchi abadis*, in Karachi. When Arif Hasan wrote about it, it had about 1.2 million people (Hasan, 2006). Sewage flowed into cesspools and open drains, and the government's programme for improving katchi abadis reached only about 1.5 per cent of them each year (Hasan, 2006). The **Orangi Pilot Project (OPP)** was set up in 1980 to find solutions that people themselves could manage, finance and build. Participatory research, in which residents helped to identify problems, found sanitation to be the most important need (Hasan, 2006).

<Figure id="fig-U4-5" src="/img/figures/gcce-400/unit-04/fig-U4-5.svg" alt="Timeline of the Orangi Pilot Project: set up in 1980; infant mortality in lanes that built sewers falling from 128 to 37 per 1,000 between 1983 and 1993; becoming four institutions in 1988; a youth mapping programme and adoption of its method by the Sindh Katchi Abadi Authority in 1994; an invitation in 2004 to join the Karachi district government's group on nalas and drains; and use of the method in 248 other locations by 2006." />

**How it worked.** The OPP divided sanitation into two parts (Hasan, 2006):

- **Internal work**, which the community did: latrines inside homes, sewers under each lane, and neighbourhood collector sewers.
- **External work**, which government should do: large trunk sewers and treatment plants.

For internal work, the steps in each lane were simple. Residents held meetings to form a lane organisation. They chose a lane manager, who applied to the OPP for help. The OPP survey team mapped the lane, prepared plans and estimates of cost, lent tools and trained residents. Then the lane manager and committee collected money from households and organised the work themselves (Hasan, 2006).

**What it achieved.** According to Hasan (2006):

- Residents built sewers in 5,479 lanes containing 98,527 houses.
- More than 95,000 houses invested about Rs 90 million. The same work by local government would have cost about Rs 630 million.
- In communities that built sanitation, infant mortality fell from 128 per 1,000 in 1983 to 37 per 1,000 in 1993.
- In 1994 the Sindh Katchi Abadi Authority decided to follow the OPP's method, and by 2006 the approach had been used in 248 other locations in Pakistan.

**A worked example: why did it succeed?** Use the six practices of effective engagement as a lens.

| Practice | Evidence from Orangi |
|---|---|
| Community's own priority | Residents identified sanitation as the main need. |
| Meet people where they are | Work was organised lane by lane, among neighbours who already knew one another. |
| Include those affected | Every household in a lane took part in deciding and paying. |
| Honest information, including money | Residents collected and spent their own money; the OPP did not handle it. |
| Build local leadership | Lane managers led the work; young people were trained in surveying and mapping from 1994. |
| Follow through, partner with government | Good maps and relations with local government engineers led to support for external sewers. |

The case also links back to Topic 4.1. The money stayed in the hands of the people who gave it, so accountability was built in. And the project shows that community development is not "the government's job alone" nor "the community's job alone". It is shared work.

**Limits.** No case is perfect. External sewers still depended on government, and Hasan (2006) notes that new local government arrangements at the time were facing serious problems. The work took years. Teachers should present Orangi as an example to learn from, not as a formula that works everywhere.

### The school as a community institution: school-community relationship and parental involvement

A school is not separate from its community. It is one of the community's most important institutions. Its pupils come from local families, its building is often used for local events, and in many villages the school's teachers are among the most educated people around. What happens in the community comes into the classroom: harvest work, floods, weddings, debts. And what happens in the classroom goes home with every child.

<Glossary term="Parental involvement">**Parental involvement**</Glossary> means parents taking part in their children's education, at home and at school. It takes several forms:

1. **Communicating.** Teachers and parents exchange news about the child, in both directions.
2. **Learning at home.** Parents ask about schoolwork, give time and a place for homework, and value reading, even if they cannot read themselves.
3. **Volunteering.** Parents help with events, repairs or walking children to school.
4. **Decision-making.** Parents sit on a school management committee or parent-teacher body.
5. **Linking with the community.** The school uses local resources, such as a health worker's talk, and serves the community, such as an adult literacy class.

**School management committees.** Many schools in Pakistan, government and private, have a **school management committee (SMC)** or a parent-teacher association. Retallick (2005) describes one successful girls' school in the Northern Areas of Pakistan. Its SMC had 15 members, mostly parents, chosen by the community; teachers could not be members. It met every month. It helped maintain the building, raised funds and paid two community teachers. Its women members visited the homes of absent pupils to find out why, and the committee checked teachers' punctuality and pupils' test results. Retallick (2005) also lists three other channels for parents and the community at that school: a parent-teacher association, a mothers' committee and a local women's organisation. The diagram below shows how these links connect a school to its community.

<Figure id="fig-U4-4" src="/img/figures/gcce-400/unit-04/fig-U4-4.svg" alt="Diagram of a school at the centre of its community, linked by two-way arrows to parents and families, the school management committee, local organisations such as health workers and community halls, and local government such as the union council and education offices. Each link shows what flows in each direction, and a dotted circle labelled the community surrounds them all." />

**Correcting a common misconception: "Parents in low-income areas are not interested in their children's schooling."** Low attendance at meetings is often taken as a lack of interest. Usually there are other reasons:

- **Time.** Daily-wage and farm work leaves no free hours during the school day.
- **Literacy.** A parent who cannot read may feel ashamed in a school, or may not understand a written notice.
- **Language.** Meetings held in Urdu or English may shut out parents who speak Sindhi, Saraiki, Balochi or another language.
- **Feeling unwelcome.** If the only reason a school ever calls a parent is to complain about the child, parents learn to stay away.
- **Gender norms.** Mothers may not be able to attend a meeting held in a place where men gather.

The Thatta meeting at the start of this topic shows the change. When the school changed the time, the place, the language and the way it invited people, forty parents came and offered real solutions. The parents had been interested all along. The school had not yet engaged them well.

**A worked example: redesigning a parents' meeting.** Imran's first meeting failed. Here is how the head teacher used the ideas in this topic.

| Feature | First meeting | Second meeting | Idea from this topic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Time | 10 a.m. on a weekday | Friday after midday prayer | Meet people where they are |
| Place | Head teacher's office | Village otaq, with a separate space for women | Inclusion |
| Language | Urdu notice sent home | Sindhi, with personal invitations by mothers from the SMC | Meet people where they are |
| Purpose | Teachers complained about absence | Teachers asked why children were absent | Start from the community's priority |
| Result | Six parents came | Forty parents came and agreed a plan | Engagement builds cohesion |

Schools are also places where young people learn civic life by taking part in it. Youniss and Levine (2009) bring together work on how schools and community organisations can engage young people in civic life. A school that works openly with parents and the community shows pupils, every day, what community engagement looks like.

## Activity: Map our school's community

Work in groups of five. About 30 minutes.

1. Choose a real school one of you knows well. Draw it in the centre of a chart paper.
2. Around it, draw every group or institution it is linked to: parents, the SMC, mosques, temples or churches, shops, health workers, the union council, NGOs, former pupils. Draw an arrow for each link and write on it what flows each way.
3. Mark any link that is weak or missing with a dotted line.
4. Choose one weak link. Using the engagement cycle (identify a need, mobilise, plan together, act, review), plan in five steps how the school could strengthen it. Make sure your plan includes at least one group that is usually left out.

Pass your chart to the next group. They write one question about your plan, using an ethical consideration from Topic 4.1, and you answer it.

## Summary

A community is a group of diverse people linked by social ties, sharing something and acting together in a place or setting; living near one another is not enough. Community development is shared work that improves conditions and builds people's own ability to solve problems. Engagement can range from informing people to letting them decide, and it is effective when it starts from the community's priority, includes everyone, shares information honestly and follows through. Done well, it builds social cohesion. The Orangi Pilot Project shows what organised residents can achieve in partnership with government. The school is itself a community institution, and parents who seem uninterested are often parents who have not yet been engaged well.
