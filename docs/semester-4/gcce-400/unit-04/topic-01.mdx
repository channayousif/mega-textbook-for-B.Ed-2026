## A real classroom situation

Rabia is a B.Ed trainee on practicum at a government girls' primary school in a village near Sukkur. The school's only hand pump has been broken for three months. Pupils bring water from home in plastic bottles, and the youngest ones often have none by noon.

At a parents' meeting, feelings run high. One father says, "Nobody listens to letters. Let us block the highway tomorrow and burn tyres until the officials come." Another parent wants to post a video on social media saying the head teacher has stolen the repair money, although nobody has any proof. The head teacher asks for a different plan: a signed application to the union council chairman, a visit by five parents, and a meeting two weeks later where the chairman must report what has been done.

Everyone in the room wants the same thing: clean water for the girls. They disagree about how to get it. Rabia wonders how to help her Class 5 pupils think about this. By the end of this topic you will have a way to judge which methods are right, and why.

## Explanation

Unit 1 showed that civic engagement means acting to improve community life or to influence public decisions. This topic asks a harder question. When we act, what makes our action right? It looks at three ideas: accountability, non-violence, and the wider ethics of civic engagement.

### Accountability

<Glossary term="Accountability">**Accountability**</Glossary> means being answerable for your duties and decisions. A person or body that is accountable must do three things when asked:

1. **Give an account.** Explain what was done, what was spent and why.
2. **Accept responsibility.** Admit what went wrong instead of blaming others.
3. **Put things right.** Correct a mistake, or face a fair consequence if they do not.

Accountability runs in two directions in civic life.

**Citizens hold those in power to account.** A union council, a school, a provincial department and a member of an assembly all hold power on behalf of the public. Citizens can ask them questions, ask for information and complain. The Constitution of Pakistan protects the tools citizens need for this. Article 19 gives freedom of speech and of the press, and Article 16 gives the right to assemble peacefully and without arms, both subject to reasonable limits set by law (Government of Pakistan, 2012). Article 25 says all citizens are equal before law, so an official owes the same answer to a poor parent as to a rich one.

**Citizens' own groups must also be accountable.** A neighbourhood committee that collects money for a repair holds power too, over other people's money and trust. In the Orangi Pilot Project in Karachi, residents of each lane chose a lane manager, and the lane committee collected the money from households and organised the work themselves. The project's office did not handle the residents' money (Hasan, 2006). Because the people who paid could see how their money was used, trust grew. You will study this project in Topic 4.2.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Accountability is only for officials."** Many people think accountability is something we demand from others. But a parents' group that collects Rs 200 from every family for a hand pump must also show receipts, explain its spending and report back. A group that demands accountability but refuses to give it loses its moral right to ask.

**A worked example: three accountability questions.** For any public problem, ask three questions. The table applies them to the broken hand pump in Rabia's school.

| Question | Answer in the hand pump case |
|---|---|
| Who is responsible for the duty? | Public offices, such as the education department and the local council, are responsible for keeping government school facilities working. The head teacher is responsible for reporting the fault. |
| To whom do they answer? | To the parents and the community they serve, and to higher offices above them. |
| How will they give an account? | The head teacher shows the dates of her earlier complaints. The chairman reports at a public meeting on a fixed date what has been done and when the pump will work. |

Notice that the head teacher's plan builds accountability in. The fixed meeting date means the chairman must give an account in front of the people he serves.

### Non-violence

<Glossary term="Non-violence">**Non-violence**</Glossary> means working for change without hurting people, without destroying property and without threats. It is a choice of method. It does not mean giving up the aim.

Non-violent action can be strong and active. It includes written applications, petitions, delegations, public meetings, peaceful and unarmed gatherings, press statements, and refusing to cooperate with an unjust order within the law. Article 16 protects peaceful assembly, but only when it is peaceful and without arms (Government of Pakistan, 2012). A gathering that turns violent loses that protection.

South Asia has a long history of non-violent civic action. One example comes from the land that is now Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. The Khudai Khidmatgar ("servants of God") movement of the Pashtuns, active from 1929 to 1948, practised non-violent resistance. The historian Sruti Bala (2013) argues that the movement drew on Islamic principles and on its own local roots. She warns against the stereotype that non-violence was strange in a culture often wrongly labelled as violent. The example shows that non-violence is not borrowed from one place or one leader. People in many cultures have chosen it.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Non-violence means doing nothing" or "non-violence is weakness."** Non-violence is not silence. The head teacher's plan in Sukkur is non-violent, yet it is firm: a signed application, a delegation, and a public meeting with a deadline. Non-violent methods often work better over time, for three reasons:

- **They protect people.** Nobody is hurt, including the pupils and passers-by.
- **They keep public support.** Neutral people are more likely to join a cause that is peaceful.
- **They keep the community together.** People who disagree today must still live and work together tomorrow.

Non-violence alone is not enough, though. Blocking a highway may hurt nobody physically. But an ambulance carrying a patient to Sukkur may be stuck behind it, and daily-wage workers may lose a day's pay. A method can be non-violent and still harm innocent people. That is why we need the wider ethical questions below.

### Ethical considerations in civic engagement

**Ethics** is about how we judge right and wrong in what we do. In civic engagement, ethics asks not only "Is our aim good?" but also "Are our methods fair to everyone they affect?" UNESCO (2015) names being "ethically responsible and engaged" as one of the qualities that citizenship education should develop. Five ethical considerations matter most, and the concept map at the start of this topic links them.

1. **Honesty.** Make only claims you have checked. Spreading a rumour that the head teacher stole money, with no proof, is dishonest. It can destroy a person's good name, and it is unfair even if the aim is good.
2. **Respect.** Treat opponents and officials with dignity. Disagree with a decision without abusing the person who made it. No hate speech against any group.
3. **Inclusion.** Ask whose voice is missing. In many villages, mothers, minority families and parents of children with disabilities are not invited to speak. A plan made without them may not meet their needs.
4. **Do no harm.** Think about people outside the dispute: patients on the road, shopkeepers, children. Choose methods that do not make innocent people pay the price.
5. **Consent and privacy.** Do not film pupils or share their names and photographs without permission. Do not publish anyone's private details.

**Correcting a common misconception: "If the aim is good, any method is acceptable."** This belief is common and dangerous. A good aim does not make a harmful method right. The methods we use also teach. When pupils see adults spread rumours or block roads to get a hand pump, they learn that these are the ways to get things done. When they see adults write, meet, wait for an answer and follow up, they learn a better lesson.

**A four-question ethical check.** Before any civic action, a group can ask:

1. **Is it true?** Have we checked every claim we will make?
2. **Is it fair?** Does everyone affected have a voice, and is anyone treated unjustly?
3. **Does it harm anyone?** Will innocent people be hurt, threatened or made to lose out?
4. **Could we explain it openly?** Would we be comfortable describing our method, and our use of any money, at a public meeting?

If any answer is "no", change the method. Keep the aim.

## Activity: Same aim, different roads

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. Your teacher gives each group one problem card, for example: "The road to the boys' high school floods every monsoon", "A factory drains dirty water into the canal used by the village", or "A Hindu family's child is refused admission to the local school."
2. In 10 minutes, plan three actions citizens could take. Write each on a separate slip of paper.
3. Swap your slips with another group. That group applies the four-question ethical check to each of your actions and writes "keep" or "change" with one reason.
4. Get your slips back. Improve any action marked "change" so that it keeps the aim but passes the check.

Each group then tells the class one action it changed and which ethical consideration made it change.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* From memory, write a one-sentence definition of accountability and name its three parts.
2. *(Remember)* Name three non-violent methods of civic action, and the article of the Constitution that protects peaceful assembly.
3. *(Understand)* A friend says, "Non-violence is for weak people who are afraid to fight." In three sentences, explain why this is mistaken.
4. *(Understand)* Explain, with an example, why a method can be non-violent and still be unethical.
5. *(Apply)* A youth group collects money to buy benches for a government primary school. Describe two things the group should do to be accountable to the families who gave money.

## Summary

Accountability means being answerable for duties and decisions: giving an account, accepting responsibility and putting things right. It applies to those in power and to citizens' own groups. Non-violence is an active choice of method that avoids harm to people and property, and it has deep roots in South Asia, including the Khudai Khidmatgar movement. But non-violence is not the whole of ethics. Honesty, respect, inclusion, avoiding harm to others, and consent also matter. A good aim never excuses a harmful method, because the methods we choose shape the community we live in and teach the children who watch us.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find out how one decision or responsibility in your practicum school is made accountable. For example: how the school reports the use of a fund, how a class monitor reports to the teacher, or how parents can raise a complaint. Write four lines: who is responsible, to whom they answer, how they give an account, and one way the process could be made more open.

## Summative task

Write 250-300 words. Choose a real civic problem in your town or village where people have been angry, such as load-shedding, a closed dispensary or a dangerous road.

1. Describe the problem and the aim citizens share.
2. Describe one method people have used or proposed that you judge to be unethical, and one ethical method that could replace it.
3. Analyse both methods using at least three of the ethical considerations from this topic, and judge which method is more likely to succeed over time and why.

*(Analyze, Evaluate)*

**Mini-rubric (10 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Problem and aim (2 marks) | Real, local and clearly stated (2) | Vague or general (1) | Absent |
| Two methods described (3 marks) | One unethical and one ethical method, both concrete (3) | Methods named but not clearly described (1-2) | None |
| Ethical analysis and judgement (5 marks) | Applies three or more considerations to both methods, and reaches a reasoned judgement about long-term success (4-5) | Applies one or two considerations, or judges without reasons (2-3); only states an opinion (1) | No analysis |

The analysis criterion rewards comparing the two methods with reasons. Listing ethical words does not meet it.
