## A real classroom situation

Asif is a B.Ed trainee on practicum at a government boys' high school in Shikarpur. On his first day with Class 9, he writes "Civics" on the board and asks what the word means. One boy answers at once: "Sir, civics is politics. It is about elections and parties." Another adds, "My father says it is only for people who want to become MNAs."

Later that week, heavy rain floods the lane behind the school. Asif watches the shopkeepers and parents on the lane meet the same evening. They collect money, hire a worker to clear the blocked drain, and send two people to the union council office with a written complaint. Nobody mentions a party or an election.

Asif wonders how to explain to his class that what happened on the lane was civics too. By the end of this topic you will be able to explain it.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What is civics? How did people come to take part in running their societies? And what does taking part look like today?

### Definition and concept of civics

**Civics** is the study of how a community or country is run, and of the rights, duties and part of the people who belong to it. The word comes from the Latin *civis*, meaning "citizen". School civics books such as *Civics Today* bring together citizenship and government for this reason: civics asks how the system works and where the ordinary person fits in it (Glencoe/McGraw-Hill, n.d.).

Civics has three parts. Dean (2005), writing about Pakistani schools, says good <Glossary term="Civic education">civic education</Glossary> needs knowledge, skills, values and dispositions, and real experience of taking part.

1. **Knowledge.** How laws are made, what a union council or a provincial assembly does, what rights the Constitution gives.
2. **Skills.** Discussing a problem calmly, finding facts, writing a complaint, working in a group, voting.
3. **Values and dispositions.** Fairness, respect for others, care for shared spaces, and the habit of acting when something is wrong.

A pupil who has memorised the names of the provinces' chief ministers has some civic knowledge. A pupil who can also explain a problem on the lane, listen to a neighbour who disagrees, and help write a complaint has learned civics more fully. Dean (2005) found that civics in many Pakistani schools was taught by rote for examinations, so pupils gained facts but not the skills and dispositions. As a teacher, you can change that.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Civics is the same subject as politics."** The two overlap, but they are not the same.

- **Politics** is the contest over power and public decisions: parties, campaigns, elections and debates over policy.
- **Civics** is wider. It studies the whole system: laws, institutions, rights and duties, and the many ways people take part, including ways that have nothing to do with parties.

**A worked example: civics, politics or both?** Use one test. Is the action mainly a contest for power, or is it about how people live together and run shared affairs?

| Action | Contest for power? | About running shared affairs? | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| A party holds a rally before an election | Yes | Partly | Politics, and part of civic life |
| Neighbours clear a blocked drain and complain to the union council | No | Yes | Civics, not party politics |
| A teacher explains how a bill becomes law | No | Yes | Civics |
| A citizen votes in a general election | Partly | Yes | Both |

The lane in Shikarpur shows the second row. The neighbours did not seek power. They solved a shared problem and used a public office in the right way. That is civics.

### Historical evolution of civic participation

**Civic participation** means people taking part in the public life of their community. Who could take part has changed a great deal over time. The timeline at the start of this topic shows the main steps.

- **Ancient Athens (5th century BCE).** Citizens met in an assembly to debate and decide public matters, and they took turns holding office. Leydet (2023) notes that in this model a citizen was one who shares in ruling. But only free adult men counted. Women, slaves and foreigners were left out.
- **Rome.** Roman citizenship spread across a large empire. It came to mean mainly being protected by the law, rather than helping to make it (Leydet, 2023). So participation could be narrow even when citizenship was wide.
- **British India.** People in South Asia could vote only on a limited basis. The provincial elections of 1946 were held on a restricted franchise (Kamran, 2009). The **franchise** is the right to vote, and "restricted" means many adults did not have it.
- **Early Pakistan.** Provincial elections were held on the basis of adult franchise in Punjab and the North-West Frontier Province in 1951, in Sindh in 1953 and in East Bengal in 1954 (Kamran, 2009).
- **1970.** Pakistan's general elections of 1970 were the first held nationally on universal adult franchise: one person, one vote (Free and Fair Election Network [FAFEN], 2026).
- **Today.** Article 51 of the Constitution gives the vote to every citizen aged 18 or over whose name is on the electoral roll (Government of Pakistan, 2012).

**A worked example: reading the timeline.** Ask the same question at each step: who was included, and who was left out?

| Step | Included | Left out | What changed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Athens | Free adult men | Women, slaves, foreigners | Direct participation, but for a few |
| 1946 elections | Adults who met the rules of the restricted franchise | Many adults | Voting existed, but was limited |
| 1970 elections | Every adult citizen | Children under the age limit | Universal adult franchise across the country |

The pattern is clear. Civic participation widened step by step, and each step came from people demanding a share in public life. It did not arrive all at once.

### Civic engagements

**Civic engagement** is any action a person takes, alone or with others, to improve the life of their community or to influence public decisions. UNESCO (2015) describes citizenship learning as having a behavioural side: learners should act effectively and responsibly for a better community, not only know facts. The concept map below groups the main forms.

<Figure id="fig-U1-2" src="/img/figures/gcce-400/unit-01/fig-U1-2.svg" alt="Concept map with civic engagement at the centre and five linked forms, each with a Pakistani example: voting in an election, volunteering at a flood relief camp, community work such as clearing a drain, public debate at a school committee meeting, and peaceful protest for clean water under Article 16." />

1. **Voting.** Choosing representatives in local government, provincial and general elections.
2. **Volunteering.** Giving time without pay, for example at a relief camp after a flood or in a literacy class.
3. **Community work.** Neighbours solving a shared problem together, such as the drain in Shikarpur.
4. **Public debate.** Speaking at a school management committee meeting, writing to a newspaper, or joining a discussion about a local problem.
5. **Peaceful protest.** Article 16 gives every citizen the right to assemble peacefully and without arms, subject to reasonable limits set by law in the interest of public order (Government of Pakistan, 2012).

**Correcting a common misconception: "Civic duty means voting and nothing else."** Voting matters, but a general election comes only once in about five years. A citizen who votes and then does nothing else has used one form out of five. Most civic engagement happens between elections, in streets, schools, mosques, temples, churches and community halls.

**A worked example: classifying a week.** Shazia, a teacher in Hyderabad, did these things in one week.

| What Shazia did | Form of civic engagement |
|---|---|
| Spoke at her school's parents' meeting about broken washrooms | Public debate |
| Taught two evening literacy classes for women without pay | Volunteering |
| Helped neighbours plant trees along their street | Community work |

She did not vote that week, because there was no election. She was still an engaged citizen.

## Activity: Civic engagement on our street

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. Each member thinks of one real action they have seen in their own neighbourhood, village or mohalla in the last year that helped the community or tried to change a public decision.
2. Take turns to describe your action in two sentences. The group decides which of the five forms it belongs to, and says why. If it fits none, say so; that is a finding too.
3. Together, find one form of engagement your group did not see at all. Discuss why it might be missing in your area.
4. Choose one problem your group knows about, such as a broken hand pump or a lane without lights. Plan, in three steps, how citizens could act on it using at least two forms.

Share your plan with another group. They must name the forms you used, without looking at your notes.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Without looking back, write a one-sentence definition of civics and name its three parts.
2. *(Remember)* List the five forms of civic engagement in this topic, with one example of each.
3. *(Understand)* A classmate says, "Civics and politics are the same thing." In three sentences, explain what is wrong with this statement.
4. *(Apply)* Bilal helps his neighbours collect money to repair the boundary wall of the village girls' school, and then goes with them to meet the district education officer. Name the forms of civic engagement in his actions and explain your answer.

## Summary

Civics is the study of how a community or country is run and of the part its people play. It needs knowledge, skills and values, not only memorised facts. It overlaps with politics but is wider, because it includes all the ways people run shared affairs. Civic participation has widened over time, from a small group of free men in Athens to universal adult franchise in Pakistan in 1970. Civic engagement takes many forms, and voting is only one of them.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher how the school involves parents and the community, for example through a school management committee, parents' meetings or a cleanliness drive. Write three lines: what happens, which form of civic engagement it is, and one way pupils could take part in it.

## Summative task

Write 250-300 words. Choose one real problem in your town or village, such as a dirty water supply, a school without a boundary wall, or a lane that floods.

1. Describe the problem and who it affects.
2. Show how citizens could respond using at least three forms of civic engagement.
3. Analyse which form would be most effective for this problem and why, and explain one limit of that form.

*(Analyze)*

**Mini-rubric (10 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Problem described (2 marks) | Clear, real and local, with the people affected (2) | Vague or general (1) | Absent |
| Forms of engagement (3 marks) | Three correct forms, each applied to the problem (3) | One or two forms, or forms named without application (1-2) | None |
| Analysis of the most effective form (5 marks) | Compares forms, chooses one with reasons tied to the problem, and names a real limit (4-5) | Chooses a form with weak reasons or no limit (2-3); only states a preference (1) | No analysis |

The analysis criterion asks you to compare and give reasons. Listing more forms does not meet it.
