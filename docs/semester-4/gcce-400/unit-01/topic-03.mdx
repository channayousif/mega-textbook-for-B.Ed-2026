## A real classroom situation

Faraz is a B.Ed trainee at a government secondary school in Sukkur. After the floods of the monsoon, he asks Class 9 how people in their families helped. The answers pour in.

"My brother gave two days at the relief camp." "My aunt started a group of women in our street to cook food for flood families every evening." "My cousin shared the phone numbers of the relief camps on WhatsApp, but first he checked they were real." "My father just voted in the by-election."

A boy at the back asks, "Sir, which of these is real citizenship?" Faraz feels that all of them are, but in different ways. He needs a clear way to tell them apart, and to explain why any of it matters. By the end of this topic you will be able to explain it.

## Explanation

This topic answers two questions. What are active, participatory and digital citizenship, and how do we tell them apart? And why is citizenship a foundation of a modern society?

### Active, participatory and digital citizenship

**Active citizenship** means taking part in public life, not only holding a legal status. An active citizen keeps informed, votes, obeys just laws, helps others and acts when something is wrong, always peacefully and within the law. UNESCO (2015) describes the citizen it hopes education will produce as informed and critically literate, socially connected and respectful of diversity, and ethically responsible and engaged. Leydet (2023) links this idea to the long tradition in which citizens share in running their community.

**Participatory citizenship** is a more organised kind of active citizenship. Westheimer and Kahne (2004) studied programmes that teach citizenship and found that they aim at different kinds of citizen. Two of their kinds help here.

- The **personally responsible citizen** acts well as an individual. In their example, this citizen gives food to a food drive.
- The **participatory citizen** is an active member of community organisations and improvement efforts. In their example, this citizen helps to organise the food drive.

They also describe a third kind, the justice-oriented citizen, who asks why people are hungry and acts on the causes. Unit 5 returns to this idea.

In this course, then, active citizenship is the wide family. Participatory citizenship is the part of it where people organise, join or lead collective efforts: committees, associations, campaigns and community projects. Article 17 of the Constitution protects this, giving every citizen the right to form associations or unions, subject to reasonable limits set by law (Government of Pakistan, 2012).

<Glossary term="Digital citizenship" /> is taking part in civic life through digital tools such as phones, the internet and social media, safely, ethically and responsibly. Mattson (2017) argues that schools should prepare pupils to engage in online communities, not only to avoid online dangers. A digital citizen might use a government online complaint portal, join a responsible campaign, or check a news story before sharing it.

**Digital citizenship depends on access.** Shair et al. (2022) studied national survey data and found a sizeable digital divide among people in Pakistan. They report that infrastructure and access to technology were the leading reasons why some people could not use digital tools. So a girl in a remote village without a signal cannot be a digital citizen in the same way as a girl in Karachi. A good teacher does not judge pupils for what they cannot reach.

**How to classify an action.** Ask three questions in order.

1. Does the person act in public life, peacefully and within the law? If yes, it is **active citizenship**.
2. Does the person organise or join a collective effort or institution? If yes, it is also **participatory citizenship**.
3. Does the action happen mainly through digital tools? If yes, it is also **digital citizenship**.

Then name the kind that best describes the action, and note any overlap.

**A worked example: Faraz's class.**

| Action | Q1 public life? | Q2 organised with others? | Q3 digital? | Best described as |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Brother gives two days at the relief camp | Yes | No, he joins as an individual helper | No | Active citizenship |
| Aunt starts a street group to cook for flood families | Yes | Yes, she organises others | No | Participatory citizenship |
| Cousin checks relief camp numbers, then shares them on WhatsApp | Yes | No | Yes | Digital citizenship |
| Father votes in the by-election | Yes | No | No | Active citizenship |

Now Faraz can answer the boy at the back: all four are real citizenship. They differ in how organised the action is and in the tools used. Notice that the cousin checked the numbers first. Sharing false information in a disaster would harm people, so responsible sharing is part of digital citizenship.

**Correcting a common misconception again: "Civic duty means voting and nothing else."** In the table, only one of four actions is voting. Each of the other three helped flood families more directly. Voting is one active form among many.

### Foundation of modern society and citizenship

A **modern society** is large and mixed. Most people in a city like Karachi or Hyderabad will never meet one another. They speak different languages, follow different faiths and come from different places. Something must hold them together and let them settle disputes peacefully. Citizenship does much of this work.

The diagram below shows four things that citizenship supplies.

<Figure id="fig-U1-6" src="/img/figures/gcce-400/unit-01/fig-U1-6.svg" alt="Diagram of a building labelled modern society, standing on a base labelled citizenship and held up by four pillars: law, with the same rules for every citizen; rights, with equal rights for all citizens under Article 25; participation, with citizens choosing and questioning those who govern under Article 51; and shared identity, a sense of belonging to one political community." />

1. **Law.** Every citizen has the right to be treated in accordance with law (Article 4) and the duty to obey the Constitution and law (Article 5). The same rules apply to everyone, so strangers can trust one another.
2. **Rights.** All citizens are equal before law (Article 25). Equal rights mean a person's place does not depend on family, caste, tribe or wealth (Government of Pakistan, 2012).
3. **Participation.** Citizens aged 18 and over choose their representatives (Article 51) and can question them between elections. Power then rests on the people's consent.
4. **Shared identity.** Leydet (2023) describes citizenship as membership in a political community that gives people a shared identity. Kymlicka and Norman (2000) ask how this shared citizenship can include people of different cultures and faiths without forcing them to be the same.

Leydet (2023) also names a civic quality that diverse societies need: public reasonableness, meaning the ability to listen to others and state your own view with respect for their different experiences. A school where pupils of different backgrounds learn to disagree politely is practising this.

**A worked example: when a pillar is weak.** Think of each pillar as something that can crack.

| Weak pillar | What it looks like | What happens to society |
|---|---|---|
| Law | Rules are applied to the poor but not to the powerful | People stop trusting the system |
| Rights | One group is treated as less equal | That group feels it does not belong |
| Participation | Many adults are not on the electoral roll | Leaders do not represent everyone |
| Shared identity | People see only their own group | Disputes turn into conflict |

This is why citizenship is called a foundation. When all four pillars are strong, a large and diverse society can live together and make decisions peacefully. When one is weak, the whole building is at risk, and active citizens are the ones who repair it.

## Activity: Sort the citizens

Work in groups of three. About 25 minutes.

Your teacher gives each group eight action cards, or you can use these:

1. A college student in Larkana reports a broken hand pump to the union council.
2. A group of parents in Badin forms a committee to get a teacher posted to their village school.
3. A retired teacher in Jacobabad runs free evening classes for children in her home.
4. A youth group in Hyderabad uses social media to collect blood donors for a hospital.
5. A shopkeeper in Thatta votes in the local government election.
6. A teacher in Karachi checks a viral message about a "school closure" with the education department before sharing it.
7. Women in a Tharparkar village join together to manage a shared water tank.
8. A Class 10 pupil in Sukkur writes a letter to a newspaper about road safety near her school.

Each member takes one of the three questions from the explanation and becomes its "judge". Go through the cards. Each judge says yes or no for their question and gives a reason. Then agree, as a group, on the kind that best describes each card, and note overlaps.

Finally, choose the card that you think does most for society, and explain which pillar of the foundation it strengthens.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State the main feature of active, participatory and digital citizenship in one sentence each.
2. *(Remember)* Name the four pillars that citizenship supplies to a modern society.
3. *(Understand)* Explain, in three sentences, why digital citizenship is not equally possible for every Pakistani.
4. *(Apply)* A group of students in Nawabshah starts an online page to organise weekly clean-ups of their town park, and then meets every Sunday to do the work. Classify this action using the three questions, and say which kinds overlap.

## Summary

Active citizenship is taking part in public life, peacefully and within the law. Participatory citizenship is the organised part of it, where people form or join collective efforts. Digital citizenship is taking part through digital tools, safely and responsibly, and it depends on access, which is unequal in Pakistan. One action can be more than one kind. Citizenship is a foundation of modern society because it supplies law, equal rights, participation and a shared identity, which let people who do not know one another live and decide together.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find one example at your practicum school of each kind of citizenship: active, participatory and digital. For example, a pupil who reports a problem, a committee of pupils or parents, or a school group that shares information online. If one kind is missing, note it and suggest one safe way pupils could practise it.

## Summative task

Write 300-350 words. Classify each of the five actions below as active, participatory or digital citizenship, and justify each choice. Where an action fits more than one kind, say which kind describes it best and why.

1. A young woman in Hyderabad registers as a voter on her 18th birthday and votes.
2. Farmers near Mirpurkhas form an association to share water fairly from a canal.
3. A Karachi university student uses an online government complaint portal to report an open manhole, and follows it up until it is covered.
4. A school teacher in Shikarpur organises an online group of parents and then leads monthly meetings with them to improve attendance.
5. A man in Dadu helps an elderly neighbour fill in a form to get her identity card.

Then choose one of the five actions and explain which pillar of the foundation of modern society it strengthens most.

*(Analyze)*

**Mini-rubric (10 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Classification (4 marks) | All five classified in a defensible way (4) | Three or four defensible (2-3); one or two (1) | None correct |
| Justification and overlaps (4 marks) | Each choice justified with the features of the kind; overlaps in actions 3 and 4 explained (4) | Some justifications, or overlaps ignored (2-3); labels without reasons (1) | No justification |
| Link to the foundation (2 marks) | Chosen action linked to a pillar with a clear reason (2) | Pillar named without a reason (1) | Absent |

The justification criterion asks you to show why each action fits a kind. A correct label without a reason earns little.
