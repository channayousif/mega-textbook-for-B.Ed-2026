## A real classroom situation

Mehwish is a B.Ed trainee at a government girls' higher secondary school in Mirpurkhas. During a Class 10 lesson, a pupil named Pushpa says proudly, "My mother got her identity card last month. Now she is a citizen."

Another girl, Saima, frowns. "My grandmother has never had a card. Does that mean she is not a citizen? She has lived in our village all her life."

The class turns to Mehwish. She knows the identity card matters, but she also feels that Saima's grandmother is a Pakistani citizen. She is not sure how to explain what citizenship really is, or what it asks of people. By the end of this topic you will be able to explain it.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What is citizenship? How has the role of citizens changed? And how is citizenship related to civics?

### Definition and concept of citizenship

A <Glossary term="Citizen" /> is a full member of a state, who holds rights from it and owes duties to it. **Citizenship** is that membership and everything that comes with it. Leydet (2023) shows that citizenship has three dimensions.

1. **Legal status.** The law of the state recognises a person as a member, with civil, political and social rights. In Pakistan, citizenship is set by law, for example by birth or descent. The identity card issued by NADRA is proof of registration. It is not what makes a person a citizen.
2. **Rights and duties in action, or political agency.** The citizen is someone who can take part in public life: vote, speak, join others and hold power to account.
3. **Membership and identity.** Citizenship is also a feeling of belonging to a political community, a shared "we".

Kymlicka and Norman (2000) make a related distinction between citizenship as a legal status and citizenship as an activity. The first asks, "Are you a member?" The second asks, "What do you do as a member?"

**Rights and duties in the Constitution of Pakistan.** The Constitution gives every citizen rights and also names duties (Government of Pakistan, 2012).

| Rights (examples) | Duties (examples) |
|---|---|
| Article 4: to enjoy the protection of law and to be treated in accordance with law | Article 5(1): loyalty to the State is the basic duty of every citizen |
| Article 16: to assemble peacefully and without arms | Article 5(2): obedience to the Constitution and law is an inviolable obligation |
| Article 19: freedom of speech and expression, within limits set by law | |
| Article 25: all citizens are equal before law | |

The rights come with limits written into the same articles, and the duties are owed by every citizen equally.

**A worked example: Saima's grandmother.** Apply the three dimensions.

| Dimension | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| Legal status | Is she a member under the law? | Yes. She was born a Pakistani citizen. A card would record this; it would not create it. |
| Rights and participation | Can she use her rights fully? | Not fully. Without a card she cannot be on the electoral roll, so she cannot vote, and many services are harder to reach. |
| Membership and identity | Does she belong? | Yes. She has lived in the community all her life. |

So Saima is right that her grandmother is a citizen. Pushpa is right that the card matters, because it helps her mother use her rights. Both are part of the picture.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Citizenship is only a legal status shown by an identity card."** The card is important evidence, but citizenship is wider. It is a status, a set of rights and duties, and a way of taking part. A person can hold a card and never use their rights. A person can belong deeply and still need help to register.

**Belonging beyond the nation.** UNESCO (2015, p. 14) describes global citizenship as "a sense of belonging to a broader community and common humanity". This does not replace Pakistani citizenship, which is a legal status. It adds a wider sense of responsibility, for example towards people affected by floods or climate change in other countries.

### Changing role of citizens

The role of the citizen has not stayed the same. The table below shows four roles, roughly in the order they grew. They do not replace each other; a citizen today can play all four.

<Figure id="fig-U1-4" src="/img/figures/gcce-400/unit-01/fig-U1-4.svg" alt="Table of four roles of the citizen in time order. A subject obeys a ruler with few rights, as in British India before 1947. A voter chooses representatives, as in the 1970 general elections. A participant takes part between elections, such as a parent on a school management committee. A digital participant takes part online, such as a youth group sharing verified flood relief information. An arrow shows the roles add up over time." />

1. **Subject.** A subject obeys a ruler and has few rights and no say. Leydet (2023) notes that in the Roman model, citizenship came to mean being protected by the law rather than helping to make it. Before 1947, people in South Asia were subjects of the British Crown.
2. **Voter.** With the vote, people choose who governs them. In Pakistan, universal adult franchise came nationally in 1970 (Topic 1.1).
3. **Participant.** The citizen also takes part between elections, through associations, meetings and community work. Leydet (2023) puts the idea simply: taking part in decisions is what makes people "citizens, not subjects".
4. **Digital participant.** Many citizens now take part online as well. Topic 1.3 looks at this role.

**Rights have grown too.** Leydet (2023) describes T. H. Marshall's well-known account of how citizens gained civil rights (such as equality before law), then political rights (such as the vote), then social rights (such as education and welfare). Pakistan shows a similar widening. In 2010 the Constitution added Article 25A, which says the State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children aged five to sixteen (Government of Pakistan, 2012). Education became a right of the citizen, not only a favour from the state.

**How schools describe the citizen's role.** What a school teaches about "the good citizen" shows which role it expects. Ahmad (2004) examined Pakistan's social studies curriculum and argued that it gave much space to ideology and little to democratic values such as participation and tolerance. Dean (2005) found that teaching often aimed at memorised facts and produced passive citizens. You do not have to accept every claim in these studies. But you can check your own school's textbook and ask: does it treat pupils as future subjects, voters or participants?

### Relationship between civics and citizenship

The diagram at the start of this topic shows the relationship. **Civics is what we learn; citizenship is who we are and what we do.**

- Civics gives knowledge: how a union council works, what Article 19 allows, how to write a complaint.
- Citizenship is the status and the practice: being a member, holding rights and duties, and acting on them.
- The link runs both ways. Civics prepares people to act as citizens. Acting as a citizen, for example by attending a school committee meeting, teaches civics that no book can.

Dean (2005) makes the same point for Pakistani schools: knowledge alone does not make active citizens. Pupils also need skills, values and real experience of taking part.

**A worked example: one lesson, both sides.** A Class 7 teacher in Sukkur teaches how the union council is elected and what it does. That is civics. The class then writes a polite letter to the union council about a broken street light near the school, and two pupils deliver it with the headteacher. That is citizenship in practice. When the light is repaired, or when it is not, the pupils learn more civics: how public offices respond, and what to do next.

## Activity: Four dimensions, four cases

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

Each member takes one case card and studies it for five minutes.

1. **Card A.** A young man in Karachi turns 18, registers as a voter and votes in the local government election.
2. **Card B.** A woman in a Tharparkar village has no identity card, but she has organised the women of her village to save money for a shared water tank.
3. **Card C.** A shopkeeper in Hyderabad has an identity card, pays his taxes and obeys the law, but he never votes or attends any meeting.
4. **Card D.** A Class 10 pupil in Nawabshah is too young to vote, but she runs her school's cleanliness committee.

Each member explains their card to the group using the three dimensions of citizenship: legal status, rights and participation, and membership. The group then decides together:

- Which person uses their citizenship most fully? Give reasons.
- Which role from the table (subject, voter, participant, digital participant) fits each person best?
- What would each person need in order to use their citizenship more fully?

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the three dimensions of citizenship and give one example of each.
2. *(Remember)* Give one right and one duty of a citizen named in the Constitution of Pakistan, with the article number.
3. *(Understand)* In three sentences, explain the difference between civics and citizenship, and how each helps the other.
4. *(Apply)* A pupil says, "My uncle lives in Dubai for work, so he is not a Pakistani citizen any more." Use the dimensions of citizenship to explain to the pupil why this is not necessarily true.

## Summary

Citizenship is full membership of a state. It has three dimensions: a legal status, rights and duties used in action, and a sense of belonging. An identity card is evidence of citizenship, not the whole of it. The role of the citizen has grown from subject to voter to participant, and now digital participant, and citizens' rights have grown from civil to political to social rights. Civics is the learning that prepares people for citizenship, and the practice of citizenship deepens that learning.

## Try this at your practicum school

Look at the civics or social studies textbook used in one class at your practicum school. Find one passage about the duties or role of a citizen. Write three lines: what the passage says, which role it expects (subject, voter or participant), and one question you could ask pupils to help them think about taking part.

## Summative task

Write 250-300 words. Choose a person you know well, such as a relative or neighbour (do not use their real name).

1. Describe their citizenship using the three dimensions: legal status, rights and participation, and membership.
2. Identify which role from this topic best fits them: subject, voter, participant or digital participant.
3. Analyse what helps or stops them from using their citizenship more fully, and suggest one realistic step that would help.
