# Introduction to Civics and Citizenship - Notes for Teachers

The course guide lists no teaching strategies for this unit, but it suggests practical activities for the course. These notes offer one way to plan the three weeks and to use the community storytelling activity.

## Sequencing the three weeks

- **Week 1 (Topic 1.1).** Start from a local problem the class knows, such as a flooded lane, before giving the definition of civics. The lane makes the difference between civics and politics concrete.
- **Week 2 (Topic 1.2).** Start from the identity card question. Most trainees hold a card and assume it is the whole of citizenship, so the three dimensions land best as a surprise.
- **Week 3 (Topic 1.3).** Use the "Sort the citizens" cards early in the week, then return to them after teaching the foundation of modern society, so trainees can link actions to pillars.

## Likely misconceptions and how to handle them

- **"Civics is politics."** Ask: "Was anyone seeking power when the neighbours cleared the drain?" Let the class reach the answer themselves.
- **"Citizenship is the identity card."** A likely wrong answer is, "Without a card you are not a citizen." Ask the trainee who says it how a newborn baby can be a citizen before any card exists.
- **"Civic duty means voting only."** Ask trainees to count how many days in five years a citizen can vote. Then ask what a citizen can do on all the other days.

**One good discussion prompt.** "Is a person who obeys every law but never takes part in anything a good citizen?" Expect a split. Use it to show the difference between citizenship as a legal status and citizenship as an activity.

## Practical work: community storytelling

The guide suggests community storytelling as one practical activity for the course. It fits this unit well.

1. In pairs, trainees interview one older member of their community, with the person's consent, about how people in the area took part in public life when they were young: voting, community work, local committees or relief work.
2. Trainees ask how this has changed, and who could or could not take part then and now.
3. Each pair presents a five-minute story to the class and links it to the timeline of civic participation and the changing role of citizens.

Follow your institution's consent rules. Do not record anyone without permission, and leave out names if the person prefers. The activity is not marked against a fixed model answer; judge whether trainees link what they heard to the ideas of the unit.
