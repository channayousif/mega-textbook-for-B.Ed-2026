# Introduction to Civics and Citizenship - Assessment and Review

## Unit summary

Civics is the study of how a community or country is run, and of the rights, duties and part of the people who belong to it. It has three parts: knowledge, skills, and values and dispositions. Civics overlaps with politics, but it is wider. Politics is the contest over power, while civics includes every way people run their shared affairs, such as neighbours clearing a drain and taking a complaint to the union council.

Civic participation widened over a long time. In ancient Athens only free adult men took part. In Rome, citizenship came to mean protection by law more than a share in making it. In British India the 1946 elections were held on a restricted franchise. Pakistan held provincial elections on adult franchise from 1951, and in 1970 it held its first general elections on universal adult franchise. Today Article 51 gives the vote to every citizen aged 18 or over on the electoral roll. Civic engagement takes many forms: voting, volunteering, community work, public debate and peaceful protest. Voting is only one of them.

Citizenship is full membership of a state. It has three dimensions: a legal status, rights and duties used in action, and a sense of belonging. An identity card is evidence of citizenship, not the whole of it. The Constitution of Pakistan gives citizens rights, such as equality before law (Article 25), and names duties, such as loyalty to the State and obedience to the Constitution and law (Article 5). The citizen's role has grown from subject to voter to participant and digital participant, and rights have grown from civil to political to social rights, such as the right to education in Article 25A. Civics is what we learn and citizenship is who we are and what we do; each strengthens the other.

Active citizenship is taking part in public life, peacefully and within the law. Participatory citizenship is its organised part, where people form or join collective efforts. Digital citizenship is taking part through digital tools, safely and responsibly, and it depends on access, which is unequal in Pakistan. One action can belong to more than one kind. Citizenship is a foundation of modern society because it supplies law, equal rights, participation and a shared identity. These let people who do not know one another live and decide together.

## Summative assessment

Attempt every question before you read the final guidance section. Each MCQ has one best answer. For restricted responses, keep to the length stated. For extended responses, build an argument with evidence, not a list of definitions. All names and places in the questions are examples for practice.

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. In this unit, civics is best described as: *(Remember)*
   - A) The contest between political parties for power
   - B) The study of how a community or country is run and of its people's rights, duties and part in it
   - C) The history of the Pakistan Movement
   - D) The study of how prices and markets work
2. In ancient Athens, who could take part in the citizens' assembly? *(Remember)*
   - A) Every adult who lived in the city
   - B) Free adult men only
   - C) Landowners of any sex
   - D) Anyone chosen by the king
3. Pakistan's first general elections held nationally on universal adult franchise took place in: *(Remember)*
   - A) 1946
   - B) 1953
   - C) 1970
   - D) 1985
4. Neighbours in Thatta meet a school management committee to ask for a second teacher, and speak about the problem at the meeting. Which form of civic engagement is this mainly? *(Apply)*
   - A) Voting
   - B) Peaceful protest
   - C) Public debate
   - D) Volunteering
5. Which of these is one of the three dimensions of citizenship described in this unit? *(Remember)*
   - A) Membership in a political community that gives a shared identity
   - B) Membership of a political party
   - C) Owning land in the country
   - D) Passing a civics examination
6. Article 5 of the Constitution of Pakistan says that the basic duty of every citizen is: *(Remember)*
   - A) Voting in every election
   - B) Paying income tax
   - C) Loyalty to the State
   - D) Joining an association
7. A resident of British India before 1947 who obeyed the ruler and had no say in government is best described by which role? *(Understand)*
   - A) Participant
   - B) Subject
   - C) Voter
   - D) Digital participant
8. Westheimer and Kahne's participatory citizen, in their food drive example: *(Understand)*
   - A) Gives food to the food drive
   - B) Asks why people are hungry
   - C) Helps to organise the food drive
   - D) Writes about the food drive online
9. A pupil in Jacobabad reports a broken school water cooler through an official online complaint form and follows it up until it is fixed. Which kind of citizenship best describes this? *(Apply)*
   - A) Digital citizenship
   - B) Participatory citizenship
   - C) Citizenship as legal status only
   - D) Personally responsible citizenship without public action
10. Shair et al. (2022) found that the leading reasons for the digital divide in Pakistan were: *(Remember)*
    - A) Lack of interest in technology among young people
    - B) Infrastructure and access to technology
    - C) A national ban on social media
    - D) High literacy in rural areas

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

1. Define civics in one sentence, and name its three parts with one example of each. *(Understand)*
2. In three sentences, explain how civics differs from politics, using one Pakistani example. *(Understand)*
3. Using the timeline in Topic 1.1, explain in three or four sentences how civic participation in Pakistan widened between 1946 and 1970. *(Understand)*
4. Explain in three sentences why an identity card is evidence of citizenship but not the whole of citizenship. *(Understand)*
5. Name two rights and one duty of citizens from the Constitution of Pakistan, with article numbers, and explain in one sentence why rights and duties go together. *(Understand)*
6. Describe in four sentences how the role of the citizen changed from subject to participant, with one example of each role. *(Understand)*
7. A teacher teaches pupils how the union council works, and then the class writes to the union council about a broken street light. Analyse, in three or four sentences, which part is civics, which part is citizenship, and how each helps the other. *(Analyze)*
8. In three sentences, explain the difference between active and participatory citizenship, with a Pakistani example of each. *(Understand)*
9. A youth group in Larkana uses WhatsApp to find blood donors for a hospital, then meets to arrange transport for them. Analyse, in three or four sentences, which kinds of citizenship this shows and why they overlap. *(Analyze)*
10. Choose one of the four pillars that citizenship supplies to a modern society. In three sentences, explain what the pillar does and what happens when it is weak. *(Analyze)*

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. **Is civic duty only voting?** A school speaker in Hyderabad says, "A good citizen votes. Everything else is extra." Evaluate this claim in 300-400 words. Use the five forms of civic engagement and the history of civic participation, with Pakistani examples. *(Evaluate)*
2. **Two pupils, two views.** One pupil says, "My grandmother has no identity card, so she is not a citizen." Another says, "My uncle has an identity card, so he is a full citizen, even though he never takes part in anything." Analyse both statements in 300-400 words, using the three dimensions of citizenship and the changing role of citizens, and reach a reasoned judgement about each. *(Analyze)*
3. **Classify five citizens.** Classify each of these actions as active, participatory or digital citizenship, and justify each choice. Where an action fits more than one kind, say which kind describes it best and why. (300-400 words) *(Analyze)*
   - A nurse in Sukkur votes in the general election after reading the candidates' promises.
   - A group of mothers in Umerkot forms a committee to make sure girls get to school safely.
   - A college student in Hyderabad checks a viral message about a polio vaccine against the health department's website and posts a correction.
   - A shopkeeper in Larkana gives his time every Friday to teach children to read.
   - A young man in Khairpur starts an online group of neighbours that then meets to repair the lane lights.
4. **A foundation under strain.** Choose one of the four pillars (law, rights, participation or shared identity). In 300-400 words, judge how strong this pillar is in your own town or district, using evidence you know. Then propose two actions that citizens, including teachers and pupils, could take to strengthen it. *(Evaluate)*
5. **Plan a civics lesson that builds citizens.** Design a 40-minute lesson for Class 8 that teaches one idea from this unit and also gives pupils a real experience of active, participatory or digital citizenship. In 350-450 words, give the learning aim, the steps of the lesson, the civic action pupils will take, and explain how the lesson links civics to citizenship. *(Create)*

## Answers and marking guidance

Use this section only after attempting the questions. It explains the reasoning behind each answer, so that you can check and improve your own. Your teacher may use different marks for formal assessment.

### MCQ answer key

1. **B.** Civics studies how a community or country is run and the part its people play. A describes politics; C and D are other subjects.
2. **B.** Only free adult men took part. Women, slaves and foreigners were left out, so A is the common mistake.
3. **C.** 1970 was the first general election on universal adult franchise. 1946 was on a restricted franchise; 1953 was a provincial election in Sindh.
4. **C.** Speaking about a problem at a committee meeting is public debate. Nobody votes, protests or gives unpaid service.
5. **A.** Membership and identity is one of the three dimensions, with legal status and political agency. Party membership, land and examinations are not dimensions of citizenship.
6. **C.** Article 5(1) names loyalty to the State as the basic duty of every citizen. Voting, tax and association are not what Article 5 names.
7. **B.** A subject obeys and has no say. The other roles all involve some share in public life.
8. **C.** The participatory citizen helps to organise the drive. A is the personally responsible citizen and B the justice-oriented citizen.
9. **A.** The action uses a digital tool to take part in public life responsibly. It is not organised with others, so B does not fit best.
10. **B.** Shair et al. name ICT infrastructure and access to technology as the leading determinants. The other options are not their findings.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. **Model:** Civics is the study of how a community or country is run and of its people's rights, duties and part in it. Its parts are knowledge (what a union council does), skills (writing a complaint, discussing calmly) and values (fairness, care for shared spaces). **Marks (3):** 1 for the definition; 2 for the three parts with examples (1 for two parts, 2 for all three).
2. **Model:** Politics is the contest over power and public decisions, such as party rallies and campaigns. Civics is wider: it studies the whole system and every way people take part. Neighbours who clear a drain and complain to the union council are doing civics without any party politics. **Marks (3):** 1 for politics; 1 for civics as wider; 1 for a fitting Pakistani example.
3. **Model:** In 1946, provincial elections were held on a restricted franchise, so many adults could not vote. After independence, provincial elections were held on adult franchise in Punjab and the North-West Frontier Province in 1951, Sindh in 1953 and East Bengal in 1954. In 1970, the first general elections across the country were held on universal adult franchise, one person, one vote. So participation widened from a limited group to every adult citizen. **Marks (3):** 1 for 1946; 1 for the provincial elections; 1 for 1970 and the overall widening.
4. **Model:** Citizenship comes from the law, for example by birth, so a person is a citizen before any card is issued. The card is proof of registration and helps a person use rights such as voting. Citizenship also includes rights, duties, participation and belonging, which a card cannot show. **Marks (3):** 1 for each point.
5. **Model:** Rights: equality before law (Article 25); peaceful assembly (Article 16); freedom of speech within limits (Article 19); protection of law (Article 4). Duty: loyalty to the State and obedience to the Constitution and law (Article 5). Rights and duties go together because a society can protect everyone's rights only if every citizen also keeps the law. **Marks (4):** 1 for each right with its article (2); 1 for the duty with its article; 1 for the explanation.
6. **Model:** A subject obeys a ruler and has no say, like people in British India before 1947. A voter chooses representatives, like citizens in the 1970 elections. A participant also acts between elections, like a parent on a school management committee. Over time the citizen gained more rights and more ways to take part. **Marks (4):** 1 for each role with an example (3); 1 for the overall change.
7. **Model:** Learning how the union council works is civics, because it is knowledge of how society is run. Writing and sending the letter is citizenship in practice, because pupils use their place as members of the community. Civics prepared them to write a sensible letter. The response, or lack of one, teaches them more civics about how offices work. **Marks (4):** 1 for civics; 1 for citizenship; 1 for civics helping citizenship; 1 for practice deepening civics.
8. **Model:** Active citizenship is any peaceful, lawful action in public life, alone or with others, such as voting or volunteering at a relief camp. Participatory citizenship is the organised part of it, where people form or join collective efforts. An example is parents forming a committee to get a teacher posted to their village school. **Marks (3):** 1 for active with example; 1 for participatory with example; 1 for showing that participatory is a kind of active citizenship.
9. **Model:** Using WhatsApp to find donors is digital citizenship, because the action happens through a digital tool. Meeting to arrange transport is participatory citizenship, because the group organises a collective effort. Both are active citizenship. They overlap because the same group uses digital tools to organise collective action, so the best description is participatory citizenship carried out partly online. **Marks (4):** 1 for digital; 1 for participatory; 1 for active; 1 for explaining the overlap.
10. **Model (rights):** The rights pillar gives all citizens equal rights, such as equality before law in Article 25. It means a person's place does not depend on family, caste or wealth. When it is weak, one group is treated as less equal and stops feeling that it belongs. Accept any pillar explained this way. **Marks (3):** 1 for what the pillar does; 1 for an accurate example; 1 for the effect of weakness.

### ERQ rubrics

Each task uses four criteria worth **0-3 marks each**, totalling 12. Across criteria, 3 means accurate, developed and supported; 2 means mostly accurate with an incomplete link; 1 means a relevant point with little explanation; 0 means absent, wrong or unrelated. Plain, short sentences can earn full marks.
