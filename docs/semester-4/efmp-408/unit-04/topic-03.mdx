# Topic 4.3 - Everyday classroom techniques and feedback

## A real classroom situation

In a crowded government elementary school in Sukkur, Ms Rukhsana teaches Class 4. She has 52 pupils and one period of 40 minutes. At the end of a lesson on two-digit multiplication, she needs to know whether the class understood the carrying step before she plans the next lesson. She cannot mark 52 exercise books tonight and act on them tomorrow. So she uses two quick techniques. She gives a five-minute quiz on the board and walks between the desks while pupils work in pairs, watching who carries and who does not. She writes three names in her notebook. The next morning she knows exactly whom to help.

These everyday techniques are the backbone of classroom assessment. They are quick, they live inside the lesson, and they only work when the teacher uses the evidence to change what happens next. This topic covers quizzes, classroom observation, peer assessment, self-assessment and the feedback that makes all four worth doing.

## Explanation

### Quizzes and classroom observation

A quiz is a short, focused check on a small amount of learning. It is not a test. A test samples a wide range of content under formal conditions; a quiz samples one or two ideas and is usually low stakes. A weekly quiz, an exit ticket in the last five minutes, or a three-question check on the board all tell the teacher what the class understood today. The HEC guide reminds teachers that assessment is already happening when they ask questions, read homework or give a quiz, and that the professional skill is to do it systematically rather than by luck (HEC, 2012).

Classroom observation is the other everyday tool. The teacher watches pupils as they work and records what is seen. The HEC guide describes several simple records: a participation record that notes who contributes to a discussion, an anecdotal note on a pupil's difficulty, and a checklist of skills seen during group work (HEC, 2012). The guide also encourages teachers to teach pupils to keep some records of their own, because record-keeping takes time and pupils can carry part of it. A good observation record has three features: it focuses on a stated learning target, it uses a consistent format, and it is short enough to complete while teaching.

> **Common Misconception: Observation is not assessment because nothing is written down.**
> Observation is assessment when the teacher watches with a focus, records what is seen, and uses the record to make a decision. A mental impression that is never written down and never acted on is a feeling, not evidence. A short note with a name, a date and one observation is evidence. The test of any observation is simple: did it change what the teacher did next?

In a Class 5 mathematics lesson in Hyderabad, a teacher uses a participation record during a discussion on fractions. She draws a simple grid with the names of six pupils she wants to watch. When a pupil explains a reasoning step, she ticks the grid. After the lesson she sees that two of the six never spoke. The next day she pairs them with pupils who can model the reasoning, and she checks again. The record took two minutes to make and changed her teaching.

### Peer assessment

<Glossary term="Peer assessment">Peer assessment</Glossary> asks pupils to judge each other's work against stated criteria. It can be as simple as two pupils exchanging drafts and ticking a three-point checklist, or as formal as a group using a rubric to score another group's project. The HEC guide builds peer feedback into the course itself: student teachers exchange work for marking and feedback, then discuss what they learned from it (HEC, 2012).

Peer assessment has real benefits. It gives pupils more feedback than one teacher can give to 50 pupils. It teaches pupils to read work against criteria, which is the same skill they need for their own writing. And it can improve the quality of a first draft before the teacher sees it. But it has risks. Pupils may be too harsh or too generous. They may copy the teacher's marks without understanding the criteria. They may feel unsafe criticising a friend. For peer assessment to work, the teacher must teach it: model how to give a comment, keep the criteria short, let pupils practise on a sample answer first, and moderate the marks so that a pupil's grade does not rest on an untrained judgement.

> **Common Misconception: Peer assessment saves the teacher time.**
> Peer assessment moves some of the feedback load to pupils, but it does not remove the teacher's work. The teacher must design the criteria, model the judgement, monitor the pairs, moderate the scores and follow up the pupils who gave or received poor feedback. Done badly, peer assessment costs more time than it saves and teaches pupils that assessment is casual. Done well, it builds a classroom where feedback is normal and the teacher is not the only reader.

### Self-assessment

<Glossary term="Self-assessment">Self-assessment</Glossary> asks pupils to judge their own work against criteria. It is the skill behind independent learning: a pupil who can say "this is good because it does X, and this needs work because it misses Y" can improve without waiting for a teacher. The HEC guide treats self-evaluation as one of the main purposes of a portfolio, because selecting work and explaining the evidence teaches pupils to evaluate their own learning (HEC, 2012).

Self-assessment is a skill, not a gift. Pupils need clear success criteria, a model of how to apply them, and a classroom where an honest "I got this wrong" is safe. A popular low-resource method is a simple traffic-light or three-box checklist at the end of a task: "I can do this," "I can do this with help," "I am not sure yet." The pupil ticks a box and writes one next step. The teacher collects the checklists and plans the next lesson around the "not sure yet" boxes.

> **Common Misconception: Pupils cannot judge their own work, so self-assessment is a waste of time.**
> Pupils judge their own work badly when the criteria are vague and no one has modelled the judgement. With a short rubric, a worked example and practice, most pupils can identify whether their work meets a stated criterion. They may not be as consistent as an experienced teacher, and the teacher should still moderate. But the goal is not perfect accuracy; it is a pupil who can see the gap and take the next step without being told.

### Feedback strategies and what makes feedback usable

<Glossary term="Feedback">Feedback</Glossary> is information about a pupil's progress toward a goal. Wiggins (2012) defines it as information about progress toward reaching a goal, and Hattie and Timperley (2007) describe it as information provided by an agent, such as a teacher, a peer, the pupil themselves or an experience, about aspects of performance or understanding. Feedback is not praise and it is not criticism. Praise tells the pupil that the work is good; criticism tells the pupil that it is bad. Neither tells the pupil what to do next.

The HEC course guide sums up the research in one finding: written comments by the teacher had a greater influence on learning than grades alone, or grades and comments together. The guide lists the features of feedback that facilitates learning:

- It is related to the learning objectives of the lesson.
- It stimulates the pupil to think.
- It enables the pupil to take a constructive action.
- It is focused on the main points of the lesson.
- It is restricted in number, so the pupil is not overwhelmed.
- Pupils get time in class to respond to it.
- It is timely.

The feedback loop turns those features into a sequence. The pupil attempts a task. The teacher gathers evidence of the attempt. The teacher gives feedback that names the gap and the next step. The pupil makes a revised attempt. The loop repeats until the target is met. Feedback that only judges ("5/10", "good", "careless") stops at the judgement. Feedback that moves learning ("Your conclusion states the result, but it does not explain why the sandy soil drained fastest; add one sentence that compares the three soils") sends the pupil back to the work.

> **Common Misconception: Feedback means telling a pupil what they got wrong.**
> Telling a pupil what is wrong is only half of feedback, and often the less useful half. Usable feedback names what the pupil is trying to do, where the work stands in relation to it, and what action closes the gap. It is specific rather than general, it focuses on a few points rather than everything at once, and it gives the pupil time to act. A comment that only says "wrong" or "redo" is a verdict, not feedback.

In a Class 6 science lesson in Jacobabad, a teacher returns a worksheet on the water cycle with one comment on each pupil's page: "You have the three stages in the right order. Add what makes the water change from gas back to liquid at the third stage." The pupils then revise that one sentence. The teacher does not mark every line, because the class has already mastered the rest. The comment is focused, timely and actionable, and the pupils use it.

## Activity: Feedback clinic

**Objective:** Turn judging comments into usable feedback, and match an everyday technique to a described classroom need.  
**Grouping:** Pairs of trainee teachers.  
**Time:** 30 minutes.

### Instructions

1. Read the four feedback comments below, collected from exercise books in Sindh government schools:
   - **A:** "Good effort. 7/10."
   - **B:** "Wrong. Read the question again."
   - **C:** "Your answer is correct, but your working is untidy."
   - **D:** "You have found the two correct factors. Now explain in one sentence why the third number cannot be a factor."
2. In your pair, decide which comments move learning forward and which only judge. Rewrite each judging comment so that it names the target, the gap and the next action.
3. Choose one everyday technique (quiz, observation, peer assessment or self-assessment) that would give the teacher the evidence needed to write comment D, and explain why it fits.
4. Exchange your rewrites with another pair. Check whether each comment tells the pupil what to do next and whether it is short enough to act on.
5. Be ready to read one rewritten comment aloud and explain the change you made.

## Check your understanding

1. Explain two differences between a quiz and a test, and give one example of when a quiz is the better choice. *(Understand)*
2. Describe two ways a teacher can record classroom observation without stopping the lesson. *(Remember)*
3. A teacher asks pupils to mark each other's homework. Explain one benefit and one risk of this practice, and state how the teacher should manage the risk. *(Apply)*
4. Read this comment: "Your introduction is clear, but paragraph two lists three facts without linking them to your question. Rewrite paragraph two so each fact answers the question." Analyse why this is usable feedback, naming at least three features from the lesson. *(Analyze)*
5. Explain why feedback that only tells a pupil they are wrong is not enough to move learning forward. *(Understand)*

## Summary

Quizzes and classroom observation are everyday tools that give the teacher fast evidence during the lesson. Peer assessment and self-assessment move some of the assessment load to pupils and build their judgement, but only when the teacher teaches the skill, keeps the criteria short and moderates the results. Feedback is usable when it is tied to the learning target, specific, focused on a few points, timely, and gives the pupil an action to take and time to take it. The feedback loop, task to evidence to feedback to revised attempt, is what turns these techniques into learning rather than marking.

## Try this at your practicum school

During your next practicum visit:

1. Ask a cooperating teacher which everyday assessment techniques they use: quizzes, observation records, peer assessment or self-assessment.
2. If the teacher uses one, ask to see the record or the criteria, and note how the teacher uses the evidence afterwards.
3. If the teacher does not use one, try a small version yourself in a lesson you teach: a five-question quiz at the end, or a participation record for four pupils.
4. Write one paragraph on what the evidence told you and how you changed your teaching the next day.

## Summative task

Plan a single 40-minute lesson segment in a named class and subject that uses one everyday technique and one feedback move. Your plan must include:

1. The learning target and the everyday technique you will use (quiz, observation, peer assessment or self-assessment).
2. The evidence you will collect, and the simple record you will keep.
3. One example of the feedback you will give, written as a comment a pupil could act on.
4. A short analysis (about 300 words) of how the technique and the feedback work together, and what you would do with the evidence if half the class showed the same gap.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Below Standard (1 mark) | Meets Standard (2 marks) | Exceeds Standard (3 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Choice and design of technique** | The technique does not match the target or the class size. | The technique matches the target and the evidence and record are workable. | The technique is well chosen, the record is simple enough to use while teaching, and the evidence is directly usable. |
| **Feedback quality** | The comment only judges, or is too general to act on. | The comment names the target, the gap and a next action. | The comment is specific, focused, timely and gives the pupil a manageable step. |
| **Analysis of the evidence loop (Analyze)** | Describes the technique and the feedback separately. | Explains how the evidence from the technique shapes the feedback. | Analyses what the evidence can and cannot show, and justifies the response to a common gap. |
| **Feasibility** | Requires time, materials or class conditions that do not exist. | Fits a normal period and a standard classroom. | Is realistic for a crowded, low-resource classroom and protects the teacher's time. |
