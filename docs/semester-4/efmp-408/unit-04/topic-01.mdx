# Topic 4.1 - Assessing what pupils can do

## A real classroom situation

In a government girls' primary school in Sanghar, Ms Kulsoom teaches Class 5 General Science. Her pupil Saima scored 11 out of 50 on the last written test on plant structure, and Ms Kulsoom recorded her as a weak student. One afternoon, while setting up the science corner, Ms Kulsoom watched Saima show a younger pupil how to use a hand lens to compare the veins on two leaves. Saima held the lens steady, adjusted the distance, and used the words "parallel" and "net-like" correctly. Ms Kulsoom realised that the written test had captured only one narrow slice of what Saima could do. The test asked her to recall labels on a diagram. It never asked her to look, handle, compare or explain.

This situation is common in Pakistani classrooms. A written test is fair in its own way, but it cannot show practical skill, reasoning aloud, confidence in front of others, or the habit of judging one's own work. Performance-based assessment is the family of tools that ask pupils to demonstrate what they know and can do, and it is the subject of this topic.

## Explanation

### Practical tests and presentations

A <Glossary term="Performance task">performance task</Glossary> asks a pupil to do something with knowledge rather than to select an answer from options. The HEC course guide for classroom assessment defines performance assessment as assessment that requires students to demonstrate knowledge and skills, including the process by which they solve problems (HEC, 2012). A practical test is one kind of performance task. It asks the pupil to carry out a procedure with their hands or their voice: measure a table with a scale, test a leaf for starch, read a passage aloud, or demonstrate a drill. A presentation asks the pupil to organise ideas and communicate them to an audience, for example a short speech on a national day or an explanation of a science model.

Written tests and performance tasks are not rivals. They answer different questions. A written test can sample many facts quickly and score them reliably. A performance task can show things a written test cannot:

- **Process and procedure:** whether the pupil can carry out the steps in the right order, not merely name them.
- **Reasoning aloud:** how the pupil thinks when faced with an unexpected result, which a multiple-choice option hides.
- **Practical handling:** steadiness with apparatus, care with materials, safety habits.
- **Communication:** whether the pupil can explain an idea clearly to another person.
- **Confidence and participation:** whether the pupil volunteers, persists, and works with others.

In a Class 6 school in Khairpur, a science teacher assesses the practical "test for starch in a leaf" with a simple checklist: boil the leaf, decolourise it, add iodine, record the colour change. A pupil who writes "iodine turns blue-black" on paper may still fail to carry out the procedure. The practical test shows the teacher which pupils can actually do the science, and it gives the others a chance to learn by doing.

> **Common Misconception: A practical test is just an activity, so it is not a real assessment.**
> Many beginning teachers treat practical work as a break from real learning and write no record of it. A practical test becomes assessment the moment it has three things: a clear learning target, a visible piece of evidence, and a stated criterion for success. Without those three things it is an activity. With them, the teacher can make a defensible judgement about what the pupil can do.

### Portfolio assessment

A <Glossary term="Portfolio">portfolio</Glossary> is a purposeful collection of a pupil's work that tells a story of effort, progress or achievement over time. The HEC guide describes a portfolio as an assessment tool and, at the same time, a teaching tool, because making a portfolio teaches the pupil to evaluate their own work (HEC, 2012). A portfolio is not a random pile of worksheets. It is built around a purpose, and the purpose decides what goes in.

The guide names several types of portfolio, each with a different purpose:

- **Project portfolio:** the work produced for one project, from plan to finished product.
- **Growth portfolio:** a selection of work from different times that shows improvement, usually chosen by the pupil with the teacher.
- **Achievement portfolio:** work that shows what the pupil can do at a point in time, often against a standard.
- **Competence portfolio:** work that demonstrates competence against professional or curricular standards, common in teacher education.
- **Celebration portfolio:** a small collection of the pupil's best or favourite pieces, often shared with parents.

A portfolio has a structure. The teacher decides the purpose and audience first, then the kinds of entries, then who selects them, then the criteria for judging them. Pupils write a short reflection on each entry, explaining what it shows and what they would do differently. A portfolio conference, a short conversation between pupil and teacher, lets the pupil talk through the evidence.

Assessing a portfolio is rewarding but demanding. Portfolios are excellent for teaching and for formative assessment, because selecting and reflecting on work uses the higher-order skills of analysis and evaluation. They are difficult to use for summative decisions, because two teachers may score the same portfolio differently. The guide's advice is to make the criteria explicit and to keep the portfolio focused, so that scoring becomes as consistent as it can be.

> **Common Misconception: A portfolio is simply a folder of the pupil's best work.**
> A folder of best work is a celebration portfolio, which is only one type. A growth portfolio contains early and late work so the pupil can see change. A competence portfolio contains evidence against stated standards. In every case the entries are selected for a purpose, accompanied by reflection, and judged against criteria. A folder with no purpose, no reflection and no criteria is not an assessment.

In a Class 6 Urdu class in Sukkur, a teacher keeps a growth portfolio for each pupil. Over one term, each pupil selects three pieces of writing: one from week 2, one from week 6 and one from week 10, and writes a short reflection on each. At the end of the term, pupil and teacher read the three reflections together and agree on one target for the next term. The portfolio shows progress that a single terminal test would have hidden.

### Projects as assessment

A project is an extended performance task that runs over days or weeks. It usually asks the pupil to research a question, plan a product, and present the result. A project differs from a practical test in duration and scope: a practical test takes one period and targets one skill, while a project takes several sessions and integrates several skills. A project differs from a portfolio in that a portfolio collects work from many tasks over time, while a project is one sustained task with a single product.

A project is assessed on both process and product. The product may be a poster, a model, a report or a presentation. The process evidence may be a plan, a diary of decisions, an interview notes sheet, or a record of feedback received. Because a project is long, the teacher can build in milestones, so that pupils are not left to discover a problem the night before submission.

#### Worked example: a short performance task and its evidence

Consider a Class 5 General Science task in a government primary school in Larkana: "Find out which of three soils holds the most water."

- **Target:** the pupil can follow a fair test and record results in a simple table.
- **Task:** the pupil takes three equal amounts of soil, adds the same volume of water to each, and measures what drains through.
- **Evidence:** the completed table, the pupil's drawing of the setup, and a two-sentence conclusion.
- **Criteria:** same volume of water used for each soil; results recorded in a table; conclusion states which soil held the most water and why.

The teacher scores the work with a short rubric, then returns it with one comment that names the next step. The pupil then repeats the test with a fourth soil. The task, the evidence and the criteria together make this an assessment, not merely an activity.

## Activity: Design a performance task and a portfolio entry

**Objective:** Design a short performance task with clear evidence and criteria, and select one portfolio entry that would show the same learning over time.  
**Grouping:** Small groups of 3 to 4 trainee teachers.  
**Time:** 35 minutes.

### Instructions

1. Choose one topic from Class 4 to Class 7 in a Pakistani school: General Science, Mathematics, English, Urdu or Social Studies.
2. In your group, design a performance task that takes no more than one 40-minute period. Write down:
   - The learning target in one sentence.
   - The task instructions the pupils will receive.
   - The evidence the pupil will produce.
   - Three criteria you will use to judge the evidence.
3. Now design one portfolio entry that would show the same learning across a term. Write down:
   - The type of portfolio and its purpose.
   - The entry the pupil would include.
   - The reflection question the pupil would answer.
4. Exchange your task and portfolio entry with another group. Check whether the criteria are observable, whether the evidence matches the target, and whether the reflection asks the pupil to judge their own work.
5. Revise your design where the other group found a gap, and be ready to explain one change you made.

## Check your understanding

1. Define a performance task in one sentence, and explain the difference between a practical test and a presentation. *(Understand)*
2. A Class 6 teacher asks pupils to write the steps of a science experiment from memory. Explain what this written task cannot show about practical skill, and describe one performance task that would show it. *(Apply)*
3. List three types of portfolio and the purpose each one serves. *(Remember)*
4. Read this description: "A teacher collects every worksheet a pupil completes during the term and files them in a folder, then gives the folder a mark out of ten." Analyse why this is not a portfolio assessment, naming at least two missing features. *(Analyze)*

## Summary

Performance-based assessment asks pupils to demonstrate knowledge and skill, including the process they use to solve a problem. Practical tests and presentations show process, reasoning, practical handling and communication that a written test cannot. A portfolio is a purposeful collection of work that tells a story of effort, progress or achievement, and it teaches pupils to evaluate their own work. A project is an extended task assessed on both process and product. All three become assessment only when they have a clear target, visible evidence and stated criteria.

- [ ] I can name three types of portfolio and the purpose each serves.
- [ ] I can design a short performance task with a target, evidence and observable criteria.
- [ ] I can explain why a folder of worksheets is not automatically a portfolio assessment.
- [ ] I can distinguish a project from a practical test and from a portfolio.

## Try this at your practicum school

During your next practicum visit:

1. Ask a cooperating teacher whether they use any practical test, presentation, portfolio or project in the classes you observe.
2. If one is used, collect a copy of the task sheet or the pupil's work (with the teacher's permission) and note the target, the evidence and the criteria the teacher used.
3. If none is used, ask the teacher what makes it difficult to assess practical work in that school, and write one paragraph on how a simple checklist might help.
4. Bring your notes to your next B.Ed class and compare them with a peer who visited a different school.

## Summative task

Design a performance assessment pack for a named class and subject (for example, Class 5 General Science or Class 6 English). Your pack must include:

1. A short performance task with a target, instructions, the evidence pupils will produce and three observable criteria.
2. One portfolio entry that would show the same learning across a term, with its type, purpose and a reflection question.
3. A short analysis (about 300 words) of how the task and the portfolio entry complement each other, and why neither alone would give a full picture of the pupil's learning.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Below Standard (1 mark) | Meets Standard (2 marks) | Exceeds Standard (3 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Task design** | The target is vague, or the evidence does not match what the task asks. | The target is observable, the instructions are clear and the evidence matches the target. | The target, instructions and evidence are tightly aligned and the criteria are genuinely observable. |
| **Portfolio design** | The entry is a worksheet with no purpose or reflection. | The entry has a stated type, purpose and a reflection question. | The entry and reflection make the pupil's progress visible and the purpose drives the selection. |
| **Analysis of complementary evidence (Analyze)** | Describes the two tools separately without explaining how they complement each other. | Explains how the task captures a single performance and the portfolio captures progress over time. | Analyses precisely what each tool can and cannot show, and justifies using both with reference to the target. |
| **Feasibility in a Pakistani classroom** | Requires materials or time a government school does not have. | Uses low-cost materials and fits a normal period or week. | Is realistic for a crowded, low-resource classroom and includes a simple way to record evidence. |
