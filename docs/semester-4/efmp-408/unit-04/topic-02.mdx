# Topic 4.2 - Rubrics

## A real classroom situation

At a government boys' high school in Larkana, Mr Jameel asks his Class 7 English class to write a short report titled "Save our canal." He collects 34 reports and asks a colleague, Mr Shah, to mark a sample of them. Mr Jameel marks one report at 18 out of 25. Mr Shah marks the same report at 12 out of 25. When they compare, they find they were looking for different things. Mr Jameel rewarded a clear structure and a strong opening. Mr Shah penalised weak grammar and missing facts. Both were marking the same report against a private idea of quality, and neither could show the pupil exactly where the work stood or how to improve it.

This is the problem a rubric solves. A rubric makes the criteria public before the work begins, so the pupil knows what counts, the teacher scores against a shared description, and two teachers are more likely to agree.

## Explanation

### Analytic rubrics

A <Glossary term="Rubric">rubric</Glossary> is a set of criteria and performance levels with descriptions for each level. An <Glossary term="Analytic rubric">analytic rubric</Glossary> breaks the work into separate criteria and scores each one on its own scale, then adds the scores together. For a Class 6 Urdu composition, the criteria might be content, organisation, language and presentation. Each criterion has three or four levels, and each level has a descriptor in plain language: what a pupil at that level actually does.

A short worked example makes the structure clear. The task is a Class 6 English paragraph, "My favourite festival," marked out of 12.

| Criterion | Level 1 | Level 2 | Level 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Content** (4 marks) | One or two ideas, not developed | Several ideas, some developed | Clear ideas, well developed with detail |
| **Organisation** (3 marks) | Ideas in no clear order | Some order, weak beginning or end | Clear beginning, middle and end |
| **Language** (3 marks) | Many errors that block meaning | Some errors, meaning still clear | Errors rare, meaning clear |
| **Presentation** (2 marks) | Untidy, hard to read | Mostly tidy | Neat and well laid out |

The pupil who writes three clear ideas in a muddled order, with some grammar errors but a neat page, might score 3 for content, 1 for organisation, 2 for language and 2 for presentation, giving 8 out of 12. The teacher can now say precisely: "Your ideas are strong. Your order needs work." That is the central strength of analytic scoring. It gives detailed feedback, it shows the pupil's strengths and gaps, and it makes two teachers more likely to agree.

> **Common Misconception: A rubric is just another name for a mark scheme.**
> A mark scheme belongs to one task and one expected answer. It lists the points the expected answer contains and the marks each point earns. A rubric describes levels of quality for criteria that can apply to many tasks, and it can be shared with pupils before they start. A mark scheme tells a teacher what to reward. A rubric tells everyone what good work looks like. The two can sit side by side: a test paper may have a mark scheme for its items and a rubric for its essay.

An analytic rubric also has costs. Writing one takes time. Scoring with one takes longer than a single overall judgement. And if the criteria are poorly chosen, the total can mislead: a pupil can score well on presentation and language while saying very little. For that reason, the criteria must come from the learning target, not from habit.

### Holistic rubrics

A <Glossary term="Holistic rubric">holistic rubric</Glossary> gives one overall score against a single descriptive scale. Instead of separating content, organisation and language, the teacher reads the whole piece and decides which level it matches best. The same Class 6 paragraph might have four levels:

- **Level 4:** Clear, well-organised ideas with detail; few errors; a pleasure to read.
- **Level 3:** Clear ideas and mostly sound order; some errors that do not block meaning.
- **Level 2:** Some ideas but weak order; errors that sometimes block meaning.
- **Level 1:** Few ideas and no clear order; errors that make the meaning hard to follow.

Holistic scoring is faster than analytic scoring, and it suits short tasks, quick screening and situations where the qualities of good work cannot be separated. It is also closer to how a skilled reader naturally judges a piece. Its weakness is feedback. A pupil who receives "Level 2" knows the overall standard but not which part to improve. For that reason, many teachers use a holistic score for the first read and then add one or two analytic comments on the most important criterion.

#### Worked example: the same report scored two ways

The report "Save our canal" is scored with both rubrics. The analytic rubric gives: content 3 out of 4, organisation 2 out of 3, language 1 out of 3 and presentation 1 out of 2, a total of 7 out of 12. The holistic rubric gives Level 2 out of 4. The two scores describe the same work differently. The analytic score shows that the pupil has useful content but weak language and presentation. The holistic score gives a quick overall judgement. A teacher who needs to mark 34 reports quickly may use the holistic score and then write one comment on the single most important gap. A teacher who needs to plan remedial lessons may use the analytic score to see which criterion the whole class struggled with.

> **Common Misconception: Analytic rubrics are always better than holistic rubrics.**
> The best rubric is the one that serves the purpose. For a short weekly task in a crowded class, a holistic rubric may be the honest choice. For a task where the pupil needs detailed feedback on several separate skills, an analytic rubric is better. Choosing analytic because it sounds more rigorous, when the teacher has 40 scripts and one evening, produces rubrics that are written and then ignored.

## Activity: Build and use an analytic rubric

**Objective:** Build a one-page analytic rubric for a short task and use it to score a sample of pupil work consistently.  
**Grouping:** Small groups of 3 to 4 trainee teachers.  
**Time:** 30 minutes.

### Instructions

1. Choose a short task from Class 5 to Class 8: a paragraph, a short report, a science conclusion or a book review.
2. In your group, decide the learning target and choose three criteria that follow from it. Write one descriptor for each of three levels for each criterion.
3. Swap your rubric with another group. Score the same two sample answers using the rubric you received, without discussing it with the group that wrote it.
4. Compare your scores with the scores given by another group that used the same rubric. Where the scores differ, identify the descriptor that was ambiguous and rewrite it.
5. As a whole class, discuss one question: what makes a rubric descriptor clear enough that two teachers can apply it the same way?

## Check your understanding

1. Name the three parts of a rubric row, and explain what each part does. *(Remember)*
2. Explain the difference between an analytic rubric and a holistic rubric, and give one situation where each is the better choice. *(Understand)*
3. A teacher gives every pupil the rubric before they start the task. Explain two ways this changes the assessment. *(Apply)*
4. Two teachers use the same holistic rubric and give the same essay different levels. Analyse two possible reasons for the difference and suggest how a rubric could be improved to reduce it. *(Analyze)*

## Summary

A rubric makes the criteria for good work public. An analytic rubric scores separate criteria and gives detailed feedback, at the cost of time. A holistic rubric gives one overall score quickly, but tells the pupil less about how to improve. A rubric is not a mark scheme: a mark scheme belongs to one task and one expected answer, while a rubric describes levels of quality that pupils can see in advance. The right rubric is the one that matches the purpose, the task and the time the teacher has.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can name the criterion, the levels and the descriptors as the three parts of a rubric row.
- [ ] I can explain the difference between analytic and holistic scoring and choose the right one for a described task.
- [ ] I can build a short analytic rubric with observable descriptors.
- [ ] I can explain why a rubric is not the same thing as a mark scheme.
- [ ] I can score a sample of pupil work with a rubric and give feedback that names the next step.

## Try this at your practicum school

During your next practicum visit:

1. Ask a cooperating teacher whether they use a rubric or a mark scheme for any written task. Request a copy or a description of it.
2. Note whether the criteria are shared with pupils before the task, and whether the scores are analytic or holistic.
3. If the teacher does not use a rubric, offer to draft a short analytic rubric for one upcoming task and ask for feedback on it.
4. Write a short reflection on what the teacher found useful and what they would change.

## Summative task

Build a one-page analytic rubric for a named task (for example, a Class 6 English paragraph or a Class 7 science conclusion) and use it to score two short sample answers. Your submission must include:

1. The task and its learning target.
2. The rubric: three criteria, three levels each, with a descriptor for every cell.
3. Two scored sample answers, with the score for each criterion and a one-sentence comment on each.
4. A short analysis (about 250 words) of how the analytic rubric changed the feedback you could give, compared with giving one overall mark.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Below Standard (1 mark) | Meets Standard (2 marks) | Exceeds Standard (3 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rubric construction** | Criteria do not follow from the target, or descriptors are vague. | Criteria follow from the target and descriptors are mostly observable. | Criteria and descriptors are precise, observable and clearly different from level to level. |
| **Application to samples** | Scores are inconsistent with the descriptors. | Scores match the descriptors and each criterion is marked. | Scores are consistent and the per-criterion comments show why each score was given. |
| **Analysis of feedback value (Analyze)** | States that the rubric is useful without explaining how. | Explains how analytic scoring reveals strengths and gaps that one mark hides. | Analyses the trade-off between analytic detail and marking time, and justifies the criteria chosen. |
| **Feasibility** | The rubric is too long to use in a normal lesson. | The rubric fits one page and can be used in the time available. | The rubric is concise, reusable and easy for pupils to read. |
