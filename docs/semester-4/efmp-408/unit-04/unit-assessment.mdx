# Unit 4: Performance-Based and Classroom Assessment - Unit Assessment

## Unit summary

This unit examined the assessments that ask pupils to do something rather than choose something. Performance tasks, practical tests and presentations require pupils to demonstrate knowledge and skill, including the process they use to solve a problem. They show process, reasoning, practical handling and communication that a written test cannot. A portfolio is a purposeful collection of a pupil's work that tells a story of effort, progress or achievement, and it teaches pupils to evaluate their own work. A project is an extended task assessed on both process and product.

Because performance work is open-ended, it needs a public scoring rule. A rubric makes the criteria and levels explicit. An analytic rubric scores separate criteria and gives detailed feedback; a holistic rubric gives one overall score quickly but tells the pupil less about how to improve. A rubric is not a mark scheme: a mark scheme belongs to one task and one expected answer, while a rubric describes levels of quality that pupils can see in advance.

The unit then turned to the everyday techniques that keep assessment inside the lesson. Quizzes and classroom observation give the teacher fast evidence. Peer assessment and self-assessment move some of the assessment load to pupils and build their judgement, provided the teacher teaches the skill, keeps the criteria short and moderates the results. Finally, feedback is usable when it is tied to the learning target, specific, focused, timely, and gives the pupil an action to take and time to take it. The feedback loop, task to evidence to feedback to revised attempt, turns these techniques into learning.

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. Which statement best defines a performance task? *(Understand)*  
   A. A written test with many short questions that can be marked quickly.  
   B. A task that asks a pupil to demonstrate knowledge and skill, including the process used to solve a problem.  
   C. A low-stakes quiz given at the end of every week.  
   D. A test that compares pupils against one another for ranking.

2. Which of the following is something a practical test can show that a written test usually cannot? *(Remember)*  
   A. Recall of definitions and facts.  
   B. Whether the pupil can carry out a procedure in the right order.  
   C. The pupil's ability to eliminate wrong options.  
   D. The pupil's speed at answering many items.

3. A portfolio is best defined as: *(Remember)*  
   A. A folder containing every worksheet the pupil completed during the term.  
   B. A purposeful collection of a pupil's work that tells a story of effort, progress or achievement.  
   C. A single piece of work chosen by the teacher as the pupil's best.  
   D. A record of the marks a pupil earned on weekly quizzes.

4. A project differs from a practical test mainly in its: *(Understand)*  
   A. Duration and scope, since a project runs over days or weeks and integrates several skills.  
   B. Reliance on multiple-choice items.  
   C. Requirement that pupils work alone without any plan.  
   D. Focus on recalling facts under time pressure.

5. Which of the following best describes an analytic rubric? *(Understand)*  
   A. One overall score given against a single descriptive scale.  
   B. A set of criteria scored separately, each with levels and descriptors, then added together.  
   C. A list of the points contained in the expected answer to one task.  
   D. A comment that praises the pupil's effort and gives a grade.

6. A holistic rubric is the better choice when a teacher: *(Apply)*  
   A. Needs detailed feedback on three separate skills for every pupil.  
   B. Must mark 40 short tasks quickly and the qualities of good work cannot be separated.  
   C. Wants to diagnose exactly which criterion the whole class struggled with.  
   D. Is writing a mark scheme for a single examination paper.

7. A rubric differs from a mark scheme because a rubric: *(Understand)*  
   A. Is written after the work is marked and kept private from pupils.  
   B. Describes levels of quality for criteria that can apply to many tasks and can be shared in advance.  
   C. Lists the exact answer expected for one task.  
   D. Is used only for written tests, never for performance work.

8. Which comment is the clearest example of feedback that moves learning forward? *(Apply)*  
   A. "Good effort. 7/10."  
   B. "Wrong. Read the question again."  
   C. "Your introduction is clear. Add one sentence that links your second fact to the question."  
   D. "Untidy presentation."

9. Peer assessment works best when the teacher: *(Apply)*  
   A. Lets pupils mark each other's work without criteria so that they learn independence.  
   B. Keeps the criteria short, models the judgement, monitors the pairs and moderates the marks.  
   C. Allows pupils to decide the final grade for each other without any oversight.  
   D. Uses it only for summative reports to parents.

10. A teacher watches pupils during group work and records who contributes to the discussion. This is an example of: *(Remember)*  
    A. Classroom observation with a participation record.  
    B. A norm-referenced test.  
    C. A portfolio conference.  
    D. A standardised aptitude test.

5. Explain two situations in which a holistic rubric is the better choice than an analytic rubric. *(Understand)*

7. Explain two differences between a quiz and a test. *(Understand)*
8. Explain one benefit and one risk of peer assessment, and state how the teacher should manage the risk. *(Analyze)*
9. Describe what makes self-assessment a skill that pupils can learn. *(Understand)*

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. **Designing performance evidence:** Choose a class and subject in a Pakistani school (for example, Class 5 General Science or Class 6 English).  
   - (a) Design a short performance task with a learning target, instructions, the evidence pupils will produce and three observable criteria. *(Apply)*  
   - (b) Design one portfolio entry that would show the same learning across a term, with its type, purpose and a reflection question. *(Create)*

2. **Critiquing a rubric:** A Class 6 English teacher uses this analytic rubric for a paragraph: one criterion, "Quality," with three levels: "Poor (0)", "Good (1)", "Very good (2)".  
   - (a) Analyse two weaknesses of this rubric as a scoring and feedback tool. *(Analyze)*  
   - (b) Rewrite it as a rubric with three criteria and three levels each, and explain how your version gives the pupil better feedback. *(Evaluate)*

3. **Designing feedback:** A Class 7 mathematics teacher finds that half the class used the wrong operation in a word problem.  
   - (a) Design a short follow-up lesson segment that uses one everyday technique (quiz, observation, peer assessment or self-assessment) to find out why. *(Apply)*  
   - (b) Write one example of the feedback the teacher will give, and explain how the pupil will use it. *(Create)*

4. **Designing a performance task with an analytic rubric (Blueprint Create Item):** Choose a named grade and subject, for example Class 6 General Science or Class 7 Social Studies.  
   - (a) Design a performance task with a learning target, instructions, the evidence pupils will produce and a realistic time allowance. *(Create)*  
   - (b) Build a one-page analytic rubric for the task, with three criteria, three levels each and a descriptor for every cell. *(Create)*  
   - (c) Explain how the rubric would help two teachers mark the task consistently, and how it would help a pupil improve. *(Evaluate)*

5. **Analysing a classroom assessment practice:** Read the following scenario.  
   *In a government primary school in Sanghar, Mr Atif teaches Class 5 General Science. At the end of every lesson he gives a five-minute written quiz of three questions and records the marks in his register. He returns the papers the next day but does not discuss them. Once a term, he asks pupils to put their three best science drawings in a folder, which he sends home to parents. He has never used a rubric, and he believes that pupils are not old enough to assess their own or each other's work.*  
   - (a) Identify the everyday techniques Mr Atif uses, and classify each by purpose (formative or summative) and by the kind of evidence it produces. *(Analyze)*  
   - (b) Analyse two missed opportunities in the scenario, explaining what the teacher could have done differently and why. *(Analyze)*  
   - (c) Propose one concrete change that would make his assessment more formative, and justify it with reference to the feedback loop. *(Evaluate)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### MCQ answer key

1. **B** - A performance task asks the pupil to demonstrate knowledge and skill, including the process used to solve a problem (HEC, 2012).
2. **B** - A practical test shows whether the pupil can carry out a procedure, which a written item usually cannot reveal.
3. **B** - A portfolio is a purposeful collection of work that tells a story of effort, progress or achievement, not a folder of everything.
4. **A** - A project runs over days or weeks and integrates several skills, unlike a one-period practical test.
5. **B** - An analytic rubric scores separate criteria with levels and descriptors, then adds the scores.
6. **B** - Holistic scoring suits quick marking of short tasks whose qualities cannot be separated; analytic is better for detailed feedback.
7. **B** - A rubric describes levels of quality for criteria that can apply to many tasks and can be shared before the work begins.
8. **C** - This comment names the strength, the gap and a concrete next action, which is what makes feedback usable.
9. **B** - Peer assessment needs short criteria, modelling, monitoring and moderation by the teacher.
10. **A** - Watching pupils and recording who contributes is classroom observation using a participation record.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. **Difference and examples:**  
   - *Model answer:* A practical test asks the pupil to carry out a procedure with their hands or voice, such as testing a leaf for starch or measuring a table with a scale. A presentation asks the pupil to organise ideas and communicate them to an audience, such as a Class 4 speech on a national day or an explanation of a science model.  
   - *Mark scheme (3 marks):* 1 mark for defining a practical test; 1 mark for defining a presentation; 1 mark for one valid example of each.

2. **Why the folder is not a portfolio:**  
   - *Model answer:* (1) The entries are not selected for a stated purpose; every worksheet is kept, so the folder shows volume rather than progress or achievement. (2) There is no reflection by the pupil on what each entry shows, and no criteria for judging the entries. A portfolio needs a purpose, selection, reflection and criteria.  
   - *Mark scheme (2 marks):* 1 mark for each distinct missing feature, explained.

3. **Project versus portfolio:**  
   - *Model answer:* (1) A project is one sustained task with a single product, while a portfolio collects work from many tasks over time. (2) A project is assessed on both process and product with milestones, while a portfolio is assessed on the selection, reflection and evidence of growth across entries.  
   - *Mark scheme (2 marks):* 1 mark for each difference, clearly stated.

4. **Parts of a rubric row:**  
   - *Model answer:* (1) The criterion, which names the aspect of the work being judged. (2) The levels, which describe how good the work is, such as Level 1, 2 and 3. (3) The descriptor, which says in plain language what a pupil at each level actually does.  
   - *Mark scheme (3 marks):* 1 mark for each part, with a correct explanation.

5. **When holistic is better:**  
   - *Model answer:* (1) When the teacher must mark many short tasks quickly and detailed feedback is not the priority. (2) When the qualities of good work cannot be separated, such as a short creative piece where content, structure and language are judged together.  
   - *Mark scheme (2 marks):* 1 mark for each situation, with a brief reason.

6. **Why scores differ:**  
   - *Model answer:* (1) A descriptor may be ambiguous, so the two teachers read it differently. (2) One teacher may weight a criterion more heavily, or may bring different expectations to the work. To reduce the difference, the teachers can moderate by marking a sample together and rewriting any descriptor that produced disagreement.  
   - *Mark scheme (3 marks):* 1 mark for each reason; 1 mark for a practical way to reduce the difference.

7. **Quiz versus test:**  
   - *Model answer:* (1) A quiz samples one or two ideas and is usually low stakes; a test samples a wider range of content under formal conditions. (2) A quiz is quick to give and mark, while a test is planned with a table of specifications and carries higher stakes.  
   - *Mark scheme (2 marks):* 1 mark for each difference.

8. **Peer assessment benefit and risk:**  
   - *Model answer:* Benefit: pupils receive more feedback than one teacher can give, and reading work against criteria builds their own judgement. Risk: pupils may be too harsh or too generous, or may mark without understanding the criteria. The teacher manages the risk by keeping criteria short, modelling the judgement, monitoring the pairs and moderating the marks.  
   - *Mark scheme (3 marks):* 1 mark for the benefit; 1 mark for the risk; 1 mark for the management strategy.

9. **Self-assessment as a skill:**  
   - *Model answer:* Self-assessment is a skill because pupils need clear success criteria, a model of how to apply them and practice before they can judge their own work reliably. With those supports, most pupils can tell whether their work meets a stated criterion, though the teacher still moderates.  
   - *Mark scheme (3 marks):* 1 mark for naming the criteria; 1 mark for modelling and practice; 1 mark for the teacher's moderation.

10. **Why "wrong" is not usable, and a rewrite:**  
    - *Model answer:* "Wrong" only gives a verdict. It does not name the target, the gap or the next action, so the pupil does not know what to change. A usable rewrite: "You have the first two steps in the right order. Now explain why the third step uses division rather than multiplication."  
    - *Mark scheme (3 marks):* 1 mark for explaining why a verdict is not enough; 1 mark for naming the missing features; 1 mark for a rewrite that includes the target, the gap and an action.

### ERQ rubrics

#### ERQ 1: Designing performance evidence (Total: 10 marks)

- **Criterion 1: Performance task design (Apply, 4 marks):**  
  - *Below Standard (1-2 marks):* The target is vague, or the evidence does not match the task.  
  - *Meets Standard (3 marks):* The target is observable, the instructions are clear and the evidence matches the target.  
  - *Exceeds Standard (4 marks):* The target, instructions and evidence are tightly aligned and the three criteria are genuinely observable.
- **Criterion 2: Portfolio entry (Create, 4 marks):**  
  - *Below Standard (1-2 marks):* The entry is a worksheet with no purpose or reflection.  
  - *Meets Standard (3 marks):* The entry has a stated type, purpose and a reflection question.  
  - *Exceeds Standard (4 marks):* The entry and reflection make progress visible and the purpose drives the selection.
- **Criterion 3: Feasibility (2 marks):**  
  - 2 marks for a design that uses low-cost materials and fits a normal period or week in a government school.

#### ERQ 2: Critiquing a rubric (Total: 10 marks)

- **Criterion 1: Analysis of weaknesses (Analyze, 4 marks):**  
  - *Score 0-1:* States that the rubric is poor without explaining why.  
  - *Score 2-3:* Identifies that the rubric has one vague criterion, that the levels do not describe observable behaviour, and that it gives the pupil no diagnostic feedback.  
  - *Score 4:* Explains how a single "Quality" criterion hides separate strengths and gaps, and how the descriptors fail to guide improvement.
- **Criterion 2: Improved rubric and justification (Evaluate, 4 marks):**  
  - *Below Standard (1-2 marks):* The rewrite still uses vague labels such as "good" or "very good".  
  - *Meets Standard (3 marks):* The rewrite has three criteria and three levels with observable descriptors, and the explanation says how feedback improves.  
  - *Exceeds Standard (4 marks):* The rewrite is precise and the explanation weighs the analytic detail against the time it takes to apply.
- **Criterion 3: Professional register (2 marks):**  
  - 2 marks for clear, professional English and a well-organised answer.

#### ERQ 3: Designing feedback (Total: 10 marks)

- **Criterion 1: Everyday technique (Apply, 4 marks):**  
  - *Below Standard (1-2 marks):* The technique does not match the target or the class size.  
  - *Meets Standard (3 marks):* The technique matches the target and the evidence and record are workable.  
  - *Exceeds Standard (4 marks):* The record is simple enough to use while teaching, and the evidence directly reveals the misconception.
- **Criterion 2: Feedback example and use (Create, 4 marks):**  
  - *Below Standard (1-2 marks):* The comment only judges, or is too general to act on.  
  - *Meets Standard (3 marks):* The comment names the target, the gap and a next action, and the pupil has time to act.  
  - *Exceeds Standard (4 marks):* The feedback is specific, focused and timely, and the explanation shows how the pupil will use it to revise.
- **Criterion 3: Feasibility (2 marks):**  
  - 2 marks for a plan that fits one period in a standard classroom and protects the teacher's time.

#### ERQ 4: Designing a performance task with an analytic rubric (Blueprint Create Item) (Total: 10 marks)

- **Criterion 1: Performance task design (Create, 4 marks):**  
  - *Below Standard (1-2 marks):* The task is unclear, or the evidence does not match the target.  
  - *Meets Standard (3 marks):* The task has a clear target, workable instructions, identifiable evidence and a realistic time allowance.  
  - *Exceeds Standard (4 marks):* The task is authentic to the named grade and subject, and the evidence captures both the product and the process.
- **Criterion 2: Analytic rubric quality (Create, 4 marks):**  
  - *Below Standard (1-2 marks):* The rubric has vague criteria or levels that are not observable.  
  - *Meets Standard (3 marks):* The rubric has three criteria, three levels and a descriptor for every cell.  
  - *Exceeds Standard (4 marks):* The descriptors are precise and clearly different from level to level, so two teachers could apply them consistently.
- **Criterion 3: Explanation of consistency and improvement (Evaluate, 2 marks):**  
  - 1 mark for explaining how the rubric supports consistent marking; 1 mark for explaining how it helps a pupil improve.

#### ERQ 5: Analysing a classroom assessment practice (Total: 10 marks)

- **Criterion 1: Identification and classification (Analyze, 4 marks):**  
  - *Score 0-1:* Names the techniques without classifying them.  
  - *Score 2-3:* Identifies the quiz and the folder as everyday techniques, and classifies the quiz as summative in use because the marks are recorded and never discussed.  
  - *Score 4:* Classifies each technique by purpose and by the evidence it produces, and notes that the folder is not a portfolio because it lacks purpose, selection and reflection.
- **Criterion 2: Analysis of missed opportunities (Analyze, 4 marks):**  
  - *Score 0-1:* Describes the scenario without analysing it.  
  - *Score 2-3:* Identifies that the quiz evidence is not used to adjust teaching, and that pupils are not taught to assess their own or each other's work.  
  - *Score 4:* Explains the consequences for learning and links the missed opportunities to the feedback loop.
- **Criterion 3: Proposed change and justification (Evaluate, 2 marks):**  
  - 1 mark for a concrete change; 1 mark for justifying it with reference to the feedback loop or to the research on comments and grades.
