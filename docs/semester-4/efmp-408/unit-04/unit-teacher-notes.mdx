# Unit 4: Performance-Based and Classroom Assessment - Teacher Notes

## Instructional rationale and sequencing

Unit 4 moves from the written test to the assessments that ask pupils to do something. Many prospective teachers enter the course believing that a written examination is the only real assessment, because that is what their own schooling rewarded. The pedagogical goal of the unit is to widen that view without abandoning rigour. A performance task, a portfolio or a rubric is not a softer option. It is a different instrument that needs its own discipline: a clear target, visible evidence and public criteria.

### Recommended sequencing across two weeks

1. **Week 9, Session 1:** Open with the classroom vignette from Topic 4.1. Use Figure 4.1 to compare practical tests, presentations, portfolios and projects. Ask trainee teachers which of these they experienced as school pupils.
2. **Week 9, Session 2:** Teach portfolios and projects, using Figure 4.5 to trace one pupil's portfolio across a term. Have trainees design a short performance task in pairs, then critique each other's criteria.
3. **Week 9, Session 3:** Move to Topic 4.2. Use Figure 4.2 to show the same report scored analytically and holistically, and Figure 4.6 to dissect one rubric row. Have trainees build a one-page analytic rubric and score a sample answer with it.
4. **Week 10, Session 1:** Teach quizzes and classroom observation, then peer and self-assessment. Use a participation record as a live demonstration.
5. **Week 10, Session 2:** Teach feedback. Use Figure 4.3 to walk the feedback loop, and Figure 4.7 to contrast feedback that moves learning with feedback that only judges. Have trainees rewrite real feedback comments.
6. **Week 10, Session 3:** Hold a rubric and feedback clinic. Trainees bring a task they will actually use at their practicum school and leave with a rubric and a feedback plan.

## Managing common student misconceptions

- **Misconception 1: "A rubric is just a mark scheme."**  
  *Strategy:* Keep the two side by side. A mark scheme lists the points in the expected answer to one task. A rubric describes levels of quality for criteria that apply to many tasks, and it can be shown to pupils before they start. Ask trainees to convert a mark scheme into a rubric and notice what changes.
- **Misconception 2: "Peer assessment saves the teacher time."**  
  *Strategy:* Be honest about the workload. Peer assessment needs criteria, modelling, monitoring and moderation. Done well it builds pupils' judgement and gives more feedback; done casually it costs time and teaches carelessness. Have trainees design the moderation step, not just the pair task.
- **Misconception 3: "Feedback means telling a pupil what they got wrong."**  
  *Strategy:* Contrast a verdict with a usable comment on the same piece of work. A verdict stops at the judgement; usable feedback names the target, the gap and the next action, and gives the pupil time to act. Use the feedback loop in Figure 4.3 as the organising idea.
- **Misconception 4: "Observation is not assessment because nothing is written down."**  
  *Strategy:* Give trainees a one-page participation record and make them use it while watching a short video or a peer teach. The record turns a feeling into evidence that can change the next lesson.

## Practical work and classroom exercises

The University of Sindh course guide emphasises practical and interactive learning. In addition to the textbook activities, use the following exercises:

### 1. Build a rubric from a pupil's answer
Give each pair a short, anonymous pupil answer to a Class 6 question. Ask them to decide three criteria, write three descriptors for each, then score the answer. Pairs then swap rubrics and score the same answer with a rubric they did not write. Compare the scores and rewrite the descriptors that caused disagreement.

### 2. Portfolio selection conference
Ask each trainee to bring three pieces of their own work from the B.Ed programme, as a growth portfolio. In pairs, they select one piece to keep, one to replace and one to add, and explain the evidence for each decision. This models the reflection and conference they will later use with pupils.

### 3. Feedback rewriting clinic
Collect four or five short feedback comments from local schools, or write typical ones yourself. Trainees work in pairs to turn each verdict into usable feedback, then read their rewrites aloud. The class votes on the rewrite that gives the pupil the clearest next action.

### 4. Observation record demonstration
Teach a short micro-lesson to the class, or show a recorded lesson. Give each trainee a participation record for four named pupils. Afterwards, compare records across the class and discuss why observers recorded different things, and how a shared focus makes the record more reliable.

### 5. Performance task fair test
Give groups a simple task such as "which soil holds the most water" or "which paper bridge holds the most weight." Each group must state the target, the evidence and three criteria before they begin. After the task, they score another group's evidence with their rubric and explain one strength and one gap.
