# Topic 6.4 - Assessment policy and practice in Pakistan

:::info Curriculum enrichment topic
This topic explores the broader policy and professional landscape of educational assessment in Pakistan, addressing Licensing Objective 5 and National Professional Standards for Teachers in Pakistan (NPSTP). It extends beyond the basic course outline to provide prospective educators with an understanding of national systemic frameworks and institutional assessment cultures.
:::

## A real classroom situation

During a professional development seminar in Sukkur, two primary school teachers compared their assessment routines. Ms Shazia taught Grade 4 in a rural government primary school, while Mr Bilal taught the same grade in a private English-medium school in the district headquarters.

Ms Shazia described her assessment calendar: "In our government school, our official assessment routine is strictly dictated by the provincial examination schedule. We conduct mid-term examinations in December and final annual examinations in March. Throughout the remaining months, my primary duty is completing textbook chapters so that my students can answer the standardized paper provided by the district education office. If a child fails the final paper, they repeat the class. I rarely have time or resources to record daily observations or maintain diagnostic portfolios."

Mr Bilal shared a contrasting experience: "In our private school system, we administer bi-weekly diagnostic quizzes, track reading fluency levels every month, and hold student-led parent conferences where children present their work portfolios. However, our administration also places immense marketing pressure on teachers to ensure that 90 percent of pupils receive 'A' grades, leading to grade inflation and relentless drilling for competitive entrance tests."

This dialogue illustrates the stark dualities that characterize Pakistan's assessment landscape. Prospective teachers do not operate in a vacuum; they teach within specific institutional policies, regulatory mandates, and cultural expectations. Understanding these systemic realities - and mastering the national standards designed to reform them - is essential for every transformative educator.

## Explanation

### Assessment policy and practice in Pakistani government and private schools

The landscape of educational assessment in Pakistan is highly fragmented, reflecting sharp socio-economic, linguistic, and regulatory divides between government, low-cost private, and elite private schooling sectors (HEC, 2012).

#### The public (government) school sector
In government schools across Pakistan, assessment policy is heavily centralized under provincial education departments and statutory examination boards:
- **Terminal exam-centric tradition:** Historically, educational evaluation has been dominated by high-stakes annual examinations at the end of Grades 5, 8, 9, 10, 11, and 12. These examinations predominantly feature traditional essay prompts and closed recall items directly transcribed from government textbooks.
- **Formative assessment gaps:** While national curriculum policies explicitly advocate continuous formative assessment, classroom reality in public schools is often constrained by high pupil-teacher ratios (frequently exceeding 50 students per class in primary grades), multi-grade teaching environments in rural areas, and insufficient teacher training in assessment literacy. Consequently, classroom assessment often defaults to occasional rote blackboard quizzes and homework marking.
- **Diagnostic monitoring reforms:** Through initiatives such as the Sindh Education Foundation (SEF) and provincial quality assurance bodies (PEAC in Punjab and PEAC in Sindh), standardized diagnostic assessments have increasingly been introduced to sample school-level student learning outcomes without punitive high stakes.

#### The private school sector
The private schooling sector in Pakistan encompasses a wide spectrum:
- **Low-cost private schools:** Frequently mirror the rote examination practices of government schools, often using proprietary past-paper booklets and commercial "guide books" to prepare students for public board examinations.
- **Elite and franchise school systems:** Often align their curricula with international examination systems, such as the Cambridge International Education (CIE) O-Levels and A-Levels or the International Baccalaureate (IB). These systems place heavy emphasis on structured rubrics, continuous coursework, performance-based tasks, and cognitive command words (such as "evaluate", "synthesise", and "justify"), providing rich formative feedback. However, these systems serve only a tiny affluent minority of the population, exacerbating educational inequality.

### NPSTP Standard 5: the national assessment standard for teachers

To unify and elevate teaching quality across all sectors, the Ministry of Education, in collaboration with provincial education departments and international partners, promulgated the National Professional Standards for Teachers in Pakistan (NPSTP) in 2009.

Standard 5 of the NPSTP focuses explicitly on **Assessment** (Ministry of Education, 2009; HEC, 2012). It establishes the definitive benchmark for what a qualified Pakistani teacher must know, understand, and be able to demonstrate in the classroom.

<Figure id="fig-U6-8" src="/img/figures/efmp-408/unit-06/fig-U6-8.svg" alt="Concept map illustrating the three core dimensions of NPSTP Standard 5 (Knowledge, Dispositions, Performance) linked to specific classroom teaching and assessment practices." />

Standard 5 establishes three mutually reinforcing dimensions:

#### 1. Knowledge and understanding (What teachers must know)
A qualified teacher understands:
- The historical evolution, purposes, and limitations of diverse assessment methods (formal, informal, formative, summative, diagnostic, and performance-based).
- How to design reliable assessment instruments aligned with national curriculum student learning outcomes (SLOs).
- The measurement concepts of validity, reliability, item difficulty, and discrimination.
- How to use assessment data to identify learning difficulties, misconceptions, and instructional gaps.
- Ethical, legal, and confidentiality guidelines governing assessment records.

#### 2. Dispositions (What teachers must value)
A qualified teacher values:
- Assessment as an integral, ongoing component of the teaching-learning process rather than a punitive terminal event.
- The fundamental dignity, developmental potential, and unique learning pace of every student.
- Fair, equitable, and unbiased assessment practices that welcome cultural, linguistic, and physical diversity.
- Active student involvement in self-assessment, peer feedback, and reflective goal-setting.

#### 3. Performance and skills (What teachers must be able to do)
A qualified teacher demonstrates the ability to:
- Formulate explicit, aligned assessment criteria and share them with students prior to instruction.
- Implement diverse informal and formal assessment strategies (dialogue, observation checklists, rubrics, portfolio reviews, and written tests).
- Provide constructive, timely, and specific descriptive feedback that guides student revision.
- Modify daily instructional strategies based on diagnostic evidence gathered during lessons.
- Communicate student progress accurately, sensitively, and confidentially to parents and educational stakeholders.

### Test-based versus assessment-based evaluation cultures

The ultimate challenge confronting educational reform in Pakistan is cultural: transitioning from an entrenched **test-based culture** toward an empowering **assessment-based culture** (Scheerens et al., 2003; HEC, 2012).

#### The test-based culture (The traditional paradigm)
In a test-based culture:
- Testing is an intermittent event that occurs after instruction has finished.
- The primary purpose of testing is sorting, ranking, grading, and selecting students, separating "successful" students from "failures".
- Marks are treated as punitive levers: high marks reward obedience and textbook regurgitation, while low marks punish errors.
- Learning is driven by extrinsic fear: students ask, "Will this be in the exam?"
- The teacher functions as a passive proctor and mechanical scorer of standardized answers.
- Systemic failure is blamed entirely on individual student deficiency.

#### The assessment-based culture (The developmental paradigm)
In an assessment-based culture:
- Assessment is continuous, collaborative, and embedded within every lesson.
- The primary purpose of assessment is gathering evidence to improve learning and guide instructional adaptations while learning is occurring.
- Errors and misconceptions are welcomed as valuable diagnostic evidence that reveals student thinking and directs targeted feedforward.
- Learning is driven by intrinsic curiosity, self-monitoring, and mastery motivation.
- The teacher functions as an active pedagogical researcher who diagnoses learning gaps and creates supportive learning environments.
- Systemic data informs institutional self-reflection and professional development.

By embracing NPSTP Standard 5, prospective teachers become agents of change who replace cultures of anxiety and ranking with classrooms of inquiry, equity, and continuous growth.

## Activity: Auditing school assessment cultures

**Type:** Collaborative institutional audit in pairs  
**Time:** 25 minutes  
**Context:** Prospective teachers analyze the assessment culture of a typical Pakistani secondary school.

### Instructions

1. Review the following six observable features of a secondary school's assessment practice:
   - Feature 1: The school holds monthly 100-mark written tests, and student ranks (1st, 2nd, 3rd to 45th position) are announced over the school loudspeaker during morning assembly.
   - Feature 2: When students submit science homework, the teacher places a large red tick with the word "Seen" and no written comments.
   - Feature 3: Past five-year BISE board papers are distributed at the start of the academic year, and teachers exclusively assign questions from these papers for classroom practice.
   - Feature 4: A teacher maintains an informal notebook recording which students struggled with fraction division during paired activities, and uses the first ten minutes of the next lesson for targeted remediation.
   - Feature 5: Students maintain writing portfolios containing early drafts, teacher feedback, and revised essays, self-assessing their writing growth over the semester.
   - Feature 6: Parents only receive information about their child's academic progress on the final annual report card containing percentage marks.
2. For each of the six features:
   - Classify whether it reflects a **test-based culture** or an **assessment-based culture**.
   - Identify which dimension of NPSTP Standard 5 (Knowledge, Dispositions, Performance) is supported or violated by the practice.
3. Formulate three concrete institutional reforms the school head could introduce to shift the school's overall culture from test-driven anxiety to learning-centered assessment.

## Summary

Educational assessment policy in Pakistan is characterized by structural divides between public and private sectors, historically dominated by high-stakes, exam-centric paradigms that reward textbook memorisation. To elevate classroom practice, NPSTP Standard 5 defines the national professional standard for assessment across knowledge, dispositions, and performance dimensions, requiring educators to use diverse, aligned, and ethical assessment strategies. Reforming Pakistani education demands a systemic shift from a punitive test-based culture that sorts and shames learners toward a formative, assessment-based culture that embeds diagnostic evidence, descriptive feedforward, and reflective mastery into daily classroom life.

## Try this at your practicum school

Conduct a professional inquiry into assessment culture at your practicum placement:

1. Request permission to review a school report card or student progress report. Analyze what information it communicates: does it provide only numerical marks and class ranks, or does it offer qualitative comments on specific learning competencies and strengths?
2. Observe how students react when a teacher announces a quiz or test: do you observe anxiety, fear, and memorisation cramming, or do students treat the check as a natural opportunity to gauge their understanding?
3. Ask your mentor teacher how they use daily classroom observations to adapt their lesson plans for the following day.

Write a reflective summary (300 words) evaluating whether your practicum school leans more toward a test-based or an assessment-based culture, citing specific evidence from your observations.

## Summative task

Write a policy advocacy paper (400 to 500 words) addressed to a District Education Officer in Sindh titled:

> *"Implementing NPSTP Standard 5: Moving from a Test-Based Culture to an Assessment-Based Culture in District Primary Schools."*

Your policy paper must include:
1. An evidence-based analysis of the pedagogical damage inflicted on primary school children by the current test-based culture of rote examination and public ranking.
2. A structured explanation of how NPSTP Standard 5 redefines the professional role of the teacher as an assessor of learning.
3. Three feasible, low-cost policy interventions that the district education office can implement immediately to support teachers in embedding continuous formative assessment in everyday classrooms.
