# Topic 6.2 - Ethics in assessment

## A real classroom situation

During the mid-term examinations at a boys' high school in Larkana, Mr Shahzad was appointed superintendent of an examination hall. Within the first thirty minutes, he noticed two students passing folded sheets of paper under their desks. Upon confiscating the slips, he found summaries of textbook science answers written in tiny handwriting. When he confronted the students, one of them pleaded: "Sir, please forgive us. The teacher at our evening tuition academy provided these notes and told us everyone uses them. If we fail this paper, our parents will withdraw us from school to work in the bazaar."

Later that afternoon, another ethical dilemma arose. The school principal called Mr Shahzad into his office and asked him to display the complete marks sheet, including each student's name, roll number, father's name, and subject scores, on the public gate outside the school. The principal explained: "Publicly posting the scores shames lazy students and pressures their families to ensure better study habits." Furthermore, the principal suggested adjusting the marks of several struggling students whose families were prominent local figures to maintain the school's reputation.

These incidents confront teachers with profound ethical questions that extend far beyond simple rule enforcement. Assessment decisions hold life-changing power over students, influencing their educational progression, self-worth, and career trajectories. When assessments are unfair, corrupted by cheating, or weaponized through public humiliation, the moral foundation of education collapses.

## Explanation

### Bias and fairness revisited at system scale

Assessment fairness is not merely a technical property of an examination paper; it is a fundamental human rights issue within education. An assessment is ethically fair when it provides all learners with an equitable opportunity to demonstrate what they know and can do, regardless of their gender, socio-economic background, geographic origin, mother tongue, religious affiliation, or disability status (HEC, 2012).

At a systemic scale, unfairness arises when tests introduce construct-irrelevant barriers. A construct is the specific knowledge, capability, or cognitive skill an assessment intends to measure (such as mathematical reasoning or historical understanding). A construct-irrelevant barrier is any element of the test that hinders a student from demonstrating their true ability for reasons unrelated to the construct itself.

Systemic bias manifests in three prevalent forms in Pakistani education:

1. **Linguistic bias:** In many Pakistani schools, assessments in science, social studies, and mathematics are presented in complex English or formal Urdu that differs substantially from the learner's home language (such as Sindhi, Balochi, Pashto, or Punjabi). When a Grade 5 science problem tests reading comprehension rather than scientific reasoning, the item exhibits linguistic bias.
2. **Cultural and contextual bias:** Test items frequently use scenarios, terminology, and cultural assumptions familiar only to affluent urban children (for example, scenarios referencing air travel, elevators, online shopping, or tennis courts). A rural student in Jacobabad may solve the underlying arithmetic easily but fail the question because the scenario is alien to their lived experience.
3. **Accessibility and physical barriers:** Administering identical visual paper tests to students with visual impairments, or requiring fast handwritten responses from learners with motor coordination difficulties without accommodations, violates systemic fairness.

> **Common Misconception: Fairness means treating every student identically during testing.**
> Many teachers assume that fairness requires strictly identical treatment: identical print size, identical time limits, identical seating, and no exceptions. In assessment ethics, this confuses equality with equity. If a student has severe visual impairment, providing the exact same small-print paper does not provide fairness; it guarantees exclusion. Fair assessment provides reasonable accommodations (such as enlarged text, translated glossaries for unfamiliar idiom, or extended time for medical conditions) so that all students can demonstrate their true mastery of the construct without unfair disadvantage.

### Cheating and academic integrity

Academic integrity is the commitment to honesty, trust, fairness, respect, and responsibility in all scholarly and assessment activities. Cheating and examination malpractice represent severe ethical crises across the Pakistani educational landscape, eroding public confidence in educational credentials and rewarding deceit over genuine effort (HEC, 2012).

#### Systemic causes of examination malpractice
Cheating rarely occurs in a vacuum. It flourishes within educational environments characterized by specific structural distortions:

- **Hyper-focus on high-stakes credentials:** When an entire year's worth of learning is judged solely by a single terminal percentage that determines college entry or employment, desperation drives malpractice.
- **Predictable, memorisation-heavy testing:** When examination boards recycle identical past-paper questions year after year, students are encouraged to purchase leaked keys or memorise commercially printed "guess papers" rather than understand concepts.
- **Commercial coaching cultures:** Unregulated evening tuition academies frequently market themselves by promising leaked examination papers or inside access to board invigilators.
- **Inadequate classroom feedback:** When teachers provide little formative assessment during the school year, learners reach final examinations unprepared and panicked.

#### Forms of malpractice and proportionate educational responses
Teachers must respond to academic dishonesty with professional integrity, balancing firm institutional standards with developmental justice. Punitive responses must be proportionate to the nature of the infraction and the developmental maturity of the student.

To build a culture of academic integrity, teachers must employ preventative pedagogical strategies:
- Design assessment tasks that require authentic application, personal reflection, and localized problem-solving rather than rote textbook definitions.
- Teach explicit citation conventions and explain why copying others' work without attribution constitutes intellectual dishonesty.
- Use multiple versions or randomized question sequences during classroom quizzes to minimize opportunistic copying.

### Confidentiality of assessment data

Assessment generates sensitive personal data regarding student cognitive strengths, learning difficulties, behavioral habits, and emotional vulnerabilities. Maintaining the confidentiality of assessment records is an essential legal and professional obligation under National Professional Standards for Teachers in Pakistan (NPSTP, Standard 5) and broader professional codes of conduct (HEC, 2012; NPSTP, 2009).

#### Ethical principles governing assessment records

1. **Restricted access and purposeful disclosure:** Assessment records must be accessible only to authorized educational professionals directly involved in supporting the student's learning (such as the classroom teacher, school counselor, and head teacher) and the student's legal guardians. Marks must never be disclosed to unauthorized third parties, commercial advertisers, or unrelated community members.
2. **Prohibition of public shaming:** In many traditional schools, teachers announce failing marks aloud in front of the entire class, rank students publicly from highest to lowest, or display failing test sheets on corridor notice boards. This practice causes severe psychological trauma, induces anxiety, damages learner self-efficacy, and encourages peer bullying. Ethical teachers deliver assessment feedback privately, treating diagnostic information as a confidential professional dialogue between teacher and learner.
3. **Secure storage and digital protection:** Physical assessment registers must be kept in locked staff cabinets, not left unattended on classroom desks where pupils or visitors can inspect them. As schools adopt digital record-keeping systems and spreadsheets, teachers must maintain strong password protection, ensure data backups, and never share login credentials with students or unauthorized personnel.
4. **Transparent right of review:** Students and parents hold an ethical right to review their own graded work, inspect the scoring criteria applied, and receive clear, respectful explanations of how marks were determined. Secrecy in grading criteria breeds mistrust and shields capricious marking from accountability.

When educators protect learner confidentiality, maintain rigorous academic integrity, and dismantle construct-irrelevant barriers, assessment transforms from a mechanism of fear into an instrument of equity and human development.

## Activity: Resolving assessment ethics dilemmas

**Type:** Structured controversy and case resolution in small groups  
**Time:** 30 minutes  
**Context:** Prospective teachers analyze complex ethical dilemmas arising in secondary school assessment.

### Dilemma cases

- **Case A (Linguistic fairness):** A Grade 7 science teacher in Hyderabad assesses a unit on water purification. A recently transferred student whose family speaks only Sindhi struggles with English spelling and grammar, scoring 14 out of 40 on the written English test. However, during oral laboratory work, the student correctly explained and demonstrated all three filtration stages in Sindhi. The teacher must decide whether to record the failing mark or award passing marks based on oral evidence.
- **Case B (Assessment data confidentiality):** A wealthy school board member visits a primary school and demands to inspect the continuous assessment records and diagnostic test profiles of all candidates competing for the annual scholarship award, including their socio-economic background information. The head teacher must decide how to respond.
- **Case C (Suspected plagiarism):** A secondary school student submits an outstanding history essay on the Indus Valley Civilization. The teacher discovers that several paragraphs were copied verbatim from an encyclopedia without quotation marks or citation, although the student wrote original introduction and conclusion sections.

### Group task

1. Form groups of three or four student teachers. Select one of the cases above.
2. Debate the dilemma using the following two questions:
   - What fundamental ethical principles (fairness, integrity, confidentiality, student development) are in conflict in this situation?
   - What would be an irresponsible, unprofessional response, and what would be an ethically defensible professional response?
3. Formulate a three-step action plan for the teacher or administrator in the selected case, ensuring that institutional standards and student welfare are both respected.
4. Appoint one spokesperson to present your group's resolution to the class in two minutes.

## Check your understanding

1. Define what is meant by a "construct-irrelevant barrier" in educational assessment, providing one concrete classroom example. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why publicly announcing student marks or ranking students on public notice boards violates professional assessment ethics. *(Understand)*
3. A teacher discovers that two students submitted nearly identical answers for a take-home mathematics assignment. Apply ethical principles to design a fair, multi-step investigation that balances accountability with developmental learning. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Ethics in assessment safeguards the dignity, rights, and learning opportunities of all students. Systemic fairness requires eliminating construct-irrelevant barriers, such as linguistic and cultural biases, while providing reasonable accommodations for learners with special educational needs. Academic integrity demands that educators deter examination malpractice, understand the systemic pressures that drive cheating, and apply proportionate, pedagogically sound consequences for dishonesty. Maintaining strict confidentiality of assessment data protects students from psychological harm, stigmatisation, and public humiliation, establishing assessment as a safe, professional channel for learning and growth.

## Try this at your practicum school

Observe an examination or classroom assessment administration during your practicum placement. Note the following dimensions:

1. What physical and psychological precautions did the teacher take to ensure fair, independent test-taking conditions?
2. Did any student experience language comprehension difficulties with the wording of the test items? How did the teacher assist them without giving away the answers?
3. How did the teacher handle the collection, marking, and return of the test papers? Were individual scores communicated privately, or were marks shared publicly with the entire class?

Discuss your observations with your cooperating mentor teacher, exploring what institutional policies exist in the school regarding student privacy and cheating prevention.

## Summative task

Read the following school assessment scenario and write a comprehensive professional evaluation (400 to 500 words):

> *"At City High School, the annual pre-board examination in Physics resulted in 45 percent of students failing. To boost school performance statistics before the official BISE registration deadline, the school administration instituted three measures: First, they offered private after-school 'guaranteed pass' coaching classes taught by the examination paper setter, costing Rs. 5,000 per student. Second, they expelled the bottom 15 percent of failing students from taking the board exam from the school platform, directing them to register as private candidates. Third, they posted the full list of student names, marks, and class rankings on the main campus entrance gate under the heading 'Honor Roll and Hall of Shame'."*

Analyze the ethical defensibility of each of the three administrative measures. For each measure:
- Identify the specific professional ethics standards and assessment principles that were violated.
- Explain the negative psychological and educational consequences inflicted upon students and the wider school community.
- Propose an ethically defensible, pedagogically constructive alternative policy the school should have implemented instead.

### Evaluation rubric

| Criterion | Level 1: Developing | Level 2: Proficient | Level 3: Exemplary |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Identification of ethical violations** | Mentions that the measures seem harsh without citing specific ethical principles or professional standards. | Identifies specific ethical breaches across all three measures, citing conflict of interest, exclusion, and confidentiality violations. | Thoroughly deconstructs the ethical breaches using professional standards, illustrating systemic corruption of assessment purposes. |
| **Analysis of consequences (Analyze)** | Describes student sadness or anger without analyzing systemic institutional and psychological impacts. | Analyzes clear educational harms, including commercial exploitation, institutional manipulation, and damage to self-efficacy. | Provides insightful systemic analysis of how these practices corrupt educational trust, reinforce socio-economic inequality, and traumatise vulnerable learners. |
| **Proposed ethical alternatives** | Suggests generic improvements without addressing root causes of failure. | Formulates realistic, pedagogically sound alternatives for diagnostic remediation, inclusive retention, and private feedback. | Formulates an integrated, highly actionable school improvement policy grounded in formative assessment, diagnostic support, and professional ethics. |
