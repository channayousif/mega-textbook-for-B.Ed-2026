# Topic 3.2 - Subjective items

## A real classroom situation

At a government boys' secondary school in Hyderabad, two senior language teachers, Mr Tariq and Mr Salman, agreed to double-mark a set of Class 8 English essays to verify scoring consistency. The examination prompt asked students to write a 250-word reflective essay: "Describe an environmental problem in your local neighbourhood and propose two practical actions the community can take to resolve it" (Total: 20 marks).

No written mark scheme or scoring criteria had been prepared. Both teachers simply read the scripts and assigned an overall impression score out of 20.

When they compared their marks for Bilal, a hardworking student, they were astonished. Mr Tariq had awarded Bilal 17 marks out of 20, adding a margin comment: "Excellent neat handwriting, fluent vocabulary, and error-free spelling." In contrast, Mr Salman had awarded the exact same script 9 marks out of 20, writing: "Vague and superficial; mentions litter on the street but fails to propose any realistic community action or municipal civic mechanism."

For another student, Danish, who wrote brilliant scientific arguments about sewage contamination and aquifer pollution in cramped, untidy handwriting with several spelling slips, Mr Tariq gave 10 marks while Mr Salman gave 18 marks.

This real classroom episode illustrates the profound dilemma of **subjective assessment**. While subjective items allow students to demonstrate complex reasoning, synthesis, and creative voice, unstandardised scoring introduces unacceptable levels of examiner variance. A student's grade can depend more on which teacher marks the paper than on the student's actual competence.

## Explanation

### Essay items: what they assess well

Subjective assessment items require learners to construct their own responses, ranging from brief phrases to multi-page essays, rather than selecting from pre-determined options (HEC, 2012; Seifert & Sutton, 2009). In educational measurement, essay questions are categorized into two structural formats:

1. **Extended-response essay items:** These provide students with broad freedom to select, organise, integrate, and evaluate ideas, determining for themselves the scope and structure of their response. For example: "Evaluate the social and economic impact of the construction of the Sukkur Barrage on agriculture in Sindh over the past century."
2. **Restricted-response essay items:** These deliberately constrain the nature, length, or organisation of the student's response by specifying the exact parameters to be addressed. For example: "Compare the reproductive cycle of flowering plants and non-flowering plants in terms of pollination, fertilization, and seed dispersal (limit your response to two pages)."

Essay items possess unique pedagogical value. They assess high-level cognitive capabilities that objective test formats cannot measure effectively:
- **Synthesis and coherent argumentation:** Demanding that learners marshal disparate facts and ideas into a structured, persuasive narrative.
- **Originality and creative problem solving:** Allowing students to propose novel solutions and articulate independent perspectives.
- **Deep conceptual integration:** Testing whether students understand how underlying principles interact within authentic, complex systems.
- **Written communication and academic literacy:** Revealing a learner's ability to express ideas clearly, logically, and persuasively using subject-specific discourse.

> **Common Misconception: "Essay questions are quicker and easier to set, so they save teacher time."**  
> Many teachers believe that setting an essay examination is easy because writing three broad prompts ("Discuss the Mughal administration") takes only five minutes. This is a dangerous illusion. While drafting a vague prompt is fast, marking 50 essay scripts reliably requires dozens of hours of intense cognitive labour. Furthermore, poorly constructed, open-ended essay prompts produce unstructured student answers that are virtually impossible to score fairly. A high-quality essay question requires careful prompt engineering, precise boundary constraints, and a pre-drafted analytic rubric before the test is printed.

### Advantages and limitations of subjective items

To deploy subjective questions effectively, educators must weigh their pedagogical benefits against their psychometric limitations (HEC, 2012).

#### Advantages
- **Elimination of guessing:** Students cannot guess the accurate response through chance or elimination; they must retrieve knowledge and formulate ideas independently.
- **Authentic cognitive engagement:** Mimics real-world professional tasks where adults must synthesise evidence and write reports.
- **Positive washback on study habits:** When students anticipate essay examinations, they tend to study for holistic understanding, structural relationships, and broad concepts rather than memorising disconnected flashcard trivia.

#### Limitations
- **Severely restricted curriculum sampling:** A two-hour examination can accommodate at most three or four extended essay questions. If a course covered twelve major topics, only a fraction can be tested, introducing high sampling error and testing luck.
- **High scoring time and marker fatigue:** Reading and evaluating detailed prose is exhausting; teacher scoring standards frequently drift as fatigue sets in.
- **Vulnerability to extraneous factors:** Student scores are heavily influenced by writing speed, penmanship, vocabulary flair, and linguistic fluency, even when the subject being assessed is science, history, or geography.

### Short-answer items

Short-answer items occupy a vital middle ground between long subjective essays and rigid objective questions. A short-answer question requires the student to respond with a single word, phrase, mathematical calculation, symbol, or a concise paragraph of two to three sentences (HEC, 2012).

Short-answer items typically take two forms:
- **Direct question format:** "What is the primary function of chlorophyll in plant leaves?"
- **Incomplete statement (completion) format:** "The chemical process through which plants convert solar energy into glucose is called __________."

#### Key benefits and rules of short-answer construction
1. **Broader curriculum sampling:** Because short-answer items require only two to four minutes each, a single 50-mark examination paper can include 15 to 20 distinct questions covering diverse syllabus units.
2. **Minimal guessing:** Like essays, short-answer items require pure recall and generation rather than recognition.
3. **Objective and rapid scoring:** When items are properly constrained, the range of acceptable answers is narrow, allowing quick and reliable marking.
4. **Essential design rule (single definite answer):** The prompt must be framed so that only one or two specific terms represent a correct response. Vague prompts like "Mohenjo-daro was __________" invite dozens of irrelevant responses ("ancient", "in Sindh", "discovered in 1922", "made of bricks"). A proper item specifies the exact historical attribute sought: "In which modern district of Sindh is the archaeological site of Mohenjo-daro located?"

### Scoring challenges in subjective items

The greatest psychometric challenge in subjective testing is **marker unreliability** (HEC, 2012; Seifert & Sutton, 2009). Without rigorous controls, teachers fall prey to five well-documented psychological scoring biases:

1. **The Halo Effect:** A teacher's overall impression of a student's prior academic record, classroom behaviour, or social background unconsciously colours the evaluation of their written paper. A teacher expects an essay written by the class monitor to be brilliant and overlooks flaws, while assuming an essay written by a disruptive student is weak.
2. **Mechanics and Presentation Bias:** Studies consistently show that attractive handwriting, neat margins, and sophisticated linguistic rhetoric receive substantially higher marks than identical content presented in messy, illegible script with phonetic spelling errors.
3. **Generosity and Severity Errors:** Some examiners are chronic "soft markers" who award 16 out of 20 to mediocre work, while others are chronic "hard markers" who never award above 12 marks even to masterly scripts.
4. **Order and Contrast Effects:** A mediocre paper read immediately after three exceptionally brilliant essays appears dreadful and receives depressed marks. Conversely, the same mediocre paper read after five completely illiterate scripts appears outstanding and receives inflated marks.
5. **Marker Fatigue and Drift:** As an examiner grades 80 consecutive examination booklets late into the night, cognitive fatigue sets in. Standards typically become either excessively harsh or careless, resulting in inconsistent marking between papers graded on Friday afternoon and those graded on Sunday morning.

#### Professional strategies to minimise scoring error

To eliminate these scoring distortions, professional assessment bodies (such as AKU-EB and the Sindh Higher Education Commission) enforce four mandatory marking protocols:

1. **Prepare model answers and analytic rubrics in advance:** Before administering the examination, write a comprehensive model answer and establish a point-by-point analytic scoring guide allocating specific marks for distinct conceptual components (e.g., 2 marks for stating the law, 2 marks for naming the variables, 2 marks for describing the experimental setup).
2. **Mark anonymously:** Conceal student names, roll numbers, and school affiliations behind anonymous candidate barcodes or folded cover sheets to prevent the halo effect.
3. **Mark question-by-question rather than paper-by-paper:** Mark Question 1 across all 50 students before reading Question 2. This keeps the specific marking criteria for that question fresh in the teacher's mind, neutralises order contrast effects between students, and prevents a student's performance on Question 1 from prejudicing the marking of Question 2.
4. **Double-mark a calibration sample:** In high-stakes examinations, two independent teachers should mark a random 10% sample of papers and calculate inter-rater reliability. Where marks diverge by more than 10%, a third senior moderator adjudicates.

## Activity: Developing an analytic scoring guide for an essay item

**Objective:** Design a fair, objective analytic rubric for a restricted-response essay question to achieve high marker reliability.  
**Grouping:** Pairs of trainee teachers.  
**Time:** 35 minutes.

### Instructions

1. Consider the following Class 7 General Science restricted-response essay item:  
   *"Explain how the respiratory system and the circulatory system work together to deliver oxygen to muscle cells during exercise, and identify two physiological changes that occur when breathing rate increases." (Total: 8 marks; recommended time: 12 minutes).*
2. In your pair, draft an **analytic scoring guide** that divides the 8 marks across specific, verifiable criteria:
   - What exact scientific concepts must the student state?
   - How many marks are awarded for explaining the exchange of gases in the lungs?
   - How many marks for explaining blood transport via hemoglobin?
   - How many marks for identifying the physiological changes (e.g., increased pulse, deeper lung volume)?
3. Draft explicit rules for how markers should handle spelling errors, poor grammar, and extra irrelevant information.
4. Exchange your scoring guide with another pair. Have one partner read aloud a simulated student response containing strong scientific concepts but messy handwriting and spelling slips. Score the response using the rubric: Did both pairs arrive at the exact same mark?

## Summary

Subjective assessment items - including extended essays, restricted-response prompts, and short-answer questions - provide indispensable windows into higher-order thinking, conceptual synthesis, and independent expression. However, their validity and reliability are severely compromised by restricted curriculum sampling and subjective marker biases (such as the halo effect, handwriting bias, and examiner fatigue). By formulating constrained prompts, writing model answers, deploying analytic scoring rubrics, and marking papers anonymously question-by-question, educators can preserve the cognitive richness of subjective assessment while guaranteeing fair and dependable evaluations.

## Try this at your practicum school

During your practicum teaching placement:

1. Obtain permission from your cooperating teacher to co-mark a set of 20 essay or short-answer classroom tests.
2. Select one essay question. First, read and mark five papers without a written rubric, recording your initial impression score.
3. Next, construct a detailed analytic scoring guide allocating specific marks for distinct ideas.
4. Re-mark the same five papers using your analytic scheme.
5. Compare the two sets of marks: Did any student's score change significantly? Did the rubric make your scoring faster and more confident? Discuss your findings with your cooperating teacher.

## Summative task

Select a challenging unit from the elementary or secondary curriculum (e.g., Class 8 General Science: "Ecology and Human Impact", or Class 9 Pakistan Studies: "Constitutional Development"). Design a comprehensive subjective assessment task consisting of:

1. **One extended-response essay question:** A thought-provoking prompt targeting Bloom's Level 4 (Analyze) or Level 5 (Evaluate), with clear operational boundaries (word count, time allocation, and required themes).
2. **Two structured short-answer questions:** High-functioning items targeting Bloom's Level 2 (Understand) and Level 3 (Apply), requiring precise conceptual definitions or applied explanations.
3. **A complete analytic scoring guide:**
   - For the essay: A point-by-point marking rubric (10 marks) detailing criteria for conceptual accuracy, evidence, and logical organisation, including clear descriptors for Below Standard, Meets Standard, and Exceeds Standard performance.
   - For the short-answer questions: Complete model answers, acceptable alternative phrasings, and explicit fractional mark breakdowns (e.g., 1 mark for identification, 1 mark for justification).

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Below Standard (1 mark) | Meets Standard (2 marks) | Exceeds Standard (3 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Prompt clarity and constraints** | Prompts are excessively broad, ambiguous, or fail to specify length, time, or required themes. | Prompts clearly specify task boundaries, expected depth, and structural parameters for students. | Prompts are exceptionally engaging, authentic, unambiguous, and provide flawless cognitive and operational scaffolding. |
| **Analysis of scoring error (Analyze)** | Fails to incorporate safeguards against subjective scoring bias; scoring guide is vague or impressionistic. | Scoring guide provides clear point allocations for distinct concepts, reducing examiner discretion. | Masterfully deconstructs potential marker biases; establishes highly objective, verifiable indicators for every mark awarded. |
| **Model answers and descriptors** | Model answers are incomplete, inaccurate, or fail to provide guidance for partially correct responses. | Model answers are thorough, accurate, and provide realistic performance descriptors for all score tiers. | Exemplary model answers and rubrics featuring comprehensive benchmark criteria, acceptable synonyms, and explicit guidance for common errors. |
