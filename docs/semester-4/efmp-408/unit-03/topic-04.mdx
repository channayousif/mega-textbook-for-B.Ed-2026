# Topic 3.4 - Writing items that work

## A real classroom situation

In a government girls' high school in Mirpurkhas, Ms Rabia walked into the staff room after invigilating a Class 8 General Science examination. Several distressed students were waiting outside the door, arguing over their answers.

Ms Rabia examined the printed examination paper to understand their confusion. She immediately spotted Question 12:

*"Which of the following is NOT an un-subsidised non-renewable energy source?"*  
*A) Coal*  
*B) Solar*  
*C) An wind turbine*  
*D) None of the above*

The students were confused by the convoluted triple-negative phrasing ("NOT an un-subsidised non-renewable"). Even pupils who understood energy resources thoroughly could not decipher what the sentence was asking. Meanwhile, one student who had never studied the science textbook told her classmates: "I picked option C because the question stem ended with 'an', and 'wind turbine' was the only option that didn't make sense with 'an', but then I saw 'coal' and guessed D because 'None of the above' is usually the trick answer."

This real classroom episode exposes how sloppy item writing destroys assessment validity. When questions contain grammatical giveaway clues, negative convolutions, or ambiguous phrasing, test scores cease to reflect student subject mastery. Instead, they measure test-wiseness, puzzle-solving agility, and linguistic deciphering. Writing high-quality test items requires disciplined adherence to professional psychometric guidelines and systematic pre-administration auditing.

## Explanation

### Guidelines for writing good objective items

Writing effective test items is an exacting craft that requires precision, clarity, and empathy for the learner's reading process (HEC, 2012; Seifert & Sutton, 2009). Decades of psychometric research have yielded five golden rules for constructing valid objective items:

#### Rule 1: State the central problem clearly and completely in the stem
The stem must be self-contained. A student who covers the response options with a hand should still understand the exact problem posed. Avoid "hanging stems" consisting of a single incomplete phrase (e.g., "The Indus River..."), which force the student to evaluate four disconnected propositions.

- *Flawed stem:* "Sindh is:"
- *Improved stem:* "In which climatic zone of Pakistan is the majority of central Sindh located?"

#### Rule 2: Keep vocabulary and sentence structure simple and focused on the construct
Test items must measure subject-matter competence, not extraneous linguistic complexity. Unless the item explicitly belongs to an advanced English vocabulary test, use straightforward, accessible language. Avoid convoluted academic syntax, archaic jargon, or unnecessarily verbose introductory filler.

#### Rule 3: Maintain strict grammatical parallel between stem and options
Every response option must fit grammatically with the stem. A classic amateur error is leaving an indefinite article ("a" or "an") at the end of the stem that matches only one option phonetically:
- *Flawed stem:* "An animal that undergoes complete metamorphosis is an:"
  - A) grasshopper
  - B) butterfly
  - C) frog
  - D) adult insect
  *(Only option D begins with a vowel sound, giving away the answer or creating immediate confusion).*
- *Corrected stem:* "Which animal undergoes complete metamorphosis?" (Options: Grasshopper, Butterfly, Frog, Housefly).

#### Rule 4: Keep all options approximately equal in length and detail
In teacher-made tests, the keyed option is frequently two to three times longer than the distractors because the teacher took pains to qualify it and make it undeniably accurate, while hastily jotting down short, one-word distractors. Test-wise students quickly learn that "when in doubt, pick the longest, most detailed option." Ensure all alternatives share comparable length, complexity, and vocabulary register.

#### Rule 5: Randomise the position of the keyed option
In unguided teacher-made tests, option 'C' is disproportionately chosen as the keyed answer, while option 'A' is severely underused. Ensure the key appears with roughly equal frequency across positions A, B, C, and D, and avoid obvious patterns (such as alternating between A and B).

### Common item-writing errors and how to avoid them

When teachers draft examination papers without formal training, five recurring technical defects routinely compromise score reliability (HEC, 2012):

#### 1. Negative and double-negative phrasing
Stems containing negative words ("Which of the following is NOT...", "All of the following EXCEPT...") cause immense cognitive interference. Under examination stress, students overlook the negative word and select the first true statement they see. If a negative phrasing is absolutely unavoidable to test an essential safety or exception rule, the negative word must be highlighted in **BOLD CAPITALS** (e.g., **NOT**, **EXCEPT**, **LEAST**). Double negatives (e.g., "Which is NOT an un-equal...") must be entirely prohibited.

#### 2. Using "All of the above" and "None of the above"
Professional assessment bodies (such as the Higher Education Commission and the Aga Khan University Examination Board) strongly advise against using "All of the above" and "None of the above" for three psychometric reasons:
- **"All of the above" encourages partial knowledge guessing:** If a student recognizes that options A and B are both correct, they know "All of the above" must be the answer without even evaluating option C.
- **"None of the above" measures error detection rather than competence:** Selecting "None of the above" tells the teacher what the student thinks the answer is *not*, but provides zero evidence of what the student knows the actual answer *is*.
- **Logical vulnerability:** If a student identifies a single flaw in one option, "All of the above" is instantly eliminated, reducing a four-choice item to a two-choice gamble.

#### 3. Non-functional, implausible distractors
Distractors that are absurd, humorous, or blatantly unrelated provide zero measurement value. In a four-option item where two distractors are laughable, the item functions as a two-option coin toss with a 50% guessing probability. Distractors must represent authentic student misconceptions, common computational slips, or logical reversals identified during classroom instruction.

#### 4. Inter-item cueing (the "puzzle clue" flaw)
Inter-item cueing occurs when information provided in one test question accidentally reveals the answer to another question on the same paper. For example:
- *Question 5:* "What gas is absorbed by plants during photosynthesis?"
- *Question 18:* "Because plants absorb carbon dioxide during photosynthesis, what effect does deforestation have on global greenhouse gases?"
A clever student reading Question 18 immediately returns to Question 5 and fills in "Carbon dioxide." Test papers must be systematically cross-checked to ensure questions remain completely independent.

#### 5. Mutually exclusive option overlaps
In quantitative items, numerical options must never overlap:
- *Flawed:* A) 1–5, B) 5–10, C) 10–15, D) 15–20. *(A student whose calculation yields exactly 5 or 10 cannot choose).*
- *Corrected:* A) 1 to 5, B) 6 to 10, C) 11 to 15, D) 16 to 20.

#### Quality control: the item review checklist
Before printing any examination paper, professional educators conduct a systematic peer review using an objective checklist adapted from the AKU-EB and IBA Sukkur test development guidelines (HEC, 2012):

| Checkpoint | Verification Question | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Construct Focus** | Does the item measure an important Student Learning Outcome rather than isolated textbook trivia? | Yes / No |
| **2. Stem Clarity** | Is the problem clearly formulated in the stem before reading the options? | Yes / No |
| **3. Negative Words** | Is the stem free of negative wording, or is the negative word emphasised in **BOLD**? | Yes / No |
| **4. Option Independence** | Are options mutually exclusive, with no "All of the above" or "None of the above"? | Yes / No |
| **5. Clue Elimination** | Is the item free of grammatical clues, phonetic leads, and inter-item clues? | Yes / No |
| **6. Distractor Plausibility** | Are all distractors plausible and grounded in documented student misconceptions? | Yes / No |

## Activity: Item sanitisation workshop

**Objective:** Audit, diagnose, and rewrite authentic flawed examination items to meet professional quality standards.  
**Grouping:** Pairs of trainee teachers.  
**Time:** 35 minutes.

### Instructions

1. Review the three flawed items below, taken from actual school tests in Sindh:
   - **Item A (General Science, Class 6):**  
     *The human skeleton is an:*  
     *A) organ*  
     *B) system consisting of 206 bones that provides structural support and protection for delicate internal organs*  
     *C) muscle*  
     *D) none of these*
   - **Item B (Social Studies, Class 7):**  
     *Which of the following cities is NOT an un-industrialised district of lower Sindh?*  
     *A) Karachi, B) Hyderabad, C) Thatta, D) All of the above.*
   - **Item C (Mathematics, Class 5):**  
     *The perimeter of a square whose side is 5 cm is:*  
     *A) 20 cm, B) 20 cm squared, C) 25 cm, D) Both A and C.*
2. For each item, identify every psychometric violation present (grammatical clues, length bias, negative phrasing, overlapping options, non-functional distractors).
3. Completely rewrite each item into an exemplary, four-option multiple-choice question that assesses genuine understanding without clues or tricks.
4. Swap your rewritten items with another pair and use the Item Review Checklist above to verify whether all flaws have been eliminated.

## Check your understanding

1. Explain the "length bias" error in multiple-choice construction, and describe how students exploit it when guessing. *(Understand)*
2. Why do psychometricians strongly discourage the use of "All of the above" and "None of the above" in objective achievement tests? *(Understand)*
3. Identify three distinct types of unintended clues that teachers accidentally include in test stems or options. *(Remember)*
4. Read the following draft item: *"An example of an amphibian is an: A) snake, B) eagle, C) adult frog, D) crocodile."* Identify two technical flaws in this item and provide a fully corrected version. *(Apply)*

## Summary

High-quality objective assessment requires rigorous elimination of technical construction errors. Flaws such as negative phrasing, grammatical giveaway clues, length disparities, overlapping options, and the inclusion of "All of the above" distort measurement, rewarding test-wise guessing while penalising careful reading. By formulating focused stems, ensuring grammatical parallelism, designing plausible diagnostic distractors, and subjecting all papers to systematic pre-administration checklist audits, teachers create assessments that faithfully measure true curriculum achievement.

## Summative task

Conduct a comprehensive forensic audit and sanitisation of a flawed classroom examination paper. Below are three severely flawed items from an elementary curriculum test:

- **Flawed Item 1 (Science):** *"Which of the following is NOT an un-healthy dietary habit that does NOT contribute to cardiovascular disease? A) Regular exercise, B) Eating fresh green leafy vegetables daily, C) Consuming excessive amounts of hydrogenated trans-fats and deep-fried roadside snacks, D) None of the above."*
- **Flawed Item 2 (Social Studies):** *"The ancient civilization of Mohenjo-daro was an: A) river valley settlement in Larkana, B) mountain empire, C) modern city, D) oceanic port."*
- **Flawed Item 3 (Mathematics):** *"What is a prime number? A) A number, B) An integer greater than 1 that has no positive divisors other than 1 and itself, C) 10, D) All of the above."*

For each item:
1. **Flaw diagnosis:** Write a detailed critique identifying every technical, grammatical, and cognitive violation.
2. **Pedagogical impact (Analyze):** Explain how the flaw distorts student test performance, identifying whether it penalises knowledgeable students or rewards ignorant guessers.
3. **Complete sanitisation (Create):** Rewrite the item into a flawless, professional multiple-choice question targeting Bloom's Level 2 (Understand) or Level 3 (Apply), complete with four parallel, plausible options and a clear, focused stem.

| Criterion | Below Standard (1 mark) | Meets Standard (2 marks) | Exceeds Standard (3 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|

| **Analysis of washback and validity (Analyze)** | Fails to explain how flaws affect student scores or measurement reliability. | Clearly explains how flaws mislead students, reward test-wiseness, or depress score validity. | Delivers penetrating insight into cognitive interference, test anxiety, guessing odds, and construct-irrelevant difficulty. |
