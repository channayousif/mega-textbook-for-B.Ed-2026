# Topic 3.1 - Planning a test before writing it

## A real classroom situation

In a government boys' higher secondary school in Larkana, Mr Farhan sat down at 8:00 PM to set the mid-term examination paper for his Class 6 General Science class. The examination was scheduled for the next morning. With the textbook open on his desk, Mr Farhan flipped through the chapters and drafted questions as they came to his mind: two definitions from Chapter 1 (Living Things), three fill-in-the-blanks from Chapter 2 (Cell Structure), a five-mark essay asking students to draw and label a flower, and several simple recall questions about the names of planets.

When the papers were marked, over half the students failed. The headmistress, Ms Rubina, reviewed the examination paper and asked Mr Farhan: "How did you decide how many marks to assign to each chapter?" Mr Farhan replied: "I simply wrote good questions until the marks added up to 50."

Ms Rubina pointed to his lesson logbook: "During the first eight weeks of term, you spent four weeks teaching energy transformations and simple electrical circuits, with several practical laboratory demonstrations. Yet on this test, there is not a single question about electricity, circuits, or energy. Meanwhile, 35 out of the 50 marks test whether pupils can memorise bold terms from the first two pages of the textbook. A hardworking student who mastered electrical circuits and scientific inquiry failed your test, while a student who crammed definitions the night before passed. You did not test what you taught, because you built the house without an architectural blueprint."

<Figure id="fig-U3-1" src="/img/figures/efmp-408/unit-03/fig-U3-1.svg" alt="Table showing a worked table of specifications for a Grade 6 general science unit on matter and energy, specifying content topics, cognitive levels, item formats, and percentage weightage." />

This dilemma is common in Pakistani schools. Setting a test without systematic pre-planning almost always leads to over-sampling simple factual recall, neglecting complex skills, and misrepresenting curriculum priorities. Professional test construction begins not with writing questions, but with building a comprehensive test blueprint.

## Explanation

### Planning a test: the table of specifications

A Table of Specifications (TOS), often referred to as a test blueprint, is a two-dimensional planning framework that guides the development of an achievement test (HEC, 2012; Seifert & Sutton, 2009). One dimension outlines the content areas or curriculum topics to be assessed. The second dimension outlines the cognitive processes or levels of learning expected of students, typically structured around Bloom's Revised Taxonomy (Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, Create).

The primary function of a Table of Specifications is to guarantee **content validity** (HEC, 2012). Content validity ensures that the assessment tasks represent a fair, balanced, and proportionate sample of the knowledge and skills specified in the curriculum and taught in class. When a teacher drafts items haphazardly without a blueprint, four systematic distortions occur:

1. **Over-concentration on low-level recall:** Questions testing factual recall (definitions, dates, names) are easiest and quickest to write. Without a blueprint specifying higher-order targets, tests become heavily skewed toward passive memorisation.
2. **Topic omission and curriculum bias:** Teachers naturally gravitate toward their personal favourite topics or the most recently taught units, inadvertently leaving out large portions of the curriculum.
3. **Mismatched instructional emphasis:** Topics that required three weeks of intensive instruction may receive only two marks on the test, while a minor topic covered in one period receives ten marks.
4. **Unpredictable item difficulty:** Without deliberate mapping, a test may swing wildly between trivial simplicity and unintended obscurity.

> **Common Misconception: "A longer test is automatically a fairer test."**  
> Many educators assume that increasing the number of questions on an exam guarantees fairness. However, if a 100-item test measures only rote memorisation of textbook trivia, making it longer merely magnifies its invalidity. Fairness is not a function of raw length; it is a function of alignment, proportional sampling, and representative coverage guided by a Table of Specifications.

### Blueprints and weightage

To build an effective Table of Specifications, a teacher must determine the appropriate **weightage** (the proportion of total marks allocated) for each content area and cognitive level. The HEC guide asks teachers to plan the content coverage, question types and distribution of marks of a test before writing it (HEC, 2012). To decide that distribution, this textbook suggests weighing three criteria (an author synthesis of common classroom practice, not a list stated in the HEC guide):

1. **Instructional time spent:** The number of classroom periods or instructional days devoted to a topic provides a baseline for its relative importance. If Topic A occupied 40% of teaching time across the term, it should roughly account for 40% of the test marks.
2. **Curricular significance:** Certain foundational concepts (such as understanding place value in mathematics or the scientific method in science) carry enduring significance for future learning, justifying a higher mark allocation even if taught in fewer periods.
3. **Cognitive complexity:** Tasks requiring multi-step problem solving, analysis, or essay synthesis demand more student thinking time and merit greater credit than single-step recall items.

#### Worked example: calculating topic weightage

Suppose Ms Farzana is preparing a 50-mark terminal examination for a Class 5 Social Studies course in Hyderabad. The course was taught over 40 instructional periods across three main units:

- **Unit 1: Geography of Sindh:** 16 periods taught.
- **Unit 2: Local Government and Civic Rights:** 14 periods taught.
- **Unit 3: Cultural Heritage of Pakistan:** 10 periods taught.

To calculate the proportional mark allocation for each unit:

**Weightage = (Periods devoted to unit / Total instructional periods) × Total test marks**

Applying this formula:

- **Unit 1:** (16 / 40) × 50 = 20 marks (40%)
- **Unit 2:** (14 / 40) × 50 = 17.5 ≈ 17 marks (34%)
- **Unit 3:** (10 / 40) × 50 = 12.5 ≈ 13 marks (26%)
- **Total:** 20 + 17 + 13 = 50 marks (100%)

Next, Ms Farzana establishes the cognitive distribution across Bloom's Taxonomy. For an elementary examination, a reasonable starting point is the split below (a suggestion of this textbook, not an official rule; teachers adjust it to their learners and objectives):
- **Remembering (Knowledge):** 30% (15 marks)
- **Understanding (Comprehension):** 40% (20 marks)
- **Applying and Analyzing (Higher Order):** 30% (15 marks)

By cross-tabulating content units against these cognitive tiers, Ms Farzana populates the cells of her Table of Specifications. Each cell specifies exactly how many marks will be assessed, through which item format (MCQ, short answer, or essay).

### Linking objectives with questions

Once the Table of Specifications defines the target marks for each cell, the teacher drafts questions that link directly to specific Student Learning Outcomes (SLOs). Constructive alignment demands that the **action verb** in the objective dictates the nature of the assessment task (HEC, 2012).

In the Pakistani national curriculum, SLOs use specific command words:
- **Recall / Identify / State:** Requires the learner to recognise or produce stored facts. Suitable for MCQs, matching, or fill-in-the-blank items. Example: "State the capital city of Sindh." *(Remember)*
- **Explain / Summarise / Describe in your own words:** Requires the learner to demonstrate conceptual understanding without merely parroting textbook phrasing. Suitable for structured short-answer items. Example: "Explain why deforestation leads to soil erosion in river basins." *(Understand)*
- **Calculate / Solve / Demonstrate:** Requires the learner to apply an abstract concept or rule to a concrete, unfamiliar situation. Suitable for problem-solving tasks and applied scenarios. Example: "Calculate the total cost of fencing a rectangular school garden measuring 12 metres by 8 metres." *(Apply)*
- **Compare / Contrast / Differentiate / Critique:** Requires the learner to break an idea into constituent parts, identify underlying assumptions, and evaluate evidence. Suitable for extended-response essay questions and data interpretation items. Example: "Differentiate between renewable and non-renewable energy sources in terms of environmental impact and economic sustainability." *(Analyze)*

#### Avoiding cognitive mismatch

A frequent flaw in teacher-made tests is a **cognitive mismatch** between the stated learning outcome and the test question. Consider this classroom example:

- **Stated Curriculum Objective:** "Pupils will analyse the economic causes of the 1857 War of Independence." *(Target: Bloom Level 4, Analyze)*
- **Teacher's Test Question:** "In what year did the War of Independence take place? A) 1847, B) 1857, C) 1867, D) 1877." *(Actual Task: Bloom Level 1, Remember)*

Although the question is ostensibly about the War of Independence, it fails to assess analysis. It tests chronological recall. A properly aligned question would read:
"Explain two distinct economic grievances held by local farmers and artisans that contributed to the uprising of 1857, and assess which grievance posed the greater threat to British colonial rule."

## Activity: Building a classroom test blueprint

**Objective:** Design a 25-mark Table of Specifications for a primary or middle school curriculum unit.  
**Grouping:** Small groups of 3 to 4 trainee teachers.  
**Time:** 40 minutes.

### Instructions

1. Select one subject and grade level familiar to your group (e.g., Class 6 General Science: "Water and the Environment", or Class 5 Mathematics: "Fractions and Decimals").
2. Identify three distinct sub-topics within that unit and allocate realistic instructional time (total 20 periods).
3. Calculate the proportional mark allocation for each sub-topic for a 25-mark achievement test.
4. Allocate marks across three cognitive levels: Remember (approx. 30%), Understand (approx. 40%), and Apply/Analyze (approx. 30%).
5. In each cell of the grid, specify the question format to be used:
   - Multiple-Choice Questions (1 mark each)
   - Short-Answer Questions (2 to 3 marks each)
   - Extended-Response / Essay Questions (4 to 5 marks each)
6. Appoint one group member to present your blueprint on chart paper. Critique another group's blueprint: Does the total add up to 25? Is the cognitive balance appropriate for elementary learners?

## Check your understanding

1. Define a Table of Specifications and explain why building one prior to drafting questions protects content validity. *(Understand)*
2. A teacher allocates 18 instructional periods to Plant Physiology and 6 instructional periods to Animal Classification. In a 60-mark term examination, calculate the equitable mark allocation for each topic based on instructional time. *(Apply)*
3. Read the following learning objective: "Students will design an experiment to test whether seeds need sunlight to germinate." Write one test task that directly matches this cognitive level, and one flawed question that commits a cognitive mismatch by testing only factual recall. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Test construction is a systematic engineering process that begins with planning rather than item writing. A Table of Specifications (test blueprint) maps curriculum content against cognitive levels, ensuring that an assessment accurately reflects instructional time, curricular priorities, and cognitive complexity. By establishing clear weightage and aligning test items with the action verbs of Student Learning Outcomes, teachers safeguard content validity, prevent over-reliance on trivial memorisation, and produce fair, dependable evaluations of student achievement.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can explain the structure and professional purpose of a Table of Specifications.
- [ ] I can calculate proportional mark weightage based on instructional periods and curriculum significance.
- [ ] I can distribute marks across Bloom's Taxonomy levels to achieve balanced cognitive depth.
- [ ] I can identify and rectify cognitive mismatches between learning objectives and examination questions.

## Try this at your practicum school

During your practicum placement at a local elementary or secondary school:

1. Obtain a copy of a recently administered terminal examination paper in your subject area, along with the textbook syllabus or scheme of work.
2. Reverse-engineer a Table of Specifications from the examination paper: classify each question by topic and estimated Bloom's cognitive level, and tally the marks in each cell.
3. Compare your reverse-engineered blueprint with the school's instructional timetable: Did the marks reflect actual teaching time? Were higher-order cognitive skills tested, or did factual recall dominate?
4. Write a two-page reflective report summarizing your findings and propose a revised blueprint that would improve the test's content validity.

## Summative task

Develop a complete 50-mark Table of Specifications for an authentic four-week unit from the Sindh elementary curriculum (e.g., Class 7 General Science or Class 8 Mathematics). Your submission must include:

1. **Contextual profile:** Grade level, subject, unit title, total instructional periods, and list of key Student Learning Outcomes.
2. **Weightage justification:** A clear explanation of how instructional time and topic significance determined mark allocations across sub-topics.
3. **The blueprint matrix:** A completed two-dimensional grid cross-tabulating sub-topics against cognitive levels (Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze), specifying item formats (MCQs, short answer, essay) and point values in every cell.
4. **Draft items:** Two sample questions targeting different cells in the matrix (one targeting Remember, one targeting Apply or Analyze), demonstrating exact alignment with their respective SLOs.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Below Standard (1 mark) | Meets Standard (2 marks) | Exceeds Standard (3 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Blueprint completeness and mathematics** | Blueprint cells do not sum to 50 marks; formulas or weightage calculations are incorrect or missing. | Blueprint accurately totals 50 marks; calculations logically reflect stated instructional periods. | Blueprint is mathematically flawless, elegant, and balances item types and time constraints with exceptional professional precision. |
| **Cognitive distribution (Analyze)** | Test is heavily skewed toward simple recall (>60%) with negligible higher-order assessment. | Cognitive distribution is balanced across Remember, Understand, and Apply/Analyze tiers suitable for the grade level. | Critically analyses cognitive demands; thoughtfully scaffolds items from basic comprehension to authentic application and analytical problem solving. |
| **Item alignment and precision** | Sample items do not align with the cognitive level or content specified in their respective blueprint cells. | Sample items align accurately with the designated content areas and cognitive levels under Bloom's Taxonomy. | Sample items exhibit exemplary alignment, sophisticated command word usage, unambiguous prompts, and authentic contextual grounding. |
