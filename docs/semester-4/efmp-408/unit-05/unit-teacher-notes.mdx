# Unit 5: Grading, Reporting and Interpreting Scores - Teacher Notes

## Why this unit needs two weeks

The guide gives Weeks 11 and 12 to grading, reporting and the statistical concepts that support them. Trainee teachers usually arrive with strong opinions about marks and grades and almost no experience of computing anything from a real mark sheet. The opinions are useful; the missing practice is not. Plan the two weeks so that every trainee computes a percentage, a grade point average, a standard deviation and two item indices with their own hands at least once. The arithmetic is intermediate-level, and the obstacle is confidence rather than ability.

## Teaching strategies

The course guide names five strategies for this course: lectures, interactive discussions, question-answer sessions, demonstration, and case-study analysis. Use them like this:

- **Lecture** for the minimum necessary definitions: what marking and grading each mean, the three measures of centre, the standard deviation formula, and the two item indices. Keep each to a few minutes and then hand the room a data set.
- **Demonstration** for every calculation. Work one complete example on the board before asking trainees to do one, and narrate the decisions as you go, not only the arithmetic. Show the mistake you would have made at their stage.
- **Question-answer sessions** to surface what trainees believe before you correct it. Ask "what would you tell this pupil's parent?" rather than "what is the mean?".
- **Interactive discussion** for the judgement calls: which scale to report, what to do with a zero discrimination index, whether a hard item should survive.
- **Case-study analysis** as the spine of Topic 5.3. The five-item quiz in the Explanation is a case; the Activity table is a harder one. Let groups argue about a real decision.

## Sequencing

1. **Start with marks, not statistics.** Topic 5.1 is where trainees recognise their own school experience. Let them describe a report card they remember before you define anything.
2. **Move to the score set.** Topic 5.2 needs the idea that a mark is one point in a distribution, which Topic 5.1 has just established.
3. **Finish with the item.** Topic 5.3 needs a completed, marked test in hand. If you can, set a short objective test in Week 10 (Unit 4) and use its real mark sheet for the item analysis, with names removed. Real data changes the quality of the discussion completely.
4. **Return to reporting at the end.** Once trainees have seen a spread and an item analysis, ask them again what a report card should carry. Their answer will be better than it was in Week 11.

## Likely misconceptions and how to handle them

| Misconception | What to do |
|---|---|
| "The average tells you how the class did." | Put two data sets with the same mean and different spreads on the board, as in Topic 5.2. Ask what a parent would conclude from each. Let the discrepancy do the work. |
| "A hard item is a bad item." | Ask what a mastery test of a basic skill should look like. Once trainees accept that an item everyone gets right can be exactly right, the general claim collapses. |
| "Percentages and grades carry the same information." | Have trainees convert one set of marks under two different bandings and watch the grades change while the marks do not. |
| "The standard deviation is a measure of how good the class is." | Ask two sections with the same mean and very different spreads, and ask which one the teacher would rather teach. The answer is not a judgement about quality. |
| "A negative discrimination index means the item was too hard." | Separate the two indices explicitly. Difficulty says how many got it right; discrimination says who got it right. A hard item can discriminate well. |
| "Grading is just marking with extra steps." | Ask which decisions need the detail and which need the category, and have trainees name one of each. |

## Practical work

The guide lists hands-on computer exercises, individual assignments, group assignments, internet-research tasks and presentations. Mapped to this unit:

- **Hands-on computer exercise (Topic 5.2):** Enter a class's marks into a spreadsheet and compute the mean, median, mode and standard deviation with built-in functions. Then change one mark and watch each statistic move. Ask trainees to report which statistic moved most and why.
- **Individual assignment (Topic 5.1):** Each trainee obtains one recent report card from their practicum school, with the pupil's name removed, and writes a 150-word critique of what it communicates to a parent. Collect these and read two aloud.
- **Group assignment (Topic 5.3):** Each group takes the Activity data set, computes both indices, and produces a one-page item-review note for the teacher who set the test. Groups exchange notes and mark each other's reasoning against the rubric in the summative task.
- **Internet-research task (Topic 5.1):** Find the grade boundaries or the grade point scale published by one Pakistani university or board, and bring a copy to class. Compare the scales across groups and note that a grade is meaningless without its scale.
- **Presentation (any topic):** Five minutes per trainee on a single misconception from the table above, with one concrete example. This works well in Week 12 as revision.

## Handling the sensitive parts

Marks and grades are not abstract to trainees; many have strong memories of a result that changed their own path. Three points keep the unit safe and useful:

- Use **hypothetical or anonymised** data for every exercise, including the data sets in this unit. Do not analyse a real class's results in front of the trainees who taught them.
- When a trainee shares a difficult experience, acknowledge it and return to the principle. The unit is about making the system fairer, not about defending the one that was there.
- Be explicit that **a grade is a decision, not a fact**. Boards and schools set boundaries, and those boundaries can be examined and questioned. That is the professional point of Topic 5.1.
