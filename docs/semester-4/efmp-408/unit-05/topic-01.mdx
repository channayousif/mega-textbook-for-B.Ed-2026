# Topic 5.1 - From marks to grades, and reporting them

## A real classroom situation

At a government boys' high school in Khairpur, Mr Raza has just finished marking the Class 9 General Mathematics paper. The paper carries 100 marks. He enters each pupil's total in the register: 78, 64, 61, 47, 39 and so on, down to 22 for a pupil who was absent for most of the term. The head teacher asks him for the result in a form the board can accept, so Mr Raza converts the marks to percentages. A parent, Mrs Siddiqui, visits the next day. Her son scored 61 percent. She wants to know what that means. "Is 61 a good mark? What is he good at? What should he practise?" Mr Raza opens the marked paper and walks her through four pages of working. The percentage alone had told her almost nothing.

## Explanation

### Marking versus grading systems

**Marking** is the process of deciding how many marks a piece of work earns. The teacher compares each response with an agreed scoring guide and records a number. Marking answers a narrow question: how much of this task did the pupil get right?

**Grading** is the process of turning one or more marks into a category that carries a shared meaning. The category may be a letter, a band, a percentage range or a word such as "pass", "merit" or "needs support". Grading answers a wider question: what does this performance amount to overall?

The two are not rivals. A teacher needs both, at different moments, and the mistake is to use one where the other belongs. **Marking preserves the exact record of what the pupil did** (item 3 wrong, item 7 partly right, 14 out of 20 on the composition) and **loses comparability**: fourteen out of 20 on a hard paper and fourteen out of 20 on an easy paper are not the same achievement. **Grading preserves a compact meaning that travels** between the teacher, the head teacher, the board and the parent, and **loses detail**: two pupils holding the same grade may differ widely, and a pupil who moves from 69 to 71 has crossed a boundary without really changing. Marking is therefore the right tool for diagnosing errors, planning the next lesson and giving feedforward; grading is the right tool for reporting progress, certifying a level and deciding promotion or awards.

The HEC course guide makes the same point from the other direction. Turning raw marks into grades through criterion referencing focuses attention on what the learner has achieved rather than on where the learner sits in a rank order, and grades are more inclusive than raw marks because everyone can succeed rather than only a fixed share of the class (HEC, 2012). The guide also draws the distinction that runs through Pakistani assessment: the Boards of Intermediate and Secondary Education use **item-based** criteria, where grade boundaries are fixed in advance as percentages of the marks available, while O-level examiners use **examinee-based** criteria, where senior examiners inspect the work of candidates near a borderline and promote those who belong with the group above.

> **Common misconception: percentages and grades carry the same information.**
> They do not. A percentage is a measurement; a grade is an interpretation placed on one or more measurements. A pupil who scores 65 percent on a paper that most of the class found easy has achieved less, in any useful sense, than a pupil who scores 65 percent on a paper that most of the class could not finish. The two percentages are identical and the two achievements are not. Grades exist precisely because raw marks under-describe performance, and they lose information in the act of summarising it. A teacher who reports only marks, or only grades, is telling half the story.

**Worked example: the same four marks, read two ways.** A Class 9 General Mathematics class of 20 pupils sits a 100-mark paper. Two pupils finish close together: Ali obtains 68 marks and Bilal obtains 72. Read as percentages, Ali is at 68 percent and Bilal at 72 percent. Read against a school banding in which A runs from 80 to 100, B from 70 to 79, C from 60 to 69 and D from 50 to 59, Ali receives a C and Bilal a B. A four-mark difference has become a grade difference, and a parent may read that as a large difference in ability. Under a different, wider banding in which A runs from 70 upward, both pupils receive an A and the four-mark difference all but disappears. Neither reading is wrong. The point is that the banding is a decision the school makes, and a teacher must be able to say so when a parent asks.

### Percentages and grade point average

A **percentage** expresses marks obtained as a fraction of the marks available, scaled to 100:

> percentage = (marks obtained / marks available) × 100

Percentages are easy to compute and easy to misunderstand. They tell you how much of *this* paper a pupil completed correctly, nothing more. They do not tell you whether the paper was hard, whether the pupil had taught support, or whether the pupil improved on the last test.

**Worked example: a board total.** A Class 9 pupil in a Sindh board school sits eight subjects and obtains 742 marks out of 1100. The percentage is (742 / 1100) × 100 = 67.45, reported as 67.5 percent. That number is accurate and, on its own, nearly useless to a parent. It says nothing about which subjects were strong, whether 67.5 percent is a good result in that cohort, or what the pupil should do next.

A **grade point average (GPA)** works differently. Each course is given a grade, each grade carries a number of grade points, and each course also carries a weight, usually its credit hours. The average is weighted by those credit hours:

> GPA = (sum of grade points multiplied by credit hours) / (sum of credit hours)

A **credit hour** is the institutional measure of how much time a course takes. A course that meets for three periods a week over a semester carries three credit hours; a shorter course carries fewer. Weighting by credit hours means that a grade in a course that took twice as much of the pupil's time counts twice as much.

The course guide lists "GPA, percentages" in its Week 11 outline without setting out a grade-point scale. The Higher Education Commission settled the question in 2017: national universities on the semester system "should preferably use four (04) grade point system", with A worth 4.0 and each lower grade one point less (HEC, 2017). A failing grade earns no points (HEC, n.d.). This is the scale used in this topic:

| Grade | A | B | C | D | F |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Grade points | 4.0 | 3.0 | 2.0 | 1.0 | 0.0 |

**Worked example: one semester's GPA.** A B.Ed (Hons) Elementary student completes four courses in a semester:

| Course | Credit hours | Grade | Grade points | Points × hours |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Educational Assessment and Evaluation | 3 | B | 3.0 | 9.0 |
| Teaching of English | 3 | A | 4.0 | 12.0 |
| Teaching of Mathematics | 3 | C | 2.0 | 6.0 |
| Pakistan Studies | 2 | B | 3.0 | 6.0 |
| **Total** | **11** | | | **33.0** |

GPA = 33.0 / 11 = **3.00** on a four-point scale.

Two things are worth noticing. First, the weighting matters. A student who takes a two-credit course and a four-credit course cannot simply average the two percentages; the four-credit course deserves twice the say. Second, a GPA is always reported with its scale. Some Pakistani universities and programmes grade on scales of 4.5, 5, 8 or 10 points, and HEC publishes the equivalent grade points for each: a B is worth 3.0 on the four-point scale, 3.75 on the five-point scale and 7.5 on the ten-point scale (HEC, 2017). A GPA of 3.00 therefore means something different on a four-point scale than on a five-point scale, and a teacher who encounters a transcript should check the scale before comparing two students.

Converting a GPA back to a percentage also follows HEC guidance. Its semester-system guidelines tie each grade to a band of marks, for example A for 85 percent and above, B for 71 to 74 percent and F for below 50 percent, and they give a CGPA the lowest percentage of its band. A CGPA of 3.00 is therefore reported as 71 percent (HEC, n.d.). Universities may publish their own tables, so the conversion printed on the transcript is the one to use.

The cumulative version, the **CGPA**, applies the same calculation across every semester a student has completed. It is the number most universities publish at graduation, and it is the reason a weak semester is never fully erased by a strong one.

> **Common misconception: a GPA is just another average, so it says the same thing as a percentage.**
> A percentage averages marks within one paper. A GPA averages grade points across several courses and weights each course by its credit hours. Because the two are built from different ingredients, they can move in different directions. A student whose percentages fall across a semester may see the GPA rise, because the grades in the heaviest courses held steady. Report both, and say which one you are quoting.

### Report cards and reporting to stakeholders

A **report card** is a structured summary of a pupil's achievement over a defined period, usually a term or a semester. In a Pakistani government school it typically carries the subjects studied, the marks or grades obtained in each, a total, an overall grade, attendance, a teacher's remark and a promotion decision.

Different readers need different things from the same card, and a card that serves one reader well may serve another badly:

1. **The pupil** needs to see progress since the last report and to know what to do next. A mark with no comparator and no comment gives a pupil nothing to act on.
2. **The parent** needs an accurate picture of the child's strengths and weaknesses, and enough detail to help. The HEC guide's teaching notes are direct about this: for students, report card grades should not only let them interpret their progress since the last report but should also indicate in what ways progress has been made and what remediation is necessary; for parents, a report card provides an accurate picture of the child's strengths and weaknesses and can inform what they need to do to help (HEC, 2012).
3. **The class teacher** needs a record that supports planning and hands over cleanly to the next teacher. A remark that names the next skill is a gift to whoever teaches the pupil next.
4. **The head teacher and the school** need aggregated evidence for promotion, streaming, intervention and reporting to the board. This is where marks, grades and percentages are combined.

Seifert and Sutton (2009) describe classroom assessment as an ongoing communication channel rather than an isolated terminal grading event. A report card is the most formal message on that channel, and it fails when it is only a mark list.

<Figure id="fig-U5-4" src="/img/figures/efmp-408/unit-05/fig-U5-4.svg" alt="An annotated school report card entry showing the mark, grade, class mean, class range and a teacher remark, with a note on what each part tells a parent." />

**Worked example: one entry, read properly.** A Class 7 pupil in a government elementary school in Larkana receives this entry at the end of a term:

- Subject: General Mathematics. Marks available: 100. Marks obtained: 58. Grade: C.
- Class mean for this paper: 61. Class range: 34 to 92. Pupil's attendance: 42 of 45 days.
- Teacher's remark: "Solves two-digit multiplication accurately and checks her own work. Needs practice with word problems about time and money. Attendance good."

Read as a bare number, 58 out of 100 looks like a middling failure. Read with the class mean and range, it says the pupil sits just below the middle of a class that spreads widely. Read with the remark, it says exactly what to practise, and it says the pupil has a strength to build on. Three entries, three different jobs. A parent who receives only the first has been told very little, and a parent who receives all three can actually help.

> **Common misconception: a report card is a mark list.**
> A mark list reports. A report explains. Boards and parents differ in what they want from a school, but both are short-changed by a document that carries numbers without comparators, without context and without a next step. A teacher who writes a report remark is doing assessment work, not clerical work.

## Activity: Two ways with one set of marks

**Objective:** Convert one set of classroom marks into percentages, grades, a grade point average and a parent-facing remark, and defend the choices you made.  
**Grouping:** Small groups of 3 to 4 trainee teachers.  
**Time:** 30 minutes.

### Instructions

1. Read the data set below. It is the General Science result of a Class 8 section in a government school in Sukkur. The paper carried 50 marks.

   | Pupil | Marks obtained |
   |---|---|
   | Sajid | 41 |
   | Uzma | 38 |
   | Farhan | 35 |
   | Nazia | 29 |
   | Imran | 24 |
   | Rabia | 18 |
   | Kamran | 12 |
   | Hina | 7 |

2. In your group, calculate the percentage for every pupil.
3. Apply the school's banding: A = 80 to 100 percent, B = 70 to 79, C = 60 to 69, D = 50 to 59, E = 40 to 49, F = below 40. Record each pupil's grade.
4. The same eight pupils take four courses in the term, each worth 3 credit hours, and their grades are: Sajid B, A, C, B; Uzma B, B, B, C; Farhan C, B, C, C; Nazia C, C, D, C; Imran D, C, D, D; Rabia D, D, E, D; Kamran E, D, E, F; Hina F, E, F, F. Calculate each pupil's grade point average on the four-point scale in the Explanation. That scale has no E grade, so count each E as an F (0.0 grade points).
5. Choose one pupil and write a report-card remark of no more than three sentences that tells the parent (a) where the pupil stands in relation to the class and (b) exactly what to practise next.
6. Appoint a rapporteur to read your group's remark aloud and to explain one place where the percentage and the grade told slightly different stories.

## Check your understanding

1. Explain in your own words what marking preserves and what grading preserves. Then name one decision a teacher makes that needs marks rather than a grade, and one that needs a grade rather than marks. *(Understand)*
2. A Class 9 pupil obtains 612 marks out of 1100 in the board examination. Calculate the percentage. Then give two reasons why that number, on its own, does not tell a parent what the pupil can do. *(Apply)*
3. A teacher reports only the class mean to parents at a meeting. Give two reasons why a parent whose child scored far above that mean, and two reasons why a parent whose child scored far below it, might both be given a misleading impression. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Marking records how much of a task a pupil got right; grading converts marks into a category with a shared meaning. Marking preserves detail and loses comparability; grading preserves a communicable judgement and loses detail. A percentage scales marks obtained against marks available, and a grade point average weights each course's grade by its credit hours before averaging, which is why a GPA and a percentage can move in different directions. A report card serves pupils, parents, teachers and the school, and it only serves them if it carries a comparator and a next step alongside the mark.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can explain the difference between marking and grading and say what each is best used for.
- [ ] I can calculate a percentage and a weighted grade point average from a small set of course results.
- [ ] I can explain why a percentage and a grade built from the same marks can tell different stories.
- [ ] I can write a report-card entry that gives a parent a comparator and a next step, not only a mark.

## Try this at your practicum school

During your next practicum visit:

1. Ask your cooperating teacher for one recent marked paper and, if the school uses one, a blank copy of its report card.
2. Choose two pupils whose marks are close together but whose work looks different. Write down what each paper shows that the mark does not.
3. Ask the teacher how the school converts marks into grades, and whether the school reports percentages, grades or both.
4. Draft a three-sentence report remark for one of the two pupils and ask the teacher whether it says something a parent could act on.

## Summative task

Write a short briefing (about 350 to 450 words) for the staff room of a government elementary school in Sindh, titled "What our report card should say". In your briefing:

1. Explain the difference between marking and grading, and argue which of the two should appear on the card itself and which should stay in the teacher's record. *(Understand)*
2. Choose either percentages or letter grades for the card, and justify your choice by explaining what each preserves and what each loses for a parent. *(Analyze)*
3. Specify three fields the card must carry besides the mark, and say what each one tells a reader. *(Apply)*

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Below Standard (1 mark) | Meets Standard (2 marks) | Exceeds Standard (3 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Marking versus grading (Understand)** | Describes one and conflates the two, or gives definitions that are vague or circular. | Distinguishes marking from grading accurately and gives a sound reason for each use. | Explains the trade-off between detail and comparability with a concrete classroom example. |
| **Choice of reporting scale (Analyze)** | Picks a scale without justifying it, or repeats the definitions instead of weighing them. | Justifies the choice of percentages or grades by naming what each preserves and loses for a parent. | Justifies the choice and anticipates the strongest objection to it, using a Pakistan school context. |
| **Report-card fields** | Lists fields without saying what each communicates. | Specifies three fields and states what each tells a reader. | Specifies three fields, states what each tells a reader, and explains how they work together to describe one pupil. |
