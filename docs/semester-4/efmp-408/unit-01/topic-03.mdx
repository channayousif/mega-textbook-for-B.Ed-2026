# Topic 1.3 - Diagnostic, placement, and two reference frames

## A real classroom situation

At the beginning of the school year in a public high school in Nawabshah, Mr Farooq welcomed 45 newly admitted Class 9 students into his physics class. On the second day of school, before introducing Newton's laws of motion, he administered a 20-item screening test. The test did not cover high school physics; instead, it assessed basic algebraic equation solving, unit conversions, and reading speed in English and Urdu.

When he scored the papers, he noticed that while almost all pupils could perform simple arithmetic, 16 students could not rearrange algebraic formulas such as solving for $a$ in $v = u + at$. Mr Farooq realised that if he began teaching kinematics immediately, these 16 students would fail, not because they lacked scientific curiosity, but because they lacked a critical prerequisite skill. He spent the remainder of the first week conducting targeted algebra workshops during zero period.

A colleague remarked: "Why did you waste time giving a test that does not count for board examination marks?" Mr Farooq replied: "This was a placement and diagnostic assessment. A placement test told me where each child stands relative to course prerequisites, and the diagnostic analysis pinpointed the exact mechanical skill holding them back. If I had not checked, I would have discovered their gap in November during the terminal examination, when it would have been too late."

## Explanation

### Diagnostic and placement assessment

While teachers are most familiar with tests administered during or at the end of units, assessment conducted before instruction begins is equally critical. Two specialised forms of assessment guide pre-instructional planning:

#### 1. Placement assessment
Placement assessment is conducted prior to instruction to determine a learner's entry level, prerequisite knowledge, and appropriate educational placement (HEC, 2012; HEC, 2013). Its central purpose is to answer the question: *Where should this student begin?*
In Pakistani education, placement assessments are used when admitting students into schools, assigning learners to specific academic streams (e.g., pre-medical, pre-engineering, or humanities at the higher secondary level), or grouping pupils for differentiated reading instruction in primary grades. Placement decisions ensure that instruction begins at an appropriate level of challenge, preventing frustration and disengagement.

#### 2. Diagnostic assessment
Diagnostic assessment seeks to identify the specific learning difficulties, persistent errors, and conceptual misunderstandings that impede a student's academic progress (HEC, 2012). While all formative assessments have a diagnostic flavor, formal diagnostic assessment resembles a medical investigation: it delves beneath surface errors to uncover root causes.
For example, if a Class 5 pupil repeatedly gets subtraction problems incorrect, a summative test merely records a low score. A diagnostic assessment investigates the error pattern, determining whether the child struggles with basic number facts, fails to borrow across zeroes, or reads the subtraction symbol from right to left. By identifying the precise conceptual breakdown, diagnostic assessment enables teachers to design targeted remedial interventions.

### Norm-referenced interpretation

A raw score on an examination (e.g., 36 out of 50) conveys little meaning in isolation. To make sense of student performance, educators interpret raw scores through one of two fundamental reference frames: norm-referenced or criterion-referenced (HEC, 2013; Seifert & Sutton, 2009).

In a norm-referenced interpretation, a student's score is compared to the performance of a reference group of peers, called the norm group (HEC, 2013). The primary question answered by a norm-referenced score is: *How well did this student perform compared to others?*

Key features of norm-referenced assessment include:

- **Relative standing:** Performance is expressed through comparative metrics such as class ranks (e.g., 5th out of 40), percentiles (e.g., 85th percentile), stanines, or standard scores.
- **Normal curve distribution:** Items are specifically chosen to maximize score variance, spreading students across a wide bell curve to distinguish high achievers from low achievers.
- **Selective function:** Norm-referenced testing is ideal when resources or spaces are fixed, such as competitive entrance examinations (e.g., MDCAT, ECAT), civil service examinations, or selecting recipients for a limited number of university merit scholarships.

However, norm-referenced interpretation has severe limitations in classroom teaching:
1. It tells you who ranked highest, but does not indicate what specific curricular competencies the students have actually mastered.
2. If an entire class is exceptional, half the class will still receive below-average norm scores. Conversely, in a severely underperforming school, the top-ranked student might still lack fundamental literacy or numeracy skills.
3. It fosters peer competition rather than collaborative learning, leading students to focus on outperforming classmates rather than mastering knowledge.

> **Common Misconception: Norm-referenced tests are inherently harder than criterion-referenced tests.**
> Many teachers assume that because norm-referenced tests are used in competitive university admissions, they must be intellectually more difficult. In reality, the difference between norm- and criterion-referenced testing is not the difficulty of the questions, but the lens used to interpret the score. A simple spelling quiz of 20 words can be interpreted in a criterion-referenced manner ("Bilal spelled 18 words correctly and met the 80% spelling standard") or in a norm-referenced manner ("Bilal ranked in the top 10% of his class on this spelling quiz"). The test content is identical; the reference frame changes.

### Criterion-referenced interpretation

In a criterion-referenced interpretation, a student's performance is compared directly against predetermined learning standards, specific behavioural objectives, or performance benchmarks, independent of how other students performed (HEC, 2013; Seifert & Sutton, 2009). The central question answered by a criterion-referenced score is: *What specific knowledge and skills has this student mastered?*

Key features of criterion-referenced assessment include:

- **Absolute standards:** Success is determined by whether the student demonstrates the required competency against an explicit cut score (e.g., answering 75% of items correctly or meeting all indicators on an analytic rubric).
- **Mastery orientation:** Every student in a class has the potential to succeed. If all 40 students demonstrate mastery of two-digit multiplication, all 40 receive passing marks. Performance is not graded on a curve.
- **Curricular transparency:** Criterion-referenced scores provide transparent, actionable diagnostic data. A report card indicates: "The student can calculate areas of triangles and rectangles, but needs additional practice on trapezoids."

In primary and secondary classrooms, criterion-referenced assessment is pedagogically superior because it supports cooperative learning, aligns directly with the Student Learning Outcomes (SLOs) of the National Curriculum, and provides clear diagnostic guidance for both teachers and parents.

### Selecting the appropriate reference frame

Professional teachers must be able to choose the appropriate reference frame based on the institutional purpose of the assessment:

- **Use Criterion-Referenced Interpretation when:**
  - Guiding daily classroom instruction, homework, and unit quizzes.
  - Determining whether pupils have mastered foundational competencies required for subsequent learning.
  - Conducting professional licensing examinations (such as the Sindh Teaching Licence), where the public requires assurance that every certified teacher meets rigorous professional standards.
  - Administering driver's licence tests or safety certifications, where passing must guarantee absolute competence rather than relative ranking.
- **Use Norm-Referenced Interpretation when:**
  - Selecting candidates for a fixed quota of seats (e.g., selecting the top 50 applicants for a government college scholarship).
  - Comparing regional or district-level educational performance across provinces on national sample surveys.
  - Screening candidates in highly competitive civil service or university entrance testing where cutoffs must adjust to candidate supply.

## Activity: Reference-frame selection workshop

**Objective:** Analyse assessment scenarios and justify whether norm-referenced or criterion-referenced interpretation is appropriate.  
**Grouping:** Small groups of 3 to 4 trainee teachers.  
**Time:** 30 minutes.

### Instructions

1. Examine the four assessment scenarios below from Pakistani education:
   - **Scenario 1:** The Sindh Education and Literacy Department administers the Sindh Teaching Licence Examination to certify prospective elementary school teachers.
   - **Scenario 2:** A medical university has 250 available seats and 8,000 applicants who sit for the centralised MDCAT examination.
   - **Scenario 3:** A Class 3 primary teacher administers an end-of-week phonics assessment to determine which children need remedial reading support on vowel digraphs.
   - **Scenario 4:** A sports academy must select the top four fastest sprinters from 100 student athletes to represent the district at the provincial games.
2. For each scenario:
   - Identify whether the decision requires a *Norm-Referenced* or *Criterion-Referenced* interpretation.
   - State the reference point (e.g., peer rank versus performance standard).
   - Explain what unintended harm would occur if the opposite reference frame were mistakenly used (e.g., what happens if a medical entrance exam uses an arbitrary 50% pass mark when seats are limited, or if a primary reading test ranks children without checking if they can read).
3. Present your group's analysis to the plenary class.

## Summary

Diagnostic assessment identifies specific underlying learning difficulties and misconceptions, while placement assessment determines prerequisite readiness and appropriate curricular entry points. Norm-referenced interpretations compare a student's score against a peer norm group to establish relative rank, making them suitable for competitive selection with fixed quotas. Criterion-referenced interpretations compare student performance directly against established learning standards and competencies, making them ideal for diagnostic feedback, classroom teaching, and professional licensing. Effective teachers select the reference frame that aligns with their educational objective, prioritizing criterion-referenced frames for everyday classroom instruction.
