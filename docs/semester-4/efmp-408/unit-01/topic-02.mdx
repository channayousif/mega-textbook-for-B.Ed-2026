# Topic 1.2 - Shaping learning and reporting it

## A real classroom situation

In a government boys' high school in Mirpurkhas, Mr Rashid is teaching a unit on linear equations to Class 7. In past years, he taught the entire three-week chapter through lecture, wrote worked examples on the chalkboard, assigned homework exercises, and administered a single 50-mark chapter examination at the end. Inevitably, more than half the class failed the test, leaving Mr Rashid frustrated and students disheartened.

This term, Mr Rashid changed his approach after attending an in-service workshop on classroom assessment. On the fourth day of teaching, instead of lecturing for the full period, he paused after fifteen minutes and gave every pupil a small slate board. He wrote $3x + 5 = 20$ on the board and asked all students to solve the first step simultaneously and hold up their slates. Scanning the room, he saw immediately that 18 out of 35 students had written $3x = 20 + 5 = 25$, incorrectly adding 5 instead of subtracting it when transposing across the equals sign.

Rather than scolding the class or recording zero marks, Mr Rashid addressed the common error immediately: "Look at your slates. Notice how many of us added 5. What is the opposite operation of adding 5?" A student answered: "Subtracting 5!" Within five minutes of targeted clarification, the entire class re-attempted two fresh problems successfully. By checking understanding mid-stream, Mr Rashid used assessment formatively to rescue learning before the summative examination took place.

## Explanation

### Formative assessment: assessment that shapes learning

Formative assessment, often referred to as assessment for learning (AfL), is an ongoing process conducted by teachers and students during instruction to provide feedback that modifies teaching and learning activities while they are happening (Black & Wiliam, 1998; HEC, 2012). It is not defined by the format of a paper or quiz, but by how the resulting evidence is used: if the information is used to adapt instructional moves and guide learner practice, the assessment is functioning formatively.

In an effective Pakistani classroom, formative assessment takes many informal and structured forms:

- **Targeted oral questioning:** Asking open-ended questions that require learners to justify their thinking (e.g., "Why did you choose to divide rather than multiply in step two?").
- **All-pupil response systems:** Using slate boards, hand signals (thumbs up, thumbs sideways), or index cards so every pupil responds simultaneously, preventing a few vocal students from masking widespread confusion.
- **Exit tickets:** Distributing a single conceptual question during the final five minutes of a period, which pupils submit upon leaving. The teacher reviews these cards to plan the starting point for tomorrow's lesson.
- **Constructive peer feedback:** Guiding pupils to review a classmate's draft essay against a clear checklist before final submission.

A central element of formative assessment is descriptive feedforward. Unlike evaluative feedback that simply awards marks or praises ability (e.g., "Good effort, 6/10"), descriptive feedback tells the learner three essential things:
1. What the learning target is (*Where am I going?*).
2. What current work demonstrates (*Where am I now?*).
3. Exactly what action will close the gap (*How do I get there?*).

> **Common Misconception: Formative assessment simply means ungraded assessment.**
> Many prospective teachers assume that if an assessment does not carry marks, it is formative, and if it carries marks, it is summative. This is inaccurate. An assessment is not formative merely because marks are omitted; it is formative only when the information gathered leads to adjustments in instruction and student learning. If a teacher administers an ungraded quiz on Friday, marks it, hands it back on Monday without discussion, and proceeds immediately to a new chapter, that quiz did not function formatively. Conversely, low-stakes weekly quizzes can serve formative ends if teacher and students analyse errors to guide subsequent revision before major exams.

### Summative assessment: assessment that reports learning

Summative assessment, frequently termed assessment of learning (AoL), occurs at the conclusion of an instructional period, such as the end of a unit, term, semester, or academic year. Its primary purpose is to judge and report cumulative student achievement against curricular standards, certify competence, assign formal marks or grades, and hold schools accountable (HEC, 2012; Seifert & Sutton, 2009).

Characteristics of summative assessment include:

- **Periodic and cumulative:** It sums up what a student knows and can do after an extended period of instruction.
- **Higher stakes:** Results often determine official report card marks, class rankings, promotion to the next grade level, scholarship eligibility, or admission into secondary and tertiary programs.
- **Standardised conditions:** To ensure fairness and comparability across pupils, summative assessments typically utilise uniform instructions, time allowances, and objective or rubric-based scoring keys.
- **External reporting:** While formative feedback is primarily for the immediate learner-teacher dialogue, summative reporting informs external audiences, including parents, head teachers, school management committees, and provincial education boards (BISE).

### Complementary roles in the Pakistani school system

Formative and summative assessments are not opposing philosophies; they are complementary components of a comprehensive assessment system. A teacher who relies exclusively on summative assessment discovers student failure only when it is too late to remediate. Conversely, a teacher who conducts only informal formative checks without periodic summative evaluations lacks verified evidence of whether cumulative curricular mastery was achieved.

As the HEC (2012) course guide emphasizes, the most powerful educational gains occur when formative and summative assessments are closely aligned:

1. **Alignment:** Formative classroom activities must mirror the cognitive rigor, problem types, and competencies that appear on end-of-term summative examinations.
2. **Feedforward to summative success:** Continuous formative checks diagnose and eliminate misconceptions early, ensuring that when students sit for summative examinations, they have developed genuine mastery.
3. **Summative data used formatively:** An experienced teacher analyses terminal examination results not just to assign report card marks, but to evaluate her own pedagogical efficacy, identifying curricular units that require improved instructional methods during the following academic year.

## Activity: Designing formative feedforward

**Objective:** Transform evaluative feedback into constructive, descriptive feedforward.  
**Grouping:** Pairs of student teachers.  
**Time:** 30 minutes.

### Instructions

1. Examine the three samples of actual teacher feedback collected from primary school exercise books in Sindh:
   - **Sample 1 (Urdu composition):** The teacher wrote "Good, but handwriting is untidy. 6/10."
   - **Sample 2 (General Science):** The teacher crossed out an answer on plant germination with a large red 'X' and wrote "Wrong! Read page 42 again. 2/5."
   - **Sample 3 (Mathematics):** The teacher marked three fraction problems incorrect and wrote "Careless mistakes! Practice at home. 4/10."
2. In your pair, discuss why each sample fails to support formative learning:
   - Does the student know exactly what error occurred?
   - Does the comment provide guidance on how to fix the error?
   - How might this feedback affect student motivation and self-efficacy?
3. Rewrite each comment as an exemplary piece of descriptive feedforward that specifies:
   - What the student did well (positive reinforcement).
   - The exact nature of the misconception or error.
   - A concrete prompt or question directing the student on how to correct the work.
4. Exchange your rewritten feedforward comments with another pair and provide peer feedback.

## Check your understanding

1. Explain the fundamental difference in purpose, timing, and stakes between formative and summative assessment. *(Understand)*
2. A secondary school teacher administers a short 5-minute quiz at the end of every Friday lesson. She records the marks in her grade book but never discusses the questions with students. Is this practice functioning formatively or summatively? Justify your response. *(Apply)*
3. Identify three low-resource formative assessment techniques that can be used effectively in a crowded Pakistani classroom of 50 or more pupils. *(Remember)*
4. Contrast the pedagogical impact of receiving a score of "14/20" versus receiving descriptive feedforward on a draft laboratory report. *(Analyze)*

## Summary

Formative assessment (assessment for learning) is an ongoing, low-stakes diagnostic process designed to make learning visible and guide immediate instructional adaptations. It relies on descriptive feedforward that directs students on how to close learning gaps. Summative assessment (assessment of learning) evaluates cumulative achievement at the end of an instructional period, assigning marks, certifying mastery, and reporting outcomes to external stakeholders. Effective teaching requires a balanced, aligned integration of both: formative assessment fosters mastery during learning, while summative assessment documents achievement at its completion.

## Try this at your practicum school

During your practicum teaching placement:

1. Select one lesson that you will teach to an elementary class.
2. Design two specific formative assessment checks to insert into the lesson (for example, a mid-lesson slate check or a pair-share exit ticket).
3. During the lesson, execute the checks and record what evidence you gathered about student understanding.
4. Document how you adapted your teaching on the spot based on that evidence (e.g., re-explaining a concept, pairing struggling pupils with peers, or moving faster because mastery was evident).
5. Share your reflection with your cooperative mentor teacher.

## Summative task

Develop a two-week assessment plan for a selected unit in Class 6 General Science or Social Studies (approx. 450 words). Your plan must:

1. Detail at least three distinct formative assessment strategies embedded across the two weeks, explaining how the evidence from each check will be used to guide teaching before the unit concludes.
2. Design the end-of-unit summative assessment, specifying its structure, question formats, and scoring approach.
3. Provide a clear justification analysing how the formative checks directly prepare pupils to succeed on the summative assessment without resorting to narrow rote memorisation.
