# Topic 2.1 - Objectives, domains and alignment

## A real classroom situation

At a government girls' primary school in Khairpur, Ms Saima teaches Class 6 General Science. On Monday she writes her lesson objective on the board: "Students will learn about photosynthesis." She then lectures for thirty minutes, draws a labelled leaf on the board, and reads two pages from the textbook. On Friday she sets a test item: "Design an experiment to test whether a plant needs light." Only two of her thirty-eight pupils answer it correctly, and Ms Saima concludes that the class is weak in science.

Her mentor, Mr Lohana, reviews the paper with her. He points out that the objective asked pupils to remember what photosynthesis is, while the test item asked them to create a new experimental design. "Your teaching and your test are speaking two different languages," he says. "The objective, the activity and the item must all ask for the same kind of thinking. If you want pupils to design an experiment, you must teach them to design experiments, and write that into the objective." Ms Saima's pupils were not weak; the assessment was misaligned with what had been taught and asked for.

## Explanation

### Writing instructional objectives with Bloom's Taxonomy

An <Glossary term="Instructional objective">instructional objective</Glossary> is a precise statement of what learners will be able to do by the end of a lesson, a unit or a course. It is written in observable and measurable language, so that a teacher can look at pupil work and decide whether the objective has been met. The HEC course guide for test development defines a learning outcome as a statement of what students should know, be able to do, or be able to demonstrate after completing a lesson or course, and stresses that it must use a command word that names an observable action (HEC, 2012a).

A <Glossary term="Bloom Taxonomy">Bloom's Taxonomy</Glossary> is the most widely used framework for ordering those actions by difficulty. It groups cognitive tasks into six levels, from the recall of facts to the creation of something new. The levels, with the command verbs that belong to each, are:

1. **Remember:** recall facts, terms or basic concepts. Verbs: *list, define, name, identify, state*.
2. **Understand:** explain ideas in your own words. Verbs: *explain, describe, summarise, classify, compare*.
3. **Apply:** use a procedure or concept in a new situation. Verbs: *solve, use, demonstrate, calculate, illustrate*.
4. **Analyze:** break material into parts and relate them. Verbs: *differentiate, organise, compare, examine, infer*.
5. **Evaluate:** judge the value of something against criteria. Verbs: *judge, justify, critique, recommend, defend*.
6. **Create:** combine parts to make something new. Verbs: *design, construct, formulate, compose, develop*.

The command word is what makes an objective assessable. "Students will appreciate the water cycle" names no observable action, so a teacher cannot tell whether appreciation has happened. "Students will be able to explain the water cycle using a labelled diagram" names an action a teacher can see.

> **Common Misconception: Writing an objective is just restating the textbook heading.**
> Many beginning teachers write objectives such as "Students will learn about fractions" or "Students will study the Indus Valley civilisation." A heading names content; an objective names what the learner will do with that content. "Students will learn about fractions" could be assessed by a recall test, a problem-solving task or an essay, and the teacher would not know which. A useful objective names an observable behaviour at a stated level, so the teacher knows what to teach and what to assess.

#### Worked example: from a vague objective to an assessable one

Consider a Class 5 mathematics lesson on equivalent fractions in a government primary school in Sukkur.

- **Vague objective:** "Students will learn about equivalent fractions."
- **Improved objective:** "By the end of the lesson, pupils will be able to generate two equivalent fractions for a given fraction and explain why they represent the same value." This objective sits at the Apply level and names an observable behaviour.
- **Matching item:** "Write two fractions equivalent to 3/4 and draw a diagram to show why they are equal."

The improved objective tells the teacher to give pupils practice in generating equivalents, and it tells the assessor exactly what to look for. The vague version tells neither.

### The three domains: cognitive, affective, psychomotor

Learning does not happen only in the mind. The HEC course guide distinguishes three types of learning outcome: cognitive outcomes, which concern knowledge and mental skills; affective outcomes, which concern growth in feelings, attitudes and values; and psychomotor outcomes, which concern manual and physical skills (HEC, 2012a). A teacher who writes only cognitive objectives misses two thirds of what schooling is for.

- **Cognitive domain:** knowledge and thinking. Example: "Pupils can state the three conditions seed germination needs." This is the domain most classroom tests assess, and it is the domain Bloom's Taxonomy orders.
- **Affective domain:** attitudes, motivation, values and appreciation. Example: "Pupils will show respect for a classmate's opinion during a group discussion on whether plants are living things." In a Pakistani classroom, affective objectives can support discipline, cooperation and care for living things.
- **Psychomotor domain:** physical and manual skills. Example: "Pupils will be able to use a microscope to focus a prepared slide of a leaf epidermis." In science and practical subjects, psychomotor objectives are often the real point of a lesson.

#### Worked example: one topic, three domains

A Class 6 General Science lesson on the water cycle in a school in Larkana can be planned with one objective in each domain:

1. **Cognitive (Understand):** "Pupils will be able to explain, in their own words, how evaporation and condensation produce rain."
2. **Affective:** "Pupils will value clean water by listing two reasons why protecting local ponds matters to the community."
3. **Psychomotor:** "Pupils will be able to assemble and label a simple diagram of the water cycle using cut-out cards."

A test that assesses only the cognitive objective is not wrong, but it is incomplete. A teacher who also observes the group task and the card activity gathers evidence of all three.

### Alignment between objectives, teaching and assessment

<Glossary term="Alignment (assessment)">Alignment</Glossary> means that the objective, the teaching activity and the assessment item all demand the same kind of thinking. The HEC classroom assessment guide describes a strong lesson plan as one in which instructional activities, individual learning outcomes and assessment strategies are clearly aligned (HEC, 2012b). When they are not, pupils are assessed on something they were never taught to do, which is unfair and produces misleading scores.

Alignment can break in three places:

1. **Objective to activity:** the objective asks pupils to evaluate, but the lesson only asks them to copy notes.
2. **Activity to item:** the lesson has pupils discuss and compare, but the test only asks them to recall definitions.
3. **Objective to item:** the objective names an observable behaviour, but the item tests a different, often lower, level of thinking.

#### Worked example: an aligned and a misaligned set

Consider a Class 7 English lesson on formal letter writing in a government boys' high school in Hyderabad.

- **Misaligned set:** the objective says "pupils will appreciate the format of a formal letter"; the activity has pupils copy a sample letter from the board; the item asks them to write a complete formal letter to the head teacher requesting a library card. The objective is affective and vague, the activity is passive, and the item demands a Create-level performance. Pupils who copied perfectly still fail.
- **Aligned set:** the objective says "pupils will be able to write a formal letter that includes a correct greeting, a clear purpose and a proper closing"; the activity has pupils analyse a model letter, identify its parts, draft one in pairs and give peer feedback against a checklist; the item asks them to write a formal letter to the head teacher requesting a library card. Objective, activity and item all demand the same performance.

The second set is harder to teach, but it is fair. The pupil's score now means something about letter writing, not about luck.

## Activity: Objective surgery and alignment

**Objective:** Rewrite vague objectives, place them at the correct Bloom level, and align a teaching activity and an assessment item with each.  
**Grouping:** Pairs of trainee teachers.  
**Time:** 30 minutes.

### Instructions

1. Work through the four objectives below, taken from real Pakistani primary and secondary lesson plans:
   - **A:** "Students will learn about the freedom movement."
   - **B:** "Students will understand fractions."
   - **C:** "Students will appreciate the importance of trees."
   - **D:** "Students will study the water cycle."
2. For each objective, rewrite it so that it names an observable behaviour at a stated Bloom level, suitable for the grade you expect to teach.
3. For each rewritten objective, choose one teaching activity that practises the behaviour, and write one assessment item that demands the same behaviour.
4. In your pair, check each other's work against three questions:
   - Could a teacher observe the behaviour in pupil work?
   - Does the activity give pupils practice in the behaviour?
   - Does the item demand the same level as the objective?
5. Exchange one rewritten set with another pair and comment on whether the alignment holds. Revise where it does not.

## Check your understanding

1. Define an instructional objective in one sentence, and explain why the verb "understand" is difficult to assess unless it is made more specific. *(Understand)*
2. Rewrite the vague objective "Students will learn about the water cycle" as an observable objective at the Apply level, and name one item that would match it. *(Apply)*
3. A teacher's objective says "pupils will appreciate the importance of trees", her lesson has pupils copy a list of uses of trees, and her test asks pupils to design a poster campaigning to save trees. Explain where the alignment breaks and how you would repair it. *(Apply)*

## Summary

An instructional objective states what learners will be able to do, in observable language, at a stated level of Bloom's Taxonomy. Bloom orders cognitive tasks from Remember to Create, and the cognitive, affective and psychomotor domains remind a teacher that knowledge, attitudes and physical skills are all worth teaching. Alignment means the objective, the teaching activity and the assessment item demand the same behaviour, so that a pupil's score measures what was taught rather than the accident of a mismatch.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can define an instructional objective and explain what makes one observable and measurable.
- [ ] I can name the six levels of Bloom's Taxonomy and give a command verb for each.
- [ ] I can distinguish the cognitive, affective and psychomotor domains and write one objective in each.
- [ ] I can spot where an objective, a teaching activity and an assessment item do not align, and repair the mismatch.

## Try this at your practicum school

During your next practicum visit:

1. Ask your mentor teacher for one lesson plan or, if none is written, for the topic and the test paper of a recent lesson.
2. Identify the objective the lesson was trying to reach, the main teaching activity, and the main assessment item.
3. Judge whether the three are aligned, and write a short note naming the Bloom level of the objective and the item.
4. If they are misaligned, rewrite the objective and suggest one activity and one item that would align with it. Share your note with your mentor teacher.

## Summative task

Draft an aligned assessment set (approx. 400 to 500 words) for one topic you are likely to teach, in a named grade and subject. Your set must contain:

1. One cognitive objective at Analyze level or higher, one affective objective and one psychomotor objective, each written in observable language.
2. A short description of a teaching activity that practises the cognitive objective.
3. Two assessment items: one that matches the cognitive objective and one that does not. Label each as aligned or misaligned and explain why.
4. A short justification analysing how the misaligned item could distort a pupil's score and what a teacher should change.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Below Standard (1 mark) | Meets Standard (2 marks) | Exceeds Standard (3 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Objective quality** | Objectives are vague headings or name no observable behaviour. | Objectives are observable and placed in the correct domain. | Objectives are precise, appropriately levelled and clearly distinguish the three domains. |
| **Alignment analysis (Analyze)** | Describes the activities and items without identifying the mismatch. | Identifies where the objective, activity and item diverge and explains the consequence. | Gives a precise, well-evidenced analysis of the mismatch and its effect on the meaning of the score. |
| **Repair and justification** | Suggests a generic improvement such as "make the test easier." | Proposes a realistic aligned item and a brief reason. | Proposes a well-justified aligned set and explains how the repair protects the validity of the result. |
