# Unit 2: Objectives, Alignment and the Principles of Good Assessment - Teacher Notes

## Instructional rationale and sequencing

Unit 2 moves students from the question "what is assessment?" to the question "how do I design an assessment that deserves trust?". Many prospective teachers in Pakistan arrive with the belief that a test is simply a paper set at the end of a chapter, and that a good test is one that is difficult and thorough. The pedagogical goal of this unit is to replace that belief with a design habit: write an observable objective, align teaching and assessment with it, and then check the test for validity, reliability, practicality and fairness.

### Recommended sequencing across two weeks

1. **Week 3, Session 1:** Open with the classroom situation in Topic 2.1. Use the comparison table in Figure 2.1 to introduce the six Bloom levels and their command verbs. Have students rewrite vague objectives in pairs.
2. **Week 3, Session 2:** Teach the three domains and alignment. Use the flowchart in Figure 2.4 to walk through an objective-activity-item set, and ask students to locate the break in a deliberately misaligned example.
3. **Week 4, Session 1:** Teach Topic 2.2. Use the target diagram in Figure 2.2 to separate reliability from validity, and the concept map in Figure 2.5 to organise the three validity types. Run the target-practice activity.
4. **Week 4, Session 2:** Teach Topic 2.3. Use the practicality table in Figure 2.3 and the bias diagram in Figure 2.6, then run the bias-hunt activity. Close with the unit assessment and a staff-room style discussion of a sample paper.

## Managing common student misconceptions

- **Misconception 1: "Writing an objective is just restating the textbook heading."**  
  *Strategy:* Ask students to write an objective for a familiar topic, then ask "what would a pupil do that would show you the objective was met?" The answer reveals whether the objective names an observable behaviour. Contrast "students will learn about fractions" with "students will generate two equivalent fractions for a given fraction".
- **Misconception 2: "A reliable test must be valid."**  
  *Strategy:* Use the target metaphor. Draw concentric circles on the board and ask three volunteers to throw chalk from a fixed distance. A tight group away from the centre is reliable but not valid. Then ask what a test that always measures the wrong skill looks like. Emphasise that consistency is necessary, not sufficient.
- **Misconception 3: "Validity is a property of the test rather than of an inference."**  
  *Strategy:* Take one test and ask three different questions of it: can this score show that a pupil can write an essay, can it show that a pupil can reason scientifically, can it predict next year's result? The same score answers the three questions differently, which shows that validity belongs to the inference.
- **Misconception 4: "Fair means identical for everyone."**  
  *Strategy:* Present a scenario in which a pupil with low vision is given the same paper in the same size and the same time as everyone else. Ask whether the pupil had a genuine opportunity to show the skill. Then discuss what a legitimate allowance would be, and why it protects rather than lowers the standard.

## Practical work and classroom exercises

The University of Sindh course guide lists lectures, interactive discussions, question-answer sessions, demonstration, case-study analysis, hands-on computer exercises, individual and group assignments, internet research tasks and presentations as teaching strategies for this course. The following practical exercises fit Unit 2 and need no special technology.

### 1. Objective writing clinic
Give each group four vague objectives drawn from real Pakistani lesson plans. Ask them to rewrite each one at a chosen Bloom level, then place it in the cognitive, affective or psychomotor domain. Groups exchange their rewrites and check them against one question: could a teacher see the behaviour in pupil work?

### 2. Alignment audit of a sample paper
Provide a short sample test and its stated objective. Ask pairs to trace the objective to the teaching activity and then to the item, and to mark each link as aligned or misaligned. A plenary compiles one corrected set on the board.

### 3. Marker agreement workshop
Give two teachers the same six short scripts to mark independently. Record the totals and ask the class where the markers diverge. Then hand out a simple analytic rubric and have the teachers re-mark the same scripts. Compare the two rounds and name the gain in inter-rater reliability.

### 4. Fairness and practicality review
Present a case study of a school with one teacher, 60 pupils, limited time and limited materials. Ask groups to redesign an over-ambitious examination into a practical and fair assessment plan, and to justify each change in terms of validity, reliability, practicality and fairness.
