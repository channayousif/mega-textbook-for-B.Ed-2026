# Topic 2.3 - Practicality, fairness and bias

## A real classroom situation

At a government primary school in Sanghar, Ms Riffat designs what she believes is a thorough end-of-term assessment for her 60 Class 5 pupils. She plans 40 essay items, an individual oral test for every pupil, and a three-hour written paper. On the day of the examination the school has no electricity, the ceiling fans are still, the single hall must accommodate two classes at once, and only three working pens can be found. Pupils sweat, cannot read their own writing, and finish at very different times. The oral test alone would take four days, which the timetable does not have.

Her head teacher, Mr Mahar, sits with her afterwards. "Your assessment is ambitious," he says, "but it is not practical. A good test is not the one that looks the most thorough on paper. It is the one your school can actually administer, mark and use." Together they redesign the assessment: 20 multiple-choice items, one short-answer section, one group practical task assessed with a simple checklist, and a short weekly quiz reused as the revision record. The new paper fits the hall, the timetable and the marking time available, and it still covers the unit.

## Explanation

### Practicality and usability

<Glossary term="Practicality (assessment)">Practicality</Glossary> asks whether a test can be administered, scored and used with the resources a school actually has. The HEC test development guide treats the utility of a test as a practical matter, asking whether the test can be scored, interpreted and reported with the time, staff and materials available (HEC, 2012a). A test that is valid in principle but impossible to run is of no use to the pupils who sit it.

Practicality is a set of trade-offs, not a single yes-or-no judgement:

- **Time:** how long pupils need to complete the paper and how long teachers need to mark it. A 40-item essay paper for 60 pupils can take a week to mark.
- **Staffing:** whether one teacher can supervise and mark alone, or whether the plan needs colleagues who are already teaching.
- **Materials and cost:** pens, paper, printing, apparatus, electricity and transport for practical work.
- **Length and item type:** objective items mark quickly but write slowly; essay items write quickly but mark slowly. A balanced paper trades one against the other.
- **Conditions:** heat, noise, seating, lighting and the distance between pupils all affect whether the test measures learning or endurance.

**Usability** is the partner of practicality. A test is usable when its results are easy to record, easy to interpret and useful to the people who act on them: the teacher planning the next lesson, the head teacher reviewing a class, and the parents reading a report. A complicated rubric that only the setter understands fails usability even if the test itself is sound.

#### Worked example: choosing a practical test for 60 pupils

Ms Riffat's redesigned Class 5 paper illustrates the trade-offs:

- **20 multiple-choice items** on the unit's key facts: quick to mark with a key, but only able to test recall and simple understanding.
- **One short-answer section** of four questions: still manageable to mark in an afternoon, and able to test explanation.
- **One group practical task** with a five-point checklist: assessed during the lesson, so it adds no marking evening.
- **One short weekly quiz** kept in a folder: reused as a revision record and as evidence for the report card.

The set covers recall, explanation and practical skill, and it can be administered and marked by one teacher in a single week. That is practicality in practice.

### Fairness and freedom from bias

<Glossary term="Fairness (assessment)">Fairness</Glossary> means that every pupil has a genuine opportunity to show what they know and can do. A fair test does not favour pupils because of their language, gender, neighbourhood, school or disability. The HEC classroom assessment guide warns that assessment is not a neutral enterprise: the same teacher who uses assessment data to make decisions also made the decisions that created the assessment, and assessments must be designed to be fair and unbiased (HEC, 2012b).

Bias is any feature of a test that systematically advantages one group of pupils over another for reasons unrelated to the skill being measured. Common sources in Pakistani classrooms include:

1. **Language bias:** items written in formal English for Urdu-medium pupils, or in a regional language unfamiliar to some pupils. A science item then measures reading, not science.
2. **Cultural and experiential bias:** items that assume experience with travel, airports, snow, foreign currency or city life. A pupil from interior Sindh who has never seen snow may fail a mathematics item about ski resorts, not because of mathematics, but because of context.
3. **Gender bias:** examples that show only boys as doctors and only girls as nurses, or questions that assume one gender is naturally better at a subject.
4. **Socio-economic bias:** items that assume access to books, the internet, tutoring or a quiet room to study.
5. **Disability bias:** a paper that cannot be read by a pupil with low vision, or a timed test that penalises a pupil who writes slowly for a physical reason.
6. **Teaching-to-the-test bias:** when the test covers only what the teacher happened to teach, pupils who had a different teacher or a disrupted year are penalised for circumstances beyond their control.

> **Common Misconception: Fair means identical for everyone.**
> Many teachers believe that fairness requires every pupil to sit exactly the same paper under exactly the same conditions, with no allowances. In truth, identical treatment can produce unequal opportunity. A pupil with low vision needs a larger print; a pupil who writes slowly may need extra time; a pupil new to English may need a glossary of non-technical words. Fairness is not sameness. It is the removal of barriers that are irrelevant to the skill being assessed, so that each pupil's score reflects that skill rather than an avoidable disadvantage.

#### Worked example: one item, two contexts

Consider a Class 5 mathematics item on percentages.

- **Biased version:** "A ski resort sells 40 skis in December and 60 skis in January. By what percentage did sales increase?" A pupil in a Sindhi village where there is no snow may never have seen a ski, and may struggle to picture the situation. The item may measure familiarity with winter sports as much as percentage skill.
- **Fair version:** "A fruit seller in the Sanghar market sells 40 kilograms of apples on Monday and 60 kilograms on Tuesday. By what percentage did sales increase?" The mathematical structure is identical, but the context is familiar to almost every pupil.

The fair version does not lower the demand of the item. It removes a barrier that was never part of what the item was meant to measure.

## Activity: Bias hunt and practicality fix

**Objective:** Detect sources of bias in a sample test and redesign it to be practical for a named school.  
**Grouping:** Small groups of 3 to 4 trainee teachers.  
**Time:** 30 minutes.

### Instructions

1. Read the sample Class 6 General Science test below, set for a government primary school in Tharparkar:
   - **Item 1:** "A family drives 240 kilometres in 3 hours on the motorway. What is their average speed?"
   - **Item 2:** "Explain how a refrigerator keeps food cold."
   - **Item 3:** "A boy flies a kite every evening after cricket practice. Describe two forces acting on the kite."
   - **Item 4:** "Write an essay of 300 words on the wildlife of Canada."
   - **Item 5:** "Draw a labelled diagram of a plant cell using the microscope in the laboratory."
2. For each item, identify any source of bias (language, culture, gender, socio-economic, disability, or teaching-to-the-test) and state which pupils it could disadvantage.
3. The school has one teacher, 55 pupils, no microscope, no refrigerator and one hour of electricity a day. Decide which items to keep, which to rewrite and which to replace, so the test is both fair and practical.
4. Rewrite one item to remove its bias while keeping the same skill, and explain how your version is more practical to mark.
5. Present your revised paper to the class and justify each change.

## Check your understanding

1. Define practicality in assessment, and name three resources a teacher should check before choosing a test format. *(Understand)*
2. A Class 5 test asks pupils to solve a problem about buying tickets for a foreign football match, using currency the class has never handled. Identify the source of bias and rewrite the item so that it measures the same mathematics. *(Apply)*
3. Explain why giving every pupil exactly the same paper under exactly the same conditions is not always fair, and give one example of a legitimate allowance. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Practicality asks whether a test can be administered, marked and used with the time, staff and materials a school really has, and usability asks whether its results are useful to teachers, head teachers and parents. Fairness means every pupil has a genuine opportunity to show the skill being assessed, which requires removing barriers from language, culture, gender, socio-economic background and disability. Fair is not identical: an allowance that removes an irrelevant barrier protects the meaning of the score rather than weakening it.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can define practicality and usability and name the resources that constrain a classroom test.
- [ ] I can identify at least four sources of bias in a described test.
- [ ] I can explain why fair does not mean identical, and give an example of a legitimate allowance.
- [ ] I can redesign a test to make it more practical without losing its coverage.

## Try this at your practicum school

During your practicum school visits:

1. With the class teacher's permission, take one recent test paper and note its length, item types and any context that assumes particular experience.
2. List the practical resources the school would need to administer and mark it: time, staff, paper, printing, apparatus and conditions.
3. Identify one item that could disadvantage a group of pupils for a reason unrelated to the skill being tested, and rewrite it.
4. Write a short note for your mentor teacher summarising the practical and fairness issues you found, and your suggested changes.

## Summative task

Write an evaluation (approx. 450 words) of a described assessment decision for a school staff meeting. A head teacher in a 60-pupil government primary school has decided to set one 2-hour written examination for all Class 5 pupils at the end of every subject, with no coursework, no practical tasks and no extra time for any pupil. Your evaluation must:

1. Judge the decision for practicality and usability, given the school's staffing and materials. *(Analyze)*
2. Judge the decision for fairness, identifying at least two groups of pupils who could be disadvantaged and why. *(Evaluate)*
3. Propose an alternative assessment plan that is practical for the school and fairer to pupils, explaining how it preserves the useful information the head teacher wants.
4. Justify why a fairer plan can also be a more valid one.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Below Standard (1 mark) | Meets Standard (2 marks) | Exceeds Standard (3 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Practicality analysis (Analyze)** | Describes the decision without judging resources or usability. | Clearly judges practicality and usability against the school's real constraints. | Gives a detailed, well-evidenced analysis of time, staffing, materials and usability. |
| **Fairness evaluation (Evaluate)** | Says only that the plan is "unfair" without naming who is affected. | Identifies at least two disadvantaged groups and explains the reason. | Evaluates fairness with precision, distinguishing fair from identical and citing legitimate allowances. |
| **Alternative plan and justification** | Proposes a vague alternative such as "use different tests." | Proposes a practical, fairer plan with a brief justification. | Proposes a well-justified plan and explains why fairness strengthens validity. |
