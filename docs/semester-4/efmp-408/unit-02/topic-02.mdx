# Topic 2.2 - Validity and reliability

## A real classroom situation

At a government boys' high school in Larkana, Mr Bilal sets a 20-item Class 9 mathematics test on the unit "algebraic factorisation". When he marks the scripts, he notices that the same four pupils finish at the top every time, and the same six struggle. A colleague, Mr Imran, inspects the paper and finds that twelve of the twenty items are really arithmetic simplification, not factorisation at all. "The test is reliable," Mr Bilal says, "because my best pupils always score high and my weakest always score low." Mr Imran replies: "It may be consistent, but it is not measuring factorisation. A scale that always reads two kilos too heavy is reliable, but it is not giving you a valid weight."

The two teachers agree to check the paper against the unit's content and to have a colleague mark a sample of scripts independently. Their review shows that the test is consistent but off target, and that the marking of two word problems varies widely from teacher to teacher. Consistency and accuracy, they realise, are two different qualities of a test.

## Explanation

### Validity: content, construct and criterion

<Glossary term="Validity">Validity</Glossary> is the degree to which the evidence from a test supports the inference a teacher wants to make. A score is never simply "good" or "bad"; it is good or bad *for a particular use*. The HEC classroom assessment guide is explicit that validity and reliability are not properties of tests but of the interpretations and conclusions drawn from test scores (HEC, 2012a). A mathematics score of 14 out of 20 is valid as evidence of a pupil's arithmetic skill, but invalid as evidence of their ability to write an essay.

> **Common Misconception: Validity is a property of the test itself.**
> Many teachers say "this is a valid test" as if validity were a label printed on the paper. In truth, validity belongs to the inference. A test that is valid for one purpose may be invalid for another. A short oral interview can validly show that a pupil can speak English, but it cannot validly show that the pupil can write English. The question is never "is the test valid?" but "is this inference from these scores valid?"

Three types of validity are most useful to a classroom teacher:

1. **Content validity:** does the test adequately sample the content that was taught? A Class 8 science unit on "photosynthesis and respiration" that devotes 12 of 20 items to arithmetic has weak content validity. A table of specifications, which matches items to topics and to levels, is the standard tool for protecting content validity (HEC, 2012b).
2. **Construct validity:** does the test measure the skill or trait it claims to measure? If a test of "scientific reasoning" is actually a reading comprehension test because every item uses long English passages, the construct being measured is language, not reasoning. This is a particular risk in Pakistan, where an English-medium science paper may reward vocabulary more than science (HEC, 2012b).
3. **Criterion validity:** does performance on the test relate to an external criterion? *Concurrent* criterion validity asks whether the test agrees with another accepted measure taken at the same time. *Predictive* criterion validity asks whether the test predicts later performance, for example whether a Class 9 mathematics test predicts success in Class 10 mathematics (HEC, 2012b).

#### Worked example: judging the validity of a described test

A Class 6 General Science teacher in Sukkur administers a 30-item end-of-unit test on "states of matter". The paper has 20 items on definitions, 6 on labelling diagrams and 4 on everyday applications. The unit spent four lessons on practical observation of melting, freezing and evaporation, and only one lesson on definitions. A trainee judge reasons as follows:

- **Content validity is weak:** the test over-represents definitions and under-represents the practical observation that dominated the unit.
- **Construct validity is threatened:** several application items are written in formal English that many Urdu-medium pupils cannot read, so the paper may measure reading rather than science.
- **Criterion validity is unknown:** the teacher has no other measure of the pupils' practical science skill to compare against, so no claim about later performance can be supported.

The judge's conclusion is not that the test is useless, but that the scores support only a narrow inference about recall of definitions, not about the practical science the unit taught.

### Reliability: test-retest and inter-rater

<Glossary term="Reliability">Reliability</Glossary> is the consistency of a measurement. A test is reliable when it gives roughly the same result on another occasion, in another form, or under another marker. The HEC test development guide describes reliability as consistent outcomes on repeated occasions, and identifies test-retest, parallel forms, split-half internal consistency and inter-rater reliability as the main forms (HEC, 2012b). Two matter most for a classroom teacher:

1. **Test-retest reliability:** the same test is given to the same pupils on two occasions a short time apart, and the scores are compared. If a pupil who scored 18 out of 20 on Monday scores 7 on Wednesday for no clear reason, the test is unstable. Test-retest reliability is low when the test is too short, when the items are ambiguous, or when conditions such as noise, heat or illness change between sittings.
2. **Inter-rater reliability:** two or more teachers mark the same scripts, and their scores are compared. Subjective items such as essays and open-ended problems are especially vulnerable, because one teacher's "good" can be another teacher's "average". A clear marking rubric with descriptors for each level is the standard defence (HEC, 2012b).

Reliability and validity are related but separate. A test can be highly reliable and invalid: the scale that always reads two kilos too heavy gives the same wrong answer every time. A test can also be valid in intention but unreliable: a single ambiguous essay question may target the right skill, yet two markers may award very different marks. **Reliability is necessary for validity, but it does not guarantee it.** A consistent measure of the wrong thing is consistently wrong.

> **Common Misconception: A reliable test must be valid.**
> This is the most persistent error in classroom assessment. Consistency only tells us that the scores are stable; it tells us nothing about whether they measure the intended skill. Mr Bilal's factorisation test was reliable because the same pupils always came top, yet it was invalid because it mostly measured arithmetic. A teacher must check both: first ask whether the test hits the right target (validity), then ask whether it does so consistently (reliability).

#### Worked example: two teachers mark the same scripts

In a government girls' high school in Nawabshah, two teachers each mark the same six Class 9 English essays. Their totals are:

| Pupil | Teacher A | Teacher B |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 18 | 14 |
| 2 | 15 | 9 |
| 3 | 12 | 13 |
| 4 | 9 | 15 |
| 5 | 7 | 12 |
| 6 | 4 | 6 |

The two teachers agree that pupil 1 is strongest and pupil 6 is weakest, but they disagree sharply in the middle. Their inter-rater reliability is low. A staff-room workshop gives both teachers the same analytic rubric with descriptors for content, organisation, language and presentation, and they re-mark the six scripts. The second set of marks is much closer. The essay test has not changed; the marking has become more consistent, and the scores now support a fairer rank order.

## Activity: Target practice

**Objective:** Classify assessment descriptions as questions of validity or reliability, and identify the evidence that would settle each.  
**Grouping:** Small groups of 3 to 4 trainee teachers.  
**Time:** 25 minutes.

### Instructions

1. Read the four descriptions below, each drawn from a Pakistani school:
   - **A:** A Class 7 Urdu test contains many items on Persian poetry that the class never studied. The teacher's rank order is the same on a re-test next week.
   - **B:** A Class 5 mathematics teacher marks the same set of 20 scripts twice, three days apart, and gives each pupil almost the same total.
   - **C:** A Class 9 biology practical test asks pupils to identify specimens using a textbook diagram, but the specimens on the bench are different species from the diagram.
   - **D:** Two teachers mark the same Class 8 English compositions and differ by as much as 8 marks out of 20 on the same script.
2. For each description, decide whether the central problem is one of **validity**, **reliability**, or both, and name the specific type (content, construct, criterion, test-retest or inter-rater).
3. For each, state one piece of evidence the teacher could gather to settle the question, and one practical step that would improve the test.
4. Appoint a rapporteur to present one description to the whole class, and be ready to defend your classification.

## Check your understanding

1. Explain in your own words the difference between validity and reliability, and why a test can be reliable without being valid. *(Understand)*
2. A Class 8 science teacher sets a 30-item test in which 22 items are on topics from one chapter and 8 items cover the other four chapters taught in the unit. Which type of validity is weakest, and what is the simplest way to strengthen it? *(Apply)*
3. Two teachers mark the same ten Class 9 Urdu essays and their totals differ by as many as 9 marks out of 20 on the same script. What kind of reliability is low, and what should the school put in place before the next examination? *(Apply)*

## Summary

Validity is the degree to which a score supports the inference a teacher draws from it, and it comes in three useful forms: content validity, which asks whether the test samples the taught content; construct validity, which asks whether it measures the intended skill; and criterion validity, which asks whether it agrees with an external criterion. Reliability is consistency, seen in test-retest stability and in inter-rater agreement between markers. Reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity: a test can measure the wrong thing very consistently.

## Try this at your practicum school

During your practicum placement:

1. Obtain a recent teacher-made test paper and its mark scheme, with the class teacher's permission.
2. Compare the items with the topics actually taught in the unit. Note any topic that is over-represented or missing, and judge the content validity.
3. Pick one subjective item and mark three scripts yourself. Then ask the class teacher to mark the same three. Compare the totals and note where you differ.
4. Write a one-page note recommending one change that would improve validity and one that would improve reliability, and share it with your mentor teacher.

## Summative task

Write a technical review (approx. 450 words) of a described Class 7 General Science test for a school assessment committee. The test has 30 multiple-choice items, of which 18 are on one topic; four items use vocabulary the class has not met; and the teacher has never re-marked a sample or compared marks with a colleague. Your review must:

1. Judge the test for content, construct and criterion validity, explaining the evidence for each judgement. *(Analyze)*
2. Judge the test for test-retest and inter-rater reliability, identifying what is unknown and what is likely weak.
3. Recommend one concrete step to improve validity and one to improve reliability, with a reason for each.
4. Explain why improving reliability alone would not make the test trustworthy.

### Mini-rubric

| Criterion | Below Standard (1 mark) | Meets Standard (2 marks) | Exceeds Standard (3 marks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Validity judgement (Analyze)** | Confuses validity with difficulty or length; no reference to the three types. | Correctly judges content, construct and criterion validity with evidence from the described test. | Gives a precise, well-evidenced judgement of all three types and explains how they interact. |
| **Reliability judgement** | Treats reliability as the same idea as validity. | Correctly identifies test-retest and inter-rater reliability and what is unknown. | Analyses both forms rigorously and explains what evidence would settle each. |
