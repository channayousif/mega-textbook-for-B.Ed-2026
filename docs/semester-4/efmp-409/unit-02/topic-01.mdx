## A real classroom situation

Saima is a B.Ed trainee on practicum at a government boys' primary school in Rohri, Sukkur. On her first morning she watches the day begin: the pupils line up in rows, recite from memory, sing the national anthem and listen to a short talk by the head teacher. The timetable gives six periods to English and mathematics, four to Urdu, and one each to Islamiyat and social studies. The wall outside the head teacher's office carries a poster of Quaid-e-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah and a framed verse of the Qur'an. Once a year the whole school is judged by the Class 5 examination result, which the head teacher pins to the notice board.

At tea break Saima asks, "Why is our school arranged like this?" The head teacher smiles: "Beta, this is how a school is." Saima is not satisfied. A private school in Karachi she visited last year had a science laboratory, a computer room and a debate club. A government school in a nearby village had no boundary wall and one teacher for four classes. Same country, same system on paper, very different schools.

By the end of this topic you will be able to answer Saima's question at five levels. Each level is a foundation of education, and each asks a different question about the same building.

## Explanation

This topic answers four questions. How do the philosophical and ideological foundations differ? What does the sociological foundation ask? What does the psychological foundation ask? And what does the historical foundation ask?

### Comparing the philosophical and ideological foundations of education

**The philosophical foundation.** Unit 1 introduced philosophy as the reasoned search for wisdom about the most basic questions: what is real, what is true and what is good. The philosophical foundation of education applies those questions to schooling. Curren (2025) describes philosophy of education as concerned with the nature and aims of education, with what should be taught, with how teaching and discipline should be done, with who has the authority to educate, and with what a fair share of education looks like. Notice that these are questions, not answers. A philosophical foundation asks a school to give reasons.

**The ideological foundation.** An **ideology** is a system of ideas and ideals that a society or group holds about the kind of life it wants and the kind of people it wants to raise. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that almost all present-day uses of the word turn on partiality or partisanship: an ideology is not a neutral description but a position a group takes (Roberts, 2025). The same entry distinguishes a **descriptive** use, which simply describes the ideas a society holds, from a **critical** use, which treats ideology as a set of ideas that hides inequality or serves a ruling group. Both uses matter for a teacher. A national ideology is a shared commitment; a critique of ideology asks whose interests it serves.

**How they differ.** The two foundations overlap, but they differ in three ways.

| | Philosophical foundation | Ideological foundation |
|---|---|---|
| **Question** | What is true and good? | Who are we, and what must we become? |
| **Source** | Reasoned inquiry; open to question | A shared belief a group holds about itself |
| **In a classroom** | Welcomes questioning and reasons | Often transmitted as a commitment to be accepted |

**A worked example: the morning assembly.** Look at the assembly at Saima's school through each foundation.

- *Philosophical*: what is a child, and what is worth learning? If a child is an active mind, then thirty minutes of standing in rows and reciting in unison may be a poor use of the morning. If a child is a soul to be disciplined, it may be exactly right. The philosophical foundation asks for a reason.
- *Ideological*: what kind of citizen is this school trying to form? The anthem, the uniform rows and the shared recitation all say: you belong to one nation. That is a statement about identity, not an answer to a philosophical question.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Philosophy and ideology are the same thing, and both are just opinions."** Trainees often say this. But a philosophical claim is offered with reasons and can be tested; an ideology is held by a group as a shared commitment and is often not up for debate inside that group. A teacher needs both: philosophy to examine the reasons, and an understanding of ideology to see what a nation asks its schools to do.

### The sociological foundations of education

**What the sociological foundation asks.** The sociological foundation asks how society and education shape each other. The HEC course guide says the unit should show how society and culture, social structure, history and economics influence schooling, and how education in turn strengthens the society's culture and structure (Higher Education Commission [HEC], 2012/2013). The same guide names three perspectives: functionalist, conflict and interactionist. A widely used open sociology textbook sets out the same three and adds a fourth (Conerly, Holmes, & Tamang, 2021).

**The functionalist view.** Functionalists see society as a system of parts that work together, and ask what each part does to keep it running. In education, schools socialise pupils into shared norms, pass on culture, keep order and sort people into roles. Conerly, Holmes and Tamang (2021) list the intended roles of schooling as socialisation, cultural transmission, social control and placement, and its hidden roles as courtship, social networking and group work. *In a Pakistani school*: the morning assembly, the national anthem and a common Urdu lesson help hold a very diverse country together, and the examination system sorts pupils into academic and vocational paths.

**The conflict view.** Conflict theorists ask who benefits. Schools tend to reproduce the inequalities of the wider society by class, gender, language and region. They reward the "cultural capital" of the dominant group, the habits and language that already carry prestige, and track pupils into unequal positions (Conerly et al., 2021). *In a Pakistani school*: a private English-medium school in Clifton, Karachi, and a government primary school in Tharparkar may serve pupils of similar ability, but one has laboratories and trained science teachers while the other has one teacher for four grades and no science equipment. The difference is not in the children.

**The interactionist view.** Interactionists look at the small scale: what happens between teachers and pupils every day. Labels, expectations and everyday talk shape who a pupil becomes. A teacher who calls a pupil "weak" may set off a self-fulfilling prophecy: the pupil is given easier work, expects less of herself and learns less (Conerly et al., 2021). *In a Pakistani school*: a Class 4 teacher in Hyderabad always picks the same three pupils to answer, because she believes the rest "cannot". By the end of the year those three are the only ones who volunteer.

The same textbook also treats **feminist theory** as a fourth perspective: it asks how schooling treats girls and women unequally, and how limited access to school restricts their rights and independence (Conerly et al., 2021). In Pakistan a teacher can answer it from local evidence: a village where boys ride to the high school and girls stop at the primary school is living the answer.

**Education's impact on society.** The foundation runs both ways. Education raises literacy, improves health, delays marriage, opens work and builds the skills an economy needs. Pakistan's Constitution makes the claim concrete: Article 25A says that "The State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children of the age of five to sixteen years" (Government of Pakistan, National Assembly, 2012). A state does not write an enforceable right to education into its Constitution unless it believes education changes society. *In a Pakistani community*: a girl who completes Class 8 near Sukkur is more likely to delay marriage, to read health information to her family and to teach her own children.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Sociology of education is only about social problems."** Many trainees reach this topic expecting a list of problems: out-of-school children, dropouts, inequality. Those are real, but the foundation is wider. It also explains how schools hold a society together and how a teacher's casual remark can decide a pupil's future.

### The psychological foundations of education

**What the psychological foundation asks.** The psychological foundation asks how learners grow and learn, and how teaching can be organised to help. The HEC course guide says that psychology bears on educational objectives, student characteristics, learning processes, teaching methods and evaluation procedures, and it names four perspectives: behaviourist, constructivist, social cognitivist and humanist (HEC, 2012/2013). Two open textbooks let us study all four in one place: Seifert and Sutton's *Educational Psychology* and OpenStax's *Psychology 2e* (Seifert & Sutton, 2009; Spielman, Jenkins, & Lovett, 2020).

**The behaviourist perspective.** Behaviourists study changes in what people do. Learning is a change in observable behaviour, shaped by what follows an action: reinforcement increases a behaviour, punishment decreases it (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). B. F. Skinner showed that animals and people repeat actions that are rewarded and drop actions that are not. *In a Pakistani classroom*: a teacher gives a star to every pupil who completes the multiplication table; the class practises more. Behaviourism is the most direct account of praise, stars, drills and rules.

**The constructivist perspective.** Constructivists argue that learners do not receive knowledge ready made; they build it. Jean Piaget described learning as the interplay of **assimilation** (fitting a new experience into what you already know) and **accommodation** (changing what you know because it does not fit), and he called the mental structures we use **schemas** (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). Lev Vygotsky added that learning is social: a learner can do more with help than alone, and the gap between solo and assisted performance is the **zone of proximal development** (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). *In a Pakistani classroom*: a Class 6 pupil in Larkana has a schema for "bird" that covers sparrows and crows. When he sees a bat he first assimilates it as a bird; when his teacher shows him it is a mammal, he accommodates and rebuilds the schema. A good teacher works in that zone rather than handing over the answer.

**The social cognitivist perspective.** Albert Bandura's social learning theory sits between behaviourism and constructivism. We learn by watching models, and whether we copy what we see depends on four processes: **attention**, **retention**, **reproduction** and **motivation** (Spielman et al., 2020). We copy a rewarded model more and a punished model less, which Bandura called vicarious reinforcement and vicarious punishment. He also studied **self-efficacy**, the belief that you can do a task, which shapes whether you try, how long you persist and how you face failure (Seifert & Sutton, 2009). *In a Pakistani classroom*: a Class 5 teacher in Nawabshah solves a problem on the board while talking through her reasoning; the pupils watch, remember and copy. A pupil who believes "I am not a maths person" gives up after one mistake. A teacher builds self-efficacy by giving real success, not empty praise.

**The humanist perspective.** Humanists study growth and the whole person. Abraham Maslow described a **hierarchy of needs**, from food and safety to **self-actualization**, the fulfilment of potential; needs rooted in a lack must be met before growth needs can drive us (Spielman et al., 2020). Carl Rogers stressed **self-concept** and the gap between the "ideal self" and the "real self"; a wide gap brings anxiety, and an environment of **unconditional positive regard** helps a person grow (Spielman et al., 2020). *In a Pakistani classroom*: a hungry child in Tharparkar cannot attend to fractions until the midday meal arrives; a pupil who is mocked for a wrong answer learns to hide. Humanism reminds a school that a pupil is a person, not a score.

**A worked example: one lesson, four lenses.** A Class 5 teacher in Sukkur teaches the area of a rectangle. She writes the formula on the board, works two examples, gives ten sums, and ticks correct answers with "Good!".

- *Behaviourist*: the tick and the praise reinforce the correct method; the pupil repeats it.
- *Constructivist*: the pupil already knows length and width, so the formula is assimilated into a schema for measuring space. But if she cannot explain why the formula works, the schema is thin.
- *Social cognitivist*: the pupils watch the teacher's worked examples and copy the steps; the pupil who believes she cannot do maths copies without confidence.
- *Humanist*: the pupil who is hungry or anxious about a test at home is not ready to learn; the teacher's warmth matters.

The same lesson carries all four. A teacher who knows the four perspectives can see why a lesson that "worked" for most pupils still failed one.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Psychology matters in education only for pupils with difficulties."** Many trainees say this, meaning that educational psychology is for counselling and special education. It is certainly useful there. But the four perspectives describe how every pupil learns, and objectives, methods, classroom talk and assessment all rest on a view of learning.

### The historical foundations of education

**What the historical foundation asks.** The historical foundation asks how education has developed over time, and why the past still shapes the present. The HEC course guide sets out the story in five stages: the education system before the British came to the subcontinent; Darul Uloom Deoband; Darul Uloom Nadwat-ul-Ulma; the Mohammedan Anglo Oriental College; and Pakistan's education system in the light of its policies (HEC, 2012/2013). The point is not to memorise dates. It is to see that today's arrangements have a history, and that a reform which ignores that history usually fails.

**A short trace of education in this region.** Before the British, the subcontinent had two broad traditions of organised learning: the **maktab**, attached to a mosque, where children learnt to read the Qur'an, Arabic, Persian and arithmetic; and the **madrasa**, a higher college for religious and rational sciences. The British introduced English-language schooling and universities, and with them a new idea: education as a qualification for employment in the state. A community response followed on two lines. Darul Uloom Deoband (founded 1866) worked to preserve Islamic learning, while Sir Syed Ahmed Khan's Aligarh movement worked to bring modern education to Muslims. A recent study describes Aligarh as a social movement that transformed the Muslim middle classes, and notes that many Muslims at first suspected modern education, particularly English, as a threat to their culture and religion (Khan & Khan, 2020). After 1947, Pakistan inherited this double legacy: a state system of schools and colleges, and a network of religious seminaries. Its policies have tried, with mixed success, to bring the two into one national system.

**A worked example: why is English still a medium of instruction?** Ask why so many Pakistani schools teach science and mathematics in English. The historical answer runs through the colonial period, when English became the language of government and employment, and through later policies that kept English as an official language and a medium in many schools. The present arrangement is not a natural fact. It is the result of decisions made long ago, and it can be changed by decisions made now.

**Correcting a common misconception: "The history of education is only about old dates."** A trainee may think the foundation is a list of commissions and policies to memorise for an examination. The foundation's real question is different: why is our school the way it is? The medium of instruction, the examination culture and the divide between school and seminary all have histories. A teacher who knows the history can argue for change with evidence, instead of saying "this is how it has always been".

## Activity: Five lenses on one school

Work in groups of five. About 30 minutes.

Your group is given a short description of a real school: a government middle school in a town near Dadu. It has a morning assembly with a prayer and the national anthem; six periods a day, with double periods for mathematics and English; an annual examination whose results are published; a wall display of the school's "mission: discipline, honesty and service"; and a parent-teacher meeting once a term.

1. Each member takes one foundation: philosophical, ideological, sociological, psychological or historical. In 5 minutes, write down three features of the school that your foundation helps to explain, and one sentence for each on why it matters.
2. Each member presents. The group listens for overlap: a feature explained by two foundations is often the most interesting one.
3. Together, choose one feature and write a short paragraph explaining it through two foundations, showing how the two explanations differ.
4. Finally, name one feature of the school that none of the five foundations explains well, and say what other kind of answer you would need.

Share your paragraph with another group. Compare which foundations each group found hardest to apply.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Without looking back, name the five foundations of education used in this topic and the question each one asks.
2. *(Understand)* In three sentences, explain the difference between the philosophical foundation and the ideological foundation of education.
3. *(Apply)* A head teacher says, "The morning assembly teaches pupils to stand in order and respect the national flag." Which sociological perspective best explains this claim, and what would it say the assembly is for?
4. *(Apply)* A Class 4 teacher ticks every correct sum and writes "Well done!" beside it. Which psychological perspective does this practice most directly reflect, and what is one thing that perspective cannot explain about the same lesson?
5. *(Apply)* A school in a town teaches science in English, although most pupils speak Sindhi at home. Use the historical foundation to explain one reason for this arrangement.

## Summary

Education has five foundations, and each asks a different question. The philosophical foundation asks what is real, true and good, and demands reasons. The ideological foundation asks what kind of people a nation wants to raise. The sociological foundation asks how society and schooling shape each other, through functionalist, conflict and interactionist lenses. The psychological foundation asks how learners grow, through behaviourist, constructivist, social cognitivist and humanist lenses. The historical foundation asks how education developed and why the past still shapes the present. A school rule, a timetable or a wall poster can be read through any of them, and each reading tells you something true.

## Try this at your practicum school

Collect three features of your practicum school: one rule, one timetable choice and one wall display or notice. For each, write one sentence on which foundation it reflects and one sentence on what that foundation explains about it. Then ask your cooperating teacher why they think the school made that choice, and compare their answer with yours. Bring your three notes to the next class.

## Summative task

Write 300-350 words. Choose one ordinary routine of a school you know, such as the morning assembly, the annual examination, the seating plan or a homework policy.

1. Describe the routine and what it asks pupils and teachers to do.
2. Analyse it through two of the five foundations, showing how each foundation explains a different part of it.
3. Say which foundation explains the routine best, and which explains it least well, with a reason for each.
4. Suggest one change to the routine that would serve a foundation it currently neglects, and explain why.

*(Analyze)*

**Mini-rubric (12 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Description (2 marks) | Clear, specific account of the routine (2) | Vague account (1) | Absent |
| Analysis through two foundations (5 marks) | Two foundations applied with a reason each, and the difference between the explanations shown (5) | Two foundations named but only one applied with reasons, or the difference not shown (2-4); one foundation only (1) | No analysis |
| Judgement (3 marks, **Analyze**) | A reasoned choice of the best and the weakest foundation, with evidence from the routine (3) | Names best and weakest without evidence (1-2) | Absent |
| Suggested change (2 marks) | Practical change linked to a neglected foundation (2) | Change given but not linked (1) | Absent |

The analysis criterion asks you to show how a foundation explains the routine, not merely to name it. Saying a routine is "good" or "traditional" does not meet it.
