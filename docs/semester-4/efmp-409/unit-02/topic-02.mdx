## A real classroom situation

Asad is a B.Ed trainee on practicum at a government girls' high school in Khairpur. One evening, after the pupils have gone home, he hears two teachers arguing in the staffroom. Rehana, who teaches Islamiyat, says, "We teach Islam in this school, so we are teaching the ideology of Pakistan." Tariq, who teaches Pakistan Studies, disagrees. "Islam is our religion," he says. "The ideology of Pakistan is a political idea. They are not the same thing, and a textbook that mixes them up teaches neither well."

The next day a Class 9 pupil, Maryam, stays behind. "Miss," she says to Asad, "are we Muslim first or Pakistani first?" Asad smiles, but he does not know what to say. He has said the national anthem and the Kalma in the same assembly for years without thinking about the difference.

By the end of this topic you will be able to answer Maryam. You will be able to explain what an ideology is, how it differs from a religion, and how both the Ideology of Pakistan and Islamic teachings ask something of a school. You will also be able to read a school document and tell which foundation it is speaking from.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What is the difference between ideology and religion? What is the ideological foundation of education in Pakistan? And what is the Islamic foundation?

### Ideology versus religion

**What an ideology is.** An ideology is a system of ideas and ideals that a group holds about how it wants to live. It speaks about power, identity and the good life, and it tells a group who it is and what it should become. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that almost all present-day uses of the word turn on partiality or partisanship (Roberts, 2025). Two uses matter for a teacher: a **descriptive** use, which simply describes the ideas a society holds, and a **critical** use, which treats ideology as ideas that hide inequality or serve those who rule (Roberts, 2025).

**What a religion is.** A religion is a tradition of belief and practice oriented toward the sacred. It usually has scripture, worship, a community, a moral law and a view of life after death. Islam, for example, is built on the Qur'an, the example of the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him), worship, and a law that covers conduct and daily life.

**How they differ.** The two can overlap, and in Pakistan they often do. But they differ in three ways.

| | Ideology | Religion |
|---|---|---|
| **Source** | The shared ideas and interests of a group or nation | Revelation, scripture and a living tradition |
| **Scope** | A vision of the good life and of who should hold power | A comprehensive relationship with God, the self and the world |
| **Role in education** | Frames aims, citizenship and national identity | Shapes worship, morality, meaning and conduct |

**Two founding texts, two kinds of statement.** Pakistan has two texts that are often quoted together, and they are not the same kind of document.

- The **Objectives Resolution**, adopted on 12 March 1949, is now part of the Constitution as Article 2A. It says that sovereignty over the universe belongs to Allah alone, that authority is a sacred trust exercised through the people, and that the principles of democracy, freedom, equality, tolerance and social justice "as enunciated by Islam" shall be fully observed. It also says that minorities shall freely profess and practise their religions (Government of Pakistan, National Assembly, 2012).
- The **Constituent Assembly address of 11 August 1947**, by Quaid-e-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah, tells the new state how its citizens will be treated: "You are free; you are free to go to your temples, you are free to go to your mosques or to any other place or worship in this State of Pakistan." And: "we are all citizens, and equal citizens, of one State" (Jinnah, 1947/1969).

Read them side by side. The Objectives Resolution speaks about God, sovereignty and the ordering of life by Islam. Jinnah's address speaks about citizens, equal treatment and the business of the state. A school that teaches both is not teaching one thing twice. It is teaching two things: a religious foundation and an ideological one.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Ideology and religion are the same thing."** This is the most common confusion in this topic, and the staffroom argument in Khairpur is about it. The two overlap in Pakistan: the Ideology of Pakistan draws on Islam, and Islam speaks about justice, worship and community. But they differ in source (a group's shared ideas versus revelation and tradition), in scope (a vision of political life versus a comprehensive way of life), and in role (framing citizenship versus shaping worship and conduct). A teacher who keeps the distinction clear can teach Pakistan Studies and Islamiyat without mixing the two, and can answer Maryam's question honestly.

### The ideological foundations of education

**What an ideological foundation does.** Every state educates for something. An ideological foundation makes that "something" explicit: it tells a nation what kind of people it wants to raise, and it turns that vision into aims, curriculum, language policy and citizenship education. In Pakistan the vision is usually called the **Ideology of Pakistan**.

**Where the Ideology of Pakistan comes from.** Three moments are usually named.

1. **The Two-Nation Theory.** The idea that the Muslims of the subcontinent were a separate nation, with a distinct culture and a common future, was the basis of the demand for Pakistan. A recent study notes that the question of Muslim identity was central to the partition of 1947 (Hassan, 2020).
2. **The Lahore Resolution, 1940.** In his address to the Muslim League session at Lahore in March 1940, Jinnah said that the Muslims were "a nation by any definition" and must have their homelands (Jinnah, 1940/1983). The resolution demanded autonomous Muslim-majority states in the north-west and east of India.
3. **The Objectives Resolution, 1949.** This turned the demand into constitutional principle, and Article 2A later made it part of the Constitution (Government of Pakistan, National Assembly, 2012).

**How it appears in education.** The ideological foundation shows up wherever a school states what a pupil should become. Pakistan's National Education Policy 2009 lists aims of this kind: to promote national cohesion "by respecting all faiths and religions" (aim 4); to nurture "the total personality of the individual" (aim 8); and to raise individuals "committed to democratic and moral values" (aim 9) (Government of Pakistan, 2009, pp. 10-11). The Constitution adds Article 25A, the right to free and compulsory education for children aged five to sixteen, and Article 31, which says steps shall be taken to enable Muslims to order their lives in accordance with the principles and basic concepts of Islam (Government of Pakistan, National Assembly, 2012).

**A worked example: from a resolution to a classroom.** Trace one line from the Objectives Resolution to a school desk.

1. The Resolution says sovereignty belongs to Allah and that the principles of democracy, freedom, equality, tolerance and social justice as enunciated by Islam shall be observed.
2. Article 2A makes those principles part of the Constitution.
3. Article 25A then says the State shall provide free and compulsory education to every child aged five to sixteen.
4. A government school in Khairpur admits a Class 1 pupil whose family cannot pay fees. The pupil is there because of a chain of ideas, not only because of a building.

The ideological foundation is the chain. A teacher who can trace it can explain why a school exists, and can argue for a pupil's right to be in it.

**Correcting a common misconception: "An ideology is just a slogan on a wall."** A school wall may carry a motto, and a motto may be part of an ideology. But an ideology is a system of ideas that shapes laws, budgets, curricula and examinations. The Objectives Resolution is not a slogan; it is part of the Constitution. A teacher who treats it as decoration has missed what it does.

### The Islamic foundations of education

**What the Islamic foundation asks.** The Islamic foundation asks what education means in the light of Islam. It is wider than religious instruction. In Islam, knowledge (*ilm*) is a trust and seeking it is a duty. The first word revealed to the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) was *Iqra*, "Read", and the passage says that Allah taught by the pen and taught people what they did not know (Qur'an 96:1-5). Another verse asks whether those who know and those who do not know can be equal (Qur'an 39:9). Muslim scholars use three Arabic words for education, and each adds a meaning (Susanti et al., 2023, as summarised in Unit 1): *tarbiyah*, nurturing the whole growth of a person; *ta'lim*, passing on knowledge; and *ta'dib*, knowledge that shows itself in right conduct. The HEC course guide's first unit takes the same view: it asks student teachers to understand the Islamic ideological perspectives on education, the place of peace and social justice, and the education of all Pakistanis, including religious minorities (HEC, 2012/2013).

**How it appears in national aims.** The Constitution's Article 31 says steps shall be taken to enable Muslims to order their lives in accordance with the fundamental principles and basic concepts of Islam, and Article 227 requires laws to be brought into conformity with the Qur'an and the Sunnah (Government of Pakistan, National Assembly, 2012). National policies have followed. The point for a teacher is not to recite these provisions, but to see that a school's Islamiyat lesson, its assembly and its rules about honesty all draw on a foundation that is both religious and, in this country, part of the public order.

**A worked example: the assembly and the honesty lesson.** A school begins the day with a recitation from the Qur'an and a short thought for the day on honesty. Which foundation is speaking?

- The *recitation* belongs to the Islamic foundation: it is worship, memory of the Qur'an and a shared moral vocabulary.
- The *thought on honesty* can belong to both. Honesty is an Islamic value, and it is also a civic value the state wants in its citizens. That is exactly the overlap the table above describes.
- The *national anthem* that follows belongs to the ideological foundation: it speaks about the nation, not about God.

A teacher who can separate the three can plan an assembly that teaches both foundations without confusing them.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Islam values only religious knowledge."** Some trainees believe that in the Islamic view, only sacred knowledge matters and the study of nature, mathematics or medicine is secondary. The Qur'an repeatedly invites reflection on the natural world, and the passage revealed first commands reading and teaching by the pen. The Islamic foundation values knowledge of the world as well as knowledge of the sacred; the two are not rivals. A science lesson taught well is also an act of learning that Islam encourages.

## Activity: Two texts, one school

Work in groups of four. About 30 minutes.

Your group receives two short texts.

- **Text A**: an extract from the Objectives Resolution (1949), beginning "Whereas sovereignty over the entire universe belongs to Allah Almighty alone" and including the words "democracy, freedom, equality, tolerance and social justice as enunciated by Islam".
- **Text B**: an extract from the Constituent Assembly address of 11 August 1947, beginning "You are free; you are free to go to your temples, you are free to go to your mosques" and including "we are all citizens, and equal citizens, of one State".

1. In 10 minutes, sort the sentences of both texts into two columns: sentences that speak about God, worship and the ordering of life by Islam, and sentences that speak about citizens, rights and the business of the state. Some sentences may belong in both columns; say so.
2. Each member takes one column and writes two aims for a school that follow from it.
3. As a group, compare the two lists of aims. Where do they agree, and where do they pull in different directions?
4. Choose one aim that both texts support, and write a short paragraph explaining how a school could serve it in one ordinary week.

Share your paragraph with another group and compare the aims each group chose.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State two differences between ideology and religion, and give one example of each from this topic.
2. *(Understand)* In three sentences, explain why the Ideology of Pakistan is not the same thing as Islam.
3. *(Understand)* Name two founding texts that express the Ideology of Pakistan, and give one idea from each.
4. *(Apply)* A school's mission statement says it will "raise loyal citizens who serve the nation and respect all faiths". Which foundation does this serve most directly, the ideological or the Islamic? Give a reason.
5. *(Apply)* A teacher begins every Class 3 lesson with a short verse about honesty and then asks the pupils to use it in a story. Which of *tarbiyah*, *ta'lim* or *ta'dib* does this best serve? Explain in one sentence.

## Summary

An ideology is a system of ideas a group holds about the life it wants; a religion is a tradition of belief and practice oriented toward the sacred. They differ in source, scope and role in education, though in Pakistan they overlap. The Ideology of Pakistan comes from the Two-Nation Theory, the Lahore Resolution of 1940 and the Objectives Resolution of 1949, and it appears in national aims, in Article 25A and in what a school expects a pupil to become. The Islamic foundation asks what education means in the light of Islam: knowledge sought as a duty, character formed by conduct, and the equal worth of all. A school assembly can carry both foundations at once, and a teacher who can tell them apart can teach each well.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find a document from your practicum school: its mission statement, a notice-board poster, or the order of the morning assembly. Read it line by line. For each line, write whether it speaks from the ideological foundation, the Islamic foundation, or both. Then write one aim for the school that follows from the document, and say which foundation it serves. Bring your notes to the next class.

## Summative task

Write 300-350 words. Choose one document or routine from a school you know: a mission statement, a morning-assembly order, a wall poster or a school rule.

1. Describe the document or routine and what it asks pupils to do or become.
2. Analyse it as an expression of the Ideology of Pakistan and of Islamic teachings: what it assumes, and where the two foundations overlap or differ.
3. Suggest one addition or change that would serve the foundation it currently treats less well, and explain why.

*(Analyze)*

**Mini-rubric (12 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Description (2 marks) | Clear, specific account of the document or routine (2) | Vague account (1) | Absent |
| Analysis of both foundations (5 marks) | Both the ideological and the Islamic foundation applied with a reason each, and the overlap or difference shown (5) | Both named but only one applied with reasons, or the overlap not shown (2-4); one foundation only (1) | No analysis |
| Suggested change (3 marks, **Analyze**) | A practical change linked to the foundation treated less well, with a reason drawn from the document (3) | Change given but weakly linked (1-2) | Absent |
| Use of a founding text (2 marks) | One founding text cited or quoted accurately and used to support the analysis (2) | Text named but not used (1) | Absent |

The analysis criterion asks you to show what the document assumes, not merely to say it is Islamic or national. Describing it again in other words does not meet it.
