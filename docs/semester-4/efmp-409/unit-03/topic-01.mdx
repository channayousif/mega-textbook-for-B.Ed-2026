# Topic 3.1 - Idealism and Plato

## A real classroom situation

Faraz is a B.Ed student teacher completing his practicum at a government boys' high school in Hyderabad. During an observation period, he attends a Class 9 Pakistan Studies lesson taught by Sir Javed. Instead of reading directly from the textbook or dictating notes, Sir Javed writes a single question on the blackboard: "What is justice?"

A pupil stands up and answers, "Sir, justice means obeying the police and school rules." Sir Javed smiles and asks, "If a law says that children from poor families may not attend school, would obeying that law be just?" The pupils pause, look at one another, and begin to debate eagerly. One boy argues that an unjust law cannot be true justice because justice must protect everyone equally.

After the period, Faraz asks Sir Javed why he spent forty minutes on one question instead of completing the textbook chapter on civic institutions. Sir Javed replies, "A clerk memorises rules, but a citizen must understand the eternal idea of justice. If our pupils do not grasp what is genuinely true and good in their minds, knowing the names of government offices will never make them honest leaders." Faraz wonders how a philosophy rooted in ancient ideas can guide everyday teaching in Sindh.

## Explanation

To understand Sir Javed's teaching approach, we must examine idealism, one of the oldest and most influential schools of educational philosophy.

### What is idealism?

Idealism is a philosophical perspective that asserts that reality is fundamentally mental, spiritual, and intellectual, rather than purely material (Curren, 2025; HEC, 2012/2013). The word derives from "idea", not from "ideal" in the sense of daydreams. For idealists, physical objects - such as trees, desks, and human bodies - are temporary, changing, and perishable. By contrast, ideas, universal principles, and moral values are permanent, unchangeable, and eternal.

We can analyze idealism across the three philosophical branches studied in Unit 1:

- **Metaphysics (Reality)**: Ultimate reality consists of ideas, mind, and spirit. The physical universe is a shadow or manifestation of a higher spiritual reality.
- **Epistemology (Knowledge)**: Genuine knowledge is intellectual and rational, not merely sensory. Sense organs can be deceived by appearances; true knowledge is discovered through reflection, reasoning, and the intuitive grasp of universal truths.
- **Axiology (Values)**: Values such as Truth, Goodness, and Beauty are objective, unchanging, and absolute. They do not depend on individual preference or shifting cultural fashions.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Idealism means being unrealistic."** In everyday speech, calling someone an "idealist" often implies that they are an impractical dreamer out of touch with real life. In philosophy of education, this is a serious misconception. Philosophical idealism does not mean ignoring practical necessities. Rather, it means that education should be guided by highest intellectual, moral, and spiritual standards rather than drifting with temporary pressures. An idealist teacher in a rural school in Sindh sets high standards of honesty, critical thinking, and character, refusing to accept that poverty excuses moral compromise.

### Plato as the major exponent of idealism

The Greek philosopher Plato (c. 428 - 348 BCE), a disciple of Socrates and founder of the Academy in Athens, is the foundational thinker of Western idealism (Kraut, 2004/2026).

Plato articulated his philosophy through two famous concepts that remain central to education:

1. **The Theory of Forms (Ideas)**: Plato argued that every physical object is an imperfect copy of an eternal, non-physical "Form". For example, there are many imperfect physical triangles drawn in sand or chalk, but the mathematical Form of a triangle is perfect and timeless. The highest Form is the Form of the Good, which illuminates all other truths just as the sun illuminates the physical world.
2. **The Allegory of the Cave**: In *The Republic*, Plato describes prisoners chained in a dark cave, able to see only shadows projected on a wall. Believing the shadows to be reality, they remain deceived. When one prisoner escapes into the sunlight, his eyes are initially pained, but he gradually sees real objects and the sun itself. For Plato, education (*paideia*) is the difficult turning around of the soul from the dark cave of sensory illusion to the bright light of truth and reason.

**The Socratic method (Dialectic)**: Following his teacher Socrates, Plato believed that the teacher does not pour knowledge into an empty mind. Instead, the teacher uses structured questioning and dialectical dialogue to help pupils examine their assumptions, recognize contradictions, and recall latent truth from within themselves.

<Figure id="fig-U3-2" src="/img/figures/efmp-409/unit-03/fig-U3-2.svg" alt="Diagram illustrating Plato's educational stages in The Republic across age brackets: childhood play and stories, youth gymnastics and music, young adult military service, higher mathematics and dialectic, culminating in philosopher-guardians." />

Plato outlined a comprehensive, state-regulated educational progression in *The Republic*:
- **Early childhood (Ages 0 to 6)**: Free movement, games, and carefully chosen stories that build wholesome moral dispositions.
- **Primary and secondary (Ages 7 to 17)**: Gymnastics to develop physical health, stamina, and self-discipline; music, poetry, and literature to nurture emotional harmony and moral sensitivity in the soul.
- **Military and civic service (Ages 18 to 20)**: Physical training and civic discipline to test character, courage, and loyalty.
- **Higher scientific studies (Ages 20 to 30)**: Rigorous training in arithmetic, plane and solid geometry, harmony, and astronomy to train the mind to think abstractly beyond sensory surfaces.
- **Dialectic philosophy (Ages 30 to 35)**: Pure philosophical reasoning and dialectic inquiry to contemplate universal Forms and the Good.
- **Civic leadership (Ages 35 to 50+)**: Practical administration in public offices, preparing mature individuals to serve as wise "philosopher-rulers" or guardians.

### The application of idealism in education

When idealism is applied to contemporary schooling, it establishes distinct approaches to aims, curriculum, pedagogy, and classroom relationships (Curren, 2025; HEC, 2012/2013):

- **Educational aims**: The chief aim of education is intellectual and spiritual self-realisation. Education must cultivate wisdom, moral character, aesthetic appreciation, and civic responsibility, helping every learner realize their rational potential.
- **Curriculum**: The idealist curriculum prioritises the liberal arts and humanities: literature, history, philosophy, ethics, fine arts, and pure mathematics. These subjects preserve and transmit the enduring cultural and intellectual achievements of humanity. Vocational training is recognized as necessary for livelihood, but subordinate to character formation.
- **Pedagogical methods**: Idealism favours dialogic methods: Socratic questioning, guided discussions, lecture and commentary on classic texts, and reflective writing. Rote cramming is rejected because true learning requires active intellectual insight.
- **Role of the teacher**: The teacher occupies a central, sacred position in the classroom. The teacher is not merely an information technician, but a moral role model, an intellectual guide, and a spiritual mentor who inspires pupils toward excellence.
- **Role of the pupil**: The pupil is an active, rational spiritual seeker who possesses latent capacities for truth. The pupil must discipline passions and exercise intellectual effort to achieve self-realisation.

**Worked example: Analyzing a lesson through an idealist lens**

Consider two different Class 8 lessons on environmental conservation in a Larkana school:

| Dimension | Lesson A (Utilitarian approach) | Lesson B (Idealist approach) |
|---|---|---|
| Classroom task | Pupils copy five government municipal anti-littering rules from the textbook into notebooks. | Teacher presents a case of local canal pollution and asks: "Do we have a moral duty to future generations, or only to ourselves today?" |
| Cognitive move | Memorisation and repetition of stated regulations. | Socratic dialogue examining justice, stewardship, and personal responsibility. |
| Role of teacher | Taskmaster checking copied text for spelling errors. | Moral guide challenging superficial answers and prompting deeper reasoning. |
| Idealist verdict | Fails idealism: treats learning as mechanical copying of external data. | Embodies idealism: engages reason to uncover universal ethical principles. |

## Activity: Socratic questioning in pairs

Work with a partner. Plan for about 20 minutes.

1. Select one common disciplinary or moral rule in your practicum school (for example: "Pupils must never arrive late to assembly" or "Cheating on tests brings immediate suspension").
2. One partner plays the role of the teacher using Socratic questioning; the other plays the pupil.
3. The teacher must not lecture or give the answer. Instead, ask at least three progressive questions that prompt the pupil to define the underlying virtue (such as respect, honesty, or fairness) behind the rule.
4. Switch roles and repeat with a second rule.
5. In your pairs, record: Did the questioning help clarify the principle, or did it lead to confusion? What made asking good questions difficult?

## Summary

Idealism is the philosophical school that regards ideas, spirit, and universal values as ultimate reality, viewing the physical world as changing and secondary. Founded in the West by Plato, idealism conceives education as the turning of the soul toward truth, goodness, and beauty. Through Socratic dialectic, structured developmental stages, and an enduring humanities-rich curriculum, idealist education seeks the intellectual and moral self-realisation of the learner under the guidance of a virtuous teacher.
