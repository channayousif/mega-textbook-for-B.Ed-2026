# Topic 3.6 - Western and Muslim thinkers compared

## A real classroom situation

During a B.Ed seminar at a Government Elementary College of Education in Larkana, two prospective teachers passionately debate their upcoming lesson plans for primary classes.

Hina argues, "We must follow John Dewey and modern Western child-centred methods completely. If we let children explore freely through hands-on inquiry and solve local community problems, they will naturally develop critical thinking and scientific minds."

Her peer, Tariq, responds with concern, "Hands-on projects are useful, but our primary duty as Pakistani educators is moral and spiritual guidance. If we abandon our classical Islamic heritage - the ethical character formation championed by Ibn Khaldun and the spiritual self-realisation of Allama Iqbal - we produce clever students who lack ethical responsibility and reverence for divine guidance."

Their course instructor, Dr. Aslam, steps in and asks the class: "Must we choose between modern scientific inquiry and our Islamic educational values? Or does true professional mastery require us to critically compare both traditions, recognising where they converge and where they diverge, so we can formulate a coherent teaching philosophy of our own?"

## Explanation

Across the preceding five topics, this unit examined major traditions in educational thought: Greek classical philosophy, European naturalism, American pragmatism, classical Islamic sociopedagogy (the study of education as a social and civilisational process), and modern Islamic existential educational thought. Each tradition developed distinctive answers to fundamental pedagogical questions: What is the nature of the learner? What is the ultimate aim of education? What constitutes valid knowledge? What role must the teacher play in the classroom and society?

Prospective teachers in Pakistan frequently encounter these philosophies as disconnected historical artifacts or as mutually exclusive ideological camps. However, sound professional practice requires neither uncritically copying Western curricular frameworks nor retreating into nostalgic isolation from global pedagogical research. Instead, effective educational practice requires rigorous comparative analysis. By examining Western and Muslim thinkers side by side across metaphysics, epistemology, and axiology, prospective educators identify foundational convergence, clarify irreconcilable divergences, and construct a coherent personal teaching philosophy that is both professionally rigorous and culturally grounded.

### Western scholars' and Muslim scholars' views on education

Understanding the educational landscape requires analysing how Western and Muslim scholars formulated the core problem of schooling within their respective civilisational contexts.

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| Western Tradition: Anthropocentric and Secular Trajectory                   |
| * Classical Idealism and Realism: Virtue rooted in rational cosmic order   |
| * Naturalism: Development driven by biological maturation and autonomy     |
| * Pragmatism: Social intelligence forged through democratic inquiry        |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------+
                                      vs
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| Muslim Tradition: Theocentric and Unified Trajectory                        |
| * Classical Islamic Pedagogy: Intellectual and social mastery under Tawhid  |
| * Reconstructionist Modernism: Khudi self-realisation and dynamic action    |
| * Integrated Knowledge: Seamless union of empirical and normative truth    |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

In the Western tradition, educational thought displays an evolving trajectory from cosmic rationalism toward naturalistic pragmatism. For Plato, education is the systematic turning of the soul toward transcendent eternal Forms, cultivating a just republic ruled by philosophical wisdom. Aristotle grounded virtue in empirical observation, habituation, and rational contemplation (*theoria*). Jean-Jacques Rousseau challenged classical bookish authoritarianism by championing child-centred natural development, asserting that the child is innately good and must learn through sensory experience before societal corruption takes hold. John Dewey extended this empirical focus into social democracy, arguing that education is not preparation for life but life itself, where knowledge is actively constructed through collaborative experimental inquiry (*inquiry learning*). Across this Western spectrum, particularly in modern developments, education became increasingly anthropocentric (centred on human reason and welfare), secular, and focused on democratic civic participation and empirical utility.

In contrast, Muslim educational thought is fundamentally theocentric (centred on God) while maintaining robust empirical and rational dimensions. Classical and modern Muslim scholars rooted all educational inquiry in *Tawhid* (the divine unity of God), viewing the pursuit of knowledge (*'ilm*) as a mandatory religious duty that encompasses both revealed spiritual wisdom (*naqli* sciences) and acquired empirical sciences (*'aqli* sciences). Ibn Khaldun analysed education as an empirical, socioeconomic craft (*sina'ah*) essential for human civilisation (*'umran*), cautioning against excessive abstraction, severe physical punishment, and rote memorisation while advocating graduated, contextual instruction. In the twentieth century, Allama Muhammad Iqbal addressed the colonial subjugation of Muslim societies by calling for educational reconstruction (rebuilding thought and society). Iqbal critiqued pure Western intellectualism for divorcing intellect (*'aql*) from spiritual love (*'ishq*), producing technological power without moral purpose. For Iqbal, true education awakens *Khudi* (creative selfhood), fostering dynamic individuals (*Mu'min*) capable of transforming the physical universe while acting as ethical vicegerents (*Khalifah*) of God.

### Comparison of Western and Muslim scholars

To compare these traditions systematically, educators evaluate their positions across core philosophical dimensions. The historical development of these educational traditions reveals parallel intellectual milestones across centuries, illustrating mutual borrowing, civilisational dialogue, and distinct structural transitions.

<Figure id="fig-U3-12" src="/img/figures/efmp-409/unit-03/fig-U3-12.svg" alt="Timeline displaying major Muslim educational scholars from the classical era to modern times: Al-Ghazali, Ibn Khaldun, Shah Waliullah, Sir Syed Ahmad Khan, and Allama Iqbal." />

A rigorous comparison reveals significant areas of convergence alongside fundamental divergences:

1. **Areas of Convergence:**
   - **Active Learning and Staged Progression:** Aristotle, Rousseau, Dewey, and Ibn Khaldun all insist that children cannot absorb complex intellectual abstractions before developing basic sensory, linguistic, and experiential foundations. Ibn Khaldun's three-stage spiral curriculum anticipates modern developmental sequencing by centuries.
   - **Rejection of Coercive Pedagogy:** Both Rousseau and Ibn Khaldun vigorously condemned severe physical punishments in the classroom, demonstrating that harsh discipline destroys curiosity, fosters deception, and crushes inner motivation.
   - **Holistic Development:** Plato, Aristotle, Ibn Khaldun, and Iqbal all agree that schooling must develop the whole person, integrating intellect, physical capability, emotional maturity, and civic character rather than reducing schooling to mechanical vocational training.

2. **Areas of Fundamental Divergence:**
   - **Epistemological Foundation:** Western pragmatism and secular naturalism treat truth as provisional, evolving, and humanly constructed through social experimentation. Muslim educational philosophy maintains that while empirical and historical truths are context-dependent and subject to scientific verification, ultimate moral, metaphysical, and spiritual truths are divinely revealed in the Quran and Sunnah, providing an immutable normative anchor.
   - **The Aim of Human Existence:** In Rousseau and Dewey, the primary goal of the individual is autonomous self-realisation and democratic citizenship within human society. In Islamic pedagogy, human selfhood (*Khudi*) reaches its highest maturity not in unfettered autonomy, but in willing moral obedience and active vicegerency (*Khilafah*) before God.
   - **The Role of the Teacher:** In progressive Western thought, the teacher functions primarily as a facilitator, resource manager, or democratic guide. In Islamic educational thought, while student active engagement is celebrated, the teacher retains sacred moral authority as an ethical exemplar (*Mu'addib*) and spiritual guide (*Murabbi*).

### Writing a personal teaching philosophy statement

Developing a personal teaching philosophy statement is a critical capstone requirement in professional teacher education. A teaching philosophy statement is not a vague collection of inspirational quotes or unstructured personal opinions. Rather, it is a structured, evidence-based reflection articulating how a teacher translates theoretical principles into deliberate classroom decisions.

A professional teaching philosophy statement serves three key functions in Pakistani teacher education:
1. **Self-Regulation and Reflective Practice:** It provides an explicit conceptual framework against which educators evaluate their lesson plans, classroom management strategies, and assessment designs.
2. **Professional Credentialing and Portfolios:** It forms the central anchor of the professional teaching portfolio required for graduation from B.Ed programmes and evaluation by provincial school boards.
3. **Contextual Pedagogical Integration:** It enables prospective teachers to articulate how they harmonise modern evidence-based instructional methods (such as collaborative inquiry and formative assessment) with Islamic values, local cultural dynamics, and national educational policies.

#### Structure of an effective teaching philosophy statement

A standard 250-to-300-word teaching philosophy statement follows a clear four-part structure:

1. **Foundational Beliefs (Aims and Human Nature):** State your core stance on the nature of the learner and the primary purpose of education (drawing explicitly on selected philosophical traditions).
2. **Curriculum and Epistemology (What and Why):** Explain how you select and organise learning content, balancing empirical inquiry, practical skills, and ethical values.
3. **Classroom Methodology and Teacher Role (How):** Describe your daily instructional strategies, classroom environment, teacher-student relationship, and approach to student diversity.
4. **Assessment and Social Vision (Outcome):** Articulate how you evaluate student learning authentically and what societal contribution your classroom strives to achieve.

## Worked example

### Example: Comparative analysis and philosophy formulation

#### Step 1: Comparative breakdown of a classroom challenge

*Scenario:* A Grade 8 science teacher in Sukkur notices that students memorise definitions of ecosystem dynamics from the textbook to pass quarterly examinations, but they cannot explain why local canal water contamination threatens aquatic life in their own community.

- **Dewey's Pragmatic Diagnosis:** The teacher is treating knowledge as static information disconnected from lived social reality. Solution: Organise a project-based investigation where students test canal water samples, interview local farmers, and propose water treatment solutions.
- **Ibn Khaldun's Pedagogical Diagnosis:** The teacher has violated the rule of graduated instruction by forcing verbal abstractions (*hifz*) before students grasp practical concrete examples (*amthilah*). Furthermore, rote cramming creates passive learners incapable of acquiring a stable professional faculty (*malaka*). Solution: Begin with tangible community observations, teach terminology gradually, and facilitate student dialogue.
- **Iqbal's Reconstructionist Diagnosis:** The classroom divorces intellectual knowledge (*'aql*) from moral purpose and social action. Learning about pollution without fostering ethical stewardship (*Amanah*) leaves the soul passive. Solution: Connect scientific data to environmental ethics and student civic responsibility.

#### Step 2: Sample personal teaching philosophy statement (~270 words)

> "I believe that every learner is endowed with divine potential and innate curiosity that must be nurtured through purposeful, holistic education. My pedagogical aim is to develop critical thinkers who embody ethical integrity, social responsibility, and scientific competence, harmonising modern empirical inquiry with Islamic moral values.
>
> In my classroom, knowledge is not a static body of textbook facts to be passively memorised. Guided by John Dewey's commitment to experiential learning and Ibn Khaldun's emphasis on graduated instruction, I design learning experiences where students actively investigate real-world problems. I sequence learning from concrete community observations to abstract conceptual principles, ensuring that students develop an enduring intellectual mastery (*malaka*) rather than superficial examination recall.
>
> My instructional role combines the democratic facilitation of modern inquiry with the moral stewardship of the traditional *Mu'addib*. I foster an emotionally safe, inclusive classroom where inquiry is welcomed, mistakes are treated as essential learning milestones, and coercive punishment is strictly replaced with restorative dialogue and high expectations. I incorporate collaborative group work, structured question-and-answer exchanges, and reflective writing to engage diverse learning styles.
>
> Assessment in my classroom is continuous, transparent, and formative. I evaluate not only factual comprehension through criterion-referenced tasks but also critical reasoning, ethical deliberation, and collaborative teamwork. Inspired by Allama Iqbal's philosophy of *Khudi*, I strive to empower young Pakistani learners to discover their unique creative capabilities, reject intellectual passivity, and view their education as a sacred instrument for societal reform and national uplift."

Work in pairs. Plan for about 20 minutes.

## Summary

- Western educational thought evolved from classical idealism (Plato) and realism (Aristotle) through developmental naturalism (Rousseau) to progressive social pragmatism (Dewey), shifting educational aims from cosmic contemplation toward secular democratic problem-solving.
- Muslim educational thought, rooted in the foundational principle of *Tawhid*, synthesises revealed divine guidance (*naqli*) with empirical and rational sciences (*'aqli*), viewing learning as a lifelong religious duty and social necessity.
- Key convergences between Western and Muslim traditions include the insistence on active, developmentally sequenced learning, the complete rejection of coercive physical punishment, and the commitment to educating the whole person.
- Major divergences centre on epistemology (provisional experimental truth versus eternal divine moral truth) and human ultimate destiny (autonomous social selfhood versus ethical vicegerency before God).
- A personal teaching philosophy statement is a structured, 250-to-300-word professional reflection articulating an educator's core beliefs regarding learning aims, curriculum design, classroom methodology, and ethical student assessment.
