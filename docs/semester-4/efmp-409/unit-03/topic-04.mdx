# Topic 3.4 - Pragmatism, Dewey and Ibn Khaldun

## A real classroom situation

Zainab is a B.Ed trainee visiting a government elementary school near Thatta. In the courtyard outside the Class 5 room, a drinking-water tap has been leaking constantly, creating a muddy puddle where mosquitoes breed. Inside the classroom, the teacher, Sir Aslam, is teaching a general science unit on "Water: States of Matter and Conservation".

Rather than having pupils read textbook paragraphs silently, Sir Aslam leads the children outside to the leaking tap. He gathers them in a circle and asks, "Look at this tap. How much clean drinking water are we losing every hour, and what problem does this create for our school?"

The pupils examine the dripping tap with interest. Back in class, Sir Aslam forms small working groups. One group places a measuring jug under the tap for ten minutes and calculates the daily water loss. Another group unscrews the tap head with a wrench under teacher supervision, discovering a worn rubber washer. A third group investigates the price of a replacement washer at the local bazaar. Within two days, the class buys a twenty-rupee washer, repairs the tap, drains the puddle, and writes a short guide on water conservation for other classes. Zainab observes that when learning tackles a real problem through action and reflection, every child is engaged. This embodies pragmatism and the active-learning principle.

## Explanation

Sir Aslam's lesson illustrates pragmatism and active learning, two ideas that reshaped modern schooling.

### What is pragmatism?

Pragmatism is a modern philosophical school that originated in the United States in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, developed by thinkers such as Charles Sanders Peirce, William James, and John Dewey (Hildebrand, 2018/2024; HEC, 2012/2013). The word derives from the Greek *pragma*, meaning action, deed, or practical affair.

Pragmatism analyzed across philosophical branches:

- **Metaphysics (Reality)**: Reality is not a static, finished, eternal realm of ideas (idealism), nor is it a rigid machine governed by unchanging physical laws (realism). For pragmatists, reality is an ongoing, dynamic process of change, growth, and interaction between living organisms and their environment. Experience is the core of reality.
- **Epistemology (Knowledge)**: Knowledge is not a passive mirror of external objects or an innate recollection of Forms. Knowledge is an instrument (*instrumentalism*) created through active human inquiry to solve real problems. A concept or idea is a hypothesis. The truth of an idea is judged by its practical consequences: an idea is true if it works to resolve a genuine difficulty and guides action successfully.
- **Axiology (Values)**: Values are contextual, evolving, and tested in social practice. What is good is that which promotes the continuous growth, well-being, and democratic harmony of human individuals and their communities.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Pragmatism means opportunism."** In political conversations, "pragmatism" often pejoratively means having no principles, compromising for convenience, or doing whatever is expedient. In philosophy of education, this is completely wrong. Philosophical pragmatism requires disciplined, evidence-based scientific inquiry. It demands rigorous testing of ideas against observable facts, holding teachers and pupils accountable for the real consequences of their choices.

### Dewey and the active-learning principle

John Dewey (1859 - 1952) was the foremost philosopher of pragmatism and progressive education (Hildebrand, 2018/2024). In landmark works including *Democracy and Education* (1916) and *Experience and Education* (1938), Dewey transformed educational theory.

Dewey's core educational contributions include:

1. **Education is life itself**: Dewey rejected the traditional view that education is merely preparation for distant adult life. A child is living right now. If school consists only of passive listening for a future career, it kills intrinsic motivation. Education is a continuous process of living and participating in social life.
2. **Reconstruction of experience**: Dewey defined education as "that reconstruction or reorganization of experience which adds to the meaning of experience, and which increases ability to direct the course of subsequent experience." Each new experience builds on prior knowledge, tests it in action, and reshapes understanding.
3. **The reflective inquiry cycle**: Dewey argued that authentic thinking follows a five-step inquiry cycle:
   - Encountering an authentic problem or felt difficulty in the environment.
   - Clarifying and defining the conditions of the problem through observation.
   - Generating hypotheses, creative solutions, and predicted consequences.
   - Taking active testing action or conducting an experiment in the real world.
   - Reflecting on consequences, reconstructing understanding, and forming new inquiry.
4. **The democratic school**: Dewey viewed the classroom as an embryonic democratic community. In traditional schools, competitive silence isolates pupils. In a progressive democratic school, pupils collaborate in small groups on shared tasks, learning communication, mutual respect, shared decision-making, and civic responsibility.

### Ibn-e-Khaldun's views on education

The HEC course guide pairs pragmatism with the educational insights of the great Muslim historian and sociologist Abd al-Rahman Ibn Khaldun (1332 - 1406 CE) (HEC, 2012/2013). In Chapter 6 of his celebrated *Muqaddimah* (Prolegomena to History), Ibn Khaldun presented revolutionary pedagogical observations based on empirical analysis of Islamic societies (Cheddadi, 1994).

Ibn Khaldun's key educational principles:

- **Gradual instruction (*at-tadrij*)**: Ibn Khaldun argued that human intellect cannot absorb complex scientific concepts all at once. Teaching must proceed in three gradual, cumulative cycles:
  1. *First cycle*: Present a concise, general overview of the discipline, introducing basic concepts and terms simply with concrete illustrations.
  2. *Second cycle*: Repeat the subject from beginning to end at a deeper level, explaining nuances, clarifying reasoning, and resolving common doubts.
  3. *Third cycle*: Revisit the discipline thoroughly, addressing complex exceptions, comparing scholarly debates, and guiding the learner to complete mastery.
  Ibn Khaldun warned that teachers who dump intricate commentaries and advanced disputes onto beginners cause mental exhaustion, confusion, and lifelong aversion to study.
- **Formation of a lasting habit or faculty (*malaka*)**: True learning is not the superficial memorization of technical terminology. It is the acquisition of an ingrained habit, skill, or practical faculty (*malaka*). A pupil who can recite grammatical definitions without being able to write a clear paragraph has no *malaka*. *Malaka* is developed through continuous practical exercise, application, and demonstration.
- **Strict prohibition of harshness and corporal punishment**: Five centuries before modern psychology, Ibn Khaldun fiercely condemned teachers who beat and intimidate pupils. He explained that severe punishment breaks the pupil's spirit, destroys intrinsic motivation, breeds deceit and lying to escape punishment, and produces adults who lack initiative and act rightly only under surveillance. The teacher must instruct with kindness, patience, and mutual respect.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Ibn Khaldun was a pragmatist in Dewey's sense."** The HEC course guide groups Ibn Khaldun under the pragmatism unit because of his emphasis on practical mastery, experience, and rejection of bookish pedantry. However, as noted in the content specification drafting note 7, calling Ibn Khaldun a modern pragmatist is historically inaccurate. Ibn Khaldun lived five hundred years before Dewey. Metaphysically, Ibn Khaldun was an orthodox Sunni Muslim scholar who believed firmly in divine revelation (*wahy*), absolute moral accountability before Allah, and the eternal spiritual realm. While his pedagogical methods (learning by doing, gradual mastery, anti-cruelty) anticipated practical progressive education, his worldview was grounded in Islamic revelation, not secular experimental naturalism.

### Applications of pragmatism in education

When pragmatism and active learning are applied in Pakistani schools, they transform traditional teacher-centred classrooms into vibrant learning communities:

- **Problem-based learning (PBL)**: Units of study begin with an authentic local problem (such as waste accumulation, school garden erosion, or community health hygiene) rather than textbook chapter outlines.
- **Project method**: Pupils plan, execute, and evaluate real projects in collaborative groups, applying mathematics, science, language, and social studies in unified action.
- **Integrated curriculum**: Pragmatists dismantle rigid disciplinary walls. A project on village agriculture naturally integrates biology (crop life cycles), economics (market prices), geography (soil types), and language (interviewing local farmers).
- **Teacher as facilitator**: The teacher is not a lecturing dictator on a raised platform, but a guide, resource person, and co-investigator who supports pupil inquiries.

**Worked example: Traditional versus pragmatic lesson design**

| Feature | Traditional passive lesson | Pragmatic active-learning lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Grade and Topic | Class 4 Social Studies: Local Government | Class 4 Social Studies: Local Government |
| Pupil activity | Pupils read textbook definitions of Union Councils, memorise duties of councillors, and answer end-of-chapter questions. | Pupils identify an uncollected rubbish heap near their school, draft an interview guide, interview a visiting local Union Council member, and present a pupil action plan. |
| Cognitive move | Rote recall and recognition of administrative terms. | Formulating questions, collecting empirical data, analyzing civic roles, and communicating solutions. |
| Relevance | Abstract, quickly forgotten after the examination. | Concrete civic experience developing real community participation. |

## Activity: Designing a problem-based inquiry cycle

Work in groups of three. Time allocation: about 25 minutes.

1. Identify one real, persistent challenge in your practicum school or neighborhood (for example: excessive plastic waste, lack of reading books in the library, or high absenteeism during harvest season).
2. Apply Dewey's five-step reflective inquiry cycle to design a two-week learning project for Class 6 or 7 pupils:
   - Step 1: How will pupils encounter and define the problem?
   - Step 2: What data, observations, or measurements will pupils collect?
   - Step 3: What hypotheses or action plans will pupils formulate?
   - Step 4: What concrete testing action or project will pupils carry out?
   - Step 5: How will pupils reflect on the results and evaluate their success?
3. Review your plan against Ibn Khaldun's principle of *at-tadrij*: is the project graded gradually so that pupils are not overwhelmed?
4. Present your project outline on a chart to another group.

## Summary

Pragmatism is the philosophy that views reality as dynamic change and evaluates knowledge by its workable consequences in solving real human problems. Championed by John Dewey, pragmatism established active learning, reflective inquiry, project-based curricula, and democratic classroom collaboration. Paralleling these pedagogical insights, the fourteenth-century Muslim scholar Ibn Khaldun advocated gradual instruction in three deepening cycles (*at-tadrij*), the acquisition of practical faculties (*malaka*), and the absolute rejection of corporal punishment. Both traditions show that education flourishes through purposeful practice, reflection, and humane guidance.

## Summative task

Read the following educational scenario and complete the analysis:

> A primary school teacher in Badin, Master Qasim, prepares his Class 4 pupils for annual examinations by having them memorise twelve grammatical definitions in Sindhi and Urdu. He tests them daily with a wooden cane, punishing any child who stumbles over a word. None of his pupils can compose a three-sentence letter to an uncle requesting a book. Master Qasim claims: "Severe discipline makes the mind sharp, and memorising rules is the only way to master language."

In 300 - 350 words, write an evaluation of Master Qasim's teaching from the combined perspectives of John Dewey and Ibn Khaldun:
1. Use Ibn Khaldun's concepts of *at-tadrij*, *malaka*, and prohibition of severity to analyze why Master Qasim's methods fail to produce real language competence and damage pupil character.
2. Use Dewey's active-learning principle and inquiry cycle to explain why memorising abstract grammatical rules without communicative practice fails to educate.
3. Propose a concrete three-step plan showing how Master Qasim could redesign his language teaching to build genuine linguistic ability (*malaka*) in an encouraging, active classroom.
