# Topic 3.5 - Iqbal as an educationist

## A real classroom situation

On a Friday morning at a government high school in Badin, B.Ed trainee Tariq attends the weekly school assembly. A Class 9 student steps up to the microphone and recites a famous couplet by Allama Iqbal with dramatic rhythm:

*"Khudi ko kar buland itna ke har taqdeer se pehle / Khuda bande se khud pooche, bata teri raza kya hai"*
(Elevate your selfhood so high that before every decree of destiny, God Himself asks you: tell Me, what is your desire?)

The assembly applauds politely. Later that morning, Tariq speaks with the headmaster, who remarks, "Iqbal wrote beautiful verses for national holidays and school functions, but for actual classroom teaching we need modern Western curriculum models. Poetry cannot run a school."

Tariq mentions this conversation to Sir Farooq, an experienced senior teacher of literature and Pakistan Studies. Sir Farooq shakes his head firmly. "That is the greatest tragedy of our schools," he says. "We treat Allama Iqbal as a decorative ceremonial figure, yet his entire philosophical life was a profound manifesto on education. His concept of *Khudi* directly challenges passive cramming, rote obedience, and fatalism. If we taught our pupils what Iqbal actually wrote about the creative human personality, our classrooms would be completely transformed." Tariq decides to investigate how Iqbal's philosophy provides an authentic foundation for educational practice in Pakistan.

## Explanation

Allama Muhammad Iqbal (1877 - 1938) was a philosopher, poet, jurist, and educator who studied in Lahore, Cambridge, and Munich. Through his English lectures published as *The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam* and his philosophical Urdu and Persian poetry, Iqbal developed a comprehensive, original philosophy of education (HEC, 2012/2013; Iqbal Academy Pakistan, n.d.).

### Iqbal as an educationist

Iqbal's educational philosophy is built upon the foundational concept of **Khudi** (variously translated as Selfhood, Individuality, Ego, or Personality).

- **The meaning of *Khudi***: In everyday Urdu, the word *khudi* can mean selfish pride or arrogance. In Iqbal's philosophy, this is a total misunderstanding. For Iqbal, *Khudi* is the unique, conscious, creative individuality that God has placed within every human being. It is the divine spark, the moral personality, and the capacity for self-realisation, freedom, and responsibility.
- **The aim of education**: Education is not the passive absorption of external facts, nor is it the crushing of human individuality to fit a conformist mould. The supreme aim of education is the **fortification and development of Khudi**. A good school awakens the learner's dormant individuality, strengthens self-respect, and inspires the pupil to become an active, creative, and purposeful agent in the world.
- **Dynamic struggle against fatalism (*Tag-o-Dou*)**: Iqbal vehemently rejected the fatalistic quietism that had paralyzed Muslim societies for centuries. He rejected the false belief that human beings are helpless leaves blown by destiny. Instead, he taught that life is continuous creative action, struggle, and overcoming obstacles (*tag-o-dou*). A school that trains children to be passive, silent, and compliant destroys their *Khudi*. A genuine school inspires initiative, courage, and creative ambition.
- **The individual and the community (*Millat*)**: Although Iqbal stressed individuality, he did not advocate selfish, solitary individualism. In *Rumuz-e-Bekhudi* (Mysteries of Selflessness), he explained that the individual (*fard*) finds full realization and moral purpose only through service to the community (*millat*). Education must balance individual self-realisation with civic duty, social justice, and collective solidarity.

### The contribution of Iqbal's poetry to education

Iqbal chose poetry as his primary medium because poetry reaches the heart, awakens emotion, and inspires action in ways that abstract academic prose cannot. His poetic anthologies, including *Asrar-e-Khudi*, *Bang-e-Dra*, *Bal-e-Jibril*, and *Zarb-e-Kaleem*, constitute an educational curriculum for youth (Iqbal Academy Pakistan, n.d.).

Key educational themes in Iqbal's poetry:

1. **The Symbol of the *Shaheen* (Falcon)**: Iqbal repeatedly offers the *Shaheen* as an educational ideal for youth. The falcon possesses four noble traits:
   - High soaring ambition: It flies high above mountains and scorns low ground.
   - Self-reliance: It does not build soft, comfortable nests, nor does it feed on prey killed by others.
   - Courage and solitude: It braves harsh winds and rejoices in challenge.
   - Refusal of captivity: It cannot be bought, tamed, or confined.
   Iqbal asks teachers: Are you raising falcons who soar in intellectual discovery, or pigeons who wait to be fed crumbs of memorised notes?
2. **The harmony of *Aql* (Intellect) and *Ishq* (Love / Intuition)**: One of Iqbal's most profound philosophical insights is the relationship between intellect and moral love:
   - *Aql* (Intellect): Empirical observation, scientific inquiry, logical analysis, and technological skill. Intellect is indispensable for mastering the physical world. However, intellect alone can be cold, selfish, and destructive (as witnessed in modern warfare and exploitation).
   - *Ishq* (Love): Deep moral vision, spiritual conviction, compassion, and creative devotion to high ideals.
   - The synthesis: Education must integrate both. Intellect provides the practical tools, while love provides the moral compass. Iqbal's synthesis is commonly paraphrased as: "Intellect without love is demonic; intellect with love is divine."

### Iqbal's views on the teacher, the curriculum and pedagogy

Iqbal translated his philosophical vision into concrete pedagogical guidance (HEC, 2012/2013; Iqbal Academy Pakistan, n.d.):

- **The role of the teacher**: The teacher is a *Murshid* - not in the superstitious sense of a cult figure, but as an enlightened spiritual and intellectual mentor. The teacher embodies high moral character, respects the pupil's developing *Khudi*, and ignites the learner's inner potential. A teacher who insults, humiliates, or physically beats children commits a grave crime against their *Khudi*, producing servile, cowardly personalities.
- **The curriculum**: Iqbal advocated an integrated, balanced curriculum. He strongly urged Eastern societies to adopt modern Western empirical sciences, technology, and rigorous research, arguing that scientific inquiry was historically pioneered by Muslim scholars. However, he insisted that modern science must be integrated with Islamic spiritual values, history, literature, and ethical training to prevent moral bankruptcy.
- **Pedagogy and methodology**: Iqbal opposed rote learning and passive lecturing. Learning must be dynamic, participatory, and grounded in purposeful action (*aml*). Pupils should be encouraged to ask bold questions, test received ideas, write original compositions, and engage in social service that improves their local communities.

**Worked example: Analyzing an educational activity through Iqbal's philosophy**

| Dimension | Conventional passive teaching | Teaching guided by Iqbal's *Khudi* |
|---|---|---|
| Grade and Subject | Class 8 Urdu Poetry | Class 8 Urdu Poetry |
| Activity | Teacher recites Iqbal's poem "Pahar aur Gilhari" (The Mountain and the Squirrel). Pupils copy dictionary meanings of five difficult words, memorize the summary for the exam, and recite the verse in chorus without discussion. | Pupils read the poem and debate the central theme: "Even the tiny squirrel has a unique God-given ability that the massive mountain lacks." Pupils write a 150-word reflection identifying their own unique talents and how they can serve their community. |
| Cognitive and moral move | Mechanical rote recall of rhyming words and pre-written summaries. | Self-reflection, recognizing personal dignity (*Khudi*), appreciating diversity of human talents, and developing moral agency. |
| Iqbalian verdict | Defeats Iqbal's purpose: turns a call to self-awareness into mindless repetition. | Embodies Iqbal's vision: strengthens self-worth, mutual respect, and purposeful ambition. |

## Activity: Analyzing an Iqbal verse for classroom practice

Work in pairs. Time box: about 20 minutes.

1. Examine the famous couplet from *Bang-e-Dra*:
   *"Sabaq phir parh sadaqat ka, adalat ka, shaja'at ka / Liya jaye ga tujh se kaam dunya ki imamat ka"*
   (Read again the lesson of truth, justice, and courage; you will be called upon to lead the world.)
2. Discuss in your pair:
   - What are the three core moral virtues named in the first line?
   - What is the ultimate social and civic outcome named in the second line?
3. Design one classroom practice or school routine for Class 7 pupils in Sindh that directly teaches one of these three virtues (Truth, Justice, or Courage) through action rather than mere lecturing.
4. Record your design in three clear sentences and share it with your classmate.

## Summary

Allama Muhammad Iqbal presented a vibrant, transformative educational philosophy centered on *Khudi* (selfhood and creative moral individuality). Opposing passive quietism and rote conformity, Iqbal conceived education as dynamic self-realisation achieved through continuous creative struggle (*tag-o-dou*). Through his educational poetry and the symbol of the *Shaheen*, he inspired youth toward self-reliance, high moral ambition, and civic duty. By balancing empirical scientific intellect (*aql*) with spiritual conviction and compassion (*ishq*), Iqbal's vision provides a powerful blueprint for holistic character education and intellectual modernization in Pakistan.

> A district education conference in Sindh debates the motion: "To modernize education in Pakistan, schools should focus solely on science, technology, mathematics, and English language skills; regional poetry, literature, and Islamic ethical philosophy should be removed from the curriculum as outdated burdens."

In 300 - 350 words, write an essay responding to this motion from the perspective of Allama Iqbal's educational philosophy:
1. Explain how Iqbal would endorse the call for modern science, technology, and rigorous empirical study, citing his views on intellectual progress.
2. Analyze why Iqbal would vehemently reject removing poetry, literature, and spiritual ethics, using his concepts of *Khudi* and the harmony of *Aql* and *Ishq*.
3. Conclude by outlining an integrated curriculum model that honors both modern technological mastery and the cultivation of an ethical, self-reliant personality.
