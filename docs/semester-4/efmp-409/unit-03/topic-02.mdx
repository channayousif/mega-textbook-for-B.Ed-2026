# Topic 3.2 - Aristotle and realism

## A real classroom situation

During her practicum at a government middle school in Larkana, Mehak observes a Class 6 general science lesson on plant structure taught by Madam Rashida. In the previous term, another teacher had forced pupils to copy definitions of roots, stems, and leaves from the textbook and memorise them for a written test. Many pupils had scored high marks but could not identify a taproot in their own home courtyards.

Madam Rashida does something completely different. She leads the class outside to the school garden. She divides the pupils into groups of four and asks each group to pull up a common weed, rinse the dirt from its roots in a bucket of water, and examine the specimen with magnifying glasses. The pupils touch the root hairs, observe the branching patterns, measure leaf lengths with rulers, and sketch what they see into observation notebooks.

When they return to the classroom, Madam Rashida asks, "What did you observe about how the plant anchors itself in the soil?" The pupils eagerly describe the physical structures they felt and measured. Mehak realizes that Madam Rashida is not starting with abstract definitions. She begins with concrete physical reality and lets pupils discover concepts through sensory experience. This approach embodies the philosophy of realism.

## Explanation

While idealism turns inward toward the world of pure ideas, realism turns outward toward the objective physical world.

### Aristotle as an educationist

Aristotle (384 - 322 BCE), Plato's greatest pupil and founder of the Lyceum in Athens, is regarded as the father of philosophical realism (Shields, 2008/2020). Although Aristotle respected Plato, he famously remarked that while he loved his teacher, he loved truth more. Aristotle could not accept Plato's claim that the physical world around us is merely an illusory shadow.

Aristotle developed foundational insights for education:

- **Hylomorphism (Matter and Form)**: Aristotle argued that reality consists of concrete physical particulars composed of both matter (the physical substance) and form (the organizing structure or essence). A wooden table is not a shadow of an invisible Form of Tableness; the form of the table exists directly inside the real, physical wood.
- **Education for *Eudaimonia***: In the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Aristotle asserted that the supreme goal of human life and education is *eudaimonia*, translated as human flourishing, happiness, or living well. Flourishing is achieved through the active exercise of human reason in accordance with moral and intellectual virtue.
- **Virtue through habituation**: Aristotle made a crucial distinction: intellectual virtues can be taught through direct instruction, but moral virtues (such as honesty, courage, and temperance) are acquired through **habituation** (*ethos*). We do not become honest by memorising rules; we become honest by practicing honest acts repeatedly until honesty becomes second nature. Therefore, education must form good practical habits in early childhood long before children can understand the philosophical reasons behind them.
- **Universal, state-regulated schooling**: In the *Politics*, Aristotle argued that education must be publicly organized, compulsory, and identical for all citizens. Because the preservation of a community depends on the character of its citizens, education cannot be left to private parental whim or commercial profit.

### What is realism?

Realism is the philosophy that asserts that an objective physical world exists independently of the human mind, our beliefs, or our desires (Curren, 2025; HEC, 2012/2013). Whether a human being is looking at a tree or not, the tree exists with its own physical properties and natural laws.

Realism structured across the three branches of philosophy:

- **Metaphysics (Reality)**: The physical universe of matter, energy, and physical laws is real and objective. Things exist outside our perception.
- **Epistemology (Knowledge)**: Knowledge originates in sensory perception and empirical observation. We know the world through our senses: sight, hearing, touch, taste, and smell. Through inductive logic, the human mind observes particular facts and abstracts universal principles. The realist holds to the **correspondence theory of truth**: a statement is true if and only if it accurately corresponds to objective physical reality.
- **Axiology (Values)**: Values and moral principles are derived from nature, human rational requirements, and the natural law of the universe. What is good is that which enables human beings to live in harmony with natural order and rational flourishing.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Realism rejects ideals and moral values."** Because realists emphasize physical objects, science, and facts, critics sometimes claim that realism is crude materialism that ignores morality, art, and spirituality. This is incorrect. Realism does not reject ideals; rather, it insists that moral ideals must be grounded in real human nature and tested against factual conditions. For a realist teacher, character building is not an airy daydream. It is the practical formation of observable, daily habits of justice, cleanliness, and responsibility.

### The application of realism in education

Realism provides clear guidance for the organization of curriculum and teaching:

- **Educational aims**: To equip the learner to understand, adapt to, and thrive within the natural and social world. Education prepares individuals for practical, productive, and rational living.
- **Curriculum**: The realist curriculum gives pride of place to the natural and physical sciences (biology, chemistry, physics), mathematics, geography, history, and practical technical skills. Subjects are organized systematically into distinct disciplines based on objective facts and observable phenomena.
- **Pedagogical methods**: Realist pedagogy relies on sensory experience: direct observation, object lessons, laboratory experimentation, field trips, demonstrations, and inductive reasoning. The teacher presents real specimens, models, and evidence before introducing abstract formulas.
- **Role of the teacher**: The teacher is a subject-matter expert who presents objective knowledge clearly, systematically, and without emotional distortion. The teacher arranges learning environments so that pupils encounter the real world directly.
- **Role of the pupil**: The pupil is an attentive observer, a disciplined experimenter, and an objective recorder of facts. The pupil learns to test opinions against evidence rather than accepting claims on blind authority.

**Worked example: Idealism and realism in a health education lesson**

| Dimension | Idealist lesson | Realist lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Topic | Water and sanitation | Water and sanitation |
| Starting point | Teacher reads an ethical passage on purity and bodily cleanliness as a reflection of inner spiritual purity. | Teacher displays two water samples: one clear canal water containing visible sediment and bacteria under a microscope, and one boiled, filtered water. |
| Learning move | Pupils discuss the moral duty of keeping oneself and one's home clean. | Pupils observe the microbes under the lens, record bacterial presence, test water purity with simple test strips, and boil samples to measure changes. |
| Basis of conviction | Logical coherence and moral appeal to duty. | Observable empirical evidence of physical cause and biological effect. |
| Pedagogical outcome | Inspires moral intention. | Builds scientific understanding and practical preventive habits. |

Both approaches have value, but they start from different philosophical premises. The realist insists that without understanding physical bacteria and boiling points, moral intentions alone cannot prevent waterborne disease.

## Summary

Realism is the philosophy that regards the physical world as objective reality existing independently of human perception. Founded in antiquity by Aristotle, realism identifies sensory observation and inductive reasoning as the foundation of knowledge. In education, realism champions scientific inquiry, empirical demonstration, systematic discipline, and the habituation of moral virtue, equipping learners to understand and thrive in the real physical and social world.
