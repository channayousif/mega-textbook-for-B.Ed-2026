## A real classroom situation

At a government boys' high school in Larkana, two teachers teach the water cycle to Class 6 on the same day. Sir Akbar writes the definition of evaporation on the board. The pupils copy it and learn it by heart, because it came in last year's paper. Miss Farzana hangs a wet cloth in the sun and asks the class where the water will go. The pupils guess, watch, argue and then write the definition in their own words.

At the staff meeting, the head teacher says, "Both of you finished the chapter. Why are we discussing this?" A senior teacher laughs. "Philosophy is for university people. In school we just teach the syllabus and get results."

But the two lessons were not the same. They rested on different beliefs about what knowledge is, what pupils are and what school is for. By the end of this topic you will be able to name those beliefs, and say what education is *for*.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. How are education and philosophy related? What do the two fields cover, and why do they matter? And what is education for?

### The relationship between education and philosophy

**Philosophy is the thinking side; education is the doing side.** Philosophy asks what is real, what is true and what is good (Topic 1.1). Education acts on the answers, every day, with real children. Curren (2025) notes that the reasons we give for the aims of education have clear consequences for the content, manner and methods of education. In other words, a belief about *why* we educate shapes *what* and *how* we teach.

**How a philosophy reaches the classroom.** The flowchart above shows the chain in five steps.

1. **A view of reality, knowledge and values.** What is a child? Where does knowledge come from? What is a good life?
2. **Aims.** From that view comes a belief about what education is for.
3. **Curriculum.** The aims decide what is worth teaching.
4. **Method.** The aims and the view of knowledge decide how it is taught.
5. **Roles.** All of this decides what the teacher does and what the pupil does.

**A worked example: the two water-cycle lessons.** Trace each lesson back up the chain.

| Step | Sir Akbar's lesson | Miss Farzana's lesson |
|---|---|---|
| View of knowledge | Knowledge is a fixed set of correct statements held by the book and the teacher | Knowledge is built from experience and checked against evidence |
| View of the pupil | A container to be filled | An active mind that can observe and reason |
| Aim | Pass the examination | Understand the world and learn to inquire |
| Curriculum | The textbook definition | The textbook definition plus a real event pupils can see |
| Method | Dictation and memorising | Prediction, observation and discussion |
| Roles | Teacher transmits, pupil repeats | Teacher guides, pupil investigates |

Neither teacher wrote a philosophy essay. But each lesson carried a philosophy, and the philosophy decided the method. This is why "we just teach the syllabus" is never the whole story. The same syllabus can be taught under very different philosophies.

**The link runs both ways.** Philosophy guides education, and education also tests philosophy. If Miss Farzana's pupils understand evaporation better a month later, that is evidence for her view of knowledge. If they do not, she must think again. A philosophy that never meets a classroom stays in books. Teaching that never asks *why* becomes blind routine.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Philosophy is abstract and has nothing to do with the classroom."** The senior teacher in Larkana believed this. Yet every rule in his school rests on a philosophical answer. Silence in class assumes pupils learn best by listening. A ban on questions assumes knowledge comes from authority. A prize for the top scorer assumes competition is a good motive. These may be good or bad choices, but they are philosophical choices. A teacher who never examines them is still following a philosophy, only without knowing which one.

### The scope and importance of education and philosophy

**Scope** means the range of a field: what it covers.

**The scope of education.** Education covers far more than the classroom.

- **Settings.** Education is *formal* (schools, colleges and universities, with a fixed syllabus and certificates), *non-formal* (organised but flexible programmes, such as adult literacy centres and non-formal basic schools) and *informal* (unplanned learning from family, mosque, friends, work and media).
- **Ages.** It runs from early childhood to old age.
- **The whole person.** It works on the mind, the body, character, feelings and faith.
- **A field of study.** As a subject at university, education draws on philosophy, psychology, sociology and history, and it includes curriculum, teaching methods, assessment, guidance and school management.

**The scope of philosophy of education.** Philosophy of education asks questions about the nature and aims of education, what should be taught, how teaching and discipline should be done, who has the authority to educate, and what a fair share of education looks like (Curren, 2025). Curren adds that it is concerned with "larger questions of education policy" as well. So a teacher who asks "Should I punish this pupil?" and a minister who asks "Should every child be taught in the same language?" are both asking questions in this field.

**The importance of education.** Pakistan's National Education Policy 2009 states its vision as an education that helps children and young people "realize their individual potential" and "contribute to development of society and nation" (Government of Pakistan, 2009, p. 10). That sentence names three levels at which education matters.

- **For the individual**, education develops abilities, opens work and choices, and builds character.
- **For society**, it passes on shared values, teaches people to live together and prepares citizens.
- **For the nation**, it builds the skills the economy needs and the unity a country depends on.

**The importance of philosophy for a teacher.** Philosophy helps a teacher in four practical ways.

1. **It makes aims clear.** A teacher who knows *why* she is teaching can choose *what* and *how* with purpose.
2. **It helps judge new ideas.** Every few years a new method, textbook or policy arrives. Philosophy gives the questions to judge it: what does it assume about knowledge, children and values?
3. **It brings consistency.** Without a clear philosophy, a teacher may praise questions on Monday and punish them on Tuesday.
4. **It helps with hard choices.** When two good values clash, such as finishing the syllabus and helping a slow learner, philosophy helps weigh them.

### The aims of education

**Aim and objective.** An **aim** is a broad, long-term purpose: the answer to "Why do we educate at all?" An **objective** is a narrow, short-term target for one lesson or unit, such as "Pupils will be able to define evaporation." Objectives serve aims. A lesson objective that serves no aim is a lesson without a reason.

**Five aims of education.** Educational thinkers group aims in different ways. Curren (2025), for example, describes aims that form character, aims centred on the pupil's own well-being and growth, and social, civic and economic aims. This unit uses five common groups. Pakistan's National Education Policy 2009 contains aims of every kind (Government of Pakistan, 2009, pp. 10-11).

<Figure id="fig-U1-4" src="/img/figures/efmp-409/unit-01/fig-U1-4.svg" alt="Table of five aims of education, each with what it means and an example: individual aim (develop each child's full potential, such as helping a shy pupil find her talent in poetry); social aim (prepare citizens who live together, such as a class project to clean the street); vocational aim (prepare for honest work, such as a technical stream in a high school); moral aim (build good character and conduct, such as a fair class monitor system); spiritual aim (nurture faith and inner life, such as reflecting on what a Qur'an lesson asks of us)." />

1. **Individual aim.** Education should develop each child's full potential: mind, body, feelings and talents. The policy aims at "nurturing the total personality of the individual" (Government of Pakistan, 2009, p. 11, aim 8).
2. **Social aim.** Education should prepare people to live together as good citizens. The policy aims to promote national cohesion "by respecting all faiths and religions" and recognising cultural and ethnic diversity (p. 10, aim 4).
3. **Vocational aim.** Education should prepare people to earn a living. The policy aims to enable a person to earn a livelihood "honestly through skills" (p. 11, aim 17).
4. **Moral aim.** Education should build good character: honesty, justice, responsibility. The policy aims to raise individuals "committed to democratic and moral values" (p. 11, aim 9).
5. **Spiritual aim.** Education should nurture faith and inner life. The policy speaks of the "spiritual needs of individuals and society" (p. 10, aim 1). In the Muslim view of Topic 1.1, this aim joins the others: knowledge should lead to right conduct (*ta'dib*).

**Aims compete, so they must be balanced.** A school that chases only the vocational aim may produce skilled workers with no sense of right and wrong. A school that chases only the individual aim may forget the needs of the community. Most policies, like Pakistan's, list several aims at once. The hard work is balancing them in a real timetable.

**Correcting a common misconception: "The aim of education is to pass examinations."** Ask any class of trainees what school is for, and someone will say "to pass the exam". But look at the policy aims above. None of them is "pass examinations". An examination is a *tool* for checking whether aims are being met, as a thermometer checks a fever. When the tool becomes the aim, teaching shrinks to what the paper asks, and pupils memorise what they do not understand. Sir Akbar's lesson in Larkana is an example. His pupils may pass, but the individual and moral aims of education are left behind.

**A worked example: finding the aim in a statement.** On 27 November 1947, in his message to the first All Pakistan Educational Conference in Karachi, Quaid-e-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah said that there was an "immediate and urgent need" to train people in scientific and technical education "in order to build up our economic life". He added that "greater attention should be paid to technical and vocational education" (Quaid-i-Azam Mazar Management Board [QMMB], n.d.). Which aim does this imply?

1. **Find the key words.** "Technical education", "economic life", "vocational education".
2. **Match them to an aim.** These words point to preparing people for productive work: the **vocational** aim.
3. **Look for a second aim.** "Our economic life" is about the nation, not one person, so the statement also serves a **social** aim: building the new country.
4. **Note what is absent.** The quoted lines do not mention character or faith. This does not mean Jinnah ignored them. It means this statement stresses one aim, and a fair reader says so without overclaiming.

## Activity: Balancing the aims

Work in groups of five. About 30 minutes.

Your group is the staff of a government middle school in a village near Dadu. The district office says the school may add **one extra period a week** for Class 8. Each member speaks for one aim: **individual, social, vocational, moral** or **spiritual**.

1. In 5 minutes, each member prepares a one-minute case: what should the extra period be used for, and how would it serve their aim?
2. Each member presents. Others may ask one question each.
3. As a group, agree on a plan for the period. Then use the flowchart from this topic: write down the aim you chose, what pupils will study, the method, and the role of the teacher.
4. Finally, name **one aim** your plan serves less well, and say how the school could serve that aim another way.

Share your plan with another group and compare which aims each group put first.

## Summary

Philosophy and education depend on each other. A philosophy reaches the classroom through a chain: a view of reality, knowledge and values shapes the aims, the aims shape the curriculum and method, and all of these shape the roles of teacher and pupil. Classroom results, in turn, test the philosophy. Education covers formal, non-formal and informal learning across a whole life, and philosophy of education asks what education is for, what to teach, how and by whom. Education has individual, social, vocational, moral and spiritual aims, which compete and must be balanced. Passing examinations is a way of checking progress, not the aim itself.

## Summative task

Write 300-350 words. Choose **one** school rule or routine you have seen, such as morning assembly, the seating plan, class position certificates or a homework policy.

1. Describe the rule and what it asks pupils and teachers to do.
2. Analyse the philosophy behind it: what it assumes about knowledge, about pupils, and about values. Use the chain from this topic.
3. Identify which of the five aims of education the rule serves best, and which aim it serves poorly.
4. Suggest one change that would balance the aims better, and give a reason.

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing (0) |

| Analysis of the philosophy (4 marks) | Links the rule to views of knowledge, pupils and values, with a reason for each (4) | Names one or two views without reasons (2-3); only says the rule is good or bad (1) | No analysis |
| Aims served and neglected (4 marks) | Identifies the aim served best and the aim neglected, with evidence from the rule (4) | Names aims without evidence, or only one side (2-3); lists aims with no link to the rule (1) | Absent |
