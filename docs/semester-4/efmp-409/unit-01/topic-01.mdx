## A real classroom situation

Hina is a B.Ed trainee on practicum at a government girls' primary school near Tando Allahyar. After a Class 5 lesson, a father, Ghulam Rasool, comes to collect his younger daughter, Mehak. He tells Hina, "Please make Mehak educated. Her elder sister Sana left school after Class 5. Sana is not educated."

Mehak looks confused. Her sister Sana keeps the accounts of the family's small shop. She reads the Qur'an with its meaning, and she looked after their grandmother for a whole winter. Mehak asks Hina, "Miss, is my sister educated or not?"

Hina wants to say "Of course she is." Then she stops. If Sana is educated, why did her father say she is not? Hina has used the word "education" all her life, but she has never had to say what it means. By the end of this topic you will be able to answer Mehak, and you will see why the answer depends on a philosophy.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What is this course about? What do we mean by education? And what do we mean by philosophy?

### Introduction to the course

A house stands on its **foundations**. You do not see them, but they decide how strong the house is and what can be built on top. Education has foundations too. They are the ideas and forces that decide why schools exist, what they teach and how they teach it.

The University of Sindh course guide asks this course to look at five foundations of education (Higher Education Commission [HEC], 2012/2013):

- **Philosophical**: ideas about what is real, what is true and what is good.
- **Sociological**: how society and education shape each other.
- **Psychological**: how children grow and learn.
- **Ideological**: the beliefs a nation holds about the kind of people it wants to raise.
- **Historical**: how education has developed over time.

The course has five units. Unit 1 (this unit) asks what education and philosophy are, and how they are linked. Unit 2 compares the foundations listed above. Unit 3 meets great educationists of the West and the Muslim world, such as Plato, Rousseau, Ibn Khaldun and Iqbal. Unit 4 studies Islamic perspectives on education. Unit 5 asks why Pakistan's education reports and policies say what they say, and why they often fall short.

**Why a teacher needs this course.** A teacher makes hundreds of choices each week. Should pupils memorise this, or work it out? Should a quiet girl be pushed to speak? Is this lesson preparing pupils for life, or only for the paper? The foundations do not give a ready answer to each question. They give you the reasons behind the choices, so that you can choose with your eyes open.

### The meaning and definitions of education

**Where the word comes from.** The English word "education" comes from the Latin *educatio*, "a rearing, training". Two Latin verbs stand behind it (Online Etymology Dictionary, n.d.):

- *educare*: to bring up or rear, as a parent raises a child;
- *educere*: to lead out or bring out, from *e-* ("out") and *ducere* ("to lead").

These two roots give two pictures of education. In the first, the teacher shapes and fills the child, as a gardener shapes a plant. In the second, the teacher draws out what is already inside the child: the abilities, curiosity and goodness the child was born with. Keep both pictures in mind. You will meet them again in Unit 3.

**The narrow meaning.** In its narrow meaning, education is the *deliberate teaching that happens in schools, colleges and universities*. The philosopher George Kneller called this the "technical sense" of education. In this sense, society uses its institutions to pass on its knowledge, values and skills from one generation to the next (Kneller, 1971, as quoted in HEC, 2012/2013). A timetable, a syllabus, a trained teacher and an examination belong to this meaning.

**The broad meaning.** In its broad meaning, education is *any experience that shapes a person's mind, character or physical ability*. Kneller called this the "broad sense" (HEC, 2012/2013). A child learns honesty from a mother, patience from fasting, bargaining from a shop and courage from a flood. All of this is education in the broad sense. It begins before school and continues after the last examination, so it is lifelong.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Education means schooling."** Many people, like Ghulam Rasool, use "educated" to mean "has passed many classes". Schooling is an important part of education, but it is only one part. A person can sit in school for ten years and learn little. Another person can leave school early and still grow in knowledge, skill and character. When we judge only by years in school, we miss most of what education is.

**What thinkers have said.** Definitions differ because each thinker stresses a different part of the process.

- The Swiss educator **Pestalozzi** described education as the "natural, harmonious and progressive development of man's innate powers" (quoted in HEC, 2012/2013). He stresses drawing out what a child already has, in step with nature.
- The American philosopher **John Dewey** described education as a continuous reconstruction of experience: each new experience reshapes what we already know, so that we act more wisely next time (summarised in HEC, 2012/2013).
- A recent encyclopaedia entry describes education as "the promotion of desirable forms of learning and personal development" (Curren, 2025). The word "desirable" matters. It reminds us that education is not any change in a person. It is change in a direction we think is good.

**The Muslim view.** In Islam, seeking knowledge is a duty. The first word revealed to the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) was *Iqra*, "Read!" The same passage says that Allah taught by the pen and taught people what they did not know (Qur'an 96:1-5). Another verse asks whether those who know and those who do not know can be equal (Qur'an 39:9). Muslim scholars use three Arabic words for education, and each one adds a meaning (Susanti et al., 2023):

| Term | Main meaning | What it stresses |
|---|---|---|
| *Tarbiyah* | Nurturing and bringing up | Guiding the whole growth of a person, including character |
| *Ta'lim* | Teaching and informing | Passing on complete and correct knowledge |
| *Ta'dib* | Training in *adab* (right conduct) | Knowledge that shows itself in good action. The scholar Syed Muhammad Naquib al-Attas preferred this word for education |

Notice that the Muslim view, like the broad Western meaning, does not stop at knowledge. Knowledge that does not change conduct is incomplete.

**A worked example: testing a definition.** Any definition of education should answer three questions. *Who is being educated? Who or what educates? Towards what?* Let us test a definition you will often hear: "Education is passing examinations."

| Question | Answer from this definition | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| Who is educated? | Pupils who sit examinations | Leaves out adults, and children out of school |
| Who or what educates? | Schools and examination boards | Leaves out family, mosque, work and community |
| Towards what? | A pass mark | A pass mark is a sign of learning, not the purpose of it |

The definition fails all three questions. Now test Curren's definition: it covers anyone who learns, any educator, and growth "towards" what is desirable. It is wider, but it leaves open a hard question: who decides what is desirable? That question belongs to philosophy.

**Returning to Mehak.** Hina can now answer. "In the narrow meaning, your sister's schooling stopped at Class 5. In the broad meaning, she is still being educated every day: in reading the Qur'an with its meaning, in keeping accounts, in caring for your grandmother. Both meanings are true. But I hope she gets the chance to go back to school too, because schooling opens doors that the shop cannot."

### The meaning and definitions of philosophy

**Where the word comes from.** "Philosophy" comes from two Greek words: *philia*, love, and *sophia*, wisdom. A philosopher is a lover or pursuer of wisdom (Smith, 2022). Notice the word is "lover", not "owner". Philosophy is a search, not a finished set of answers.

**What philosophy does.** Philosophy is careful, reasoned thinking about the most basic questions people can ask. It does not accept an answer only because it is old, popular or said loudly. It asks for reasons, checks whether the reasons hold together, and looks for the hidden assumption under a claim. Everyone has some beliefs about these questions. Philosophy is the habit of examining them.

**The three main branches.** Philosophy has three main branches. Each one asks a basic question, and each question becomes a practical question in a school. The concept map below shows the links.

<Figure id="fig-U1-2" src="/img/figures/efmp-409/unit-01/fig-U1-2.svg" alt="Concept map with philosophy at the centre and three branches. Metaphysics asks what is real, which leads to the school question what is a child and what is worth studying. Epistemology asks what is true and how we know, which leads to how pupils should come to know. Axiology asks what is good and valuable, divides into ethics and aesthetics, and leads to which values the school should build." />

1. **Metaphysics** asks **what is real?** It tries to identify "that which is real" (Smith, 2022): is a human being only a body, or also a mind and a soul? Is the world made only of matter? *In school*, the metaphysical question becomes: what is a child, and what is worth studying? A teacher who sees a child as a soul with a purpose will plan differently from one who sees a child as a future worker.
2. **Epistemology** asks **what is true, and how do we know?** It studies "the nature and extent of human knowledge" (Smith, 2022). Does knowledge come from revelation, reason, the senses, or authority? How can we tell knowledge from mere opinion? *In school*, the epistemological question becomes: how should pupils come to know? By memorising what the book says, by testing ideas against evidence, or by both?
3. **Axiology** (also called value theory) asks **what is good and valuable?** It is the "philosophical investigation of values" (Smith, 2022). It has two parts. **Ethics** studies right and wrong conduct. **Aesthetics** studies beauty and art. *In school*, the axiological question becomes: which values should we build, and how should we treat each other?

Philosophers also study **logic**, the rules of correct reasoning. Logic is less a branch with its own question than a tool that all three branches use.

**A worked example: one decision, three branches.** A Class 6 pupil in Hyderabad says, "Sir, the book says the summer is hot because the earth is closer to the sun. My cousin says that is wrong." Should the teacher let the class question the textbook? Look at the decision branch by branch.

- *Epistemology*: Where does knowledge come from in this classroom? If the only answer is "the book says so", questioning is pointless. If knowledge rests on evidence, the class can check the claim. (The cousin is right: the seasons come from the tilt of the earth.)
- *Axiology*: Is questioning the book disrespectful, or is it honest? Which value matters more here: obedience or truthfulness? Can the teacher keep both by showing respectful ways to question?
- *Metaphysics*: What is the pupil? An empty pot to be filled, or an active mind that can reason?

The teacher's answer to this small question rests on all three branches, whether the teacher notices or not. Topic 1.2 shows how these answers spread through a whole education system.

## Activity: Is she educated?

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

Each group receives these four short cases. Discuss them one at a time.

1. **Sana**, 17, left school after Class 5. She keeps a shop's accounts, reads the Qur'an with its meaning and nursed her grandmother.
2. **Bilal**, 18, passed matric with high marks by memorising past papers. He cannot explain any science idea in his own words.
3. **Rukhsana**, 40, never went to school. She joined an adult literacy centre in Mirpurkhas last year and now reads the newspaper.
4. **Imran**, 15, left school to work in a motorcycle workshop in Sukkur. He can repair any engine and teaches new apprentices.

For each case, decide together:

- Is this person educated in the **narrow** meaning? In the **broad** meaning?
- Which of the three Muslim terms (*tarbiyah*, *ta'lim*, *ta'dib*) best describes what this person has received? Which is missing?

Then, as a group, write **one definition of education** of no more than 25 words that fits all four cases. Test it with the three questions from the worked example. Share your definition with the next group and test theirs.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Without looking back, give the two Latin verbs behind the word "education" and what each one means.
2. *(Remember)* Name the three main branches of philosophy and the basic question each one asks.
3. *(Understand)* In three sentences, explain the difference between the narrow and the broad meaning of education, with one example of each from your own life.
4. *(Apply)* A head teacher says, "Our school's job is to give pupils knowledge. Manners are the family's business." Which of the three Muslim terms for education does this view leave out? Explain your answer in two sentences.

## Summary

Education has a narrow meaning, deliberate teaching in schools, and a broad meaning, every experience that shapes a person's mind, character or body. Schooling is one part of education, not the whole of it. Western thinkers and Muslim scholars define education in different words, but most agree that it means growth in a good direction, and Muslim scholars stress that knowledge must show in conduct. Philosophy is the reasoned search for wisdom about basic questions. Its three branches, metaphysics, epistemology and axiology, ask what is real, what is true and what is good, and each becomes a practical question for a teacher.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask three people at your practicum school, for example a teacher, a pupil and a parent or support staff member, to finish this sentence: "An educated person is someone who ..." Write down their exact words. At home, decide whether each answer uses the narrow or the broad meaning, and which branch of philosophy it touches. Bring your notes to the next class.

## Summative task

Write 250-300 words. Use the three answers you collected in "Try this at your practicum school", or three definitions from this topic if you could not collect them.

1. For each answer, say whether it reflects the narrow or the broad meaning of education, and which of *tarbiyah*, *ta'lim* or *ta'dib* it is closest to.
2. Analyse what each answer leaves out, using the three questions: who is educated, who or what educates, and towards what.
3. Write your own definition of education and explain why it is stronger than the three you analysed.

*(Analyze)*

**Mini-rubric (10 marks)**

| Criterion | Strong | Partial | Missing (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Classifying the answers (3 marks) | All three classified correctly, with a reason for each (3) | Classified with few or no reasons, or one wrong (1-2) | Absent or wrong |
| Analysis of gaps (4 marks) | Uses all three questions to show exactly what each answer leaves out (4) | Names gaps without using the questions, or for only some answers (2-3); only says an answer is "incomplete" (1) | No analysis |
| Own definition (3 marks) | Clear definition that fixes the gaps found, with a reason (3) | Definition given but not linked to the gaps (1-2) | Absent |

The analysis criterion asks you to show *what* is missing and *why it matters*. Saying an answer is "good" or "bad" does not meet it.
