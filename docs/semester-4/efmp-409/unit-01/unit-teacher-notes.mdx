# Unit 1: Philosophy of Education - Teacher Notes

## Purpose and sequencing

This unit opens the course. Most student teachers arrive believing that education means schooling, that philosophy belongs to university lecture halls, and that school exists to produce results. The unit does not attack these beliefs head on. It lets student teachers test them against real cases until they see the gaps themselves.

The course guide gives two weeks of three hours each.

- **Week 1 (Topic 1.1).** Session 1: introduce the course with the house-and-foundations image, then brainstorm "An educated person is someone who ..." and keep the answers on a chart. Session 2: the meanings and definitions of education, ending with the "Is she educated?" activity. Session 3: philosophy and its three branches, using the textbook-question worked example.
- **Week 2 (Topic 1.2).** Session 1: the relationship between philosophy and education, built on the two water-cycle lessons. Session 2: scope and importance. Session 3: the aims of education and the "Balancing the aims" activity. Return to the Week 1 brainstorm chart at the end and let student teachers revise their answers.

## Teaching strategies from the course guide

- **Lecture.** Keep each lecture segment to about 15 minutes, and end each with one question for pairs to discuss.
- **Discussion.** Use the classroom situations at the start of each topic as discussion openers. Ask "What would you say?" before teaching the concept.
- **Question-answer.** Use the "Check your understanding" items orally at the start of the next session, with books closed.
- **Brainstorming.** Use it to surface everyday definitions of education and everyday views of what school is for. Record every answer without judging it; the unit returns to them.

## Likely misconceptions and how to handle them

- **"Education means schooling."** Do not simply correct it. Ask: "Is a hafiz who never went to school educated? Is a matric pass who copied every paper educated?" Let the class find that years in school and education are different things.
- **"Philosophy is abstract."** Ask the class to list five rules of their own school. For each, ask: "What does this rule assume about how children learn?" Within ten minutes the class will be doing philosophy.
- **"The aim of education is to pass examinations."** A likely wrong answer to "What is school for?" is "to get good marks so you get a good job". Accept the vocational point, then ask what else a good adult needs that no paper tests.

**One good discussion prompt:** "If every examination in Pakistan were cancelled for one year, what should schools teach?" It separates aims from tools quickly.

## Practical work

The course guide lists four kinds of practical work. Suggested tasks for this unit:

- **Group work.** The "Is she educated?" and "Balancing the aims" activities.
- **Group assignment.** Each group collects ten answers to "An educated person is someone who ..." from people outside the college, sorts them by narrow and broad meaning, and presents the pattern on a chart.
- **Individual assignment.** The Topic 1.1 or Topic 1.2 summative task.
- **Presentation.** Each student teacher presents, in three minutes, one school rule and the philosophy behind it, using the chain from Topic 1.2.

Handle the Islamic material with care and accuracy: quote verses by reference, name the translation used, and invite student teachers to check the text themselves.
