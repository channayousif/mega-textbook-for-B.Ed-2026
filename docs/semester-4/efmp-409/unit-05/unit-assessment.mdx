# Unit 5: Education Reports and Policies - Unit Assessment

## Unit summary

Pakistan has produced education reports and policies since 1947, and each one promises a better system. An education report is a document written by a commission or committee that the government appoints to study education and recommend changes; it advises and has no force of law. An education policy is a statement of intent adopted by the government that sets aims, targets and a programme, and is meant to be applied. A commission's report often becomes the raw material for a policy, but the two are not the same.

Reports and policies are framed through terms of reference, evidence, consultation and approval. The Sharif Commission was appointed in December 1958 and reported in 1959; the 1979 policy grew out of a National Education Conference and a cabinet process; the 1998-2010 policy drew on public proposals and set out its own implementation machinery. A policy is launched by a notification, funds and offices, and applied through a chain from the federal government to the province, the district, the school and the classroom.

Most policies falter in the application stage. The 1998-2010 policy itself blamed earlier failures on poor implementation, weak political commitment, low funding and a weak or non-existent monitoring and evaluation machinery. Later research adds policy inconsistency, urban-rural disparity, corruption, administrative inefficiency and a lack of stakeholder engagement. The Sharif Commission recommended a twelve-year school structure, compulsory primary education, a national-language medium, examination reform and better teachers. The 1979 policy set out Islamic and national aims and an implementation programme, including mosque and mohalla schools. The 1998-2010 policy set universal primary education by 2010 as its cornerstone, promised new schools, literacy programmes and non-formal basic education, and created a monitoring framework.

Later evidence suggests that the 1998-2010 policy's main literacy and enrolment targets were not met: adult literacy was about 55 percent in 2010 against a 70 percent target, and gross primary enrolment was about 74 percent in 2010. The case for a new policy is that circumstances change; the case against is that implementation, not the absence of a document, is usually the constraint. A new policy is worth having only if it carries the machinery to apply it.

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. *(Remember)* Which document is produced by a commission that the government appoints to study the education system and recommend changes?
   - A) An education policy
   - B) An education report
   - C) A gazette notification
   - D) A school budget
2. *(Understand)* Which statement best describes an education policy?
   - A) A report written by a commission to advise the government
   - B) A statement of intent adopted by the government that is meant to be applied
   - C) A court order that settles a dispute about a school
   - D) A newspaper article about a new school
3. *(Understand)* What is the main difference between the Sharif Commission report of 1959 and the National Education Policy 1979?
   - A) The report advises and has no force of law, while the policy is adopted by government and meant to be applied
   - B) The report was written by a minister, while the policy was written by a commission
   - C) The report set targets, while the policy only described problems
   - D) The report was never published, while the policy was published
4. *(Apply)* A government announces that 500 new primary schools will open next year. Which level of the implementation chain must act first for the promise to reach a classroom?
   - A) The classroom teacher
   - B) The provincial and federal offices that approve sites and funds
   - C) The school management committee
   - D) The pupils who will enrol
5. *(Remember)* Who chaired the Commission on National Education that reported in 1959?
   - A) Dr. M. A. Kazi
   - B) S. M. Sharif
   - C) President Ayub Khan
   - D) Dr. I. H. Qureshi
6. *(Understand)* A policy target is missed even though funds are available, because no office was named to carry it out. Which implementation factor best explains this?
   - A) Finance
   - B) Capacity
   - C) Governance
   - D) Continuity
7. *(Apply)* A school receives a science kit under a government programme, but no teacher is trained to use it and no one checks whether it is used. Which two factors best explain why the kit stays in its box?
   - A) Capacity and data
   - B) Finance and politics
   - C) Continuity and finance
   - D) Politics and continuity
8. *(Remember)* Which target was described as the cornerstone of the National Education Policy 1998-2010?
   - A) Universal primary education by 2010
   - B) A three-year bachelor's degree
   - C) The nationalisation of private schools
   - D) Free university education for all
9. *(Understand)* In its own implementation chapter, the 1998-2010 policy mainly blamed the failure of earlier policies on which cause?
   - A) Poor implementation and a weak or non-existent monitoring and evaluation machinery
   - B) Too many commissions
   - C) A lack of public interest in education
   - D) The absence of any education report before 1947
10. *(Apply)* The 1998-2010 policy set a literacy target of 70 percent by 2010. Later evidence puts adult literacy at 55.4 percent in 2010. What is the most accurate reading of this gap?
    - A) The policy was never published
    - B) The target was not met, and the gap points to implementation and funding constraints
    - C) The target was met, because literacy rose at all
    - D) The figure is wrong, because the policy promised 55 percent

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

1. *(Understand)* In two sentences, explain the difference between an education report and an education policy.
2. *(Understand)* List three steps by which a report or a policy is framed, and name one body or consultation that shaped a named document.
3. *(Analyze)* A policy promises free textbooks for every primary school. The books arrive two years late and only in some schools. Analyse which two links in the implementation chain most likely failed, and explain why.
4. *(Understand)* Name four factors that hinder the application of reports and policies, and give one Pakistani example of any one of them.
5. *(Understand)* State three main points of the Sharif Commission of 1959.
6. *(Analyze)* A later evaluation called the Sharif Commission's recommendation on university tutorials unrealistic. Analyse why, using evidence from the evaluation.
7. *(Understand)* List three commitments of the National Education Policy 1998-2010.
8. *(Analyze)* Analyse why a policy can be well written and still fail, using one factor from this unit and a consequence for a school.
9. *(Understand)* Explain in two sentences the difference between the baseline literacy rate and the target literacy rate in the 1998-2010 policy.
10. *(Analyze)* Analyse one reason why a new education policy may not solve the problems of the previous one.

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. *(Analyze)* A government appoints a commission on primary education. The commission consults teachers and parents and writes a report. The government then announces a policy with a target, a budget and a new office to run it. Two years later, a school like Seema's in Topic 5.2 has seen no change. In 300-400 words, analyse this case at three stages of the policy cycle, and explain which stage most likely failed and why.
2. *(Analyze)* Compare the Sharif Commission report of 1959 and the National Education Policy 1979 on one theme, such as compulsory education, the medium of instruction or teachers. In 300-400 words, analyse how the two documents differ in aim and method, and argue which one was more likely to be applied, using evidence from this unit.
3. *(Evaluate)* A trainee says, "The 1998-2010 policy was a failure, so Pakistan should not make another one." In 300-400 words, evaluate this claim. Use the policy's targets and later evidence to judge its record, and argue whether a new policy is the answer.
4. *(Analyze)* A Pakistani school has ten features: its morning assembly, its language of instruction, its annual examination, its textbooks, its teacher posts, its boundary wall, its school management committee, its science kit, its enrolment camp and its classroom furniture. In 300-400 words, analyse which of these features a report could recommend and which a policy must decide and fund, and explain why the distinction matters for a teacher.
5. *(Evaluate)* Choose one stated target of the National Education Policy 1998-2010, such as universal primary education by 2010, the literacy target, or the expansion of non-formal basic education. In 300-400 words, judge whether the target was met, using evidence from a source named in this unit, and explain what the result shows about the policy's implementation.

## Answers and marking guidance

### MCQ answer key

1. B - an education report is the document a commission writes to advise the government.
2. B - a policy is a statement of intent adopted by the government and meant to be applied.
3. A - the report advises and has no force of law; the policy is adopted and meant to be applied.
4. B - the provincial and federal offices must approve sites and funds before a school can open.
5. B - the Commission on National Education was chaired by S. M. Sharif.
6. C - governance: no office was named to carry the target out.
7. A - capacity (no teacher trained) and data (no one checked whether the kit was used).
8. A - universal primary education by 2010 was the cornerstone of the 1998-2010 policy.
9. A - the policy blamed poor implementation and a weak or non-existent monitoring and evaluation machinery.
10. B - the target was not met; the gap points to implementation and funding constraints.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. Model: An education report is written by a commission to study and recommend, and has no force of law. An education policy is adopted by the government, sets aims and targets, and is meant to be applied. **Marks (2):** 1 for each accurate difference.
2. Model: Steps include appointing a body and writing terms of reference, gathering evidence and consulting, drafting and circulating for comment, and approval by a higher authority. Example: the Sharif Commission's questionnaire and meetings, or the 1979 National Education Conference. **Marks (3):** 1 for each of two steps; 1 for a correct named body or consultation.
3. Model: The late and partial arrival suggests a failure at launch or implementation: funds or procurement were delayed, or the district and school links did not distribute the books. A weak monitoring link would explain why no one noticed. **Marks (4):** 2 for naming two plausible links with a reason; 2 for explaining the consequence for schools.
4. Model: Factors include finance, governance, capacity, continuity, data and politics (any four). Example: a school receives a science kit but no teacher is trained, which is a capacity failure. **Marks (3):** 1 for each of two factors; 1 for a fitting example.
5. Model: Main points include the twelve-year pre-university structure, compulsory primary education, a national-language medium, examination reform, better teacher training and salaries, and expanded technical and women's education (any three). **Marks (3):** 1 for each accurate point.
6. Model: The evaluation says the tutorial system was taken from Oxford and Cambridge, where a teacher meets one or two students at least once or twice a week, and that such a system is prevalent only in the two most elitist universities of England. It argues that other universities could not afford it, and that it was difficult to see how it could work in a country with limited resources. **Marks (4):** 2 for naming the borrowed model; 2 for explaining why it was not feasible in Pakistan.
7. Model: Commitments include universal primary education by 2010, 40,000 new formal primary schools with evening shifts and mosque schools, a large literacy programme through the Prime Minister's Literacy Commission, expanded non-formal basic education, and a new implementation and monitoring framework (any three). **Marks (3):** 1 for each accurate commitment.
8. Model: A policy can name targets and dates but still fail because the machinery to apply it is later abolished, underfunded or ignored. Consequence: a school receives a kit or a building but no trained teacher, no supervision and no follow-up, so the promise does not change classroom practice. **Marks (4):** 2 for the explanation; 2 for a concrete consequence.
9. Model: The baseline is the literacy rate the policy started from, about 39 percent. The target is the rate it promised to reach, 55 percent in the first five years and 70 percent by 2010. **Marks (2):** 1 for the baseline; 1 for the target.
10. Model: A new policy may not solve the old problems if the constraint is implementation rather than the absence of a document. Without a named office, funds, trained staff, data and a review, a new policy repeats the cycle of unkept promises. **Marks (4):** 2 for the reason; 2 for explaining the consequence for schools or for policy continuity.

### ERQ rubrics

1. **ERQ 1 (12 marks).** *Framing stage* (0-3): 3 explains what the commission did and why it gives the report legitimacy; 1-2 gives a partial account. *Approval and launch stage* (0-3): 3 explains the target, budget and office and what could go wrong; 1-2 gives a partial account. *Implementation stage* (0-4, **Analyze**): 4 identifies the failed link with a reason and explains the consequence for the school; 2-3 names a link with a thin reason; 1 names a stage only. *Judgement* (0-2): a reasoned choice of the most likely failed stage.
2. **ERQ 2 (12 marks).** *Comparison* (0-4, **Analyze**): 4 analyses a real difference in aim and method between the two documents, with evidence; 2-3 describes both without analysing the difference; 1 describes one only. *Application judgement* (0-4): 4 argues which document was more likely to be applied, with a reason drawn from its authority or machinery; 2-3 states a preference with a weak reason. *Evidence* (0-2): accurate use of the unit's documents. *Clarity* (0-2): a clear, organised answer.
3. **ERQ 3 (12 marks).** *Record of the policy* (0-4): 4 reports the targets and later evidence accurately, including a specific figure; 2-3 gives a partial account; 1 asserts without evidence. *Evaluation* (0-4, **Evaluate**): 4 judges the claim fairly, weighing what the policy achieved against what it missed; 2-3 reaches a conclusion with partial reasoning; 1 asserts. *Argument about a new policy* (0-3): 3 argues whether a new policy is the answer, with a reason; 1-2 states a view without support. *Conclusion* (0-1): a conclusion that follows from the argument.
4. **ERQ 4 (12 marks).** *Sorting* (0-4, **Analyze**): 4 sorts the features into those a report could recommend and those a policy must decide and fund, with a reason; 2-3 sorts most features with partial reasons; 1 sorts a few only. *Why the distinction matters* (0-5): 5 explains the consequence for a teacher, such as knowing whether a feature is advice or a funded decision; 2-4 gives a partial explanation; 1 states a difference without a consequence. *Use of the unit's vocabulary* (0-3): 3 uses report, policy, implementation and target accurately; 1-2 uses them loosely.
5. **ERQ 5 (12 marks; the unit blueprint's Evaluate item).** *Target stated* (0-2): 2 names one accurate target with its date; 1 names a target without a date. *Evidence of achievement* (0-4): 4 gives a specific later figure from a source named in this unit and compares it with the target; 2-3 gives a figure or a source but not both; 1 asserts without evidence. *Judgement* (0-4, **Evaluate**): 4 judges whether the target was met and explains the result with an implementation factor; 2-3 judges with partial reasoning; 1 asserts. *What it shows* (0-2): a reasoned statement about the policy's implementation.
