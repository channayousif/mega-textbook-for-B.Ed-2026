## A real classroom situation

Imran is a B.Ed trainee at a government primary school in Larkana. In 2010, the year the National Education Policy 1998-2010 came to an end, he asked his head teacher what the policy had achieved. The head teacher counted on his fingers: the school had received a new boundary wall, two teachers had been trained in a short course, and a folder of forms had arrived from the district. But the school still had no science teacher, the Class 5 result had not improved, and the enrolment camp held every September still found children who had never been to school. Imran had read that the policy promised universal primary education by 2010. He wondered whether the promise had been kept, and whether the answer mattered for a school like his.

This topic studies the 1998-2010 policy in the same way you studied the earlier documents: what it said, how it was meant to work, and what the evidence suggests it achieved. It ends with the question the guide asks directly: is there any need for a new policy?

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What were the main points of the 1998-2010 policy? How should we analyse its record? And is a new policy the answer?

### The main points of the 1998-2010 policy

The National Education Policy 1998-2010 was issued by the Ministry of Education. Its introduction says that education is a basic human right and that universalising primary education by the year 2010 is the cornerstone of the policy. It begins with a honest account of the problem: the prevailing illiteracy rate was 61 percent, the system had advanced only fitfully despite ambitious proposals, and the results of earlier efforts were both encouraging and disappointing.

Its main commitments can be grouped under seven heads.

1. **Universal primary education by 2010.** The first priority was that every child who enrolled should actually complete the primary cycle, and the second was that every school-age child should have access to a school. The policy proposed 40,000 new formal primary schools, evening shifts in 20,000 existing schools and 20,000 mosque schools.
2. **Literacy.** A large literacy programme, run through the Prime Minister's Literacy Commission, would open 75,000 new non-formal basic education schools by 2002. The policy said the Compulsory Primary Education Act of 1962 would be amended and enforced.
3. **Non-formal and basic education.** The existing Non-Formal Basic Education community schools would increase to 82,000 by 2002, including the existing 7,000, with the aim of enrolling 5.5 million out-of-school children. A further 35,000 such schools would be opened in unreached areas.
4. **Secondary and higher education.** The policy proposed 15,000 new middle schools and 7,000 new secondary schools in the first five years, the recruitment of 15,000 additional teachers, and measures to raise the quality of secondary education. It also proposed two types of school system, an academic high school and a vocational or technical high school, each of twelve years, and the transfer of classes XI and XII from colleges to schools.
5. **Technical and vocational education.** Enrolment in polytechnics would rise from about 12,000 to 62,000, and vocational and commercial institutions would also expand, by the year 2002.
6. **Islamic and moral education.** The policy gave a major place to Islamic education and to character building, and it proposed teaching the Holy Qur'an with translation at the secondary level.
7. **Implementation machinery.** The policy devoted a full chapter to implementation, monitoring and evaluation, and proposed a National Council for Educational Development, a policy implementation committee, provincial councils, district education authorities, school management committees and a strengthened education management information system.

**A worked example: the literacy target.** The policy said the literacy rate, then about 39 percent, would be raised to 55 percent during the first five years and to 70 percent by 2010. Later data from the World Bank puts Pakistan's adult literacy rate (ages 15 and above) at 49.9 percent in 2005 and 55.4 percent in 2010. On the policy's own measure, the 70 percent target for 2010 was not met. The example shows how to read a target: name the baseline, the target, the date, and then find a later measurement from a source you trust.

**Correcting a common misconception: "The 1998-2010 policy was only about primary education."** Primary education was its first priority, but the policy also covered secondary, technical, vocational, higher, Islamic, library, physical and private-sector education, and it created a full implementation and monitoring framework. A trainee who reads only the introduction misses the breadth of the document.

### An analysis of the 1998-2010 policy

A good analysis asks two questions. Did the policy say what it intended clearly? And did the machinery exist to carry it out? The 1998-2010 policy passes the first test better than many earlier documents. It names baselines, targets, dates and offices. It admits in its own implementation chapter that earlier reforms had failed because of poor implementation, a lack of political commitment, low funding and a weak or non-existent monitoring and evaluation machinery. It even quotes the consequences: a literacy rate of 38.9 percent, female literacy in some rural areas as low as 27 percent, and a primary participation rate of 74.8 percent with female participation at 63 percent.

The second test is harder. A policy can be well written and still fail, because the machinery it creates is later abolished, underfunded or ignored. The 1998 policy says as much about the 1992 policy: infrastructure created for faithful implementation was soon abolished, leading to a situation that paralysed the spirit and achievement of the fine document. The same pattern of a strong document and a weak machine is the central finding of later research. A 2025 narrative review of education reform in Punjab concludes that chronic policy inconsistency, weak monitoring and evaluation, urban-rural disparities, corruption and administrative inefficiency, and a lack of stakeholder engagement consistently stall improvement.

**A worked example: judging one target.** Take the 2010 literacy target of 70 percent. The policy's baseline was about 39 percent. The World Bank's adult literacy measure gives 55.4 percent for 2010. The target was not met. Why? The evidence points to several causes at once: the literacy programme had to reach adults as well as children, the funds were not enough for the scale, the machinery for monitoring was weak, and earlier policies had already fallen short, so the 1998 policy inherited a large gap. A good answer does not stop at "the target was missed". It names the factors that explain the miss.

**Correcting a common misconception: "A new policy will solve problems the old one could not."** A new policy can restate a commitment and set a new date. It cannot by itself build schools, train teachers, fund salaries or make a government keep its promise across an election. The evidence in this topic suggests that the binding constraint is usually implementation, not the absence of a policy. That is why the last section of this topic asks the question carefully.

### Is there any need for a new policy?

The guide asks directly whether there is any need for a new policy. The honest answer is that both sides have a case.

The case for a new policy is strong. Circumstances change. The 1998-2010 policy was written before the devolution of education to the provinces under the Eighteenth Amendment, before the expansion of low-cost private schools, before the spread of mobile phones and computer education, and before Pakistan adopted the Sustainable Development Goals. A policy written for 1998 could not anticipate these changes. A new policy can also correct a weak earlier document, set fresh targets and renew political commitment. In fact, a new policy was made: the National Education Policy 2009 replaced the 1998-2010 policy.

The case against a new policy is equally strong. If the problem is implementation, a new document repeats the cycle. The 1998 policy itself observed that earlier policies had failed because the machinery was weak or absent, not because the words were wrong. A new policy that is not funded, staffed and monitored will produce another set of unkept promises. The 2025 review of Punjab's reforms argues for a shift away from top-down reform towards adaptive and participatory strategies, with better monitoring, transparency and community involvement. That is a diagnosis of implementation, not a demand for another document.

A balanced judgement is this. Pakistan may need a new policy when circumstances have changed enough to make the old one misleading, but a new policy is worth having only if it is paired with the machinery that earlier policies lacked: a named office, a budget, trained staff, reliable data and a review that reports honestly on progress. A teacher should welcome a new policy, and then ask the oldest question in this unit: what will it take to reach a classroom?

**A worked example: 2010 and after.** The 1998-2010 policy ended in 2010 with its universal primary education target unmet: gross primary enrolment was 73.6 percent. The National Education Policy 2009 had already set a new course. The sequence shows both things at once: a policy can be replaced because circumstances changed, and because the earlier one missed its targets. A trainee who sees only the first reason will expect too much from the next document.

**Correcting a common misconception: "If a policy fails, the answer is to try harder with the same approach."** Trying harder matters, but the evidence points to a different lesson: design the implementation before you announce the target. A policy that names its office, its budget, its data and its review is more likely to survive than a policy that only names a date.

## Activity: The case for and against a new policy

Work in two groups. About 25 minutes. This is a structured controversy.

1. Group A takes the case for a new policy; Group B takes the case against. Each group has 8 minutes to prepare three arguments, using evidence from this unit.
2. Each group presents its three arguments in two minutes. The other group listens for the strongest point against its own position.
3. The groups swap sides. Each now prepares one argument for the side it argued against, in 5 minutes.
4. Together, write a one-paragraph judgement that a B.Ed trainee could give a head teacher: does Pakistan need a new education policy? The judgement must use at least one piece of evidence and must say what would make a new policy different from the old ones.

Compare your judgement with another pair. Note where the two judgements differ, and why.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Without looking back, name three commitments of the National Education Policy 1998-2010.
2. *(Understand)* In two sentences, explain why the 1998-2010 policy is considered better written than many earlier policies.
3. *(Apply)* The policy set a literacy target of 70 percent by 2010. Later evidence puts adult literacy at 55.4 percent in 2010. Use two of the six implementation factors to explain the gap.
4. *(Analyze)* A trainee says, "The 1998 policy failed because it had too many targets." Analyse this claim using the policy's own explanation of why earlier policies failed.
5. *(Evaluate)* Judge whether Pakistan needed a new education policy after 2010. Give one reason for and one reason against, and state your judgement.

## Summary

The National Education Policy 1998-2010 set universal primary education by 2010 as its cornerstone, promised 40,000 new primary schools, evening shifts and mosque schools, expanded non-formal basic education, and created a full implementation and monitoring framework. It was better written than many earlier policies, but later evidence shows its main literacy and enrolment targets were not met. The reasons are the same factors seen in Topic 5.2: finance, governance, capacity, continuity, data and politics. The case for a new policy is that circumstances change; the case against is that implementation, not the absence of a document, is usually the constraint. A new policy is worth having only if it carries the machinery to apply it.
