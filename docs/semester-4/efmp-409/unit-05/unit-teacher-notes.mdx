# Unit 5: Education Reports and Policies - Teacher Notes

## Purpose and sequencing

This unit closes the course. It asks why Pakistan's education promises are repeated across generations, and it gives student teachers a way to read a policy target and ask what it would take to meet it. Most student teachers arrive believing that a report and a policy are the same thing, that policies fail only for want of money, and that a new policy will solve the old one's problems. The unit lets them trace a promise through the implementation chain until those beliefs loosen.

The course guide gives three weeks of three hours each for this unit, one topic per week.

- **Week 14 (Topic 5.1).** The difference between a report and a policy, how each is framed, and how each is launched and applied, with the comparison table, the policy-cycle flowchart and the "From report to classroom" activity.
- **Week 15 (Topic 5.2).** The six factors that hinder application, the main points of the Sharif Commission, and the 1979 policy, with the timeline and the "Two documents, one promise" activity.
- **Week 16 (Topic 5.3).** The main points of the 1998-2010 policy, the analysis against later evidence, and the case for and against a new policy, with the targets-against-evidence table and the structured controversy.

## Teaching strategies from the course guide

- **Lecture.** Keep each segment to about 15 minutes, and end each with one question for pairs to discuss.
- **Discussion.** Use the classroom situations at the start of each topic as openers. Ask "What would you say to Rashid, Seema or Imran?" before teaching the concept.
- **Question-answer.** Use the "Check your understanding" items orally at the start of the next session, with books closed.
- **Brainstorming.** Use it to surface the promises student teachers remember from their own schooling, such as free textbooks, a new building or a scholarship, before naming the policy cycle.

## Likely misconceptions and how to handle them

- **"A report and a policy are the same document."** Put the Sharif Commission report and the 1979 policy side by side and ask which one the government had to act on. The distinction emerges from the documents.
- **"Policies fail only because of too little money."** Ask the class to explain a school that received a science kit but no training. They will see capacity, data and governance at work, not only finance.
- **"A new policy will solve problems the old one could not."** Ask the class to design the machinery a new policy would need: an office, a budget, trained staff, data and a review. The question shifts from the document to the implementation.
- **"The 1979 policy failed because it was religious."** Ask the class to separate a policy's aims from its machinery, and to compare the 1998 policy's own judgement about implementation across very different ideologies.

**One good discussion prompt:** "If a government could only do one of these, which would you choose: write a new policy, or fund the implementation of the current one?" It forces the class to compare the two constraints the unit teaches.

## Practical work

The course guide lists four kinds of practical work. Suggested tasks:

- **Group work.** The three topic activities: "From report to classroom", "Two documents, one promise" and the structured controversy.
- **Group assignment.** Each group collects one current education promise from a newspaper or a government notice, traces it through the policy cycle and presents the stage where it is most likely to fail.
- **Individual assignment.** The Topic 5.1, 5.2 or 5.3 summative task.
- **Presentation.** Each student teacher presents, in three minutes, one policy target and the evidence for whether it was met, using one bound source.

Handle the policy documents as primary sources: show the title pages, the forewords and the dates, and let student teachers see that the documents name their own consultative bodies. Where a claim about a policy's success is contested, ask what evidence they would need rather than settling the matter in class.
