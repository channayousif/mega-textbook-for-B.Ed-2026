## A real classroom situation

Seema is a B.Ed trainee at a government girls' primary school in Sukkur. The school has no boundary wall, one of its two classrooms leaks in the monsoon, and the single science kit supplied under a government programme three years ago is still sealed in its box because no teacher has been trained to use it. Seema asks the head teacher why the school's problems have not been solved. The head teacher laughs without humour. "Every few years a commission sits, a policy is announced, and a box arrives. Nobody asks us what we need, and nobody comes back to see whether it worked." Seema has read that the Sharif Commission recommended free and compulsory primary education for every child, and that the 1979 policy promised to use mosque schools to reach children in every neighbourhood. Both promises sound like hers. Both are older than she is.

This topic asks why promises like these falter. It then studies two documents closely: the Sharif Commission report of 1959, which shaped Pakistan's school structure for a generation, and the National Education Policy 1979, which promised an Islamic and national reorientation of education.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What stops a report or a policy from being applied? What did the Sharif Commission recommend in 1959? And how should we judge the 1979 policy?

### Factors that hinder the application of reports and policies

The 1998-2010 policy gives a blunt answer of its own. Its implementation chapter says that the education system had been the target of experimentation through a number of major reforms and policy packages during the previous fifty years, and that none had produced encouraging results. It names poor implementation as the major reason: whatever was planned was neither carried out zealously nor monitored properly. It then lists the causes: a lack of proper political commitment, lower funding, and a weak or non-existent machinery for implementation, monitoring and evaluation. It adds that most of the policies and plans launched since 1947, except the 1959 policy and the 1972-1977 period, ended as massive failures.

Other scholars agree and add detail. A 2025 review of education reform in Punjab lists policy inconsistency, weak monitoring and evaluation, urban-rural disparity, corruption and administrative inefficiency, and a lack of stakeholder engagement as the main reasons reforms fail to reach classrooms. A study of three early commissions points out that each was appointed at a moment of political transition, when the old government had gone and the new one wanted a plan quickly. A commission appointed in a hurry is more likely to produce general statements than a detailed programme.

The factors fall into six groups. **Finance**: a target without money is a wish. **Governance**: if the offices that must act are not named, nothing moves. **Capacity**: teachers, head teachers and district officers need training and time. **Continuity**: a change of government can end a policy before it matures. **Data**: without reliable enrolment, attendance and learning data, nobody can tell whether a policy is working. **Politics**: education is tied to identity, language and religion, so a policy can be dropped for reasons that have little to do with schools.

**A worked example: the literacy target.** The 1998-2010 policy observed that, despite fifty years of effort, the literacy rate in Pakistan was only 38.9 percent, and in rural areas among females in some provinces it was as low as 27 percent. The primary participation rate was 74.8 percent, with female participation at 63 percent. These numbers are not only about money. They also reflect capacity (not enough schools and teachers where the children are), data (many out-of-school children are not counted), continuity (policies changed with governments) and politics (a girl's schooling can be the subject of dispute). A single cause cannot explain the gap.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Policies fail only because of too little money."** Money matters, and the 1998 policy says so. But the same document blames weak implementation machinery, poor training, low political commitment and the absence of incentives. A school like Seema's can have funds for a science kit and still fail, because no one trained the teacher, no one monitored the kit and no one returned to check. Implementation is a system, not a budget line.

### The main points of the Sharif Commission (1959)

The Commission on National Education, usually called the **Sharif Commission** after its chairman S. M. Sharif, was appointed by a government resolution on 30 December 1958 and inaugurated by President Ayub Khan on 5 January 1959. It reported in 1959. Its terms of reference asked it to recommend a reorganisation of education so that it reflected the spiritual, moral and cultural values of Pakistan, met the needs of agriculture, science and technology, built character, and made the best use of the country's limited resources.

Its main points can be grouped under eight heads.

1. **Structure of schooling.** Pre-university education should be extended from ten to twelve years, so that a student enters university after twelve years of schooling. The intermediate classes should move from the universities to the boards of secondary education.
2. **Compulsory education.** Primary education should become universal and compulsory: first a five-year compulsory course within ten years, and then an eight-year compulsory course within fifteen years. The age of admission should be five.
3. **Curriculum and subjects.** A core of basic subjects, including science, mathematics and the national language, should be taught in secondary school, with optional subjects that give vocational training and practical skills.
4. **Medium of instruction.** The national language should progressively replace English as the medium of instruction. Urdu should be the medium from Class VI onwards, and English should remain a compulsory subject.
5. **Examinations.** Examinations should test ability and achievement rather than memory. A final comprehensive university examination should count for 75 percent of the degree marks, with continuous class work counting for 25 percent.
6. **Teachers.** Teacher training, salaries and conditions of service should be improved so that good people enter and stay in the profession.
7. **Technical and vocational education.** Technical and vocational education should expand, and the dignity of labour should be taught.
8. **Equity and character.** Women's education, adult education and the education of children with special needs should expand, and education should build character, citizenship and a sense of the dignity of labour.

A later evaluation of the Sharif report notes two achievements and one weakness. The achievements were the twelve-year pre-university structure and the three-year bachelor's degree, both of which changed the system. The weakness was that some recommendations, such as tutorials at the university level, were borrowed from Oxford and Cambridge and were unrealistic in a country with limited resources.

**A worked example: the twelve-year structure.** Before the Sharif Commission, a student could enter university after about ten years of schooling. The commission argued that twelve years was the international norm. That recommendation was accepted and shaped the system later policies inherited. When you hear a debate about the "higher secondary" or "intermediate" stage in Pakistan, you are hearing the long shadow of the 1959 report.

**Correcting a common misconception: "The Sharif Commission was only about higher education."** Its higher-education recommendations are the most quoted, but the report also covered primary education, women's education, adult education, religious education, physical education, the arts and the education of children with special needs. A commission that recommends compulsory primary education and a five-year target is not only interested in universities.

### A critical review of the 1978-80 policy (National Education Policy 1979)

The National Education Policy and Implementation Programme 1979 was issued by the Ministry of Education. Its foreword, by the Education Secretary Dr. M. A. Kazi, is dated 14 February 1979. The policy said that education in Pakistan should be harmonised with the concepts of Islam and the ideology of Pakistan. It set out nine aims, including fostering a living consciousness of Islamic and Pakistani identity, producing citizens conversant with the Pakistan movement and its ideological foundations, developing character in accordance with the Qur'an and the Sunnah, ensuring equal educational opportunity and facilities for minorities, providing quality education that develops each person's potential, spreading functional literacy, and promoting scientific, vocational and technological education.

Its implementation strategy included revising curricula around Islamic thought, using the national language as the medium of instruction, expanding facilities, linking education to productive work, launching adult literacy programmes and encouraging community participation. It also created new institution types, such as mosque schools, mohalla schools and village workshop schools, to reach children who were not in school.

A critical review should be fair to what the policy tried to do and honest about what it achieved. The policy was framed through a conference and a cabinet process, so it had a public mandate. It named an implementation programme, which earlier documents had often lacked. It spoke plainly about the two parallel systems of education, the religious and the modern, and tried to bring them into an integrated national system. But the review must also notice the pattern the 1998 policy later described: most policies ended as failures because the machinery to apply them was weak or absent. A policy that announces mosque schools but does not fund teachers, buildings and supervision repeats the older cycle. A later scholar's verdict on this whole era of reports is that none of them worked out in detail an educational plan that could provide education according to the needs of the country, and that all of them made only general statements about the need for vocational training.

**A worked example: the 1979 aim and the 1998 observation.** The 1979 policy promised to spread functional literacy. The 1998 policy, written nineteen years later, observed that the literacy rate was still only 38.9 percent. That gap between a stated aim and a measured result is the clearest evidence a critical review can use. It does not prove the 1979 policy achieved nothing, but it shows the promise was not kept on the scale intended.

**Correcting a common misconception: "The 1979 policy failed because it was religious."** A policy's religious language is a matter of aim and identity, and it can be debated on its own terms. The failure this unit studies is different: it is the failure to build the machinery that turns an aim into a classroom change. The 1998 policy blames weak implementation across the whole history of Pakistani education, including policies with very different ideologies. Judging a policy only by its language misses the question a teacher should ask: what would it take to make this work in a school like Seema's?

## Activity: Two documents, one promise

Work in pairs. About 25 minutes.

1. Each pair chooses one promise that appears in both documents, such as universal primary education, the medium of instruction, or technical and vocational education.
2. One student reads the Sharif Commission summary and the other reads the 1979 policy. In 8 minutes, each writes down, in their own words, what their document promised on the chosen theme.
3. Compare the two promises. Write a short paragraph answering: are they the same promise, or do they differ in aim, scope or method? Give one piece of evidence from each document.
4. Together, name one reason why the promise may have faltered, using the six factors from this topic. Say which factor you think is strongest and why.

Share your paragraph with another pair. Compare which factor each pair chose.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Without looking back, name four factors that hinder the application of education reports and policies.
2. *(Understand)* In two sentences, explain why a commission appointed at a moment of political transition may produce general statements rather than a detailed plan.
3. *(Understand)* List three main points of the Sharif Commission and say which one changed the structure of schooling in Pakistan.
4. *(Apply)* A district education office receives a policy target for 500 new primary schools but no new staff and no extra funds. Use two of the six factors to explain why the target may be missed.
5. *(Analyze)* A friend says, "The 1979 policy failed because it was religious." Analyse this claim using evidence from the policy's own aims and from the 1998 policy's later judgement about implementation.

## Summary

Reports and policies falter for several connected reasons: not enough money, no named office to act, untrained staff, a change of government, no reliable data, and political disputes over language, identity and religion. The Sharif Commission of 1959 recommended a twelve-year school structure, compulsory primary education, a national-language medium, examination reform, better teachers and expanded technical and women's education. The 1979 policy set out Islamic and national aims and an implementation programme, including mosque and mohalla schools. A critical review judges both documents by the same test: did they build the machinery that turns a promise into a classroom change?

## Summative task

Write 300-350 words. Choose one recommendation of the Sharif Commission, such as the twelve-year school structure, compulsory primary education or the national-language medium.

1. State the recommendation and the commission that made it.
2. Explain how the recommendation was framed, naming at least two steps from the commission's work.
3. Analyse one reason why the recommendation was or was not applied in Pakistan, using one of the six implementation factors.
4. Judge whether the recommendation is still relevant to a school in your province today, with a reason.
