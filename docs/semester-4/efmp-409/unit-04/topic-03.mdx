## A real classroom situation

Rashid teaches Class 4 in a rural school near Umerkot. His room has forty-two pupils and one textbook. The brightest boy, Karim, finishes every exercise in ten minutes and then disturbs the class. The slowest girl, Shabnam, never finishes, and Rashid has no time to sit with her. He hears about a "study circle" from a colleague and thinks of As-Suffa, the platform beside the Prophet's Mosque where a small group of learners lived with their teacher.

Rashid tries something small. He splits the class into circles of five, gives each circle one task, and asks Karim to help Shabnam for ten minutes. The noise drops. Karim stops disturbing the class. Shabnam finishes her exercise. Rashid wonders how much of what he did came from a seventh-century learning circle, and how much he invented. This topic answers that.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What was As-Suffa, and who learned there? How was teaching done? And what was taught?

### As-Suffa: the first institute in Islam

**What As-Suffa was.** As-Suffa was a raised, roofed platform at the back of the Prophet's Mosque in Medina. Palm leaves were placed over it for shade, and the name suggests a shaded place. After the Hijrah of 622 CE, it became the home of a group of companions who had left Makkah and had no family or house in Medina. They were called the Ashab al-Suffa, the people of the platform.

**Why it is called the first institute.** The platform was not only a shelter. It was a place where a group of learners lived with their teacher, studied the Qur'an and the Sunnah, and devoted themselves to learning. Sani and Shaid (2019) describe As-Suffa as a residential educational institution attached to the Prophet's Mosque, offering structured training alongside boarding. That is why it is often called the first institute of learning in Islam.

**Who the learners were.** The Ashab al-Suffa were mostly poor and unmarried. They had given up their homes in Makkah for their faith. They spent their days learning, and the Prophet (peace be upon him) sat with them, talked with them and shared food and drink with them. Reports say the platform housed up to three hundred people at a time and about four hundred over the years. Many of them later became teachers and leaders in the cities of the new community.

**What the model teaches a teacher.** Three things stand out. First, learning was residential: the learners lived with the teacher, so teaching happened all day, not only in a period. Second, the group was small and close: the teacher knew each learner. Third, learning had a purpose beyond itself: the learners were trained to go out and teach others. A school that keeps pupils for six hours and sends them home may teach them, but it does not have the same closeness.

**Correcting a common misconception: "As-Suffa was a place of shelter, not of learning."** The shelter is the first thing a visitor would notice, so the mistake is understandable. But the historical sources describe the people of the platform as learners of the Qur'an and the Sunnah, and modern studies call it an educational institution (Sani & Shaid, 2019). The shelter was the condition that made the learning possible. A teacher who only feeds and houses a child has not taught them; a teacher who uses the time together to teach has.

**A Pakistan-grounded example.** In a village in Sindh, a mosque school keeps a few boys from distant settlements in a room beside the mosque. The imam teaches them the Qur'an in the morning, and they help with the garden and the water in the afternoon. The boys go home for the holidays and teach the younger children in their own villages. That is the As-Suffa pattern: learning, living and outreach together.

### The method of teaching in As-Suffa

**The circle, not the row.** Teaching at As-Suffa happened in a sitting, a halaqah. The teacher sat with the learners, and the learners sat around him. This is different from a classroom of rows facing a board. In a circle, everyone can see everyone, and the teacher can see who is following and who is lost.

**How a session worked.** A learner listened, memorised, repeated, asked questions and discussed. The teacher explained, questioned back, and set an example by his own conduct. Because the learners lived with the teacher, they also learned from his ordinary actions: how he ate, how he greeted people, how he treated a visitor. This is learning by company, not only by instruction.

**Key features of the method.**

| Feature | What it means | Present-day parallel |
|---|---|---|
| Small group | A handful of learners with one teacher | A reading group or a study circle |
| Oral and repeated | Listening, memorising, repeating aloud | Choral reading, recitation, drill |
| Question and answer | The teacher asks, the learner answers, and the reverse | Think-pair-share, a question wall |
| Learning by example | The teacher's conduct is part of the lesson | A teacher who models the behaviour they ask for |
| Learning by practice | The learner acts on what is learned | Role play, projects, peer teaching |
| Learning for outreach | The learner is trained to teach others | Pupils teaching pupils, a monitor system |

**A worked example: comparing two methods.** Imagine a Class 4 lesson on honesty. In Method A, the teacher reads a story from the book, writes the moral on the board and asks the class to copy it. In Method B, the teacher sits with five pupils, tells the story, asks "What would you have done?", lets each pupil answer, and then asks them to act out the ending. Method B has the features of the halaqah: small group, questions, example and practice. Method A has only the teacher's voice. Now you try the same comparison in the activity below.

**Correcting a common misconception: "a circle is just a seating plan."** Moving the desks into a circle does not make a halaqah. The circle matters because it changes who speaks and who listens. In a real halaqah the teacher questions, the learners answer, and the learners question back. A circle of desks with a lecture in the middle is still a lecture.

### The curriculum of As-Suffa

**What was taught.** The core of the curriculum was the Qur'an and the Sunnah: recitation, memorisation, understanding and practice. Alongside this, the learners gained the skills of reading and writing, and the Arabic that carried the sources. They also learned the duties of daily and public life, because they were being prepared to teach and to lead in the community.

**Writing mattered.** The early community treated literacy as a skill worth gaining. After the Battle of Badr, some prisoners of war taught reading and writing to Muslims in return for their freedom, as noted in Topic 4.1. A learning circle that cannot write down what it learns depends on memory alone; writing makes the learning shareable.

**The curriculum in plain terms.**

**What transfers to a present-day classroom.** Not everything transfers. A seventh-century circle of adults who lived with their teacher is not a Class 4 of forty-two pupils with one textbook. But some principles do transfer. Small study circles let a teacher see who is lost. Peer teaching lets a fast learner like Karim help a slow learner like Shabnam, and both learn more. Learning by practice turns a rule into a habit. And a class charter, from Topic 4.2, gives the group a shared source to hold to.

**A Pakistan-grounded example.** Rashid's study circles in Umerkot are a small transfer. He keeps the circles to five, gives each a task, asks the faster pupil to help the slower one, and ends each session with a question the pupils answer aloud. He does not claim to be running As-Suffa. He claims one principle: a small group with a clear task and a teacher who moves between groups.

**Analysis, not imitation.** The As-Suffa model is a source of principles, not a blueprint. Ask what need it met, what value it protected, and what in a present-day school protects the same value. That is the critical work the course guide asks for.

## Activity: Design a study circle

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

1. Choose one topic from Class 4 or Class 5, for example a science idea, a story from the textbooks, or a rule of hygiene.
2. Design a 20-minute study circle for five pupils. Write: the question the teacher asks first, the task the group does, how the faster pupils help the slower ones, and how the session ends.
3. Say which feature of As-Suffa your design keeps, and which feature you changed because of your class size.
4. Present the design in two minutes. The class then picks one design to try at a practicum school.

## Summary

As-Suffa was a shaded platform beside the Prophet's Mosque in Medina where the Ashab al-Suffa, companions without family in the city, lived and learned. It is called the first institute of learning in Islam because it joined boarding, structured study of the Qur'an and the Sunnah, and preparation to teach others. Its method was a small circle of listening, memorising, questioning, example and practice. Its curriculum joined the sources, literacy and the duties of daily life. A present-day teacher cannot copy it, but can transfer its principles: small groups, peer teaching, practice and a shared commitment.
