## A real classroom situation

Nazia teaches Class 5 at a government girls' school in Hyderabad. Her pupils come from three neighbourhoods, and two languages are spoken in the playground. One morning a dispute breaks out over a lost pencil box. A pupil says, "She is from the other side of the town, so she must have taken it." The class splits along the same lines.

Nazia stops the lesson. She wants to teach more than the syllabus that day. She thinks of two texts she read in her B.Ed course: the Charter of Medina, which brought different groups of one city into one community with rights and duties, and the Last Sermon, which told a huge gathering that no Arab has merit over a non-Arab except by piety. She asks herself, "What would a classroom look like if it took those two texts seriously?"

This topic gives you the material to answer her. It is not about teaching religion. It is about what a community owes its members, and how a teacher can build that in a school.

## Explanation

This topic answers three questions. What is the Charter of Medina, and why is it called the foundation stone of education? What is the Last Sermon, and why does it matter to a teacher? And what educational principles can we draw from it?

### Meesaq-e-Madina: the foundation stone of education

**What the Charter is.** The Charter of Medina, called Meesaq-e-Madina in Urdu, is a written agreement drawn up after the Hijrah, the migration of the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) from Makkah to Medina in 622 CE. It brought together the Muhajirun, the Muslims who had migrated from Makkah, the Ansar, the helpers of Medina, and the Jewish tribes and other groups of the city. The text survives in the early biographies and histories, above all in the recension of Ibn Hisham.

**What it did.** The Charter organised Medina as one political community while protecting the religious freedom of each group. Its clauses cover the community, the status of the Jewish tribes, shared defence, the cost of war, justice, sanctuary and the rule of law. A few of its teachings are:

- The groups of Medina form one community, distinct from other people.
- The Jewish tribes have their religion, and the Muslims have theirs.
- The groups help one another against any force that attacks Medina.
- Disputes are referred to God and to Muhammad.
- Medina is a sacred place for the people of the Charter.
- No protection is given to an oppressor or a breaker of the agreement.

**Why it is called the foundation stone of education.** The Charter is not a lesson plan. It is a constitution for a plural community. That is exactly why it belongs at the foundation of education. A school is a small society with differences of language, class, faith and ability. The Charter shows a community choosing to live together under shared rules, and it names the values that make shared life possible: freedom of conscience, equality before the law, shared responsibility, justice and the keeping of agreements. A classroom that teaches only facts, and never teaches those values, is missing the foundation.

**Two modern readings.** Tobroni (2020) argues that the Charter was a legal product of the Prophet's era rather than a mandatory model of an Islamic state, and describes it as a symbol of peace, tolerance and moderation. Anwer and Alam (2024) compare it with Magna Carta and argue that it protected Muslims, Jews and other minorities. Both readings agree that the Charter's value lies in its treatment of a diverse community, not in a single ruling.

**Correcting a common misconception: "The Charter of Medina was only a military treaty."** The Charter does contain a clause on shared defence, and it was made in a time of danger. But most of its clauses are about community, religion, justice and order. Calling it only a military treaty is like calling a school timetable only a list of break times. The treaty is one part of a larger agreement about how people will live together.

**A worked example: reading a clause for its educational value.** Take the clause that the Jewish tribes have their religion and the Muslims have theirs. A learner might say, "It is about war and peace." Look closer: the clause gives two groups the freedom to hold their own beliefs. The educational value is freedom of conscience. In a classroom, that means no pupil is insulted or excluded for their background. Now you try the same move in the activity below.

**A Pakistan-grounded example.** Nazia's Class 5 dispute over the pencil box is a small version of Medina. She asks the class to write a charter for the classroom: one community, everyone keeps their own language, everyone helps when the class is challenged, disputes go to the class not to the playground, and no one is blamed for where they come from. The pupils write it in two languages and sign it. The charter becomes the rule they use for the rest of the year.

### The Last Sermon: its relevance to education

**What the Last Sermon is.** The Last Sermon, Khutbat-ul-Wida in Urdu, is the address the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) delivered at Arafat on 9 Dhu al-Hijjah, 10 AH (6 March 632 CE), during the Farewell Pilgrimage. It is recorded in parts in the major hadith collections, including Sahih al-Bukhari, Sahih Muslim, Sunan Abu Dawud, Sunan al-Tirmidhi and Sunan Ibn Majah, and in longer versions in the Musnad of Imam Ahmad and in the writings of al-Jahiz. Farooq (2018) reviews the versions and argues that the text was assembled from several reports, so it is better read as a Farewell Message than as one speech written down word for word.

**Why it matters to a teacher.** The sermon is a public act of teaching. A teacher stands before a large gathering, at a moment of transition, and sums up what the community must hold onto. It addresses men and women together. It covers faith, conduct, law, rights and social life in one address. That is a model of teaching for a whole community, not only for a single class.

**What it teaches, in plain words.**

- **Equality.** The noblest of people in God's sight are the most God-conscious. An Arab has no merit over a non-Arab, nor a non-Arab over an Arab, except by piety.
- **Sanctity of life and property.** Blood and property are sacred, as sacred as the sacred day, the sacred month and the sacred town.
- **Trusts.** Everything held in trust must be returned to its owner.
- **Rights of women.** Wives are to be treated with kindness and provided for; they are not to be treated harshly.
- **Justice over custom.** Blood-revenge feuds and usury are abolished; the first claims cancelled are the Prophet's own.
- **Holding to the sources.** The Prophet leaves the Qur'an and his Sunnah, and tells the people to hold fast to them.

**Correcting a common misconception: "The Last Sermon is only a religious address, so it has nothing to do with a school."** The sermon is certainly religious, but its subject is how people live together: their safety, their property, their trusts, their women, their disputes. A school is a place where those things happen every day. A teacher who keeps a classroom safe, fair and honest is applying the sermon's principles, whether or not the word "sermon" is used.

**A worked example: naming the principle.** Take the sentence that everything held in trust must be returned to its owner. A learner might say, "It is about borrowing." Look closer: a trust is anything given into someone's care, from a borrowed book to a duty. The principle is accountability. In a classroom, that means a pupil who looks after the key, the register or the library books is answerable for them. Now you try the same move in the activity below.

### Analysing the educational principles of the Last Sermon

The course guide asks for "a critical analysis" of the Last Sermon. Here that means analysing the Sermon's educational principles and how they can be applied, not judging the text itself (content-spec drafting note 8). The table below sets out six principles and the educational value each carries.

<Figure id="fig-U4-4" src="/img/figures/efmp-409/unit-04/fig-U4-4.svg" alt="Table of six principles from the Charter of Medina and the Last Sermon. Each row gives the principle, where it appears, and its educational value: equality before the law; freedom of conscience; sanctity of life and property; returning trusts and accountability; rights and duties towards others; and holding to a shared source of guidance." />

**Reading the table.** Each principle can become a classroom rule or a classroom habit. Equality becomes a rule that no pupil is favoured for their family or punished for their neighbourhood. Freedom of conscience becomes a rule that no pupil mocks another's language or dress. Sanctity of property becomes a rule that borrowed things are returned. Accountability becomes a rule that the monitor, the librarian and the class representative answer for what is in their care. Rights and duties become a rule that every pupil has a voice and every voice has a limit. Holding to a shared source becomes a rule that the class agrees on its charter and returns to it.

**A Pakistan-grounded example.** A head teacher in Nawabshah notices that pupils are punished differently depending on whether their parents know the staff. She reads the equality principle and changes the rule: the same offence brings the same consequence, recorded in a sheet that anyone can read. Parents complain at first. She shows them the sheet and the principle. By the second term, the number of disputes has fallen.

**Analysis, not imitation.** The principles are universal in their direction, but the application must fit the context. A classroom charter in Sindh is not the Charter of Medina. The point of the analysis is to ask what value the principle protects, and then to find a practical way to protect the same value in a school today.

## Activity: One principle, one rule

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

Each group picks one principle from the table: equality, freedom of conscience, sanctity of life and property, returning trusts, rights and duties, or holding to a shared source.

1. State the principle in one sentence, in your own words.
2. Describe one real situation in a Pakistani school where the principle is broken. For example, a pupil is blamed for a broken window because of where they live.
3. Design one classroom rule or ritual that protects the principle. Be concrete: who does what, when, and what happens if the rule is broken.
4. Explain in two sentences why your rule protects the same value as the principle, without copying the historical document.

Each group presents its rule in two minutes. The class then chooses one rule to recommend to the school council.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the two main groups the Charter of Medina brought together, and give the year of the Hijrah.
2. *(Remember)* State where and when the Last Sermon was delivered, and name two collections that record it.
3. *(Understand)* In three sentences, explain why the Charter of Medina is called the foundation stone of education.
4. *(Apply)* A pupil in your class is insulted for the language they speak at home. Which principle from this topic does this break, and what would you do in the next five minutes?
5. *(Analyze)* Analyse how the principle of accountability could change the way a school keeps its library or its sports equipment. Use two concrete steps.

## Summary

The Charter of Medina organised a plural community in 622 CE around shared rules, religious freedom, justice and mutual defence, and it is called the foundation stone of education because those values are the foundation of a school as well as a state. The Last Sermon, delivered at Arafat in 632 CE, sums up the moral teaching of Islam in a public address: equality, the sanctity of life and property, the return of trusts, the rights of women, justice over custom, and holding to the Qur'an and the Sunnah. Read critically, both texts give a teacher principles that can be turned into classroom rules without copying the historical documents.
