## A real classroom situation

A teacher training college in Karachi hosts a visiting delegation from Bangladesh. The Pakistani trainees learn that Bangladesh, despite having lower per-capita income than Pakistan, has achieved near-universal primary enrolment and a significantly higher girls' attendance rate. One trainee asks, "How did they do that? We have better buildings and more money. Why are their results stronger?" The visiting speaker replies, "Because they focused on community schools, female teacher recruitment and school meals at a time when Pakistan was still debating language policy." The trainee sits back, puzzled. The comparison raises a question this topic answers: what can Pakistan learn from how other developing countries educate their children?

## Explanation

### Education policies in developing countries

Pakistan is one of many developing countries trying to provide quality education with limited resources. Looking at how others approach similar problems is not about copying them. It is about understanding which policies produce results, under what conditions, and why. Several developing countries offer useful comparisons.

**Bangladesh** invested heavily in community-based schools and female teacher recruitment from the 1990s onward. Non-government organisations such as BRAC ran large-scale parallel school programmes that reached children the government system missed. The result: girls' primary enrolment rose sharply, and the gender gap narrowed faster than in Pakistan (khan2018). The policy lesson is that access improves when schools are physically close to communities and staffed by women from those communities.

**Sri Lanka** achieved high literacy rates early through a policy of free education from primary to university level, combined with a strong network of central schools. The country spends a smaller share of GDP on education than some neighbours but achieves better outcomes because of efficient teacher deployment and a long-standing cultural value placed on schooling (khan2018). The lesson is that spending alone does not determine results; how resources are deployed matters.

**Nepal** faced geographic challenges similar to Pakistan's northern areas. Its policy response was to establish cluster schools and provide scholarships for girls in remote districts. Enrolment in targeted areas improved, though quality remains a challenge (khan2018). The lesson is that targeted geographic policies can overcome access barriers, but they must be paired with quality-focused planning.

### Lessons for Pakistan from international comparisons

Three lessons stand out from these comparisons:

1. **Access policies work when they are specific.** Bangladesh did not simply announce "universal enrolment." It built community schools, recruited local women as teachers and provided meals. The policy was detailed enough to implement.
2. **Gender-focused policies accelerate progress.** Both Bangladesh and Nepal showed that policies targeting girls' enrolment (female teachers, scholarships, community acceptance campaigns) produce faster results than general access policies.
3. **Spending efficiency matters as much as spending levels.** Sri Lanka shows that a country can achieve strong outcomes with modest spending if teachers are deployed efficiently and the system minimises waste.

Pakistan's National Education Policy 2017 acknowledges several of these lessons. It emphasises public-private partnerships, targeted interventions for out-of-school children, and improved teacher deployment (nep2017). The question is whether the planning and implementation match the policy intent, a challenge this course has examined in earlier units.

**Common misconception.** Some trainees assume that "what works in one country works everywhere." It does not. Bangladesh's community school model worked because of specific social conditions: dense populations, a strong NGO sector and cultural acceptance of female teachers. Policies must be adapted to local conditions, not transplanted wholesale. A policy that succeeds in one context may fail in another if the supporting conditions are different.

### Global educational goals (SDGs) and local relevance

In 2015, United Nations member states adopted the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), a set of 17 global targets. <Glossary term="Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)" /> Goal 4 (SDG4) commits countries to "ensure inclusive and equitable quality education and promote lifelong learning opportunities for all" by 2030. SDG4 has seven targets covering early childhood, primary and secondary education, technical and vocational skills, literacy, inclusion, and global citizenship (UNESCO, 2017).

For Pakistan, SDG4 is not an external imposition. It aligns with existing policy commitments in the National Education Policy 2017: free and compulsory education, improved quality, gender equity, and expanded technical education (nep2017). The SDG framework gives Pakistan a global benchmark against which to measure progress and a structure for reporting to international partners.

However, the targets are ambitious. SDG4's call for universal secondary education and substantial literacy gains by 2030 requires planning at a scale Pakistan has not yet achieved. This is where the course's earlier themes of planning (Unit 3), monitoring (Unit 4) and quality assurance (Unit 5) become essential. Global goals are only as credible as the national planning that backs them.

## Activity: Comparing two countries

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Choose two developing countries from this topic (Bangladesh, Sri Lanka or Nepal). For each, identify one policy choice that improved education outcomes in that country. Then evaluate whether that policy could work in your province in Pakistan. Consider:

- Is the social and cultural context similar enough?
- Does the government have the capacity to implement it?
- What planning steps would be needed to adapt it?

Write a short paragraph (60-90 words) for each country explaining your reasoning.

## Summary

Other developing countries face challenges similar to Pakistan's but have achieved different results through specific policy choices. Bangladesh improved girls' access through community schools and female teachers. Sri Lanka achieved efficiency through free education and teacher deployment. Nepal used targeted geographic policies. SDG4 provides a global framework for education goals that aligns with Pakistan's own policy commitments. The lesson is that comparison helps, but policies must fit local conditions. The next topic looks at how technology is changing the tools available to planners.

Find out whether any global education goal is mentioned in the school's planning documents. Write a short paragraph (50-80 words) on whether the school connects its work to any international target and, if so, how.

## Summative task

Choose one developing country from this topic. Write a short paragraph (120-160 words) that identifies two specific policy lessons Pakistan could learn from that country, explains why each lesson is relevant to Pakistan's context, and identifies one condition that would need to be in place for the lesson to work. Use the "adapted, not transplanted" principle from this topic.
