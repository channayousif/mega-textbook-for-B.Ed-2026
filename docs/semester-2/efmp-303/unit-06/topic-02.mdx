## A real classroom situation

The District Education Officer in Larkana receives a monthly report. It shows that three union councils have seen a sudden drop in primary enrolment. A decade ago, this information would have arrived months late, on paper, and only after a manual tally. Today, the Education Management Information System (EMIS) dashboard flags the drop in near real time. The officer can see which schools are affected, compare the data with previous years, and dispatch a team to investigate within days. This is one example of how technology is changing education planning. This topic examines the tools behind this change and what they mean for planners and teachers.

## Explanation

### ICT in education management

<Glossary term="ICT in education" /> refers to the use of computers, software, networks and digital devices to manage and deliver education. In Pakistan, ICT has moved from being a classroom subject (computer studies as a curriculum topic) to being a management tool that affects how the whole system operates. The most important management application is the Education Management Information System (EMIS).

An EMIS is a digital system that collects, stores and reports education data: enrolment numbers, teacher counts, school infrastructure, examination results and budget utilisation. Before EMIS, this data was collected on paper, compiled slowly and often out of date by the time it reached planners. Pakistan's provinces now operate EMIS units that feed data from individual schools up to provincial and federal levels (khan2018).

ICT supports five key management functions:

1. **Enrolment tracking.** Schools enter enrolment data each term. Planners see trends across districts and can identify where enrolment is falling before it becomes a crisis.
2. **Teacher deployment.** ICT systems track where teachers are posted, where vacancies exist and where pupil-teacher ratios are worst. This supports the kind of efficient deployment Sri Lanka achieves manually.
3. **Budget allocation.** When data shows which schools lack buildings or materials, budget requests can be grounded in evidence rather than political influence.
4. **Monitoring and evaluation.** Digital dashboards let monitoring officers see performance indicators in near real time, not months later.
5. **Policy feedback.** When a new policy is introduced, ICT systems can track whether enrolment, attendance or results change in the expected direction.

### Data-driven planning

<Glossary term="Data-driven planning" /> means basing planning decisions on evidence rather than on habit, assumption or political pressure. Pakistan's planning has historically relied on aggregated national figures that masked local variation. Data-driven planning uses disaggregated data: by district, by gender, by school type and by year.

The cycle works as follows. Data is collected (from EMIS, surveys and examinations). It is cleaned and organised (errors removed, categories standardised). Patterns are analysed (which districts have falling enrolment, which schools have low pass rates). Targets are set based on evidence (not political promises). Resources are allocated to where the data shows they are most needed. Implementation is monitored using the same data sources. The cycle then repeats (nep2017).

Pakistan's National Education Policy 2017 explicitly calls for strengthened data systems to support planning. The policy recognises that without reliable data, planners cannot know whether policies are working or where resources should go (nep2017).

### Digital transformation in the education sector

<Glossary term="Digital transformation" /> goes beyond using computers for existing tasks. It means rethinking how the education system works because new tools are available. For Pakistan, digital transformation is at an early stage but visible in several areas.

**Digital attendance systems** have been introduced in some provinces. Teachers mark attendance on tablets, and the data feeds directly into the EMIS. This reduces the gap between what happens on the ground and what the system records.

**Online teacher training** platforms, accelerated after 2020, allow teachers in remote areas to access professional development without travelling to a training centre. This addresses a long-standing barrier to quality improvement in areas where training venues are scarce.

**Open data initiatives** make education statistics available to researchers, journalists and the public. When citizens can see how their district performs compared to others, accountability increases.

However, digital transformation faces real challenges. Internet connectivity is unreliable in many rural areas. Devices break down and are not replaced. Teachers need training to use new systems, and that training itself requires planning. Technology is a tool, not a substitute for the political will and institutional capacity that planning requires.

**Common misconception.** Some trainees assume that "technology replaces the need for planning." It does not. Technology makes planning faster and more evidence-based, but it does not remove the need for decisions. A dashboard that shows falling enrolment does not fix the problem; it shows where a plan is needed. Planning is still the human work of setting goals, allocating resources and making judgements about priorities. Technology supports that work; it does not replace it.

## Activity: Reading a data dashboard

Work in small groups. About 20 minutes.

Imagine your group receives a simplified EMIS report for one district. It shows:

- Primary enrolment down 8 per cent in two union councils over three years.
- Teacher vacancies concentrated in those same union councils.
- Budget utilisation in those councils at only 60 per cent of allocated funds.

Discuss and answer:

1. What does the data suggest is the likely cause of the enrolment drop?
2. What planning action would you recommend to address it?
3. What additional data would you want before acting?

Write your answers in a shared paragraph (80-100 words) and be ready to present to another group.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name three management functions that ICT supports in education planning.
2. *(Understand)* Explain what "data-driven planning" means and how it differs from planning based on assumption.
3. *(Apply)* A district's EMIS shows that girls' enrolment is 20 per cent lower than boys' enrolment in rural schools. Using your understanding of data-driven planning, suggest two planning actions this data could support.
4. *(Analyze)* Why does the topic describe technology as "a tool, not a substitute" for planning? Use one example from the topic.

## Summary

ICT in education management gives planners faster, more detailed data through systems like EMIS. Data-driven planning uses that evidence to set targets, allocate resources and monitor results in a repeating cycle. Digital transformation rethinks how the system works, from digital attendance to online teacher training. But technology requires connectivity, devices and training, and it does not remove the need for human planning decisions. The next topic looks at how policy design itself is changing and who makes policies work in practice.

Find out whether your school reports data to a district EMIS or a similar system. Ask one teacher or administrator: what data does the school send, how often, and who uses it? Write a short paragraph (50-80 words) on what you learn and whether the data comes back to the school in any useful form.

## Summative task

Choose one education management challenge in Pakistan (for example, teacher absenteeism, falling enrolment in a region, or slow budget utilisation). Write a short paragraph (120-160 words) that explains how ICT and data-driven planning could help address the challenge. Identify what data would be needed, how it would be used, and one limitation of a technology-based approach. Draw on the "tool, not a substitute" principle from this topic.
