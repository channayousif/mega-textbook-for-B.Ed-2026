## A real classroom situation

The District Education Officer in Larkana receives a quarterly report from the provincial education department. The report shows that 85 per cent of primary schools in the district received their textbooks on time this term. She is pleased. But when she visits three schools in one Union Council, she finds that the textbooks arrived after the term started and that one school received only half its allocation. The report and the reality do not match. She wonders whether the reporting system she relies on is telling her what she needs to know.

This situation is common across Pakistan's education system. Reports are filed. Numbers are reported. But the numbers do not always reflect what is happening in classrooms. This topic explains why that gap exists and what monitoring and evaluation, done well, can do to close it.

## Explanation

### Monitoring and evaluation: meaning and purpose

<Glossary term="Monitoring" /> is the regular collection of information about a program or policy while it is running. It answers the question: "Is this happening the way we planned?" Monitoring tracks inputs (textbooks delivered, teachers trained), activities (classes held, lessons taught) and outputs (enrolment numbers, completion rates). It is ongoing. A district education office that checks monthly attendance figures is monitoring.

<Glossary term="Evaluation" /> is a deeper judgement. It asks: "Is this program achieving its goals? Is it worth the resources we spent?" Evaluation looks at outcomes (did learning improve?) and impact (did the policy change the system in the way it intended?). It happens at specific points, such as mid-term or at the end of a program cycle, and it often uses monitoring data as one of its sources.

The purpose of both is the same: to give decision-makers evidence they can act on. Without monitoring, a district office does not know which schools are falling behind. Without evaluation, the same office does not know whether its interventions are actually improving learning. Together they form a cycle: monitoring provides the data, evaluation judges what the data means, and both feed back into improved planning and implementation (UNESCO IIEP, n.d.).

### The process of monitoring and evaluation

M&E is not a single event. It is a cycle with distinct stages:

1. **Set indicators and targets.** Before a program starts, planners decide what success looks like and how it will be measured. An indicator for a textbook distribution program might be "90 per cent of schools receive books within two weeks of term start." The target is the specific number the program aims to reach.

2. **Collect data.** Data collection is the heart of monitoring. It can be routine (monthly school returns, attendance registers) or periodic (annual learning assessments, mid-term surveys). The method depends on what the indicator measures and what resources are available.

3. **Analyse the data.** Raw numbers alone are not useful. Analysis means comparing what happened against what was planned. If the target was 90 per cent and the data shows 72 per cent, the analysis asks: where did the gap occur? Was it a delay in printing, a transport problem, or a reporting error?

4. **Report findings.** Analysis must reach the people who can act on it. A monitoring report for a district education officer is different from a policy brief for a provincial minister. Good reporting matches the audience and highlights what needs attention.

5. **Use the findings.** This is the stage most often skipped. Findings that sit in a report on a shelf have no effect. Findings that reach a planning meeting, a budget discussion or a program redesign meeting can change what happens next. The cycle then begins again with improved planning.

A worked example: suppose a program aims to raise girls' enrolment in rural Sindh by 15 per cent over three years. The indicator is the enrolment figure collected each September. After year one, monitoring data shows a 5 per cent increase. The analysis asks whether the increase came from the program areas or from a general trend. The report goes to the district team, who use it to adjust the program in year two (for example, adding transport support where enrolment did not rise). Evaluation at the end of year three judges whether the 15 per cent target was met and whether the increase was sustained.

### Appropriate tools and resources for monitoring

A <Glossary term="Tool" /> in M&E is any instrument used to collect or organise data. The right tool depends on what you are measuring. Using the wrong tool gives you the wrong picture. Common tools in Pakistan's education system include:

- **Attendance registers and school records.** These are the simplest tools. They track enrolment, attendance and dropout on a daily or monthly basis. Every government school maintains them. They are cheap and continuous but depend on accurate record-keeping by the school head.

- **Test and examination results.** Standardised tests (such as the annual grade 5 and grade 8 assessments run by some provinces) measure learning outcomes. They give a snapshot of what pupils have learned but do not explain why learning is high or low.

- **School visits and observation checklists.** Inspectors and monitoring officers visit schools and complete checklists covering teacher presence, classroom conditions and the availability of materials. These tools capture what registers cannot: the quality of what is happening.

- **Budget and expenditure reports.** These track financial inputs: how much was allocated, how much was released and how much was spent. They are essential for detecting whether funds are reaching schools or being delayed at some level of the system.

- **Stakeholder interviews and surveys.** Talking to teachers, parents and community members captures experiences that numbers miss. A survey might reveal that textbooks arrived but teachers were not trained to use them, which an attendance register would never show.

No single tool tells the whole story. Good M&E combines several tools. The district education officer in the opening situation learned this: her quarterly report (a budget/attendance tool) showed 85 per cent delivery, but school visits (an observation tool) revealed a different reality. Both tools were needed.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees assume that monitoring and evaluation are the same activity because both involve collecting information about a program. They are not. Monitoring is ongoing tracking: "Is this happening as planned?" Evaluation is periodic judgement: "Is this working, and is it worth it?" Confusing the two leads to a system that tracks everything and judges nothing, or that judges without first building the data to support the judgement.

Read each of the three scenarios below. For each one, choose the most appropriate M&E tool from the list in the explanation (attendance registers, test results, school visit checklists, budget reports, stakeholder interviews). Write one sentence explaining your choice.

1. A district wants to know whether teachers in remote schools are actually present during school hours.

## Summary

Monitoring is the regular tracking of whether a program is running as planned. Evaluation is the periodic judgement of whether it is achieving its goals. The M&E cycle has five stages: set indicators, collect data, analyse, report and use findings. No single tool captures the whole picture; good M&E combines attendance records, test results, school visits, budget reports and stakeholder feedback. Without M&E, policymakers are making decisions in the dark.
