## A real classroom situation

A teacher in a government primary school in rural Balochistan attends a two-day training session on the new national curriculum. She learns that the curriculum now includes more activities, less rote memorisation, and a focus on understanding. She returns to her school, opens the old textbook she has been using for five years, and finds no copies of the new textbook. She has 60 pupils in one room and no training materials. She continues with the old textbook.

This teacher's situation is not unusual. A policy announced at the federal level must travel through provincial departments, district offices, publishing houses and supply chains before it reaches a classroom. This topic examines the National Education Policy 2017 and how it tried to bridge that gap.

## Explanation

### NEP 2017: Vision and policy framework

By the time the National Education Policy 2017 was being developed, the government had a decade of experience with the 2009 policy. The 2017 policy acknowledged both the achievements and the shortcomings of its predecessor. It noted that enrolment had increased but quality remained uneven, that Article 25-A had created a right to education but many children were still out of school, and that devolution had given provinces more responsibility but not always more capacity (Government of Pakistan, 2017).

The 2017 policy set out a vision of "quality education for all" as the foundation for Pakistan's social and economic development. Its framework rested on five pillars:

1. **Access and equity.** Bringing out-of-school children into the system, with a focus on girls, children with disabilities, and those in remote areas.
2. **Quality.** Improving what pupils actually learn, not just whether they attend school.
3. **Governance.** Strengthening the management of schools at the provincial and district levels.
4. **Relevance.** Connecting education to employment and the needs of a changing economy.
5. **Partnership.** Engaging the private sector, civil society and communities in supporting education (Government of Pakistan, 2017).

The 2017 policy also responded to a political reality: after the 18th Amendment, the federal government's role in education had changed. It could no longer directly manage schools. Its role became setting national standards, coordinating between provinces, and providing technical and financial support.

### NEP 2017: Curriculum reforms and quality assurance

One of the most concrete elements of the 2017 policy was its commitment to <Glossary term="Curriculum reform">curriculum reform</Glossary>. The policy called for a national curriculum that would:

- Reduce the amount of content pupils were expected to memorise.
- Introduce more activity-based and inquiry-based learning.
- Include life skills, critical thinking and problem-solving.
- Reflect Pakistan's diverse cultures and languages, not only a single national narrative.
- Align with international standards so that Pakistani qualifications would be comparable across borders (Government of Pakistan, 2017).

The policy also strengthened <Glossary term="Quality assurance">quality assurance</Glossary> mechanisms. It proposed:

- **National standards for education.** Clear benchmarks that every school, public or private, should meet.
- **Accreditation systems.** Independent bodies that would evaluate schools and teacher education institutions against these standards.
- **Regular assessments.** National learning assessments that would track whether pupils were actually learning, not just attending.
- **Accountability.** Schools and education managers would be held responsible for meeting standards and improving outcomes.

These quality assurance measures were a step beyond the 2009 policy, which had emphasised inputs (teachers, buildings, budgets) more than outputs (learning results).

### NEP 2017: Policy priorities

The 2017 policy identified three cross-cutting priorities:

1. **Literacy and non-formal education.** Pakistan's adult literacy rate remained low, especially among women. The policy called for adult literacy programmes, non-formal schools for out-of-school children, and second-chance education for those who had dropped out.

2. **Higher education reform.** The policy recognised that higher education was producing graduates who often lacked the skills employers needed. It proposed linking university programmes to labour market demands, improving research capacity, and strengthening the Higher Education Commission's role in quality assurance.

3. **Technical and vocational education.** With a large youth population, Pakistan needed more pathways into skilled employment. The policy called for expanding technical and vocational education and training (TVET), making it more accessible and more connected to industry needs (Government of Pakistan, 2017).

**Common misconception.** Some trainees assume that the 2009 and 2017 policies are identical because they share similar goals. In fact, they differ in important ways. The 2009 policy focused on expanding access and setting up governance structures. The 2017 policy placed more emphasis on quality, learning outcomes, curriculum content and accountability. The 2017 policy also acknowledged the challenges that the 2009 policy had not solved and tried to address them with more specific strategies. Recognising the difference between the two policies is important for understanding how Pakistan's education policy evolved.

## Summary

The National Education Policy 2017 built on the foundations of the 2009 policy but shifted the emphasis toward quality, learning outcomes and accountability. Its five pillars - access and equity, quality, governance, relevance and partnership - framed a more comprehensive approach. Curriculum reform, quality assurance, literacy, higher education reform and technical education were its priority areas. The policy acknowledged that access alone was not enough; what mattered was what pupils learned and whether that learning prepared them for life and work. Whether this policy succeeds depends on the same factors that shaped the 2009 policy's outcomes: planning, funding, administrative capacity and sustained political commitment.
