## A real classroom situation

A senior teacher at a government boys' school in Lahore has taught through four different education policies. Each time a new policy was announced, he attended a one-day orientation at the district office. He received a summary document, listened to a presentation and returned to his classroom. The curriculum changed once, in 1979, when new textbooks arrived. The other three policies, he says, "came and went without changing anything I did."

His experience raises a question this topic addresses: if Pakistan produced so many policies between 1947 and 2000, why did the classroom change so little?

## Explanation

### 1947-1970: Early policies

Pakistan inherited a small, uneven education system at independence. Literacy was low, access was limited, and the system served mainly urban areas. The government's first response was the 1947 Education Conference, which called for free and compulsory primary education, an Islamic orientation in the curriculum, and Urdu as the national language of instruction (Khan, 2018).

The conference set ambitious goals but did not produce a detailed plan. Building schools and training teachers across a new country with limited funds proved far harder than declaring the intention. A decade later, the 1959 Commission on National Education (commonly called the Sharif Commission) reviewed the system and recommended major structural changes. It proposed the 5-3-2-2 grade structure (five years primary, three years middle, two years secondary, two years higher secondary) that still underpins Pakistan's schools today (Khan, 2018). The Sharif Commission also emphasised science education and teacher training.

This period shows a pattern that repeated throughout Pakistan's policy history: a commission or conference would identify the problems, make sound recommendations, and then implementation would stall because of budget constraints, political instability or administrative weakness.

### 1970-1990: Policies and reforms

The 1970 National Education Policy, introduced under Yahya Khan, raised the education budget and promised universal primary enrolment. After the separation of East Pakistan, the 1972 Education Policy under Zulfikar Ali Bhutto went further. It nationalised private schools and colleges, bringing them under government control. The goal was to reduce inequality and make education accessible to all, regardless of income (Khan, 2018).

Nationalisation expanded the government's role in education significantly. Thousands of private institutions were taken over. But the policy also strained the system. Government schools had to absorb more pupils without a matching increase in teachers, buildings or budgets. Quality suffered in many places.

The 1979 Education Policy under General Zia-ul-Haq shifted the focus again. This policy emphasised Islamization of the curriculum, introducing Islamic studies as a compulsory subject and aligning textbook content with a particular ideological vision. New textbooks were produced, and the 1979 policy is one of the few that the teacher in the opening situation actually noticed, because the materials in his classroom changed.

Each of these policies responded to the political priorities of the government in power. Nationalisation served Bhutto's socialist agenda. Islamization served Zia's programme. Education policy became a tool for political goals, not a long-term strategy for the education system.

### 1990-2000: Policy shifts and challenges

The 1992 Education Policy and the 1998 National Education Policy both promised to reverse some of the earlier trends. They spoke of improving quality, reversing the decline in government schools, and encouraging private sector participation. The 1998 policy specifically promised to raise education spending to four per cent of GDP, a target that was not met.

By 2000, Pakistan's education system faced serious challenges. Literacy rates remained low, especially for women and in rural areas. Government schools were often poorly staffed and under-resourced. Private schools had expanded rapidly, creating a two-tier system where quality depended on ability to pay. Many of the problems identified in 1947 were still present, described in different policy language.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees assume that a policy on paper is a policy in practice. The history of education policies from 1947 to 2000 shows this is not true. A <Glossary term="National education policy">national education policy</Glossary> is a statement of intent by the federal government. It sets out goals and principles. But whether it changes what happens in a classroom depends on planning, funding, administrative capacity and political will. Without these, the policy remains a document.

2. *(Understand)* In one or two sentences, explain why the 1972 nationalisation policy had both positive and negative effects on the education system.
3. *(Apply)* A new policy promises to raise the education budget to four per cent of GDP. Based on what you have learned from this topic, explain two planning steps that must happen for this promise to become reality.

## Summary

From 1947 to 2000, Pakistan introduced at least six major education policies. Each responded to the political and social context of its time: Islamic orientation after independence, nationalisation under Bhutto, Islamization under Zia, and quality improvement pledges in the 1990s. The 1959 Sharif Commission's grade structure is one of the few recommendations that endured. Many other policy goals - universal enrolment, increased spending, improved quality - were repeated across decades without being fully achieved. The gap between policy intent and classroom reality is the central challenge this topic reveals.
