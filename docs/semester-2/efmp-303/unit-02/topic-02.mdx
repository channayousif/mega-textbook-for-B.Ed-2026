## A real classroom situation

The head teacher of a government girls' school in Peshawar receives a copy of the National Education Policy 2009 from the district education office. She reads the foreword and the first chapter. The policy promises free and compulsory education up to class 10, a standardised curriculum, better-trained teachers and improved school facilities. She looks at her school: two of her six teachers are absent today, the science laboratory has no equipment, and the roof of one classroom leaks. She puts the policy in a filing cabinet and locks it.

The head teacher's reaction is understandable. The National Education Policy 2009 was ambitious. But ambition alone does not change schools. This topic examines what the policy set out to do, how it planned to do it, and what actually happened.

## Explanation

### NEP 2009: Objectives and guiding principles

The National Education Policy 2009 was introduced after several years of review. The government recognised that earlier policies had not delivered the results they promised. The 2009 policy identified several core problems: low enrolment (especially among girls and in rural areas), high dropout rates, poor quality of education, weak governance and an outdated curriculum (Government of Pakistan, 2009).

The policy set out clear objectives:

1. **Free and compulsory education.** Every child would have the right to education up to class 10 (matriculation). This was later given constitutional force through Article 25-A, added by the 18th Amendment in 2010.
2. **Quality improvement.** The policy promised to raise the quality of teaching, learning materials and school management.
3. **Curriculum reform.** A revised national curriculum would replace outdated content and introduce more relevant, skills-based learning (Government of Pakistan, 2009).
4. **Teacher quality.** Better recruitment, training and working conditions for teachers.
5. **Governance reform.** Education management would be devolved to provincial governments, allowing more local decision-making.

The guiding principles emphasised equity, quality, access and relevance. The policy recognised that education must serve Pakistan's social and economic development, not just produce certificates.

### NEP 2009: Implementation strategies

The policy proposed several implementation strategies to achieve its objectives:

- **Curriculum revision.** A new national curriculum would be developed, replacing the existing one. Textbooks would be rewritten to match the new curriculum. Examination systems would be reformed to test understanding, not just memorisation.
- **Teacher training.** In-service training programmes would upgrade the skills of existing teachers. Pre-service training would be strengthened through improvements to teacher education institutions.
- **Governance devolution.** The 18th Constitutional Amendment (2010) transferred education from the federal to the provincial level. Provincial education departments would now manage schools, teachers and budgets.
- **Public-private partnerships.** The policy encouraged private sector involvement in education, recognising that government resources alone were insufficient.
- **Increased spending.** The policy called for education spending to rise to seven per cent of GDP within ten years (Government of Pakistan, 2009).

Each strategy required detailed planning, funding and administrative follow-through. The policy document described what should happen, but the "how" depended on provincial governments, district offices and schools carrying it out.

### NEP 2009: Outcomes and challenges

The National Education Policy 2009 produced mixed results. On the positive side:

- **Article 25-A** gave constitutional recognition to the right to education, creating a legal foundation for future efforts.
- **Enrolment increased** in many areas, partly because of government stipend programmes (such as the Girls' Stipend Programme in some provinces) and growing demand for education.
- **Curriculum revision** was undertaken, and a new national curriculum was developed, though full implementation took years and faced resistance from textbook publishers and some teacher groups.

However, significant challenges remained:

- **Spending targets were not met.** Education spending stayed well below seven per cent of GDP. In many provinces, the education budget actually shrank as a share of total spending.
- **Quality gaps persisted.** Learning outcomes in government schools remained low. National assessments showed that many pupils in class 5 could not read at a class 2 level.
- **Devolution created new problems.** After the 18th Amendment, provinces had to build capacity to manage education systems they had not previously run. Some provinces were better prepared than others. Sindh and Punjab invested more; Balochistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa faced greater capacity constraints.
- **Teacher quality remained a weakness.** Training programmes were sporadic and often of short duration. Absenteeism, especially in rural areas, continued.

**Common misconception.** Some trainees believe that policies fail only because of money. The NEP 2009 experience shows the problem is broader. Funding matters, but so does administrative capacity, political commitment, coordination between federal and provincial governments, and the ability to translate a national policy into local action. A well-funded policy that is poorly planned can fail just as surely as an underfunded one.

## Activity: Objective-to-outcome mapping

Work in small groups (3-4). About 25 minutes.

Take one objective of the NEP 2009 (for example, curriculum reform). Trace it through the implementation strategy to the actual outcome. Write one sentence for each step:

1. What was the objective?
2. What strategy was proposed to achieve it?
3. What actually happened (outcome)?
4. What gap or challenge explains the difference between the objective and the outcome?

Each group presents one objective chain. As a class, discuss: which objective had the best outcome, and why?

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name three objectives of the National Education Policy 2009.
2. *(Understand)* Explain in one or two sentences why the devolution of education to provinces after the 18th Amendment was both an opportunity and a challenge.
3. *(Apply)* A provincial government wants to improve teacher quality as NEP 2009 proposed. List three planning steps it must take beyond simply announcing the goal.

## Summary

The National Education Policy 2009 set out ambitious objectives: free and compulsory education up to class 10, curriculum reform, better teacher quality and governance devolution. Its implementation strategies - curriculum revision, teacher training, devolution and public-private partnerships - were sound in design but uneven in execution. Article 25-A and increased enrolment were real achievements. But spending targets were missed, quality gaps persisted and devolution exposed capacity weaknesses. The policy showed that even a well-designed national education policy depends on planning, resources and implementation capacity to produce results.
