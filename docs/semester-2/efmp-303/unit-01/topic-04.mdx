## A real classroom situation

A teacher preparation class is discussing education policy. One trainee says, "Education in Pakistan
has not changed much since independence. The same problems keep coming up in every new policy."
Another disagrees: "That is not true. There have been many policies and plans. Things have changed." A
third asks, "Can anyone name them in order?" Nobody can.

Both trainees in that conversation have a point. Pakistan has produced many education policies and
plans. Access has expanded, structures have been reformed and new challenges have emerged. Yet many
problems - low funding, uneven quality, gender gaps - recur. This topic gives you the landmarks you
need to hold the whole story in your mind.

## Explanation

### Why milestones matter

A milestone is a moment that changes the direction or shape of the system. Knowing the milestones in
order helps you see:

- Where a current policy came from.
- Why certain structures exist.
- Which problems have been addressed and which keep recurring.

This is the timeline from independence to the present, with each milestone's significance explained.

### The timeline of milestones

**1947: Pakistan Education Conference.** Held shortly after independence, this conference set the
direction for the new nation's education: free and compulsory primary education, a shift toward
national languages, and an Islamic orientation. It did not produce a detailed plan, but it established
the principles that later policies would work from.

**1955-1960: First Five-Year Plan.** The first national development plan gave education a share of
the budget and set targets for primary school expansion and teacher training. It showed that planning
- not just policy statements - would drive expansion.

**1959: Sharif Commission.** The Commission on National Education, chaired by S. M. Sharif,
recommended the grade structure (5-3-2-2) that still exists in modified form. It also emphasised
science education, character building and the promotion of Urdu. Its recommendations shaped the next
three decades of education.

**1970: National Education Policy.** Under President Yahya Khan, this policy addressed curriculum
reform and the expansion of primary education. It was interrupted by the 1971 war and the creation of
Bangladesh, but its curriculum recommendations influenced what followed.

**1972: Education Policy.** Under Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, this policy nationalised private schools and
colleges, expanding government control over education. The goal was to make education more equitable
and accessible. The nationalisation had lasting effects: many private institutions were brought under
government management, and the state's role in education grew significantly.

**1979: Education Policy.** Under General Zia-ul-Haq, this policy emphasised Islamization of the
curriculum and the promotion of Islamic values. Pakistan studies and Islamic studies became
compulsory subjects. This policy shaped the curriculum content that persisted for decades.

**1992: Education Policy.** This policy attempted to address the accumulated challenges of the
previous decades: low literacy, gender gaps and uneven quality. It set new targets but faced the
implementation challenges that had limited earlier policies.

**1998: National Education Policy.** Building on the 1992 policy, this one focused on expanding
access to primary and secondary education and improving quality. It coincided with the early phase of
the devolution debate that would later reshape governance.

**2009: National Education Policy.** After a long gap, this comprehensive policy addressed
curriculum reform, teacher education, literacy, technical education and quality assurance. It is
discussed in detail in Unit 2. It was the most detailed policy document produced to that point.

**2017: National Education Policy.** The most recent major policy, focusing on curriculum alignment
with SDGs, quality assurance, and the integration of out-of-school children. Also discussed in Unit 2.

### What the timeline shows

Several patterns emerge from this timeline:

1. **Policies respond to political change.** Each new government has produced its own education
   policy, reflecting the priorities of the ruling party or regime.
2. **The same problems recur.** Low literacy, gender gaps and insufficient funding appear in policy
   after policy. New policies name them; few resolve them.
3. **Structures persist.** The grade system from 1959, the examination system from the colonial
   period and the federal-provincial division of education management have remained through every
   policy change.
4. **Expansion has been real.** Despite recurring problems, access has grown enormously. Pakistan
   had fewer than 10,000 primary schools at independence; today it has over 150,000.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees assume that having many policies means the system has been
reformed many times. In reality, the number of policy documents is not the same as the depth of
change. Many policies have been announced, but the structures they were meant to reform have proved
resistant to change. Understanding the timeline helps you see the difference between policy
announcements and actual transformation.

## Activity: Place the milestones

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Your teacher gives you a set of cards, each with one milestone on it (year and name only). Arrange
the cards in chronological order from earliest to most recent. Then, for each card, write one
sentence explaining its significance.

If you do not have cards, write the milestones on slips of paper and arrange them. Check your order
against the timeline in this topic.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* List in order any five milestones in Pakistan's education system from 1947 to the present.
2. *(Understand)* Explain the significance of the 1959 Sharif Commission in one or two sentences.
3. *(Apply)* The timeline shows that many policies name the same problems. Using this pattern, explain why a new policy might fail to solve a problem that earlier policies also named.

## Summary

Pakistan's education system has passed through a series of milestones since independence: the 1947
Education Conference, the First Five-Year Plan, the Sharif Commission and a succession of national
policies from 1970 onward. Each milestone reflected the political moment and set new directions.
Yet core structures proved persistent, and recurring problems - low literacy, uneven quality,
insufficient funding - appeared in policy after policy. Knowing this timeline helps you understand
that policy is a continuous process, not a one-time fix.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your head teacher or a senior colleague which education policy they remember most clearly from
their career. Ask what changed because of it and what stayed the same. Write a short note connecting
their answer to one milestone from this topic.

## Summative task

Choose any two milestones from the timeline. Write a paragraph (100-140 words) explaining how the
first milestone influenced or led to the second. Use specific details from this topic and state the
significance of the connection.

| Criterion | Levels |
