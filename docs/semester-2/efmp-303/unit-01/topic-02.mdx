## A real classroom situation

In a rural girls' school near Jacobabad, the enrolment officer visits a village to encourage families
to send their daughters to school. One father says, "What will she do with education? She will
marry and go to another village. The boys need the schooling because they will earn." The officer
replies that educated girls earn more, have healthier children and contribute to the family's income.
The father listens but is not convinced. He asks, "Has any girl from this village become a officer
because of school?"

The father's question is not unreasonable. He is asking whether education actually delivers the
benefits that society and policy claim it will. This topic examines the three-way relationship
between education, society and development that his question points to.

## Explanation

### The three-way relationship

Education does not exist in isolation. It is shaped by the society around it, and in turn it shapes
that society and the country's economic development. Think of three forces connected in a triangle:

- **Education** provides knowledge, skills and values.
- **Society** sets the norms, expectations and conditions within which education operates.
- **Development** is the improvement in people's living conditions that both education and society
  contribute to.

Each side of the triangle influences the other two.

### How society shapes education

The society around a school determines much of what happens inside it. Consider these examples from
Pakistan:

- **Language.** Urdu is the national language and the medium of instruction in many government
  schools, but Sindhi, Punjabi, Pashto and Balochi are spoken at home in different provinces.
  Society's language preferences shape the medium of instruction, which in turn affects how well
  pupils learn.
- **Gender norms.** In some communities, girls are expected to stay home after primary school. In
  others, higher education for girls is valued. These norms determine enrolment patterns more
  powerfully than any policy document.
- **Poverty.** A family that depends on a child's labour for income cannot easily send that child
  to school, regardless of what the law says.

Society sets the demand for education and the conditions under which it operates. Policy that ignores
these social realities struggles to succeed.

### How education shapes society and development

Education also pushes back. It changes the society that produced it:

- **Health.** Educated mothers have healthier children and lower infant mortality. This is one of
  the most consistent findings in development research.
- **Economic growth.** Countries with higher literacy rates tend to have higher productivity.
  Education builds <Glossary term="Human capital" /> - the skilled workforce that an economy needs.
- **Civic participation.** Educated citizens are more likely to vote, engage with local government
  and hold institutions accountable.
- **Social mobility.** Education is one of the few paths by which a child from a poor family can
  move into a different economic position.

The father in the opening situation is weighing the costs of sending his daughter to school against
the benefits he can see. If he has never seen a girl from his village gain employment through
education, his scepticism is rational from where he stands. Policy and planning must bridge that
gap between the promise of education and its visible reality.

### Why this matters for policy and planning

Understanding the education-society-development triangle has practical consequences for policy:

1. **Policy must fit social reality.** A policy that requires all children to attend school ignores
   the social and economic reasons some families cannot comply. Effective policy accounts for those
   barriers.
2. **Planning must address both supply and demand.** Building schools is one side (supply).
   Convincing families to send children is the other (demand). Planning that addresses only one
   side produces half the intended result.
3. **Development goals depend on education quality, not just access.** A country where children
   attend school but learn little is not building human capital. Development requires learning,
   not just enrolment.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees assume that education automatically leads to development.
The relationship is real but not automatic. Education must be of sufficient quality, relevant to
the economy, and accessible to all groups. Poor-quality education or education that only reaches
some communities can actually increase inequality rather than reduce it.

## Activity: Map your community

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Think of the community around your practicum school or your home village. For each of the three
sides below, write one sentence describing how it operates in that community:

1. How does society shape education here? (Consider language, gender, poverty, distance.)
2. How does education shape society here? (Consider any visible benefits of schooling.)
3. What development goals does the community have, and how does education connect to them?

Share your answers with another pair. Identify one place where policy and planning could better
address the gap between education and development in that community.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the three elements in the education-society-development triangle and give one example of how each influences the others.
2. *(Understand)* Explain in one or two sentences why a policy that ignores social reality may fail in practice.
3. *(Apply)* A new policy provides free textbooks to every child in a district. Using the triangle, identify one social factor and one development goal that planning must consider for this policy to work.

## Summary

Education, society and development form a triangle of mutual influence. Society shapes who goes to
school and under what conditions. Education shapes the society and economy around it through human
capital, health and civic participation. Policy that ignores this triangle produces documents that
do not match reality. Planning that addresses both supply and demand is what turns policy into
results.

## Try this at your practicum school

Interview one parent or community member near your school about what they expect education to deliver
for their children. Ask: "What do you hope your child will gain from school?" Write down their
answer and compare it with the development goals described in this topic.

## Summative task

Choose one province of Pakistan. Write a short paragraph (100-140 words) describing how at least two
social factors in that province shape education, and explaining one way education in that province
contributes to economic or social development. Use specific examples.
