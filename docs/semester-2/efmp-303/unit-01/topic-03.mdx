## A real classroom situation

A teacher in Lahore takes her class through the history of Pakistan's education system. A pupil asks,
"Sir, why do we still have the same examination system the British used? Why did we not make a new one
after independence?" The teacher pauses. She knows the exams are still called "Board Examinations"
and that the structure has not changed much, but she is not sure why. She promises to find out.

That pupil's question is a good one. Much of Pakistan's education system carries features inherited
from the colonial period. Understanding what came before independence and what changed after it helps
explain why the system looks the way it does today.

## Explanation

### Education before independence

The education system that Pakistan inherited in 1947 was shaped by nearly two centuries of British
colonial policy. Its main features:

- **Purpose.** Colonial education was designed to produce clerks, administrators and a small educated
  class to serve the colonial government. It was not designed for mass education or for building a
  self-governing nation's workforce. Macaulay's famous Minute of 1835 aimed to create "a class of
  persons, Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals, and in
  intellect" (as cited in the course guide's reading list).
- **Language.** English became the medium of higher education and government. Vernacular languages
  were used at the primary level but carried lower prestige. This language hierarchy persisted long
  after independence.
- **Structure.** The system was layered: vernacular primary schools, English-medium high schools,
  and a small number of universities. Access was narrow - literacy in 1947 was roughly 15 per cent
  across the territory that became Pakistan, and even lower among women and in rural areas.
- **Examination system.** Board examinations at the end of secondary school, modelled on the British
  system, became the gateway to employment and higher education. This structure remained after
  independence.

Several commissions shaped colonial education policy. The 1854 Wood's Dispatch laid out the
hierarchical system. The 1882 Hunter Commission examined the state of primary education. The 1913
Calcutta University Commission looked at higher education. Each commission responded to colonial
needs rather than to the needs of the population it served.

### Post-independence developments (1947-1970)

After independence in 1947, Pakistan faced the task of building an education system for a new nation.
The first two decades saw several key developments:

- **The 1947 Pakistan Education Conference.** Held within months of independence, it called for free
  and compulsory primary education, a shift toward Urdu and other national languages, and an education
  system that reflected Pakistan's Islamic identity.
- **The First Five-Year Plan (1955-1960).** Education received a share of the national budget, with
  emphasis on primary school expansion and teacher training.
- **The 1959 Commission on National Education (Sharif Commission).** Named after its chairman, this
  commission produced one of the most influential reports in Pakistan's education history. It
  recommended a structured system: five years of primary, three years of middle, two years of
  secondary and two years of higher secondary education. It also emphasised science education and
  character building.
- **The Second Five-Year Plan (1960-1965).** Continued expansion of schools and universities, with
  attention to technical education.
- **The 1960s curriculum reforms.** New national curricula were developed, and Urdu was promoted as
  the medium of instruction at the primary level.

The post-independence period was one of institution building. Many of the structures that still
exist - the Sharif Commission's grade structure, the board system, the pattern of federal and
provincial education ministries - date from this period.

### What changed and what did not

Some things changed after independence. The goals shifted from serving a colonial administration to
serving national development. Urdu replaced English as the national language. Islamic studies became
part of the curriculum. Enrolment expanded.

But much stayed the same. The examination system remained. The hierarchical structure of schools
remained. The gap between urban and rural education remained. The language hierarchy, though
officially shifted, still privileged English in higher education and professional life. The pupil's
question in the opening situation points to this: the system inherited its basic shape from the
colonial period, and the decades since have modified it more than remade it.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees assume that Pakistan built an entirely new education system
after independence. In reality, the new nation inherited a colonial structure and worked within it,
adding new goals and expanding access. The legacy of that inheritance is visible in everything from
the grade structure to the examination system to the ongoing debate about the medium of instruction.

## Activity: Then and now

Work in groups of three. About 25 minutes.

Each group member takes one of the following features of Pakistan's education system today:

1. The Board Examination system (Matric and Intermediate).
2. The grade structure (primary, middle, secondary, higher secondary).
3. The medium of instruction debate (Urdu, English, Sindhi, etc.).

For your chosen feature, answer two questions:

- Where did this feature come from? (Was it inherited from the colonial period or introduced after independence?)
- Has it changed or stayed the same since independence?

Present your findings to the group. As a group, agree on one feature that has changed the most and
one that has changed the least.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name two features of Pakistan's education system that were inherited from the colonial period.
2. *(Understand)* What was the main purpose of colonial education, and how did that purpose change after independence?
3. *(Apply)* The Sharif Commission recommended a specific grade structure. Using what you have learned, explain one way that structure is still visible in today's schools.

## Summary

Pakistan inherited a colonial education system designed for a narrow purpose: producing administrators
for a foreign government. After independence, the nation expanded access, shifted goals toward
national development, and promoted national languages. But the basic structure - the grade system,
the examination system, the language hierarchy - remained largely intact. Understanding this history
helps explain why the system looks the way it does today and why reforming it is a slow process.
