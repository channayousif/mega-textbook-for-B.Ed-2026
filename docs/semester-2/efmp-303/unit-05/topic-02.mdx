## A real classroom situation

In a village near Shikarpur, a government primary school runs two shifts: a morning shift for boys and an afternoon shift for girls. The boys' shift has six teachers for 240 pupils. The girls' shift has three teachers for 90 pupils. Last year, three girls from the village passed the middle-school examination and were offered places at the government girls' middle school in the nearby town. Their fathers refused to send them. The school is eight kilometres away, there is no transport, and the families do not believe it is safe for girls to travel alone. The girls stayed home. One was married within the year.

The answer lies in the connection between education and the wider social and economic conditions in which schools operate. This topic examines that connection.

## Explanation

### Role of education in human capital formation

<Glossary term="Human capital" /> is the stock of knowledge, skills and abilities that a person has and that make them productive in economic life. Education is the main way that human capital is built. When a child learns to read, solve problems and work with others, they are acquiring skills that make them more productive as an adult. When a large share of the population has these skills, the whole economy benefits.

The link between education and human capital is well established in economic research. Countries where more people complete secondary and tertiary education tend to have higher productivity and higher incomes. In Pakistan, this link is visible but weak. Despite decades of education expansion, the country's human capital index remains low. The World Bank's Human Capital Index ranked Pakistan below most South Asian neighbours, reflecting low learning levels, high dropout rates and poor health outcomes among children (Khan, 2018).

The weakness of the education-human capital link in Pakistan has several causes. Many children attend school but learn little. The skills taught in school do not always match what the economy needs. And large numbers of children, especially girls and children in rural areas, never complete even basic education. Building human capital requires not just more schools but better learning, better health and better alignment between what schools teach and what society needs. The World Bank's Human Capital Index ranked Pakistan below most South Asian neighbours, reflecting these combined deficits in learning, health and survival (World Bank, 2020).

### Reducing inequality and poverty through education

Education can be a powerful tool for reducing inequality. When children from poor families gain the same knowledge and skills as children from wealthy families, they have a better chance of moving into better jobs and higher incomes. This is one of the strongest arguments for public investment in education: it gives children from disadvantaged backgrounds a path out of poverty.

But education can also reinforce inequality. In Pakistan, the education system is deeply unequal. Children in urban areas generally have better schools, better teachers and better learning outcomes than children in rural areas. Children in wealthy families can afford private schools that offer better quality than government schools. Girls, children with disabilities, and children from minority communities face additional barriers.

The National Education Policy 2009 acknowledged this tension. It stated that education policy must actively work to reduce disparities, not assume that expanding access alone will be enough (Government of Pakistan, 2009). Reducing inequality through education requires targeted action: better schools in disadvantaged areas, support for children who face barriers to attendance, and policies that ensure all children learn to a common standard.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees assume that education alone can eliminate poverty. It cannot. Education improves individuals' chances, but poverty is also caused by unemployment, poor health, discrimination and unequal distribution of resources. Without jobs for educated workers, without health and nutrition that support learning, and without policies that address structural inequality, education alone will not end poverty. It is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one.

### Gender and inclusive education policies

<Glossary term="Inclusive Education" /> means an education system that includes all children, regardless of gender, disability, language, ethnicity or economic status. In Pakistan, inclusive education is both a policy goal and a persistent challenge. Girls' enrolment has improved significantly over the past two decades, but large gaps remain, especially in rural Sindh and Balochistan.

Gender policy in Pakistan's education has focused heavily on girls' enrolment. The National Education Policy 2009 set targets for closing the gender gap at all levels. Provincial governments have introduced stipends, free textbooks and school construction programmes aimed at girls. These efforts have raised enrolment, but retention and learning outcomes for girls still lag behind boys in many districts.

Inclusive education goes beyond gender. It also includes children with disabilities, children from religious and ethnic minority groups, and children in remote or conflict-affected areas. Pakistan's education system serves these groups poorly. Children with disabilities are often excluded from mainstream schools due to a lack of trained teachers, accessible buildings and appropriate materials. Children in remote areas may live too far from the nearest school to attend regularly.

Gender policy is not only about girls' enrolment. It also concerns the content of what is taught (whether textbooks show girls and women in diverse roles), the safety of the school environment (whether girls are safe travelling to and inside school), and the support systems that keep girls in school (whether schools have female teachers, separate toilets and policies against harassment). A narrow focus on enrolment numbers misses these deeper dimensions of gender equity (Khan, 2018).

## Activity: Education and development in your province

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

1. Choose one social group in your province that faces barriers to education (for example, girls in rural areas, children with disabilities, children from landless families).
2. List three specific barriers this group faces in accessing and benefiting from education.
3. For each barrier, suggest one policy action that could address it.
4. Share your suggestions with another pair. Discuss which barrier you think is the most urgent and why.

## Summary

Education contributes to social and economic development in three main ways. It builds human capital by giving people the knowledge and skills they need to be productive. It can reduce inequality by giving children from disadvantaged backgrounds a path to better opportunities. And it promotes inclusion when policies ensure that all children, regardless of gender, disability or background, can access and benefit from learning. In Pakistan, all three contributions are real but limited. Learning outcomes remain low, inequality persists across regions and groups, and inclusive education is more a goal than a reality. Effective policy must address all three dimensions together.
