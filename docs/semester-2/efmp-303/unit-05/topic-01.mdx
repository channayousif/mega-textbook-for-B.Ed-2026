## A real classroom situation

A government girls' school in Larkana district was inspected last month. The inspection team checked the building, counted the desks, and asked to see the attendance register. They noted that the building was in good condition and the registers were up to date. They gave the school a satisfactory rating. But when the district education officer visited a week later, she found that half the teachers had not completed the syllabus, that science lessons were being taught without any practical work, and that several teachers had not attended any training in the past five years. The building was fine. The learning was not.

This situation shows a gap that many schools face. The building passes inspection, but the teaching and learning do not meet expectations. Quality assurance is supposed to catch both. This topic explains how Pakistan's quality assurance system works and where it often falls short.

## Explanation

### Accreditation and evaluation systems

<Glossary term="Accreditation" /> is the formal process by which an institution or programme is judged to meet a set of defined standards. In Pakistan, accreditation applies to schools, colleges, teacher education programmes and universities. The body responsible depends on the level. The Higher Education Commission (HEC) accredits degree programmes. Provincial education departments and district-level bodies inspect schools. Professional councils, such as the Pakistan Nursing Council, accredit specialized programmes.

Accreditation is not a one-time event. A programme that is accredited today must demonstrate continued quality over time. This means periodic reviews, self-evaluation reports and evidence that the institution has acted on previous feedback (Khan, 2018). In practice, however, accreditation in Pakistan often focuses heavily on inputs: buildings, equipment, teacher numbers and enrolment. The teaching process and learning outcomes receive less attention. This is why the school in the opening situation could pass inspection while its actual teaching remained weak.

The National Education Policy 2009 recognized this gap. It called for an accreditation system that covers not only infrastructure but also the quality of teaching, learning outcomes and institutional management (Government of Pakistan, 2009). The policy proposed a shift from checking buildings to checking whether students are actually learning.

### Standardization of curricula

<Glossary term="Standardization" /> in education means setting common standards for what students should learn and be able to do at each level. In Pakistan, the curriculum is developed at the federal level for many subjects and adapted at the provincial level. The Single National Curriculum (SNC), introduced in 2020, is the most recent effort to standardize what is taught across public and private schools in all provinces.

Standardization serves several purposes. It ensures that a student in Karachi and a student in a rural school in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa covers the same core content. It makes examination and assessment more consistent. It also provides a common framework for teacher training and textbook development. When the curriculum is standardized, teacher education programmes know what their trainees will need to teach, and textbook publishers know what content to include.

But standardization does not mean every school teaches in the same way. A standard curriculum sets out what to teach, not how to teach it. Teachers still need to adapt their methods to their students' needs, their local context and the resources available. The misconception that standardization removes teacher judgment is one that this topic addresses below.

### Teacher training and professional development

Teachers are at the centre of quality assurance. No curriculum, however well designed, improves learning without a teacher who understands it and can teach it effectively. Pakistan's teacher training system includes both pre-service training (before a person enters teaching) and in-service training (during their career).

Pre-service training is provided through teacher education colleges and universities. The Bachelor of Education (B.Ed.) and Associate Degree in Education (ADE) are the main entry qualifications. The National Education Policy 2009 set a target that all teachers should hold at least a B.Ed. by 2018, though this target has not been fully met (Government of Pakistan, 2009).

In-service training is meant to update teachers' skills and knowledge throughout their career. Provincial governments run training programmes through teacher training institutes and district training centres. The National Professional Standards for Teachers, developed in 2009, provide a framework for what teachers should know and be able to do at different stages of their career.

A persistent challenge is that in-service training in Pakistan is often sporadic, short and disconnected from teachers' actual classroom needs. Teachers attend a workshop, receive a certificate, and return to the same conditions. Without follow-up, support and accountability, training alone does not change classroom practice (Khan, 2018). Quality assurance in teacher development requires not just training events but a continuous cycle of learning, practice, feedback and improvement.

**Common misconception.** Many people assume that accreditation is only about infrastructure, that standardization means every school teaches the same way, or that teacher training is a one-time event. None of these is accurate. Accreditation, done well, examines teaching and learning, not just buildings. Standardization sets common content standards but leaves teaching methods to the teacher's professional judgment. And effective teacher development is continuous, not a single workshop. When these misconceptions go uncorrected, quality assurance efforts focus on the wrong things and real improvement does not happen.

## Activity: Mapping quality assurance in your district

Work in small groups of three or four. About 25 minutes.

1. On a large sheet, draw three columns: Accreditation, Standardization, Teacher Training.
2. For each column, list the mechanisms that exist in your district. Who conducts accreditation or inspection? What standardized assessments are used? What teacher training programmes are available?
3. Beside each mechanism, note one strength and one weakness you have observed or heard about.
4. As a group, identify one change that would most improve quality in your district. Write a short justification (two or three sentences) for your choice.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* In your own words, what is the difference between accreditation and inspection?
2. *(Understand)* List two reasons why standardization of the curriculum matters in Pakistan.
3. *(Apply)* A school has good buildings and high enrolment, but its students perform poorly on standardized tests. Using the quality assurance cycle, explain what step might have been missed.

## Summary

Quality assurance in Pakistan operates through three main mechanisms. Accreditation judges whether institutions meet defined standards, though it often focuses on inputs rather than learning outcomes. Standardization sets common content expectations across schools and provinces, while leaving teaching methods to teachers' professional judgment. Teacher training and professional development are meant to ensure that teachers can deliver the curriculum effectively, but in-service training is often too sporadic to produce lasting change. All three mechanisms must work together for quality to improve across the system.
