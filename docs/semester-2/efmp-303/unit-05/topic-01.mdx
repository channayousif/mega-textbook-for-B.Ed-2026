## A real classroom situation

Consider the following constructed illustration set in Sindh, not a report of an observed event: in a government girls' primary school, an external inspection team checks physical infrastructure, counts student desks, and inspects attendance registers. The building is in good repair and records are up to date, yielding a satisfactory compliance rating. When visited soon afterwards to evaluate curriculum delivery, however, observers find that half the teachers have not completed the syllabus, science classes proceed without practical work, and multiple teachers have attended no professional development in several years. The physical facility satisfied basic inspection standards, but actual instructional quality and student learning remained unmonitored.

## Explanation

### Accreditation and evaluation systems

<Glossary term="Accreditation" /> is the formal process by which an institution or programme is judged to meet a set of defined standards. In Pakistan, accreditation applies to schools, colleges, teacher education programmes and universities. The body responsible depends on the level and the discipline. The Higher Education Commission (HEC) establishes the regulatory framework and recognizes degree programmes, while discipline-specific accreditation is handled by professional councils. For teacher education, the National Accreditation Council for Teacher Education (NACTE) accredits programmes such as the B.Ed. and ADE. Provincial education departments and district-level bodies inspect schools. Other professional councils, such as the Pakistan Nursing Council, accredit specialized programmes in their fields.

Accreditation is not a one-time event. A programme that is accredited today must demonstrate continued quality over time. This means periodic reviews, self-evaluation reports and evidence that the institution has acted on previous feedback. In practice, however, accreditation in Pakistan often focuses heavily on inputs: buildings, equipment, teacher numbers and enrolment. The teaching process and learning outcomes receive less attention. This is why the school in the opening situation could pass inspection while its actual teaching remained weak.

The National Education Policy 2009 treats quality as involving teachers, curriculum, textbooks, assessment, learning environments and relevance to employment (paragraph 104, p. 33). Its higher education chapter calls for internal and external evaluation and transparent programme and university accreditation (paragraph 137, p. 47). The school scenario illustrates why instructional evidence matters; it is not evidence that this university accreditation provision applies to schools.

### Standardization of curricula

<Glossary term="Standardization" /> in education means establishing common standards for what students are expected to learn and demonstrate at each developmental stage. The National Curriculum Council's National Curriculum of Pakistan FAQ describes a core curriculum as minimum learning expectations, allowing schools to teach additional content. It also describes work with provinces according to their contextual needs, including teacher development with Sindh (NCC, n.d., accessed 3 November 2026). Shared expectations should therefore not be confused with identical implementation in every province. This is a dated description of the framework, not a claim that it is the latest reform.

Standardization serves several purposes. It aims to give students in different locations comparable core learning expectations where those standards are adopted. It makes examination and assessment more consistent. It also provides a common framework for teacher training and textbook development. When the curriculum is standardized, teacher education programmes know what their trainees will need to teach, and textbook publishers know what content to include.

But standardization does not mean every school teaches in the same way. A standard curriculum sets out what to teach, not how to teach it. Teachers still need to adapt their methods to their students' needs, their local context and the resources available. The misconception that standardization removes teacher judgment is one that this topic addresses below.

### Teacher training and professional development

Teachers are at the centre of quality assurance. No curriculum, however well designed, improves learning without a teacher who understands it and can teach it effectively. Pakistan's teacher training system includes both pre-service training (before a person enters teaching) and in-service training (during their career).

Pre-service training is provided through teacher education colleges and universities. The Bachelor of Education (B.Ed.) and Associate Degree in Education (ADE) became standard professional credentials. Under the National Education Policy 2009 (Section 6.1, Policy Action 1, p. 33), elementary teachers were to hold a bachelor degree with a B.Ed.; secondary and higher secondary teachers were to hold a master degree with a B.Ed. by 2018. Existing PTC and CT holders were encouraged to upgrade. The policy allowed exceptions in less developed areas lacking qualified teachers, with D.Ed. as an interim qualification. These were policy targets, not evidence of universal implementation (Government of Pakistan, 2009).

In-service training is meant to update teachers' skills and knowledge throughout their career. Provincial governments run training programmes through teacher training institutes and district training centres. The National Professional Standards for Teachers, developed in 2009, provide a framework for what teachers should know and be able to do at different stages of their career.

A persistent challenge is that in-service training in Pakistan is often sporadic, short and disconnected from teachers' actual classroom needs. Teachers attend a workshop, receive a certificate, and return to the same conditions. Without follow-up, support and accountability, training alone does not change classroom practice. Quality assurance in teacher development requires not just training events but a continuous cycle of learning, practice, feedback and improvement.

**Common misconception.** Many people assume that accreditation is only about infrastructure, that standardization means every school teaches the same way, or that teacher training is a one-time event. None of these is accurate. Accreditation, done well, examines teaching and learning, not just buildings. Standardization sets common content standards but leaves teaching methods to the teacher's professional judgment. And effective teacher development is continuous, not a single workshop. When these misconceptions go uncorrected, quality assurance efforts focus on the wrong things and real improvement does not happen.

## Activity: Mapping quality assurance in your district

Work in small groups of three or four. About 25 minutes.

1. On a large sheet, draw three columns: Accreditation, Standardization, Teacher Training.
2. For each column, list the mechanisms that exist in your district. Who conducts accreditation or inspection? What standardized assessments are used? What teacher training programmes are available?
3. Beside each mechanism, note one strength and one weakness you have observed or heard about.
4. As a group, identify one change that would most improve quality in your district. Write a short justification (two or three sentences) for your choice.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* In your own words, what is the difference between accreditation and inspection?
2. *(Understand)* List two reasons why standardization of the curriculum matters in Pakistan.
3. *(Apply)* A school has good buildings and high enrolment, but its students perform poorly on standardized tests. Using the quality assurance cycle, explain what step might have been missed.

## Summary

Quality assurance in Pakistan operates through three main mechanisms. Accreditation judges whether institutions meet defined standards, though it often focuses on inputs rather than learning outcomes. Standardization sets common content expectations across schools and provinces, while leaving teaching methods to teachers' professional judgment. Teacher training and professional development are meant to ensure that teachers can deliver the curriculum effectively, but in-service training is often too sporadic to produce lasting change. All three mechanisms must work together for quality to improve across the system.
