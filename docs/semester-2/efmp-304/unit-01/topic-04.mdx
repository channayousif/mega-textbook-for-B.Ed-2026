## A real classroom situation

A teacher training college in Punjab sets this assignment: "Standardised testing improves the quality
of education." Trainees must write a short response. One pupil writes, "I disagree." Another writes,
"Standardised testing improves education because it lets the government compare schools and direct
resources where they are most needed." A third writes, "Standardised testing is one form of
assessment." The tutor wants to mark not just whether the trainees agree, but whether they can
reason - and that means knowing which of the three is an argument.

## Explanation

### Statements

A <Glossary term="Statement" /> is a sentence that claims something is (or is not) the case - something
that can, at least in principle, be true or false. "The school has 400 pupils." "Monday is the first
day of the week." Not every sentence is a statement: a question ("May I come in?") or an exclamation
("Well done!") makes no claim about what is true, so it is not a statement in the logical sense.

### Claims

A <Glossary term="Claim" /> is a statement that someone puts forward as true, often in the face of
possible disagreement. In practice the words "statement" and "claim" overlap a great deal. The useful
difference is emphasis: calling something a *claim* signals that it needs support. When a colleague
says "homework always improves learning", treating that as a claim means asking "what is the
evidence?" rather than just accepting it.

### Issues

An <Glossary term="Issue" /> is a question that invites more than one reasonable answer - something
people can disagree about. "Should homework be set in grade 1?" is an issue. "How many pupils are
in this class?" is not, because it has a single factual answer. Critical thinking spends its time on
issues, because they are the questions that need reasoning rather than just looking something up.

### Arguments

An <Glossary term="Argument" /> is the unit this whole topic has been building towards. An argument
is **a claim plus at least one reason offered to support it**. The claim is the *conclusion*; the
reasons are the *premises*. This is the meaning of "argument" in critical thinking, and it is not the
everyday meaning of "quarrel". A calm, well-supported case is an argument in the logical sense.

How do you tell an argument from a mere claim? Use a short decision path:

1. Does the text assert something is true? If not, it is not an argument.
2. Is a reason given for the assertion? If there is only a claim, it is a mere claim.
3. Is the reason *meant to support* the claim, or only to explain it? If the text says "X because Y"
   and Y is offered as evidence for X, that is an argument. If Y only explains *how* X came to be,
   that is an explanation, not an argument.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees arrive believing "a claim and an argument are the same
thing". The difference matters because an argument gives you more to judge. A claim alone ("streaming
is unfair") asks only whether you agree. An argument ("streaming is unfair because it fixes labels
too early and the evidence shows pupils move between levels") lets you ask whether the reasons
actually support the conclusion - and that is where critical thinking gets to work.

## Activity: Argument or not?

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

For each of the three short reasoning below, decide whether it is an *argument*, a *mere claim*, or
an *explanation*. Write one sentence each, naming the conclusion and the reason where they exist.

1. "Attendance is poor on Mondays because many pupils travel back from their villages on Monday morning."
2. "The school should start the week on Tuesday."
3. "Practical work helps pupils understand science, because it lets them see the idea rather than only hearing it."

For the two that are arguments, state the conclusion and the reason in your own words.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the four basic units of reasoning and state which one a critical thinker spends most time on.
2. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between a mere claim and an argument in one or two sentences.
3. *(Apply)* A colleague says, "These pupils cannot read Urdu because the textbook is in English." Is that reasoning an argument, a mere claim, or an explanation? Give one reason for your answer.

## Summary

The basic units of reasoning are statements, claims, issues and arguments. An argument - a claim plus
at least one supporting reason - is the unit critical thinking evaluates. Telling an argument from a
mere claim or an explanation lets you ask the question that matters: do the reasons actually
support the conclusion?

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can distinguish a statement, a claim, an issue and an argument.
- [ ] I can use a decision path to decide whether a piece of text is an argument.
- [ ] I can explain why an argument gives a critical thinker more to evaluate than a claim.

## Try this at your practicum school

Take one paragraph from a textbook you are using or from a notice in the staffroom. Identify whether
it contains an argument, a mere claim, or an explanation. If it is an argument, write down the
conclusion and the reason in your own words.

## Summative task

Here is a short piece of reasoning: **"Morning assembly should be shortened. The first period after
assembly always loses ten minutes, and the term timetable is already too full to spare them."** Write
a short paragraph (80-120 words) that (a) states whether this is an argument, a claim or an
explanation, (b) gives one reason for your classification, and (c) names the conclusion and the
reason if it is an argument.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Classification* | 0-2: correctly classifies and gives a valid reason / correct classification but weak reason / wrong. |
| *Use of scenario evidence* | 0-3: cites specific words from the text to justify the classification / vague / none. (**Analyze**: ties the classification to the actual reasoning in the scenario.) |
| *Clarity* | 0:2: paragraph coherent and within word band / unclear or over length. |
