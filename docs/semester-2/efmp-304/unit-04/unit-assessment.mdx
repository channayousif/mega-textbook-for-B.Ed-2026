## Unit summary

This unit distinguished three terms that are often used as synonyms and showed that each names a larger
thing than the one before it.

- **Topic 4.1** separated reflective thinking (stepping back from an experience), the reflective
  process (that thinking made into a sequence) and reflective practice (the process made habitual).
  Each layer contains the one before and adds something: structure, then sustainability.
- **Topic 4.2** examined the claims made for reflection. The evidence is strongest for better
  instructional decisions and weakest for direct outcome claims like "results will improve". The most
  defensible claim is also the most uncomfortable: experience without reflection does not produce
  learning.
- **Topic 4.3** walked the reflective process end to end: disrupted expectation, description,
  analysis against evidence, alternatives, decision and trial, re-examination. The stage most often
  skipped is analysis, and skipping it means the decision rests on an assumption rather than a finding.
- **Topic 4.4** named three levels of reflection - technical, contextual, dialectical - and showed
  that each requires different activities. A private journal mostly produces technical reflection;
  the higher levels require other people or theory.

Three habits should survive this unit. First, name the layer: "I noticed something" is not the same
as "I worked through it" or "I have made this a habit". Second, slow down at the analysis stage,
because that is where most reflection fails. Third, build at least one activity into your routine
that takes you beyond the technical level, because a teacher who only refines techniques never
questions whether the techniques are the right ones.

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

Choose the single best answer for each question.

1. *(Remember)* Reflective thinking is best described as:
   - A) The habit of questioning every teaching decision over a career
   - B) The act of stepping back from an experience with the intention of learning from it
   - C) The sequence of description, analysis, alternatives and decision
   - D) A journal kept by a teacher after every lesson

2. *(Understand)* What does the reflective process add to reflective thinking?
   - A) A career-long habit
   - B) A sequence of stages that turns an insight into a learning cycle
   - C) An emotional response to a lesson
   - D) A record of what happened

3. *(Apply)* A teacher writes, "The group work was noisy. I will use a timer next time." This account
   best reaches which layer?
   - A) Reflective practice
   - B) The reflective process
   - C) Reflective thinking
   - D) Reflective journal writing

4. *(Remember)* The stage of the reflective process most often skipped is:
   - A) Description
   - B) Analysis against evidence
   - C) The disrupted expectation
   - D) Re-examination

5. *(Apply)* Which claim about reflection is best supported by evidence?
   - A) Reflection raises pupils' examination results within one term
   - B) Reflection improves the quality of instructional decisions
   - C) Reflection eliminates classroom behaviour problems
   - D) Reflection makes teachers more popular with parents

6. *(Understand)* "Experience alone does not produce learning" means that:
   - A) Experience is useless without a qualification
   - B) A process is required to turn experience into learning
   - C) Teachers should not rely on their own experience
   - D) Learning only happens in training workshops

7. *(Apply)* A teacher writes, "I have been assuming that quiet pupils are content. Three of my
   quietest pupils failed the test this week." This is best described as which level of reflection?
   - A) Technical
   - B) Contextual
   - C) Dialectical
   - D) Not a reflection

8. *(Remember)* Technical reflection asks:
   - A) What beliefs are shaping what I see as normal?
   - B) Did the technique work, and how can I make it work better?
   - C) What is going on around this technique that I have not been seeing?
   - D) Whose interests are served by the way I organise my classroom?

9. *(Understand)* A private journal mostly produces technical reflection because:
   - A) Teachers do not know how to write at higher levels
   - B) The higher levels require other people or theory to challenge what the teacher cannot see alone
   - C) Journals are too short for deeper reflection
   - D) Technical reflection is the only level that uses writing

10. *(Apply)* A teacher writes, "Next time I will demonstrate the setup before the lesson and have the
    laboratory assistant check the tube angle." This statement best shows which stage of the reflective
    process?
    - A) Description
    - B) Analysis
    - C) Decision and trial
    - D) The disrupted expectation

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

1. *(Understand)* In two sentences, state the difference between reflective thinking and reflective practice.

2. *(Understand)* Explain in two or three sentences why the analysis stage must come before the alternatives stage in the reflective process.

3. *(Apply)* A teacher says, "The pupils were confused. I will explain it again." Using the three-layer model from Topic 4.1, name which layer this reaches and what the next layer would add.

4. *(Apply)* A workshop facilitator claims, "Reflective journals improve teaching within one term." Using Topic 4.2, give one reason this claim is overstated and one reason it is not worthless.

5. *(Understand)* Name two differences between technical reflection and contextual reflection, using the questions each asks.

6. *(Apply)* Classify this account as technical, contextual or dialectical reflection and justify in one sentence: "I have been calling on the same four pupils all term. The others have stopped putting their hands up. I think I created that."

7. *(Analyze)* A teacher writes, "The demonstration failed because the apparatus was old." Using Topic 4.3, name which stage of the process this is, say whether it is adequate for that stage, and what Stage 3 would add.

8. *(Apply)* Name one activity that promotes contextual reflection and one that promotes dialectical reflection, with a reason for each.

9. *(Understand)* In two sentences, explain the difference between "a teacher with fifteen years of experience" and "a teacher who has learned from fifteen years of experience."

10. *(Analyze)* Take this account: "I set a quiz. Half the class failed. I will make the next quiz easier." Name the reflective level, say what the next level would ask, and rewrite the last sentence at the next level.

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. *(Analyze)* Here is a teacher's journal entry:

    > "The group discussion did not work. Only three pupils spoke. I will try a different grouping
    > strategy next time."

    In 150 to 200 words, name the reflective layer and level this entry reaches, say what it omits at the analysis stage, and rewrite the entry so it reaches the analysis stage with at least one testable hypothesis.

2. *(Evaluate)* A head teacher asks all teachers to keep a reflective journal and writes: "This will
   raise results by the end of the term." In 200 to 250 words, evaluate this claim against the evidence
   from Topic 4.2, name the part that is most defensible, and say what condition would have to be met
   for the claim to be even partly true.

3. *(Analyze)* A teacher writes:

    > "I praised Ali for his answer. The rest of the class went quiet."

    In 200 to 250 words, take this incident through the technical, contextual and dialectical levels of
   reflection (Topic 4.4). For each level, write two or three sentences showing the question it asks
   and the kind of change it could produce.

4. *(Evaluate)* Two teachers reflect on the same problem: pupils are not completing homework. Teacher
   A writes: "I will set shorter homework." Teacher B writes: "I have been assuming that pupils have
   a quiet place to work at home. I need to find out whether that is true before I decide what to
   change." In 200 to 250 words, compare the two reflections by layer and level, say which is more
   likely to lead to a lasting change, and justify your judgement from the content of each reflection.

5. *(Create)* **Integrative.** Choose one lesson you have taught recently that did not go as planned.
   In 250 to 300 words, produce a reflective account that (a) distinguishes description from analysis
   by writing both, (b) gives at least two testable hypotheses at the analysis stage, (c) names the
   reflective level your account reaches, and (d) says what activity would help you reach the next
   level and why.

## Answers and marking guidance

This section is for self-study. Work each question before reading it.

### MCQ answer key

1. B - reflective thinking is the act of stepping back with the intention of learning; A describes reflective practice, C describes the process.
2. B - the process adds a sequence of stages; A describes reflective practice.
3. C - the teacher noticed a problem and formed a resolution; there is no analysis or sequence, so it stops at thinking.
4. B - analysis is the stage teachers skip most often, moving from description to decision without testing their assumptions.
5. B - better instructional decisions is the best-supported claim; A, C and D are asserted rather than evidenced.
6. B - a process is required to turn experience into learning; the claim is not that experience is useless but that it is insufficient.
7. B - the teacher is broadening the lens from the technique (quiet = good) to the context (quiet pupils may be invisible); that is contextual reflection.
8. B - technical reflection asks whether the technique worked and how to improve it; A and D are dialectical, C is contextual.
9. B - the higher levels require other people or theory to challenge what the teacher cannot see alone; a private journal lacks that challenge.
10. C - the teacher has chosen a specific alternative to try; this is the decision-and-trial stage.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. Model: reflective thinking is the act of stepping back from an experience to learn from it; reflective practice is the reflective process made habitual across a career. **Marks (3):** 1 - defines reflective thinking; 1 - defines reflective practice; 1 - contrasts them by scope (single act vs career-long habit).

2. Model: analysis produces an evidence-based finding about why something happened; alternatives are generated from that finding. Without analysis, alternatives rest on assumptions. Example: "the demonstration failed because the tube was blocked" (analysis) leads to "check the tube angle before the lesson" (alternative); "the demonstration failed because the apparatus was old" (assumption) leads to "buy new apparatus" (alternative). **Marks (3):** 1 - states that analysis produces findings; 1 - states that alternatives come from findings; 1 - a school example showing the difference.

3. Model: reflective thinking. The teacher noticed a problem and formed a resolution. The next layer (the process) would add: what exactly did "confused" look like, what was the cause, what alternatives exist besides repeating the explanation, and how to know the alternative worked. **Marks (3):** 1 - names the layer correctly; 1 - says what the process adds; 1 - a specific addition tied to the example.

4. Model: it is overstated because the evidence for direct outcome claims (results, behaviour) is thin; the evidence is strongest for better decision-making. It is not worthless because reflection does improve instructional decisions, and better decisions may eventually improve results. **Marks (3):** 1 - names the overstated part with a reason; 1 - names the defensible part with a reason; 1 - distinguishes the two.

5. Model: technical reflection asks whether the technique worked and how to improve it; contextual reflection asks what is going on around the technique that has not been seen. Technical produces better execution; contextual produces a changed understanding of the situation. **Marks (3):** 1 - states the technical question; 1 - states the contextual question; 1 - states what each can produce.

6. Model: contextual. The teacher has broadened from the technique (calling on pupils) to the context (who gets called on and what effect it has). **Marks (2):** 1 - correct level; 1 - a reason tied to the definition.

7. Model: it is Stage 2 (description/assumption) masquerading as Stage 3. It is not adequate analysis because it has not been tested against evidence. Stage 3 would ask: what evidence shows the apparatus was old? Was it the tubing, the cork, or the chemical? Each hypothesis is testable. **Marks (3):** 1 - identifies the stage; 1 - says it is not adequate analysis; 1 - names what Stage 3 would add.

8. Model: contextual - a critical-friend conversation, because a trusted colleague asks "what else might be going on?" Dialectical - action research or reading theory, because these challenge the teacher's assumptions about what is normal. **Marks (3):** 1 - one activity for contextual with a reason; 1 - one activity for dialectical with a reason; 1 - the reason matches the level's definition.

9. Model: a teacher with fifteen years of experience has repeated the process fifteen times. A teacher who has learned from those years has reflected on the experience and changed practice as a result. The difference is the reflection, not the years. **Marks (2):** 1 - defines the difference; 1 - names reflection as the mechanism.

10. Model: technical. The teacher asks whether the technique (the quiz) worked and tweaks it. The next level (contextual) would ask: what is going on around the quiz that I am not seeing? Rewrite: "I have been assuming that failure means the quiz was too hard. I need to look at what the wrong answers show before I decide whether to make it easier." **Marks (4):** 1 - names the level; 1 - names the next level's question; 1 - a rewrite that reaches the next level; 1 - the rewrite is tied to the specific incident.

### ERQ rubrics

1. Analytic rubric - *Layer naming* (0-2: correct with evidence / a general reason / wrong) · *Level naming* (0-2: correct with evidence / wrong) · *What the analysis stage would add* (0-2: names a testable hypothesis tied to the incident / a general suggestion / none) · *Rewrite quality* (0-2: reaches the analysis stage with a hypothesis / stays at the same level / none) · *Clarity* (0-2).

2. Analytic rubric - *Claim evaluation* (0-2: evaluates against the evidence from Topic 4.2 with named distinctions / a general evaluation / wrong) · *Most defensible part* (0-2: names better decision-making with a reason / a general reason / wrong) · *Condition* (0-3: names a condition - quality of reflection, follow-through, time scale - that would make the claim partly true (**Evaluate*: the condition is specific and evidence-based) / a general condition / none) · *Clarity* (0-2).

3. Analytic rubric - *Technical level* (0-2: correctly identifies the technical question and the kind of change it produces / a general description / wrong) · *Contextual level* (0-2: correctly identifies the contextual question and what it adds / wrong) · *Dialectical level* (0-2: correctly identifies the dialectical question and what it adds / wrong) · *Progression across levels* (0-2: each level is visibly deeper than the last / levels repeat each other / no progression) · *Clarity* (0-2).

4. Analytic rubric - *Layer and level comparison* (0-2: both correctly identified by layer and level / one correct / neither) · *Which is more likely to last* (0-2: names Teacher B with a reason tied to the content of both reflections / a general reason / wrong) · *Justification* (0-3: justifies from the specific wording of each reflection, showing why analysis-before-decision matters (**Evaluate*: the comparison turns on the actual content, not on a general preference for longer answers) / general reasons / a verdict asserted) · *Clarity* (0-2).

5. Analytic rubric - *Description vs analysis distinguished* (0-2: both written and visibly different / one written / neither) · *Testable hypotheses* (0-2: at least two, tied to the specific lesson / one / none) · *Reflective level named* (0-2: correct with evidence / wrong) · *Activity for next level* (0-2: named with a reason tied to the level's definition / a general suggestion / none) · *Clarity* (0-2).
