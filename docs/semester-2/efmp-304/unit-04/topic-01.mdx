## A real classroom situation

Miss Hina teaches grade 7 in a government girls' high school in Larkana. After a lesson on fractions
that did not go well, she writes in her notebook: "The pupils were confused. I should have used
concrete objects." She feels she has reflected. Her colleague, Miss Rabia, reads the same sentence
and asks, "Why were they confused? What exactly did you try? What would you do differently next time,
and why would that work better?" Miss Hina has no answers yet. She has noticed a problem. She has not
yet reflected on it.

<Figure id="fig-U4-1" kind="table" src="/img/figures/efmp-304/unit-04/fig-U4-1.svg" alt="Table comparing reflective thinking, the reflective process and reflective practice across four rows: what each names, its scope in time, what it adds to the previous layer, and a one-line marker of each." width={820} height={470} />

That gap between noticing and reflecting is the subject of this topic. The three words the course
guide uses - reflective thinking, reflective process, reflective practice - are not synonyms. Each one
names a larger thing than the one before, and the difference between them is what separates a teacher
who occasionally thinks about their lessons from one whose reflection is a professional habit.

## Explanation

### Reflective thinking

<Glossary term="Reflective thinking" /> is the smallest of the three. It is the act of stepping back
from an experience and thinking about it with the intention of learning from it. Jasper (2003)
describes it as "purposeful thinking", which is the key word: the thinking is directed at something
specific, not drifting. A teacher who finishes a lesson and thinks, "That group work was chaos - I
need to give clearer instructions next time" is doing reflective thinking. The scope is a single
moment, and the output is a single insight.

Reflective thinking is necessary but not sufficient. A teacher can think reflectively after every
lesson and still never change anything, because thinking about a problem is not the same as working
through it.

### The reflective process

The <Glossary term="Reflective process" /> is reflective thinking made into a sequence. It is not
just "I noticed something" but "I noticed something, I described it, I analysed it against evidence,
I considered alternatives, I decided what to try, and I will check whether it worked." That sequence
is the process, and it is what turns a one-off insight into a learning cycle.

Osterman and Kottkamp (2004) treat the process as the core mechanism of professional development: the
experience alone does not teach, but the experience taken through the process does. The process has a
beginning (an incident that disrupted your expectation), a middle (description, analysis, alternatives)
and an end (a decision to try something different and a way to check whether it worked). Miss Hina's
notebook entry had a beginning. It had no middle and no end. That is why it stopped at noticing.

### Reflective practice

<Glossary term="Reflective Practice" /> is the reflective process made habitual across a career. It is
not something you do after a bad lesson; it is the professional stance that you bring to every lesson,
whether it went well or badly. Brookfield (2017) describes it as "the sustained habit of interrogating
your own assumptions", which is a larger claim than either of the two layers beneath it. A teacher in
reflective practice does not wait for a crisis. They assume that every teaching decision rests on
assumptions worth examining, and they have a regular process for examining them.

<Figure id="fig-U4-2" kind="diagram" src="/img/figures/efmp-304/unit-04/fig-U4-2.svg" alt="Three concentric layers: reflective thinking at the centre, the reflective process as the middle ring, and reflective practice as the outer ring, with what is added at each layer labelled." width={780} height={470} />

**Common misconception: "reflection means writing about your feelings."** It does not. Writing about
your feelings is one possible input to reflection, but reflection is the *analysis* of an experience
against evidence, not the expression of emotion about it. A journal entry that says "I felt frustrated
today" is not a reflection. One that says "I felt frustrated because the pupils could not transfer the
skill to a new problem, which suggests I taught the procedure without the concept" is. The difference
is the evidence, not the emotion.

**Common misconception: "reflecting is the same as reviewing a lesson."** A review is a summary of
what happened. A reflection is an analysis of *why* it happened and *what to do about it*. Most
post-lesson notes in Pakistani schools are reviews: "Completed exercise 3. Four pupils still confused.
Will revise tomorrow." That is useful record-keeping. It is not reflection, because it does not ask
*why* the four pupils were confused or *what specifically* will be different about tomorrow's
revision.

### A worked example before you try it

Here is the move performed once, slowly, on a teacher's own account.

> "I explained the water cycle three times and they still could not label the diagram."

**Step 1 - which layer is this?** It is a noticing. The teacher has identified a gap between intention
and outcome. This is reflective thinking, and it is a legitimate start.

**Step 2 - what would the process add?** The process would ask: what exactly did I do in those three
explanations? Were they the same explanation repeated, or three different approaches? What did the
pupils' wrong labels tell me - did they confuse evaporation with condensation, or did they not
understand the diagram's conventions? What alternative explanation could I try? How will I know
whether the alternative worked?

**Step 3 - what would practice add?** Practice would ask the deeper question: what assumption was I
making that led me to explain three times in the first place? Perhaps I assumed that repetition is the
right response to confusion. That assumption is worth examining, because it may be wrong.

Now you try the same three steps in the activity.

## Activity: Which layer is this?

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Each pair takes the five teacher statements below. For each one, decide together whether it shows
reflective thinking, the reflective process, or reflective practice. Write one sentence naming the
evidence for your decision.

1. "I asked the class to discuss in groups, but only two groups actually discussed. I will try it again with a clearer task."
2. "I asked the class to discuss in groups, but only two groups actually discussed. I think the task was too open. Next time I will give each group a specific question and a time limit, and I will check whether the quality of discussion improves."
3. "Every Friday I write down one thing that surprised me in the week's teaching, what I think caused it, and what I will try differently. I have been doing this since September."
4. "The pupils were quiet today. I do not know why."
5. "The pupils were quiet today. I think it was because I asked closed questions all period. Tomorrow I will open with a question that has more than one right answer and compare the response."

Then swap with another pair. Where you disagree, say which of the three definitions settles it.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the three terms this topic distinguishes, and state the scope of each in time.
2. *(Understand)* In two or three sentences, explain what the reflective process adds to reflective thinking. Use a school example.
3. *(Apply)* A colleague says, "I reflect every day - I think about my lessons on the way home." Using the three-layer model, explain in two sentences what that probably is and what it probably is not.
4. *(Apply)* Take the statement "I taught the causes of the 1857 war and the pupils were bored. I will use pictures next time." Name which layer it reaches and what the next layer would add.

## Summary

Reflective thinking is the act of stepping back from an experience to learn from it. The reflective
process is that thinking made into a sequence: description, analysis, alternatives, decision, trial.
Reflective practice is the process made habitual across a career. Each layer contains the one before it
and adds something: the process adds structure to thinking, and practice adds sustainability to the
process. Most teachers who say "I already reflect" are doing the first layer and mistaking it for the
third.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can state the difference between reflective thinking, the reflective process and reflective practice.
- [ ] I can explain what each layer adds to the one before it.
- [ ] I can take a teacher's account and name which layer it reaches, with evidence from the text.
- [ ] I can explain why an emotional account is not necessarily a reflection.

## Try this at your practicum school

Take one lesson you taught this week. Write two sentences about it. The first sentence should be a
review (what happened). The second should be a reflection (why it happened and what you would do
differently, with a reason). Bring both sentences to your tutorial and be ready to explain the
difference.

## Summative task

Here are three teacher statements about the same lesson:

> **A.** "The group activity took too long and we did not finish the exercise."
>
> **B.** "The group activity took longer than I planned because I gave one set of materials for four groups. Next time I will prepare one set per group and time each phase."
>
> **C.** "I have started assuming that group work saves time because pupils teach each other. This lesson showed me it does not - it costs time I had not budgeted for. I need to decide when the learning gain is worth the time cost and when it is not."

Write 150 to 200 words that (a) names which layer each statement reaches, (b) explains what each
statement does that the one below it does not, and (c) says which statement is most likely to lead to
a lasting change in practice, with a reason.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Layer naming* | 0-2: all three correctly named with evidence / one or two correct / none. |
| *What each adds* | 0-3: explains what B does that A does not and what C does that B does not, tied to the definitions (**Analyze**: the explanation turns on the specific content of each statement, not on general plausibility) / a partial explanation / none. |
| *Lasting change* | 0-2: names C and justifies with a reason tied to the practice layer / a general reason / wrong choice. |
| *Clarity* | 0-1: within the word band and organised under the three parts / not. |

## Further reading

Jasper, M. (2003). *Beginning reflective practice*. Nelson Thornes. Chapter 1 introduces reflective thinking as purposeful thinking and distinguishes it from casual review.

Osterman, K. F., & Kottkamp, R. B. (2004). *Reflective practice for educators: Professional development to improve student learning* (2nd ed.). Corwin Press. Chapter 2 sets out the reflective process as a learning cycle.

Brookfield, S. D. (2017). *Becoming a critically reflective teacher* (2nd ed.). Jossey-Bass. Chapter 1 frames reflective practice as a sustained habit across a career.
