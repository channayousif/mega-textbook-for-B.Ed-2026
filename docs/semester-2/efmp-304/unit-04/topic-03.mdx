## A real classroom situation

Miss Naima teaches chemistry to grade 9 at a government girls' high school in Nawabshah. She planned
a demonstration: heating copper carbonate and asking pupils to observe the colour change. The gas
delivery tube was blocked, the cork blew off, and the demonstration failed. The pupils laughed. Miss
Naima finished the period by writing the reaction on the board and telling them to memorise it.

That evening she writes in her notebook: "Demonstration failed. Apparatus faulty. Will do it again
next week." She has recorded what happened. She has not yet reflected on it.

This topic takes you through the process Miss Naima has not yet used. It is not complicated, but it
has stages, and each stage produces something the next one needs. Skip a stage and the later stages
rest on nothing.

## Explanation

### The stages of the reflective process

The reflective process has six stages. They are sequential, but they are not rigid: a teacher in
practice may return to an earlier stage when new evidence arrives. For learning the process, though,
work through them in order.

**Stage 1 - The disrupted expectation.** Every reflection starts with something that did not go as
planned. It does not have to be a disaster. A lesson that worked *better* than expected is equally
worth reflecting on, because there is something to learn from success as well as from failure. Miss
Naima's disrupted expectation was straightforward: the demonstration was supposed to produce a clear
observation. It produced a mess.

**Stage 2 - Description.** Write down what happened, as specifically as you can, without judgement.
"The demonstration failed" is not a description; it is a verdict. A description says: "The tube was
inserted into the cork, the Bunsen was lit, no gas came out of the end of the tube, the cork blew off
after approximately thirty seconds." The difference matters because a verdict cannot be analysed. You
can only analyse a description.

**Stage 3 - Analysis against evidence.** This is the stage most teachers skip, and it is the one that
makes reflection reflection rather than complaint. Analysis asks: *why* did it happen, and it
answers with evidence, not assumption. Miss Naima could assume "the apparatus was faulty", which is
what she wrote. But analysis would check: was the tube actually blocked, or was it inserted at the
wrong angle? Was the cork the right size? Was the copper carbonate damp? Each of these is a testable
hypothesis, and checking them is what turns "the apparatus was faulty" into a finding.

Brookfield (2017) calls this the stage of "hunting for assumptions". The first explanation is almost
always an assumption, and the work of analysis is to test it against the evidence you have.

**Stage 4 - Alternatives.** Once you have an evidence-based analysis, you can generate alternatives
that are also evidence-based. Miss Naima's assumption ("apparatus was faulty") leads to one
alternative: replace the apparatus. Her analysis ("the tube was inserted at the wrong angle, which
I have not demonstrated before") leads to a different one: demonstrate the setup *before* the
lesson, then have a pupil check it. Both are alternatives, but the second one is grounded in the
finding rather than the assumption, and it is the one more likely to work.

**Stage 5 - Decision and trial.** Choose one alternative and try it. This is where reflection becomes
action. A reflection that stops at analysis is an essay. A reflection that leads to a trial is
professional development. The trial should be specific enough to test: "I will demonstrate the setup
before the lesson on Thursday and have the laboratory assistant check the tube angle before I begin."

**Stage 6 - Re-examination.** After the trial, look again. Did it work? If yes, what have you learned
that applies to other lessons? If not, what does the failure tell you? This stage closes the loop and
opens the next cycle. It is also the stage that makes reflection a *practice* rather than a
one-off: the re-examination becomes the disrupted expectation for the next cycle.

**Common misconception: "the cycle has to be completed in one sitting."** It does not. Stage 1 and 2
often happen on the same day as the incident. Stage 3 may take longer if you need to check a fact or
ask a colleague. Stage 5 happens in the next lesson. The whole cycle may take a week. What matters is
not the speed but that no stage is skipped.

### A worked example before you try it

Here is the process performed once, slowly, on a different lesson.

> Mr Tariq's grade 6 geography lesson on map reading. He gave each group a map and asked them to
> find the distance between two cities. Three of the five groups got the wrong answer.

**Stage 1 - disrupted expectation.** Groups using the same map should get the same answer. Three did
not.

**Stage 2 - description.** Three groups measured the straight-line distance. Two groups measured
along the road. The map had both a straight-line scale and a road-distance symbol in the key, but
the key was on the back of the sheet.

**Stage 3 - analysis.** The error was not random. It was systematic: groups that looked at the key
got the right answer; groups that did not used the straight-line scale for a road-distance
question. The cause was the placement of the key, not the pupils' ability.

**Stage 4 - alternatives.** (a) Tell every group to check the key first. (b) Attach a small copy of
the key to the front of each map. (c) Redesign the task to use straight-line distances only.

**Stage 5 - decision.** Mr Tariq chose (b) and tried it in the parallel section the next day.

**Stage 6 - re-examination.** All five groups got the right answer. The finding: key placement
matters more than instruction clarity for map tasks. This applies to every map-based exercise he
sets.

Now you try the same six stages in the activity.

## Activity: Walk the cycle

Work in pairs. About 25 minutes.

Take this incident:

> Mr Fawad's grade 10 English lesson. He asked pupils to write a paragraph in fifteen minutes.
> When he collected the work, six pupils had written fewer than three sentences. He had expected
> most to finish.

Walk the incident through all six stages. For Stage 3, write at least two testable hypotheses for
why six pupils wrote so little. For Stage 4, write one alternative for each hypothesis. For Stage 5,
choose one and describe what the trial would look like. For Stage 6, say what would count as evidence
that it worked.

Then swap with another pair. Where your Stage 3 hypotheses differ, say which one the evidence from
the description supports better.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the six stages of the reflective process in order.
2. *(Understand)* In two or three sentences, explain why the analysis stage has to come before the alternatives stage. Use a school example.
3. *(Apply)* A teacher writes, "The pupils were noisy. I will use stricter discipline." Using the six-stage model, name which stage was skipped and what it would have added.
4. *(Apply)* Take the statement "The demonstration did not work because the chemicals were old." Is this Stage 2 (description) or Stage 3 (analysis)? Justify your answer in one sentence, and say what would make it the other stage.

## Summary

The reflective process has six stages: disrupted expectation, description, analysis against evidence,
alternatives, decision and trial, and re-examination. Each stage produces what the next one needs.
The stage most often skipped is analysis: teachers move from description straight to a decision, and
the decision then rests on an assumption rather than a finding. The remedy is to slow down at Stage 3
and ask what evidence supports the explanation, the same way you would check a pupil's answer rather
than accepting it because it sounds right.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can name the six stages of the reflective process and state what each produces.
- [ ] I can distinguish a description from an analysis and explain why the distinction matters.
- [ ] I can apply the full process to a described lesson and reach Stage 3 with evidence-based hypotheses.
- [ ] I can say what would count as evidence that a reflective trial worked.

## Try this at your practicum school

Take one lesson this week that did not go as planned. Walk it through all six stages in writing. Do
not skip Stage 3: write at least two testable hypotheses and say what evidence would support each.
Bring the written cycle to your tutorial.

## Summative task

Here is a teacher's account of a lesson:

> "I asked the pupils to solve the problem on the board. No one could do it. I explained it again.
> They still could not. I gave up and told them to copy the solution. I was very frustrated."

Write 200 to 250 words that (a) takes this account through Stages 2 to 5 of the reflective process,
(b) produces at least two testable hypotheses for Stage 3, and (c) names one alternative for each
hypothesis with a reason tied to the evidence.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Process stages* | 0-2: Stages 2 to 5 clearly distinguished and each produces what the next needs / stages muddled or one missing / process not used. |
| *Testable hypotheses* | 0-3: two hypotheses that are genuinely testable and tied to evidence from the description (**Analyze**: the hypotheses turn on the specific content of the account, not on generic teaching problems) / one testable / neither testable. |
| *Alternatives tied to evidence* | 0-3: one alternative per hypothesis, each with a reason that links it to the hypothesis / alternatives without reasons / none. |
| *Clarity* | 0-2: within the word band and organised under the three parts / unclear or over length. |
