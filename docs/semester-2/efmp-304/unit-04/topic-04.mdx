## A real classroom situation

Two teachers at a private school in Hyderabad both keep reflective journals. Miss Seema writes about
her lessons every day. Her entries sound like this: "Today I used the timer for group work and it
went better. Next time I will give clearer instructions before the timer starts." Mr Aamir writes once
a week, but his entries sound different: "I have been assuming that quiet pupils are content pupils.
This week three of my quietest pupils failed the test. I think I have been teaching to the ones who
answer, and the quiet ones have been invisible. What does that say about how I distribute my attention?"

<Figure id="fig-U4-7" kind="table" src="/img/figures/efmp-304/unit-04/fig-U4-7.svg" alt="Table of the three types of reflection (technical, contextual, dialectical), one row each, with the question it asks, the kind of change it can produce, and a one-line example." width={820} height={470} />

Both teachers reflect. But they reflect at different levels, and the level matters for what the
reflection can change. Miss Seema's reflection improves her techniques. Mr Aamir's reflection
questions the assumption beneath his techniques. This topic names the levels and shows you which
activities produce each one.

## Explanation

### The levels of reflection

Taggart and Wilson (2005) describe a levels-of-reflection framework with three stages. The framework
is a ladder a teacher climbs on one issue, not a ranking of teachers: a single teacher may reflect
technically on Monday and dialectically on Wednesday, depending on the issue. The levels are:

**Level 1 - Technical reflection.** This asks: *did the technique work, and how can I make it work
better?* It is reflection on the effectiveness of a specific strategy against its own goal. Miss
Seema's entry is technical: the timer was the technique, "went better" is the judgement against its
goal, and "clearer instructions" is the refinement. Technical reflection produces better execution
of existing plans. It does not question the plan itself.

**Level 2 - Contextual reflection.** This asks: *what is going on around this technique that I have
not been seeing?* It broadens the lens from the technique to the context: the pupils, the
classroom dynamics, the assumptions about who is learning and who is not. Mr Aamir's entry is
contextual: he noticed that his attention distribution favoured one group of pupils, and that the
quiet ones were failing despite appearing fine. Contextual reflection can change a teacher's
understanding of the situation, and that changed understanding often leads to a different kind of
intervention - not "use the timer better" but "restructure who I call on".

**Level 3 - Dialectical reflection.** This asks: *what beliefs, values or power relations are
shaping what I see as normal?* It is the widest lens, and the one most teachers never reach on their
own. It asks about the system, not just the classroom: why do I assume that quiet is good? What does
my school's culture reward - obedience or inquiry? Whose interests are served by the way I organise
the room? Dialectical reflection can change a teacher's professional identity, not just their
technique.

**Common misconception: "the model with the most stages is the most thorough."** The levels are not
a staircase you climb once and stay on. They are lenses you bring to different issues. A teacher who
reflects dialectically about attendance policy but technically about their use of a textbook is not
inconsistent; they are using the right lens for each question. Taggart and Wilson (2005) are explicit
that the framework describes the *depth* of reflection on an issue, not the overall quality of a
teacher.

### Activities that produce each level

Not every reflective activity reaches every level. Matching the activity to the level is a design
choice, and most Pakistani teacher-training programmes default to the technical because it is the
easiest to structure.

<Figure id="fig-U4-8" kind="diagram" src="/img/figures/efmp-304/unit-04/fig-U4-8.svg" alt="Diagram showing activities arranged by what they require: a solo reflection journal (solitude) for technical level, peer discussion (a colleague) for contextual, action research with pupils (a class) for dialectical, and reading theory (a text) for all levels." width={820} height={470} />

**Activities that promote technical reflection:**
- A post-lesson review with a checklist: "Did the timing work? Did the materials arrive? Did the
  pupils complete the task?"
- Video review of your own teaching, focused on a specific technique.
- A supervisor's observation with feedback on a named strategy.

**Activities that promote contextual reflection:**
- A critical-friend conversation: a trusted colleague who asks "what else might be going on here?"
  rather than "here is what you should do."
- Pupil feedback, collected anonymously and read for patterns rather than complaints.
- A reflective journal that is reviewed by a peer, not just kept privately.

**Activities that promote dialectical reflection:**
- Action research: a systematic inquiry into your own practice that questions an assumption and
  gathers evidence from multiple sources.
- Reading theory (Brookfield, 2017, is explicit about this): encountering a framework that names
  something you had not named, and applying it to your own practice.
- The four-lens exercise (autobiographical, students' eyes, colleagues' perceptions, theory), which
  is Brookfield's own method for reaching this level.

The key point for your own practice is this: if you only keep a private journal, you will mostly
reflect technically. If you want to reach the higher levels, you need other people - a colleague to
challenge your reading, pupils to show you what you are not seeing, a text to name what you have
been assuming.

### A worked example before you try it

Here is the same incident reflected at all three levels.

> "I set a group task. Two groups finished early and were disruptive. Two groups did not finish."

**Technical reflection:** "Next time I will set a time limit and have an extension task ready for the
early finishers." This improves the technique. It does not ask *why* the groups differed.

**Contextual reflection:** "The two groups that finished early had a dominant member who did the work
while the others watched. The two groups that did not finish had no one who took the lead. The issue
is not time management; it is how roles are distributed within groups." This changes the diagnosis,
and therefore the intervention: assign roles rather than set a timer.

**Dialectical reflection:** "I assume that group work is inherently good because it promotes
cooperation. But in my classroom, it reproduces the existing social hierarchies: the confident
pupils lead and the quiet ones follow. What am I doing when I call this 'cooperation'?" This questions
the assumption beneath the technique, and it may lead to a fundamental redesign rather than a tweak.

Now you try the same three levels in the activity.

## Activity: Three lenses on one incident

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Take this incident:

> "I asked a question. Ali answered correctly. I praised him. The rest of the class went quiet."

Write three reflections on this incident, one at each level:
- **Technical:** what technique would you change and how?
- **Contextual:** what might be going on that the technical reading misses?
- **Dialectical:** what assumption about questioning, praise or participation might be shaping what happened?

Then swap with another pair. For each level, say whether the other pair's reflection genuinely
operates at that level or has slipped to a different one. Where it has slipped, name the evidence.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the three levels of reflection and state the question each asks.
2. *(Understand)* In two or three sentences, explain why a private journal tends to produce technical reflection rather than dialectical reflection.
3. *(Apply)* A teacher writes, "I will use a rubric so the pupils know what I expect." Name the level of this reflection and say what would move it to the next level.
4. *(Apply)* A head teacher tells a teacher, "Ask your pupils what they think of your teaching." Which level of reflection is this activity most likely to produce, and why?

## Summary

Reflection has three levels: technical (did the technique work?), contextual (what else is going on?)
and dialectical (what assumptions am I making?). Most private reflection stays at the technical level
because the higher levels require other people - a colleague, a pupil, a theory - to challenge what
the teacher cannot see alone. The activities that promote each level are different: a checklist
promotes technical reflection, a critical-friend conversation promotes contextual, and action research
or theory-reading promotes dialectical. A teacher who wants to grow beyond better techniques needs to
build at least one of these into their routine.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can name the three levels of reflection and the question each asks.
- [ ] I can distinguish a technical reflection from a contextual one, with evidence from the text.
- [ ] I can match at least three activities to the level each promotes.
- [ ] I can take one incident and write reflections at all three levels.

## Try this at your practicum school

This week, have a five-minute conversation with a colleague after one lesson. Do not ask "what should
I do?" Ask "what do you think is going on in my classroom that I might not be seeing?" Write down
their answer. Compare it with your own post-lesson note. Which level was your note? Which level was
their answer? Bring both to your tutorial.

## Summative task

Here is a teacher's written account:

> "I have started using pair work instead of individual work because the pupils were copying from each
> other. But now the pairs that have a strong pupil and a weak pupil are producing identical work, and
> the weak pupil is not learning - they are just writing what the strong pupil says. I need to pair
> pupils of similar ability."

Write 200 to 250 words that (a) names the level of reflection this account currently reaches, (b)
rewrites it at the next level up, and (c) names one activity that would help this teacher reach the
highest level on this issue, with a reason.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Level naming* | 0-2: correctly identifies the level with evidence from the text / a general reason / wrong. |
| *Rewrite at next level* | 0-3: a rewrite that genuinely reaches the next level and is tied to the specific content of the original (**Analyze**: the rewrite turns on what the next level's question would ask about this incident) / a rewrite that stays at the same level with different words / no real rewrite. |
| *Activity for highest level* | 0-3: names an activity that genuinely promotes dialectical reflection with a reason tied to the level's definition / a plausible activity without a reason / none. |
| *Clarity* | 0-2: within the word band and organised under the three parts / unclear or over length. |
