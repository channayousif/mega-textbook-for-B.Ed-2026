## A real classroom situation

A workshop facilitator in a teacher-training institute in Sukkur tells the participants: "If you
reflect on your teaching, your results will improve, your pupils will behave better, and you will
grow as a professional." The teachers nod. One of them, Mr Javed, thinks: "I have been teaching for
twelve years. I think about my lessons every evening. My results have not improved in the last five
years." He does not say this out loud, but he stops listening to the facilitator, because the claim
does not match his experience.

<Figure id="fig-U4-3" kind="concept-map" src="/img/figures/efmp-304/unit-04/fig-U4-3.svg" alt="Concept map showing what reflection is claimed to buy: better instructional decisions, greater adaptability, improved problem-solving and professional growth, each branch marked with the evidence status of the claim (strong, moderate, limited, asserted)." width={820} height={520} />

Mr Javed's objection is a fair one, and it is the subject of this topic. Reflection *is* claimed to
buy a great deal. Some of those claims are well supported. Some are not. A teacher who cannot tell the
difference is vulnerable to two errors: rejecting reflection because an overstated claim failed to
deliver, or accepting a weak programme because it promised everything. This topic gives you the
evidence to tell the difference.

## Explanation

### What reflection is claimed to buy

The four claims made most often about reflective practice are these:

1. **Better instructional decisions.** A teacher who reflects chooses teaching strategies with reasons
   rather than by habit.
2. **Greater adaptability.** A teacher who reflects adjusts when a lesson does not go as planned,
   rather than sticking to the plan regardless.
3. **Improved problem-solving.** A teacher who reflects diagnoses the cause of a classroom problem
   rather than treating its symptoms.
4. **Professional growth.** A teacher who reflects gets better at teaching over time, rather than
   repeating the same year indefinitely.

These are the right claims to examine, because if none of them holds, there is no professional reason
to reflect.

### What the evidence actually shows

The evidence is not uniform across the four claims. That is the point trainees most often miss.

**Better instructional decisions** is the best-supported claim. Osterman and Kottkamp (2004) argue
that reflection changes behaviour because it makes the *reason* for a decision visible: a teacher who
can say "I chose group work because the skill required peer explanation, not because the textbook
said so" is making an informed choice. The mechanism is sound, though the literature measures
self-reported decision quality more often than pupil outcomes directly.

**Greater adaptability** has moderate support. Reflection-on-action (thinking after the lesson) is
common and well described. Reflection-in-action (adjusting *during* the lesson) is harder to evidence:
it happens in the moment, is difficult to observe, and the scholarship - drawing originally on
Schön (1983) - describes it more often than it measures it. Taggart and Wilson (2005) list it as a
capacity that reflective practice develops, but the empirical base is thinner than for the other
claims.

**Improved problem-solving** is plausible but leans on the mechanism rather than the outcome. A
teacher who follows the reflective process (Topic 4.3) is more likely to find the cause of a problem,
because the process requires analysis against evidence rather than assumption. Whether that better
diagnosis then leads to a better solution is a further step the evidence tracks less carefully.

**Professional growth** is the claim that sounds most obvious and is hardest to pin down. The
strongest version of it is not "reflection makes you grow" but "experience without reflection does
not". This is the finding that matters for Pakistani teachers in particular, because the
alternative to reflection is not some other development activity. It is repetition.

**Common misconception: "experience automatically produces learning."** It does not. A teacher who
teaches for fifteen years has fifteen years of experience. A teacher who has *learned* from fifteen
years of experience has reflected on it. The difference is not in the years but in what was done with
them. This is not a new idea - it is the central argument of the experiential-learning tradition
(Boud, Keogh & Walker, 2013) - but it is the one most often ignored in practice. In a system where
seniority is valued over effectiveness, the claim that "fifteen years of teaching" and "one year of
teaching repeated fifteen times" are different things is a uncomfortable one. It is also true.

### What this means for you

The honest answer is not "reflection solves everything". The honest answer is: reflection is the best
available mechanism for turning experience into learning, but it has to be done well. A teacher who
writes "the lesson went badly, I will try harder" every day for five years has not reflected. A
teacher who takes one lesson through the full process (Topic 4.3) once a month has. The second
teacher is more likely to grow, because the process is what makes the experience teach.

This is why the standards from Unit 1 matter here. Reflection without a standard is just thinking
aloud. A reflection that says "I will use more examples next time" is a resolution. A reflection that
says "I will use more examples next time, and I will know it worked if the pupils who currently score
below 40 per cent on application questions score above it" is a testable decision. The standard of
*clarity* - is the plan specific enough to act on? - and the standard of *depth* - does it address
the cause or only the symptom? - are what separate the two.

## Activity: Claim or evidence?

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Each pair takes the five claims below. For each one, decide together whether it is **well supported**
(the evidence directly shows it), **moderately supported** (the mechanism is sound but the evidence
is indirect), or **asserted** (it sounds right but the evidence is thin). Write one sentence of
justification.

1. "Teachers who reflect make better choices about which method to use."
2. "Teachers who reflect have better-behaved classes."
3. "Teachers who reflect are more likely to find the real cause of a problem."
4. "Teachers who reflect grow faster in their careers."
5. "Experience alone, without reflection, does not produce professional growth."

Then swap with another pair. Where you disagree, cite the evidence distinction (mechanism vs outcome,
direct vs indirect) that settles it.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the four benefits reflection is commonly claimed to produce.
2. *(Understand)* In two or three sentences, explain the difference between "experience produces learning" and "experience plus reflection produces learning". Use a school example.
3. *(Apply)* A head teacher says, "I want every teacher to keep a reflective journal because it will raise results." Using the evidence from this topic, give one reason that claim is overstated and one reason it is not worthless.
4. *(Apply)* A trainee says, "I have been teaching for three years, so I have professional growth." Using the distinction from this topic, explain in two sentences what is missing from that claim.

## Summary

Reflection is claimed to buy better decisions, greater adaptability, improved problem-solving and
professional growth. The evidence is strongest for better decisions and weakest for direct outcome
claims like "results will improve". The most defensible claim is also the most uncomfortable: experience
without reflection does not produce growth, and a teacher who has taught for fifteen years may have
repeated one year fifteen times. The quality of the reflection matters more than the quantity of the
experience.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can name the four claimed benefits of reflection and state the evidence level for each.
- [ ] I can explain why "experience alone does not produce learning" and give a classroom example.
- [ ] I can tell the difference between a claim that is well supported and one that is merely asserted.
- [ ] I can apply the Unit 1 standards to judge whether a reflective decision is specific enough to act on.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find one claim about reflection made in a training document, a staff meeting or a policy statement
at your school (for example, "action research raises results" or "reflective journals improve
teaching"). Write it down. Then write one sentence saying what evidence would be needed to support
it, and one sentence saying what evidence currently exists. Bring both to your tutorial.

## Summative task

A workshop facilitator makes this claim to a group of teachers:

> "If you reflect for ten minutes after every lesson, your teaching will improve within one term, your
> pupils' behaviour will get better, and you will become a more confident professional."

Write 150 to 200 words that (a) identifies which part of the claim is best supported by evidence, (b)
which part is most overstated, and (c) rewrites the claim in a form that is both honest and still
worth acting on.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Best-supported part* | 0-2: correctly identifies the decision-making claim with a reason / a general reason / wrong. |
| *Most overstated part* | 0-2: correctly identifies the behaviour or outcome claim and names why the evidence is thin / a general reason / wrong. |
| *Honest rewrite* | 0-3: a rewritten claim that keeps the defensible part, drops the unsupported part, and adds a condition (e.g. quality of reflection, follow-through) that makes it testable (**Analyze**: the rewrite turns on the specific evidence distinction, not on general caution) / a rewrite that drops too much / no real rewrite. |
| *Clarity* | 0-1: within the word band / not. |

## Further reading

Boud, D., Keogh, R., & Walker, D. (Eds.) (2013). *Reflection: Turning experience into learning*. Routledge. The opening chapter sets out the argument that experience alone does not teach - a process is required to turn it into learning.

Osterman, K. F., & Kottkamp, R. B. (2004). *Reflective practice for educators: Professional development to improve student learning* (2nd ed.). Corwin Press. Chapter 1 reviews the claimed benefits and the evidence for each.

Taggart, G. L., & Wilson, A. P. (2005). *Promoting reflective thinking in teachers: 50 action strategies* (2nd ed.). Corwin Press. The introduction lists adaptability and growth as outcomes and notes where the evidence is direct and where it is inferred.
