## A real classroom situation

A trainer at a teacher-education institute in Karachi tells the participants: "Use Gibbs' reflective
cycle. It has six stages. Start with 'Description', then 'Feelings', then 'Evaluation', and so on."
The participants copy the six stages into their notebooks. One of them, Miss Sana, asks: "Why six
stages? Why these six? Who decided?" The trainer says, "That is how Gibbs designed it." Miss Sana
thinks but does not say: "But *why* did Gibbs design it that way?"

<Figure id="fig-U5-5" kind="timeline" src="/img/figures/efmp-304/unit-05/fig-U5-5.svg" alt="Timeline showing where the ideas behind reflective practice came from: Dewey (1933, education, reflective thought), Schön (1983, professional practice, reflection-in-action), Kolb (1984, experiential learning, the cycle), Boud et al. Miss Sana's question is the right one, and this topic answers it. Every reflective model you will
meet is an answer to a specific question asked in a specific field. Knowing the question tells you
what the model is good for and, just as importantly, what it is *not* designed to do.

## Explanation

### The theoretical strands

Reflective practice in teaching does not come from a single source. It is a convergence of several
strands, each answering a different question. Three matter most for this course.

**Strand 1 - Experiential learning (Dewey, Kolb).** The question: *how do people learn from
experience?* John Dewey (1933) argued that reflection is the active, careful consideration of a
belief in light of the grounds that support it. He was writing about education generally, not
teaching specifically, and his concern was intellectual: how does a learner move from impulse to
informed action? David Kolb (1984) built on Dewey and proposed a four-stage cycle: concrete
experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualisation, active experimentation. Kolb's cycle
is the one most teacher-training programmes borrow from, and it is the basis for Gibbs' six-stage
model. The field is education and psychology. The limitation: Kolb's cycle describes *how* learning
happens, not *what* the learner should question. It is a process model, not a critical one.

**Strand 2 - Reflective consciousness (Schön).** The question: *how do professionals think in the
moment of practice?* Donald Schön (1983) studied architects, engineers and therapists and noticed
that they do not only reflect *after* an action (reflection-on-action). They also reflect *during*
it (reflection-in-action): adjusting, improvising, and rethinking in the moment. Schön's work is
the source of the distinction Topic 4.1 drew between reflection-on-action and reflection-in-action.
The field is professional practice generally. The limitation: Schön described the capacity more
than he taught it. He showed that good professionals do it; he did not produce a method for training
it.

**Strand 3 - Critical reflection (Brookfield, Freire).** The question: *what assumptions and power
relations shape my practice, and how do I examine them?* Stephen Brookfield (2017) and, before him,
Paulo Freire argued that reflection without a critical dimension becomes self-justification: a
teacher reflects on how to deliver the curriculum more efficiently without ever asking whether the
curriculum itself is just. Brookfield's four lenses (autobiographical, students' eyes, colleagues'
perceptions, theory) are a method for reaching this critical dimension. The field is critical
pedagogy and adult education. The limitation: critical reflection is demanding and uncomfortable,
and most teachers avoid it unless the structure requires it.

**Common misconception: "there is one correct model."** There is not. Each model is an answer to a
specific question, and the question you are asking determines which model fits. If you want to
understand *how* you learn from experience, Kolb or Gibbs is the right tool. If you want to
understand *what you assume* about your pupils, Brookfield's four lenses is the right tool. If you
want to understand *how you think during a lesson*, Schön is the right source. Using Gibbs to answer
Brookfield's question (or vice versa) is like using a thermometer to measure weight: the instrument
is not broken, but it was not built for that job.

### What this means for Pakistani teachers

Most teacher-education programmes in Pakistan teach one model - usually Gibbs or a simplified version
of Kolb - and present it as *the* reflective cycle. This is a limitation for two reasons.

First, it hides the critical strand. A teacher who only knows Gibbs will reflect on how to teach
better within the existing system. They will not be equipped to ask whether the system itself is
just. Brookfield's strand is the one that makes that question possible, and it is the strand most
often missing from Pakistani training.

Second, it hides the setting mismatch. Kolb's cycle was developed from studies of managers and
professionals in Western organisations. Gibbs' model was designed for nursing education. Neither was
designed for a Pakistani secondary classroom with forty-five pupils, a single textbook, and a
examination system that rewards recall. This does not mean the models are useless - the underlying
cycle is sound - but it means they have to be *adapted*, not adopted wholesale. A teacher who knows
the theory can adapt. A teacher who only knows the model's stages cannot.

### A worked example before you try it

Here is the theory applied to a choice of model.

> A teacher wants to reflect on why her pupils are silent during question-and-answer sessions. She
> has two options: Gibbs' cycle or Brookfield's four lenses.

**Gibbs would ask:** What happened? What did you feel? What was good and bad? What sense can you
make of it? What will you do differently? This is useful for diagnosing a *technique* problem (are
the questions too hard? Are they pitched at the wrong level?).

**Brookfield would ask:** What does this look like from the pupils' perspective? What does it look
like through the lens of the school's culture? What theory about participation am I assuming? This
is useful for diagnosing an *assumption* problem (do I assume silence means understanding? Do I
assume only certain pupils should answer?).

The teacher in this case suspects the problem is not the questions but her assumption about who
should speak. Brookfield is the better tool. If she used Gibbs, she would refine her questioning
technique and never reach the real issue.

Now you try the same reasoning in the activity.

## Activity: Which strand fits?

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Each pair takes the four reflection tasks below. For each one, decide which theoretical strand
(experiential learning, reflective consciousness, or critical reflection) is the best fit and which
model (Kolb/Gibbs, Schön, or Brookfield) would serve it best. Write one sentence of justification.

1. "I want to understand why my pupils' homework completion dropped after I changed the seating plan."
2. "I want to understand what I assumed about my pupils' home circumstances when I set homework."
3. "I want to understand how I adjusted my explanation mid-lesson when I saw confusion on their faces."
4. "I want to understand why I feel more confident teaching one section than another."

Then swap with another pair. Where you disagree, say which strand's *question* settles it.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the three theoretical strands and the field each emerged from.
2. *(Understand)* In two or three sentences, explain why Gibbs' model and Brookfield's four lenses are not competitors but answers to different questions.
3. *(Apply)* A teacher says, "I will use Gibbs' cycle to examine whether my assumptions about girls' participation are fair." Using the theory from this topic, explain in two sentences why that is a mismatch and what would be a better fit.
4. *(Apply)* A teacher wants to understand how she makes decisions during a lesson. Name the strand and the thinker most relevant to that question.

## Summary

Reflective practice draws on three theoretical strands: experiential learning (Dewey, Kolb) which
gives the cycle; reflective consciousness (Schön) which gives reflection-in-action; and critical
reflection (Brookfield, Freire) which gives the examination of assumptions and power. Each answers a
different question, and each has a different model attached. A teacher who knows the strands can
choose the right model for the question they are asking. A teacher who only knows one model will use
it for every question, including the ones it was not built to answer.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find one reflective model or cycle taught in a training document or a textbook at your school. Write
down its stages. Then ask: which theoretical strand does it belong to? What question was it built to
answer? What question would it *not* be good for? Bring your analysis to your tutorial.

## Summative task

A teacher-training handbook recommends Gibbs' reflective cycle for all reflective writing. It says:
"Gibbs' six-stage model is the complete framework for teacher reflection."

Write 200 to 250 words that (a) names the theoretical strand Gibbs belongs to and the question it
was built to answer, (b) names one question about teaching that Gibbs is *not* well suited to answer
and which strand would be a better fit, and (c) evaluates the handbook's claim that Gibbs is "the
complete framework" against the three-strand model from this topic.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Strand identification* | 0-2: correctly identifies Gibbs' strand and its question / a general reason / wrong. |
| *Limitation identified* | 0-2: names a question Gibbs is not suited for and names a better strand / a general limitation / none. |
| *Evaluation of claim* | 0-3: evaluates the "complete framework" claim against the three strands, showing what Gibbs covers and what it leaves out (**Analyze*: the evaluation turns on the specific content of the three-strand model) / a general evaluation / a verdict asserted. |
| *Clarity* | 0-2: within the word band and organised under the three parts / unclear or over length. |
