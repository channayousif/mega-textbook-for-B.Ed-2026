## A real classroom situation

Two teachers at a government high school in Dadu have both been teaching for eighteen years. Miss
Kiran is the teacher the head sends difficult classes to. She manages behaviour that other teachers
cannot, she adapts her explanations when pupils do not follow, and younger teachers ask her for
 advice. Mr Usman is the teacher the head keeps away from difficult classes. He teaches the same way
 he did in his first year, blames the pupils when they do not learn, and has been overheard saying,
 "I have done my time. They should make the new teachers deal with it."

<Figure id="fig-U5-1" kind="diagram" src="/img/figures/efmp-304/unit-05/fig-U5-1.svg" alt="Diagram showing experience passing through reflection into learning (the main route) with a bypass route drawn beside it labelled 'fifteen years, once' leading to repetition rather than learning." width={780} height={470} />

Both teachers have the same amount of experience. What they do with it is completely different. This
topic explains why that difference exists and what it means for your own career.

## Explanation

### Experience is not the same as learning from experience

The single most important idea in this unit is a simple one: **experience and learning from experience
are not the same thing.** A teacher who teaches a lesson has had an experience. A teacher who teaches
a lesson, notices what did not work, analyses why, tries something different, and checks whether it
worked has *learned* from it. The difference is the process in the middle.

Boud, Keogh and Walker (2013) describe reflection as the mechanism that turns experience into
learning. Without it, experience produces only repetition. With it, experience produces growth. The
mechanism works in three steps:

1. **Returning to the experience.** Not just remembering it, but attending to what actually happened
   and how you felt about it. Miss Kiran does this naturally: after a difficult lesson, she can tell
   you exactly where the confusion started and how she felt at that moment.
2. **Attending to feelings.** Both positive and negative feelings carry information. Frustration
   signals that an expectation was violated. Satisfaction signals that a strategy worked. Ignoring
   the feelings means ignoring part of the data. Mr Usman dismisses his frustration as "the pupils'
   fault", which means he never learns from it.
3. **Re-evaluating the experience.** This is the stage where new understanding is formed. It connects
   the specific incident to a broader principle: "When I explain a new concept without checking prior
   understanding first, I lose the bottom third of the class." That principle can be carried forward
   to the next lesson.

**Common misconception: "experience automatically produces learning."** It does not, and the
evidence for that claim is the existence of twenty-year teachers who have not improved since year
two. The research consensus, across professions, is that experience is necessary but not sufficient:
something has to happen *to* the experience for it to become learning, and that something is
reflection (Boud et al., 2013; Osterman & Kottkamp, 2004). In Pakistani schools, where professional
development beyond the initial training is limited, the risk is particularly high: a teacher can serve
for decades without ever being asked to reflect systematically.

### The bypass route

The diagram in this topic shows two routes from experience. The main route goes through reflection
into learning. The bypass route goes straight from experience to repetition, and it is labelled
"fifteen years, once" - the experience is had once and then repeated, without ever being examined.

Most Pakistani teachers are on the bypass route for most of their career, not because they are lazy
but because the system does not require the main route. There is no time built into the school day
for reflection. Teacher appraisal, where it exists, focuses on attendance and coverage, not on
learning from practice. The result is a profession in which the most common form of professional
development is "I have always done it this way and it works" - which is a description of routine, not
of growth.

This is why the course you are reading makes reflection a graded component, not an optional extra.
The reflection file the guide requires (10 marks) is an attempt to put teachers on the main route by
making the process assessable.

### A worked example before you try it

Here is the distinction performed once, slowly, on a single lesson.

> Mr Faisal teaches the same lesson on photosynthesis in two sections, A and B. In section A, the
> pupils draw accurate diagrams. In section B, most diagrams are wrong.

**On the bypass route:** Mr Faisal concludes, "Section B is weak. They cannot even copy a diagram."
He teaches photosynthesis next year the same way and expects the same result. He has repeated the
experience without learning from it.

**On the main route:** Mr Faisal notices the difference and asks why. He discovers that in section A,
he happened to use the board drawing *before* he explained, so the pupils saw the parts first. In
section B, he explained first and then drew, and the pupils had nothing to anchor the words to. He has
learned something about sequencing explanation and visual support. Next year, he will draw first in
both sections, and he will check whether the result is different.

Now you try the same distinction in the activity.

## Activity: Which route?

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Each pair takes the four statements below. For each one, decide whether it shows the teacher on the
**main route** (experience being turned into learning through reflection) or the **bypass route**
(experience being repeated without examination). Write one sentence naming the evidence.

1. "I have taught this poem five years in a row. Every year the pupils find the same lines difficult. I now spend extra time on those lines."
2. "I have taught this poem five years in a row. The pupils still find the same lines difficult, but they always have. You cannot change how pupils respond to poetry."
3. "Last year my pupils did badly on the essay question. I asked a colleague to watch my next essay lesson and tell me what she saw. She noticed I was modelling the introduction but not the conclusion. I changed that, and the essays improved."
4. "Last year my pupils did badly on the essay question. I made them write more practice essays this year. The marks were about the same."

Then swap with another pair. Where you disagree, say which of the three steps of the main route (returning, attending, re-evaluating) is present or absent.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the three steps of the mechanism that turns experience into learning, in order.
2. *(Understand)* In two or three sentences, explain the difference between "fifteen years of experience" and "one year of experience repeated fifteen times".
3. *(Apply)* A teacher says, "I have been teaching for twelve years, so I know what works." Using the distinction from this topic, explain in two sentences what that claim assumes and what it may be missing.
4. *(Apply)* Take the statement "The pupils failed the test again this year. The paper was harder." Is this the main route or the bypass route? Justify in one sentence.

## Summary

Experience alone does not produce learning. Reflection - the process of returning to an experience,
attending to the feelings it produced, and re-evaluating it against a broader principle - is the
mechanism that turns experience into learning. Without it, a teacher can serve for decades and improve
not at all. With it, a single lesson can teach something that changes practice for years. The bypass
route (repetition without reflection) is the default in most Pakistani schools because the system does
not require the main route. This course attempts to change that default.

## Try this at your practicum school

At your next staff meeting or informal conversation, listen for a statement that begins "I have
always done it this way" or "they never learn no matter what I do". Write it down. Then write one
sentence saying whether it is on the main route or the bypass route, and one sentence saying what
the first step of the main route would look like for that teacher.

## Summative task

Here are two teachers with the same amount of experience:

> **Miss Parveen**, fifteen years. She can describe a specific lesson from year three that changed
> how she introduces new vocabulary, a specific colleague whose feedback changed her assessment
> practice in year seven, and a mistake she made last year that she is still correcting.
>
> **Mr Rashid**, fifteen years. He says, "I have seen everything. The pupils get weaker every year.
> I teach the same way. If they want to learn, they will."

Write 150 to 200 words that (a) names which teacher is on the main route and which is on the bypass
route, (b) gives two pieces of evidence from each description, and (c) says which teacher is more
likely to improve next year and why.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Route identification* | 0-2: both correctly identified / one correct / neither. |
| *Evidence from text* | 0-3: two pieces of evidence from each description, tied to specific wording (**Analyze**: the evidence turns on the actual content of each description, not on general impressions) / one piece from each / evidence absent. |
| *Likely improvement* | 0-2: names Miss Parveen with a reason tied to the main route / a general reason / wrong choice. |
| *Clarity* | 0-1: within the word band / not. |
