## A real classroom situation

Three trainee teachers at a teacher-training college in Sukkur are each asked to keep a reflective
journal for a term. All three are given the same instruction: "Reflect on your teaching weekly."

Miss Ayesha chooses Gibbs' six-stage model. Her entries are structured: description, feelings,
evaluation, analysis, conclusion, action plan. They are easy to follow and her supervisor likes them.

Miss Hira chooses Brookfield's four lenses. Her entries are less structured but more unsettling.
She writes about what her pupils might think of her, what her colleague noticed, and what a reading
about power in classrooms made her question about her own practice. Her supervisor finds them
"interesting but not always clear".

Miss Rabia tries to use both models every week. Her entries are long, sometimes confused, and by week
six she abandons structure altogether and writes whatever comes to mind.

## Explanation

### The models compared

No single model is best. Each is good for some things and poor for others. The comparison below
covers the three models this course has introduced.

**Kolb's four-stage cycle (1984).** Stages: concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract
conceptualisation, active experimentation. Good for: understanding how you learn from experience. Costs:
it is descriptive, not critical - it will not surface your assumptions about power or justice. It came
from studies of managers and professionals in Western organisations. For a Pakistani teacher: useful
for technique improvement; limited for examining *why* you teach the way you do.

**Gibbs' six-stage cycle (1988, building on Kolb).** Stages: description, feelings, evaluation,
analysis, conclusion, action plan. Good for: a structured, repeatable process that produces clear
action points. Costs: it can become mechanical - fill in the six boxes and stop thinking. It was
designed for nursing education, not for secondary teaching. For a Pakistani teacher: useful for
your assessed reflection file because it is easy to assess; limited if you use it for everything,
because it does not ask you to question your assumptions.

**Brookfield's four lenses (2017).** The lenses: autobiographical, students' eyes, colleagues'
perceptions, theoretical literature. Good for: surfacing assumptions you did not know you held. Costs:
it is demanding, it requires access to pupils, colleagues and reading, and it is uncomfortable -
the assumptions it surfaces are often ones you would rather not examine. It came from adult education
and critical pedagogy. For a Pakistani teacher: the most powerful tool for genuine professional
growth; the hardest to sustain without support.

**Common misconception: "the model with the most stages is the most thorough."** It is not. Gibbs
has six stages and Brookfield has four lenses, but Brookfield's four lenses can produce deeper
reflection than Gibbs' six stages because they ask questions Gibbs does not ask. Thoroughness is
about the *depth* of the question, not the *number* of the stages.

### How to choose

Choose a model based on three considerations:

1. **What question are you asking?** If it is a technique question ("how do I make this work better?"),
   Gibbs is efficient. If it is an assumption question ("what do I take for granted about my pupils?"),
   Brookfield is necessary. Miss Ayesha chose Gibbs because her question was mostly technical. That
   was a reasonable choice for a first journal, but it will not take her to the critical level.

2. **What does the assessment require?** The course's assessed reflection file (10 marks) needs a
   structured product your examiners can mark. Gibbs gives you that structure. Brookfield gives you
   depth that is harder to present in a standardised format. A practical choice: use Gibbs as your
   scaffolding and bring in Brookfield's lenses for the entries where the question demands it.

3. **What can you sustain?** Miss Rabia's failure is the most common one: she tried to do
   everything and ended up doing nothing. Choose one model, use it for at least four weeks, and only
   then decide whether it is serving you. A simple model used consistently is worth more than a
   sophisticated model abandoned in week six.

### Knowing yourself as a practitioner

Underneath the choice of model is a deeper question: *what kind of reflector are you?* Taggart and
Wilson (2005) argue that teachers tend to have a default level of reflection (Topic 4.4) and a
default preference for structure. Some teachers are most comfortable with a clear template and
resist open-ended reflection. Some are most comfortable with free writing and resist templates. Both
can reflect well; the risk is always the same - staying at the level that feels comfortable and never
pushing to the next one.

The honest self-assessment is this: if you naturally prefer structure, you will default to technical
reflection and need to push yourself toward the critical. If you naturally prefer open reflection,
you may stay at the descriptive level and need to add the discipline of evidence. Knowing your
default is what lets you compensate for it.

### A worked example before you try it

Here is the choice performed once, on a situation.

> A teacher has been teaching for five years. She is competent but feels stuck. She wants her
> reflection to help her move forward.

**Option A - Gibbs.** She would produce structured entries, identify specific improvements, and
execute them. The risk: she refines her existing practice without questioning whether the practice
itself is the right one. After a year, she is a better version of the same teacher.

**Option B - Brookfield.** She would examine her assumptions about teaching, pupils and learning.
The discomfort would be real: she might discover that she has been teaching to the examination at
the expense of understanding, or that she has been favouring pupils who speak her home language.
After a year, she may be a fundamentally different teacher, but the process will have been harder.

**Option C - Both.** She uses Gibbs as her weekly structure and applies one of Brookfield's lenses
per month. This is the most sustainable for most teachers: the structure keeps the habit alive, and
the lenses keep it from becoming mechanical.

Now you try the same reasoning in the activity.

## Activity: Choose and justify

Work in pairs. About 25 minutes.

Each pair takes the three situations below. For each one, choose one model (Gibbs, Brookfield, or a
combination) and justify the choice against at least one alternative. Write no more than three
sentences per situation.

1. A first-year teacher who struggles with classroom management and wants a clear structure for weekly reflection.
2. A ten-year teacher who feels stale and wants to understand why their practice has not changed in years.
3. A teacher preparing the assessed reflection file who needs a markable product but also wants at least one entry to reach the critical level.

Then swap with another pair. Where you disagree, say which of the three considerations (question, assessment, sustainability) settles it.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the three models compared in this topic and the field each came from.
2. *(Understand)* In two or three sentences, explain why a teacher might choose Gibbs for weekly reflection and Brookfield for a monthly deeper entry, rather than using one model for everything.
3. *(Apply)* A teacher says, "I will use Brookfield's four lenses for every journal entry because it is the most thorough." Using the comparison from this topic, explain in two sentences why that may not work and what a better plan would be.
4. *(Apply)* A trainee has been asked to keep a structured journal that will be marked. Which model best fits that requirement, and what would be lost by using only that model?

## Summary

The main reflective models are Kolb/Gibbs (structured, technique-focused, from education/nursing),
Brookfield (critical, assumption-focused, from adult education) and Schön (reflection-in-action,
from professional practice). No model is best for everything. Choose based on the question you are
asking, what the assessment requires, and what you can sustain. For most Pakistani teachers, a
combination - Gibbs for structure, Brookfield for depth - is the most sustainable. The key is to
know your default and compensate for it: if you prefer structure, push toward the critical; if you
prefer open reflection, add the discipline of evidence.

## Try this at your practicum school

Write one reflective entry this week using Gibbs' six stages. Then write a second entry on the *same*
incident using Brookfield's four lenses (autobiographical and one other). Compare the two entries.
Which one produced a more useful finding? Which one was harder to write? Bring both to your tutorial.

## Summative task

Here is a situation:

> Mr Jamshed has been teaching for eight years. He is a good teacher by conventional measures - his
> results are acceptable, his pupils behave, his lessons are organised. But he feels he has stopped
> growing. He wants a reflective practice that will challenge him, not just confirm that he is doing
> fine.

Write 200 to 250 words that (a) recommends one model or a combination for Mr Jamshed, (b) justifies
the recommendation against a named alternative, and (c) names one activity that will help him reach
the critical level, with a reason tied to the theoretical strands from Topic 5.3.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Model recommendation* | 0-2: a specific recommendation tied to Mr Jamshed's situation / a general recommendation / wrong choice. |
| *Justification against alternative* | 0-3: justifies the choice against a named alternative using the three considerations from this topic (**Analyze*: the justification turns on the specific content of the comparison, not on general preference) / a general reason / a verdict asserted. |
| *Activity for critical level* | 0-2: names an activity tied to the critical strand from Topic 5.3 with a reason / a general activity / none. |
| *Clarity* | 0-2: within the word band and organised under the three parts / unclear or over length. |
