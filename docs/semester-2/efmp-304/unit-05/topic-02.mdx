## A real classroom situation

Miss Hina (who first appeared in Unit 4) has kept a journal for two months now. An entry reads:

> "Grade 7, fractions. I explained with the fraction wall. Half the pupils still cannot add unlike
> denominators. I am frustrated. Tomorrow I will do more examples on the board."

Miss Hina's journal supervisor writes one comment back: "What would show you that 'more examples'
works? What if it is not the number of examples but the way you check understanding between them?"

<Figure id="fig-U5-3" kind="flowchart" src="/img/figures/efmp-304/unit-05/fig-U5-3.svg" alt="Flowchart of the reflective cycle as a closed loop: description, analysis, alternatives, decision, trial, re-examination, with the return arrow labelled 'what was learned and what to try next'." width={820} height={520} />

That single comment points to the gap in Miss Hina's cycle. She has done the first two stages and
jumped to a decision. The supervisor is asking her to close the loop: how will you *know* your trial
worked? Without that question, the cycle does not close, and the next entry starts from scratch rather
than from what was learned.

## Explanation

### The cycle in detail

Topic 4.3 gave you six stages. Topic 5.2 takes the same stages and treats them as a *cycle* - a loop
that returns to its beginning carrying what was learned. The cycle has five stages plus the return
arrow, and each one produces something the next needs.

<Figure id="fig-U5-4" kind="table" src="/img/figures/efmp-304/unit-05/fig-U5-4.svg" alt="Table of each cycle stage with three columns: the question it asks, the output it produces, and the characteristic way it is skipped." width={820} height={470} />

**Stage 1 - Description.** *What happened?* The output is an account specific enough to analyse. The
characteristic way it is skipped: the teacher moves straight to judgement ("it went badly") without
recording what actually occurred. Miss Hina's description is adequate: she named the class, the
topic, the method, and the outcome.

**Stage 2 - Analysis.** *Why did it happen, and what does the evidence say?* The output is an
evidence-based finding, not an assumption. The characteristic way it is skipped: the teacher forms
a hypothesis and treats it as a fact ("they do not understand because they are weak"). Miss Hina
skipped this stage. She wrote "half the pupils still cannot" (description) and then "I will do more
examples" (decision) without ever asking *why* half could not. The supervisor's comment is asking
her to go back to this stage.

**Stage 3 - Alternatives and decision.** *What could I try differently, and which will I choose?* The
output is a specific, evidence-based plan. The characteristic way it is skipped: the teacher chooses
the first idea that comes to mind without generating alternatives. Miss Hina chose "more examples"
without considering whether the problem is actually the number of examples or something else.

**Stage 4 - Trial.** *Try the plan in practice.* The output is a changed lesson and an observation of
what happened. The characteristic way it is skipped: the teacher makes a resolution ("I will do
X next time") and never actually tries it, or tries it once without recording the result.

**Stage 5 - Re-examination.** *Did it work, and what have I learned that carries forward?* The output
is a finding that feeds the next cycle. The characteristic way it is skipped: the teacher tries the
plan, it either works or it does not, and they move on without naming the principle. This is the
stage the supervisor's comment targets: "What would show you it worked?" is the re-examination
question, and Miss Hina has not answered it.

**The return arrow.** The cycle is not complete until the finding from Stage 5 is carried back to
Stage 1 of the *next* cycle. Miss Hina's next journal entry should begin with what she learned from
the "more examples" trial, not with a fresh complaint. That is what closes the loop.

**Common misconception: "the cycle has to be completed in one sitting."** It does not, as Topic 4.3
said. But the version of this misconception that bites at this stage is different: a teacher thinks
that because the cycle can be spread over days, it does not need to be completed at all. It does.
A reflection that stops at Stage 2 every time is a collection of complaints. A reflection that
reaches Stage 5 and then carries the finding forward is professional growth. The difference is the
return arrow.

### Why the cycle matters more than the model

Topic 5.4 will give you several named models - Kolb, Gibbs, Schön, Brookfield. They differ in the
number of stages and the words they use. What they share is the underlying cycle: experience,
examination, learning, trial. A teacher who understands the cycle can use any model and adapt it to
their context. A teacher who memorises one model without understanding the cycle will apply it
mechanically and stop when the model's stages end, even if the return arrow has not been drawn.

Osterman and Kottkamp (2004) make this point explicitly: the cycle is the *process*, and the models
are *scaffolding* for the process. The scaffolding should be chosen for the situation and removed
when it is no longer needed. For your assessed reflection file, you will use one model as your
scaffolding (Unit 6 teaches this). For your career, you need to understand the cycle well enough to
keep reflecting even when no model is in front of you.

### A worked example before you try it

Here is the cycle performed once, with the return arrow made visible.

> **Stage 1 - Description.** Mr Sajid's grade 9 mathematics lesson on linear equations. He worked
> three examples on the board, then gave five for independent practice. Eight pupils finished. Four
> pupils asked for help and received it. Ten pupils did nothing.
>
> **Stage 2 - Analysis.** The ten pupils who did nothing were not the lowest-attaining. They were the
> middle group who usually work slowly but finish. The five practice problems were too many for the
> time left. They started the first one, realised they would not finish, and stopped.
>
> **Stage 3 - Alternatives.** (a) Reduce to three practice problems. (b) Give all five but allow the
> slow workers to finish in the next lesson. (c) Pair the slow workers with a faster peer.
>
> **Stage 4 - Decision and trial.** Chose (a). Next lesson, set three problems. All pupils finished.
>
> **Stage 5 - Re-examination.** The problem was not ability but time. The principle: the number of
> independent problems should match the time available for the slowest worker, not the average one.
>
> **Return arrow.** Next journal entry begins: "Last time I found that matching problems to time
> changed completion rates. This week I am trying the same principle with homework."

Now you try the same cycle in the activity.

## Activity: Find the gap

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Each pair takes the four journal entries below. For each one, name which stage of the cycle the
teacher has reached and which stage they skipped. Write one sentence saying what the skipped stage
would add.

1. "Grade 8, essay writing. The introductions were weak. I will teach a formula for introductions next week."
2. "Grade 8, essay writing. The introductions were weak. I think it is because they have not seen a modelled example. I will model one next week and see whether the next set of essays improves."
3. "Grade 8, essay writing. The introductions were weak. I will model one and use a planning template. If at least half the class writes a clear first paragraph, I will know the combination worked."
4. "Grade 8, essay writing. The introductions were weak. I felt frustrated. I have always found essay teaching difficult."

Then rank the four entries from least to most complete and say what the top-ranked one has that the
others lack.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the five stages of the reflective cycle in order.
2. *(Understand)* In two or three sentences, explain why the return arrow matters and what it carries.
3. *(Apply)* A teacher writes, "I will try group work next week and see how it goes." Using the cycle, name the stage this reaches and what Stage 5 would add.
4. *(Apply)* A journal entry describes a problem, analyses why it happened, and chooses an alternative, but the next entry starts with a completely different topic. What has the teacher missed, and why does it matter?

## Summary

The reflective cycle has five stages - description, analysis, alternatives and decision, trial, and
re-examination - plus a return arrow that carries the finding back to the next cycle. Each stage
produces what the next one needs, and each has a characteristic way of being skipped. The return arrow
is what makes the cycle a *cycle* rather than a list: without it, every entry starts from scratch and
no learning accumulates. The cycle is more important than any particular model of it, because the
models are scaffolding and the cycle is the process.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can name the five stages of the reflective cycle and state what each produces.
- [ ] I can identify the characteristic way each stage is skipped.
- [ ] I can take a journal entry and say which stage it reaches and which it skips.
- [ ] I can explain why the return arrow closes the loop and what it carries.

## Try this at your practicum school

Take one of your own recent journal entries (or write one if you have not yet). Walk it through all
five stages explicitly. For Stage 5, write one sentence naming what you learned and how you will
check it. For the return arrow, write the first sentence of your *next* entry, beginning with what
this one taught you.

## Summative task

Here is a teacher's journal entry:

> "Grade 6, reading comprehension. I gave the passage and five questions. Only four pupils got question
> 3 right. I think it was too difficult. I will make the next passage easier."

Write 200 to 250 words that (a) names which stage of the cycle this entry reaches and which stages it
skips, (b) rewrites the entry so it includes the analysis stage with at least one testable hypothesis
and the re-examination stage with a clear success criterion, and (c) writes the first sentence of the
next entry showing the return arrow.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Stage identification* | 0-2: correct stage named, correct skipped stages named / one correct / neither. |
| *Rewrite with analysis* | 0-3: includes a testable hypothesis tied to the specific lesson, not a generic reading problem (**Analyze*: the hypothesis turns on the actual content of the lesson) / a general hypothesis / none. |
| *Re-examination criterion* | 0-2: a clear, specific success criterion (not "see if it goes better") / a vague criterion / none. |
| *Return arrow* | 0-2: the first sentence of the next entry shows what was learned and carries it forward / a general sentence / none. |
| *Clarity* | 0-1: within the word band / not. |
