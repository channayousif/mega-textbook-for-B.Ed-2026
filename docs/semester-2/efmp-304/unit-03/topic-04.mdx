## A real classroom situation

Two teachers argue about whether the school should change its examination schedule. Their arguments
are different in kind, and judging them requires four terms this topic will teach you.

Teacher 1: "All schools that set their annual examination before the board date get their results
earlier. We are setting ours before the board date. So we will get our results earlier."

Teacher 2: "The three schools in our union council that set their examination before the board date
all got their results earlier. We are like those schools in size and process. So we will probably
get our results earlier."

Both reach the same conclusion. Both look reasonable. But they are tested in different ways, and
this topic gives you the terms to say precisely how each one succeeds or fails.

## Explanation

### Deductive validity and inductive strength

The previous three topics built the pieces. This topic puts them together.

A <Glossary term="Deductive argument" /> is judged by two questions:

1. **Validity:** does the conclusion follow from the premises? If the premises were true, would the
   conclusion have to be true? This is a question about the **link** between premises and
   conclusion.

2. **Soundness:** is the argument valid **and** are the premises actually true? A sound argument
   is one you can rely on. A valid argument with a false premise is not sound.

A <Glossary term="Inductive argument" /> is judged by two parallel questions:

1. **Strength:** do the premises make the conclusion probable? This is the inductive parallel of
   validity. A strong inductive argument is one where the premises, if true, make the conclusion
   likely.

2. **Cogency:** is the argument strong **and** are the premises actually true? A cogent argument is
   one you can rely on. A strong inductive argument with a false premise is not cogent.

### The grid

The four terms fit into a grid that is worth learning, because it stops the most common error in
this unit: using "valid" when you mean "true" or "convincing".

| | Premises true | Premises false |
|---|---|---|
| **Valid** (conclusion follows) | **Sound** | Valid but unsound |
| **Invalid** (conclusion does not follow) | Invalid | Invalid |

For inductive arguments, replace valid with strong, sound with cogent, and unsound with uncogent.

The grid shows why the misconceptions in the content-spec are mistakes:

**"Valid means true."** No. Valid means the conclusion follows from the premises. An argument can
be valid and have a false conclusion, if a premise is false. "All teachers are head teachers. Mr
Soomro is a teacher. So Mr Soomro is a head teacher" is valid but unsound (and false).

**"A sound argument is one that sounds convincing."** No. Sound means valid plus true premises. An
argument can sound convincing and be invalid, and an argument can be sound but reach a conclusion
you do not like.

**"If the conclusion is false the argument is invalid."** No. A false conclusion only tells you that
either the argument is invalid or a premise is false (or both). It does not tell you which.

### Swapping the tests: what goes wrong

The content-spec's worked example for this unit is two staffroom arguments about the same pupil,
one deductive and one inductive, judged by the wrong test each way. Here is what that looks like
in practice.

**A valid deductive argument dismissed as "only a guess."** A clerk argues: "All pupils on the fee
list have paid. Ayesha is on the list. So she has paid." This is valid. If you dismiss it by saying
"you cannot be certain, the list might have a mistake", you have judged a deductive argument by the
inductive standard of probability. The argument never claimed certainty about the list; it claimed
that **if** the list is correct, the conclusion follows. The right criticism is either "the list is
wrong" (attacking a premise) or "the argument is invalid" (attacking the link). "You cannot be
certain" is neither.

**A strong inductive argument attacked for not being certain.** A teacher argues: "The last three
cohorts that had a practical orientation week scored higher. So this cohort should have one." This
is a generalisation. If you attack it by saying "you cannot know that for certain", you have judged
an inductive argument by the deductive standard of guarantee. The argument never claimed certainty.
The right criticism is either "this cohort is different in a way that matters" (attacking strength)
or "the premise is false" (attacking the evidence). "You cannot know for certain" is the wrong
test.

Bassham et al. (2010) describe this as the most persistent error in introductory critical thinking:
learners treat "I am not convinced" as a refutation, without asking whether the argument promised
certainty or only probability. The four terms exist to make that distinction precise. Yu and Zenker
(2022) make the same point from the analysis side: describing an argument's structure should precede
judging its strength, because the two tasks require different judgements and doing them out of order
means describing your own reaction rather than the argument.

### A worked example before you try it

Here is the move performed once, slowly, on a pair of arguments about the same decision.

> **A.** "If we set the examination before the board date, we get our results earlier. We are setting
> it before the board date. So we will get our results earlier."
>
> **B.** "Three schools that set their examination before the board date got their results earlier.
> So we probably will too."

**Step 1 - classify.** A is deductive (if-then, claiming a guarantee). B is inductive
(generalisation from three cases).

**Step 2 - judge A by its test.** A is modus ponens, and it is valid. Whether it is sound depends
on whether the first premise is true: do all schools that set early actually get earlier results?
If that is false, A is valid but unsound.

**Step 3 - judge B by its test.** B is a generalisation from three cases. It is stronger if the
three schools are like this one in size and process, and weaker if they differ. It is not a
question of "is it certain?" but "is the sample good enough?"

**Step 4 - do not swap the tests.** A is not weak because it is "only a conditional." B is not
weak because it is "only a guess." Each is judged by its own standard.

## Activity: Judge by the right test

Work in pairs. About 25 minutes.

Each pair takes the four pairs of arguments below. For each pair, (a) classify each argument as
deductive or inductive, (b) judge it by the right test (valid/invalid or strong/weak), (c) say
whether it is sound or cogent, and (d) say what would go wrong if you judged it by the other test.

1. **A.** "All teachers who have a B.Ed are eligible for promotion. Miss Rabia has a B.Ed. So she is eligible."
   **B.** "The last four teachers who were promoted had a B.Ed. So Miss Rabia will probably be promoted."

2. **A.** "If the grant arrives, the laboratory is repaired. The grant arrived. So the laboratory is repaired."
   **B.** "The laboratory was not repaired. So the grant did not arrive." (Assume the same conditional.)

3. **A.** "Every school that submitted the report on time received the grant. We submitted on time. So we received the grant."
   **B.** "Three schools that submitted on time received the grant. So we probably received ours."

4. **A.** "All pupils who attend regularly pass. Ahmed attends regularly. So he will pass."
   **B.** "The last five pupils who attended regularly all passed. Ahmed attends regularly. So he will probably pass."

Finish by agreeing, as a whole class, on one argument that is valid but unsound, and on what would
have to change for it to become sound.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the four terms in this topic and state which apply to deductive arguments and which to inductive.
2. *(Understand)* In two or three sentences, explain the difference between a valid argument and a sound one. Do not use the word "true" in your answer.
3. *(Understand)* A trainee says, "A strong inductive argument is just a valid argument that is not quite certain." Give two reasons that is wrong.
4. *(Apply)* Classify this argument and say whether it is valid: "If the teacher is absent, the period is free. The period is not free. So the teacher is not absent."
5. *(Apply)* Classify this argument and say whether it is strong or weak: "Two of the three schools I visited had a library. So most schools in our union council probably have one."

## Summary

Validity and soundness are the deductive pair: validity asks whether the conclusion follows, soundness
asks whether it follows from true premises. Strength and cogency are the inductive pair: strength
asks whether the premises make the conclusion probable, cogency asks whether they do so and are
themselves true. The grid holds the four terms together. The most common error is to judge one
kind of argument by the other kind's test: to dismiss a valid argument for not being certain, or to
accept a strong generalisation without asking whether the evidence supports it. The skill is to
classify first, then apply the matching test.

## Summative task

Here are four staffroom arguments about the same decision: whether to introduce a Saturday
coaching class.

> **A.** "All schools that introduced a Saturday class saw results improve. We are introducing one.
> So our results will improve."
>
> **B.** "If a school introduces a Saturday class, it needs a teacher willing to come in. We have
> a teacher willing to come in. So we can introduce a Saturday class."
>
> **C.** "Three of the four schools in our union council that introduced a Saturday class saw
> results improve. So we probably will too."
>
> **D.** "If a school introduces a Saturday class, it needs a teacher willing to come in. We do not
> have a teacher willing to come in. So we cannot introduce a Saturday class."

Write 250 to 300 words that (a) classifies each argument as deductive or inductive, (b) judges
each by the right test, (c) says which are sound or cogent and which are not, and (d) says what
would have to change for the failing ones to succeed.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Classification* | 0-4: all four correctly classified / three / two / one / none. |
| *Judgement by the right test* | 0-4: each judged by its own test with a reason / most correct / unreliable. |
| *Soundness or cogency* | 0-4: correct for each argument / most correct / unreliable. |
| *What would change* | 0-4: names a specific change for each failing argument / general / none. |
| *Clarity* | 0-2: within the word band and organised under the four parts / not. |
