# Basic Logic Concepts and Analyzing the Argument

Unit 2 taught you to find an argument: to tell it from an explanation, to spot its premises and
conclusion, and to name its type. This unit teaches you to **judge** what you have found.

Judging an argument means asking whether the premises actually support the conclusion. That sounds
like one question, but it is two. Some arguments claim their premises **guarantee** the conclusion;
others claim the premises make the conclusion **probable**. These are different promises, and they
are tested in different ways. If you apply the wrong test, you will dismiss a good argument for
failing to do something it never promised to do, or accept a bad argument because it looked
convincing. Both mistakes happen in staffrooms every week.

This unit keeps the treatment in ordinary language. You will not need symbols or a formal logic
course. You will need the skill Unit 2 built: the ability to look at a piece of reasoning and say
what its conclusion is and what is offered in support of it.

## Unit learning outcomes

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. Distinguish a deductive argument from an inductive argument, and say what each claims.
2. Name the common patterns of deductive reasoning and the common patterns of inductive reasoning.
3. Tell what deductive validity demands and what inductive strength demands.
4. Judge an argument by the right test, and explain what goes wrong when the tests are swapped.
5. Name and avoid the most common misconceptions about validity, soundness, strength and cogency.

## Prerequisite knowledge

This unit assumes Unit 2 in full: you can locate a conclusion, tell a premise from a conclusion, and
classify an argument by its type. A learner who cannot locate a conclusion cannot judge validity,
so this unit does not open until premises and conclusions are secure.

Nothing else is assumed. No formal logic, no symbolic notation.

## In this unit

1. [Two kinds of argument, and why the difference matters](./topic-01) - distinguishing deductive from inductive arguments, and the test that applies to each.
2. [Common patterns of deductive reasoning](./topic-02) - the patterns that guarantee their conclusion when they work, and the fallacies that mimic them.
3. [Common patterns of inductive reasoning](./topic-03) - generalisation, analogy, causal inference and prediction, and what makes each stronger or weaker.
4. [Validity, soundness, strength and cogency](./topic-04) - the four terms defined, the grid that holds them, and what happens when you judge one kind of argument by the other kind's test.

## How to use this unit

Each topic is one learning cycle: a classroom situation, the teaching, an activity, retrieval
questions, a summary, a self-check, a practicum task and a summative task. Work through them in
order, because Topic 3.4 uses every concept the first three topics introduce.

This unit rewards collecting real arguments from staffroom talk and testing them, more than
re-reading. Keep a notebook: every time you hear someone in a meeting say "so we should" or "that
means", write down what they said and bring it to the next topic. Judging reasoning you found
yourself, rather than tidy textbook examples, is what makes the skill transfer.

The unit ends with a summative assessment that draws the four topics together.
