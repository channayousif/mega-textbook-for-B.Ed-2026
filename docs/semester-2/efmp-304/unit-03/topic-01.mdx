## A real classroom situation

Ayesha in grade 7 has not paid her laboratory fee. The school rule says: "All pupils who have not
paid the fee by 15 September may not use the laboratory." The clerk checks the register and tells
the science teacher: "Ayesha has not paid. She may not use the laboratory."

Now read a different conversation. The same science teacher says to a colleague: "The last three
cohorts that started the year with a practical orientation week scored higher in the board
examination. This year's cohort should get one too."

Both sentences move from some facts to a recommendation. But they move in different ways, and the
difference matters for how you judge them.

## Explanation

### Distinguishing deductive from inductive arguments

Unit 2 taught you to find an argument and set it out in classical form. This topic asks a further
question about any argument you have found: **what kind of support do the premises claim to offer?**

A <Glossary term="Deductive argument" /> claims that its premises, if true, **guarantee** the
conclusion. The conclusion follows necessarily. There is no room for the premises to be true and
the conclusion false. The clerk's argument about Ayesha is this type:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Premise 1** | All pupils who have not paid the fee by 15 September may not use the laboratory. |
| **Premise 2** | Ayesha has not paid the fee by 15 September. |
| **Conclusion** | Ayesha may not use the laboratory. |

If both premises are true, the conclusion cannot be false. That is what makes it deductive. The
conclusion is already contained in the premises; drawing it out does not add new information.

An <Glossary term="Inductive argument" /> claims that its premises, if true, make the conclusion
**probable** or **likely**, but do not guarantee it. The science teacher's argument about the
orientation week is this type:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Premise 1** | The last three cohorts that started with a practical orientation week scored higher. |
| **Premise 2** | This year's cohort is like those cohorts in the respects that matter. |
| **Conclusion** | This year's cohort will probably score higher if it gets an orientation week. |

Even if both premises are true, the conclusion might be false. This year's cohort might differ in
some way that matters. The premises make the conclusion likely; they do not guarantee it.

**The test.** Ask: *do the premises claim to guarantee the conclusion, or only to make it probable?*

- If the argument claims a guarantee, it is deductive, and the test is **validity**: does the
  conclusion actually follow from the premises?
- If the argument claims probability, it is inductive, and the test is **strength**: do the
  premises actually make the conclusion likely?

<Figure id="fig-U3-2" kind="flowchart" src="/img/figures/efmp-304/unit-03/fig-U3-2.svg" alt="Flowchart for deciding whether an argument is deductive or inductive, starting from 'do the premises claim to guarantee the conclusion?' and branching to the appropriate test." width={780} height={520} />

**Common misconception 1: "a deductive argument is a better argument than an inductive one."** It is
not better; it makes a different claim. Most arguments a teacher meets in a staff meeting are
inductive, and that is appropriate: a teacher generalising from last year's results, or comparing
their school to a neighbouring one, is not claiming a guarantee. Judging an inductive argument for
not being certain is like criticising a weather forecast for not being a calendar.

**Common misconception 2: "if the conclusion turns out to be false, the argument was deductively
invalid."** A false conclusion does not tell you the argument was invalid. A deductive argument
is invalid only if the conclusion does not follow from the premises. A valid deductive argument
with a false premise can produce a false conclusion. The clerk's argument is valid even if, say,
Ayesha actually paid and the register is wrong. Validity is about the **link** between premises
and conclusion, not about whether the premises are true.

### What the wrong test produces

The reason the distinction matters is that the two tests are not interchangeable, and swapping
them produces exactly the staffroom error this unit is designed to prevent.

**Judging a deductive argument by the inductive test.** A parent argues: "My child's teacher gave
low marks in the previous school too. This teacher is unfair." That is an inductive generalisation
from one case. If you attack it by demanding certainty, "you cannot know that from one case", you
have judged it by the wrong test. The argument never claimed certainty. The right question is
whether one case is enough to make the generalisation likely. It usually is not, but that is a
different criticism.

**Judging an inductive argument by the deductive test.** A teacher argues: "All the pupils on the
fee list have paid. Ayesha is on the list. So she has paid." That is a deductive argument. If you
accept it because "it sounds reasonable" without checking whether Ayesha is actually on the list,
you have judged it by the wrong test. The argument claims a guarantee. If Ayesha is not on the
list, the argument fails, regardless of how reasonable it sounded.

Bassham et al. (2010) treat this as the central reason to teach the distinction: without it, learners
dismiss valid arguments for not being certain and accept strong generalisations without asking
whether the evidence supports them. The two errors are symmetrical, and both come from applying
the wrong test.

### A worked example before you try it

Here is the move performed once, slowly, on a staffroom sentence.

> "Every class that Miss Fatima taught last year scored above the school average. She is teaching
> grade 8 this year. So grade 8 will probably score above the school average this year."

**Step 1 - which kind?** The conclusion is "probably". The premises do not guarantee it; they make
it likely. This is an **inductive** argument.

**Step 2 - what is the test?** The test is **strength**: do the premises make the conclusion
likely? That depends on whether last year's classes are like this year's grade 8 in the respects
that matter. If Miss Fatima taught grade 7 last year and grade 8 is very different, the argument
is weak. If she taught grade 8 last year under similar conditions, it is stronger.

**Step 3 - judge it.** The argument is plausible but leans on an unstated premise: that this year's
grade 8 is like last year's classes. That premise may or may not be true. The argument is not
invalid (it never claimed a guarantee); it is **weak** if the similarity does not hold.

Now you try the same three steps in the activity.

## Activity: Which test applies?

Work in pairs. About 25 minutes.

Each pair takes the five arguments below. For each one, decide together whether it is **deductive**
(premises claim to guarantee the conclusion) or **inductive** (premises claim to make the conclusion
probable). Write one sentence naming the test that applies.

1. "The school rule says late arrivals get a warning. Ahmed arrived late. So Ahmed gets a warning."
2. "Three of the four schools in our union council that added a library saw reading scores improve. We should add one."
3. "If the grant does not arrive by March, the science laboratory cannot be repaired this year. The grant has not arrived. So the laboratory cannot be repaired."
4. "Miss Saira's classes always do well in the board examination. She is taking grade 10 this year. So grade 10 will do well."
5. "The timetable says Friday is a half day. Today is Friday. So we dismiss at noon."

Then swap papers with another pair. For each item the other pair marked as **deductive**, check
whether the conclusion actually follows from the premises. For each item marked as **inductive**,
name one piece of information that would make the conclusion more likely and one that would make it
less likely.

Finish by agreeing, as a whole class, on one argument that could be read either way, and on what
extra information would settle it.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the two kinds of argument in this topic, and state what each claims its premises do.
2. *(Understand)* In two or three sentences, explain the difference between the deductive test and the inductive test. Do not use the word "logic" in your answer.
3. *(Understand)* A trainee says, "A deductive argument is just a stronger kind of inductive argument." Give two reasons that is wrong.
4. *(Apply)* Classify this argument and name the test that applies: "All pupils in the scholarship list have submitted their forms. Bilal is on the list. So Bilal has submitted his form."
5. *(Apply)* Classify this argument and name the test that applies: "The last five times we held exams in the main hall, the results were better. We should hold them there again."

## Summary

A deductive argument claims its premises guarantee the conclusion; the test is validity. An
inductive argument claims its premises make the conclusion probable; the test is strength. The two
tests are not interchangeable. Judging a deductive argument by the inductive test means demanding a
guarantee from an argument that never offered one; judging an inductive argument by the deductive
test means accepting a generalisation because it sounded convincing. The skill is to name the kind
first, then apply the matching test.

## Try this at your practicum school

Listen for one argument in a staff meeting or a conversation this week. Write it down as closely as
you can to the words used. Then do three things with it: classify it as deductive or inductive, name
the test that applies, and say whether it passes that test. Do not tell the speaker. Bring the note
to your tutorial.

## Summative task

Here are two arguments about the same pupil, one deductive and one inductive.

> **A.** "All pupils on the scholarship list have submitted their documents. Ayesha is on the list.
> So Ayesha has submitted her documents."
>
> **B.** "The last four pupils from this neighbourhood who attended regularly all passed the board
> examination. Ayesha is from this neighbourhood and attends regularly. So Ayesha will probably
> pass."

Write 150 to 200 words that (a) classifies each argument as deductive or inductive, (b) states the
test that applies to each, (c) judges each argument by its own test, and (d) says what would go
wrong if you judged argument A by the inductive test or argument B by the deductive test.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Classification* | 0-2: both correctly classified / one correct / neither. |
| *Test named* | 0-2: correct test for each / correct for one / wrong for both. |
| *Judgement by the right test* | 0-3: each argument judged by its own test with a reason tied to its specific premises (**Analyze**: the judgement turns on the actual content of the argument, not on general plausibility) / a judgement with general reasons / a verdict asserted. |
| *The swapped-test error* | 0-2: names a specific error for each swap / names a general error / none. |
| *Clarity* | 0-1: within the word band and organised under the four parts / not. |
