## A real classroom situation

A teacher says in a staff meeting: "Every class I have taught since I started at this school has
had at least one pupil who could not read at grade level. So the new grade 6 I am taking will have
one too."

Nobody disagrees with the facts. The question is whether the facts support the conclusion. That
question is the subject of this topic.

## Explanation

### Common patterns of inductive reasoning

Topic 3.1 said an inductive argument claims that its premises make the conclusion probable. This
topic names the patterns that do that job. Four cover almost everything a teacher meets. Each has
a characteristic way of getting stronger and a characteristic way of getting weaker, and the skill
is to ask the right question for the pattern you are facing.

<Figure id="fig-U3-5" kind="table" src="/img/figures/efmp-304/unit-03/fig-U3-5.svg" alt="Table of four inductive patterns: generalisation, analogy, causal inference and prediction, each with what makes it stronger and what makes it weaker." width={880} height={520} />

#### 1. Generalisation

The pattern: some number of observed cases share a property; so the next case (or all cases of
that kind) probably share it too.

> "Every class I have taught has had at least one non-reader. So my new grade 6 will have one."

*What makes it stronger:* more cases, cases that vary (different grades, different years), and no
known counter-example. Twenty classes over five years is stronger than three classes in one term.

*What makes it weaker:* few cases, cases that are all alike in some other respect, or a known
counter-example. "Every class I taught last year" is weaker than "every class over five years"
because one year might have been unusual.

**The question to ask:** how many cases, how varied, and is there a counter-example the arguer has
not addressed?

#### 2. Argument from analogy

The pattern: two things are alike in some respects; so they are probably alike in another respect.

> "The school we visited in Sukkur used a reading period and their scores improved. We are like them
> in size and intake. So a reading period would probably help us too."

*What makes it stronger:* many relevant similarities, few relevant differences, and the similarity
cited is the one that matters for the conclusion. If both schools have similar pupil backgrounds
and teacher qualifications, the analogy is stronger.

*What makes it weaker:* a relevant difference. If Sukkur had a librarian and you do not, the reading
period may not work the same way. The difference is directly relevant to the conclusion.

**The question to ask:** what is the relevant similarity, and is there a difference that breaks the
comparison? Topic 2.3 asked the same question about arguments from analogy; this topic adds the
inductive test: even a good analogy only makes the conclusion probable.

#### 3. Causal inference

The pattern: two things are associated; so one probably causes the other.

> "Classes that had a morning assembly with physical exercise had better attendance. So exercise
> probably causes better attendance."

*What makes it stronger:* the association is consistent, the cause precedes the effect, there is a
plausible mechanism, and other possible causes have been ruled out. If every class that exercised
had better attendance, and classes that did not exercise did not, the causal claim is stronger.

*What makes it weaker:* an alternative cause. Perhaps the classes that exercised also had a
teacher who took the register more carefully. The exercise and the better attendance might both be
caused by the teacher's diligence. This is the **alternative cause** problem, and it is the most
common weakness in causal reasoning about schools.

**The question to ask:** is there an alternative cause that could explain the association?

#### 4. Prediction

The pattern: things have gone a certain way up to now; so they will probably continue that way.

> "Enrolment has risen every year for the past five years. So it will probably rise next year."

*What makes it stronger:* a long, consistent trend with no obvious reason for it to change. Five
years of steady rise with no policy change or demographic shift is a reasonable basis for
prediction.

*What makes it weaker:* a short trend, or a known change in circumstances. If a new school opened
nearby this year, the trend may not continue. Predictions are only as stable as the conditions
that produced the trend.

**The question to ask:** how long has the trend held, and has anything changed that might break it?

### The common failure: hasty generalisation

All four patterns fail in the same way when the arguer draws a conclusion from too little evidence.
"Hasty generalisation" is the name for it, and it is the inductive near-miss. A teacher who says "I
taught one class of sixty last year and the results were fine, so class size does not matter" has
generalised from one case. A teacher who says "the academy next door starts early and their results
are better, so we should start early" has drawn an analogy on one similarity. Both are hasty.

The cure is always the same: **more evidence, and more varied evidence.** One case can refute a
claim that something is universal. It cannot establish one.

### A worked example before you try it

Here is the move performed once, slowly, on a staffroom sentence.

> "Three of the four schools in our union council that introduced a Saturday coaching class saw
> their matriculation results improve the following year. We should introduce one."

**Step 1 - which pattern?** Generalisation (from three schools to a recommendation for this one) with
a causal claim (the Saturday class caused the improvement).

**Step 2 - how strong is it?** Three schools is a small sample. The argument needs the unstated
premise that nothing else relevant differed between the schools that added the class and those
that did not. That premise is doing most of the work.

**Step 3 - what would change your judgement?** A fourth school that added the class and saw no
improvement would weaken the argument. Evidence that the three schools also hired a new teacher at
the same time would weaken it further by supplying an alternative cause.

Now you try the same three steps in the activity.

## Activity: Strengthen or weaken?

Work in pairs. About 30 minutes.

Each pair takes the four arguments below. For each one, name the **pattern** (generalisation,
analogy, causal inference or prediction) and say whether it is **strong or weak** as stated. Then
write one sentence naming a piece of information that would make it **stronger** and one that would
make it **weaker**.

1. "Every year we have held the annual speech contest, at least one pupil from grade 9 has won a district prize. So a grade 9 pupil will win this year."
2. "The school in Nawabshah that started a parent reading group saw reading scores rise. We have a similar parent community. So a reading group would help us too."
3. "Pupils who ate the school breakfast had better punctuality than those who did not. So the breakfast probably improves punctuality."
4. "Our pass rate has been above the district average for four years. So it will be above the district average this year."

Then swap papers with another pair. For each argument, check whether the "weaker" information your
partners named genuinely attacks the premise the argument leans on, or merely changes the subject.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the four inductive patterns in this topic and, for each, state in a few words what makes it stronger.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why an argument from analogy is tested differently from a deductive argument, even when both compare two cases.
3. *(Apply)* Classify this argument and say whether it is strong or weak: "The last three teachers who transferred to that school asked to come back within a year. So the next teacher who transfers there will probably want to come back."
4. *(Apply)* A teacher argues: "Every time I have used group work, pupils have understood better. So group work causes better understanding." Name the pattern and say what alternative cause a critic should look for.
5. *(Analyze)* "The school next door has a smaller budget and better results. So spending more does not improve results." Name the pattern, say whether it is strong or weak, and say what the arguer has not considered (**Analyze**: the criticism turns on what the argument has left out, not on whether you agree with the conclusion).

## Summary

Inductive reasoning fails in a characteristic way: the hasty generalisation, the broken analogy, the
alternative cause, the trend that meets a changed circumstance. The four patterns are
generalisation, analogy, causal inference and prediction. Each has a question that tests its
strength: how many cases and how varied; what relevant difference; what alternative cause; what
changed circumstance. The skill is to ask that question before deciding whether the argument is
convincing.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can name the four inductive patterns and recognise each in a staffroom argument.
- [ ] I can state what makes each pattern stronger and what makes it weaker.
- [ ] I can ask the right question for the pattern I am facing.
- [ ] I can name an alternative cause that would weaken a causal claim about a school.
- [ ] I can distinguish a strong generalisation from a hasty one.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find one inductive argument in a meeting or conversation this week. Write it down. Then do three
things: name the pattern, say whether it is strong or weak, and name one piece of evidence that
would most change your judgement. Bring the note to your tutorial.

## Summative task

Read these two arguments about the same decision.

> **A.** "Four of the five schools in our district that started a morning reading period saw their
> language scores improve within two years. We should start one."
>
> **B.** "Our neighbour school runs a morning reading period and their language scores are higher
> than ours. We should start one."

Write 200 to 250 words that (a) names the pattern in each argument, (b) says which is stronger and
why, (c) names the specific weakness in the weaker argument, and (d) says what evidence would
most strengthen the weaker one.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Pattern naming* | 0-2: both correctly named / one correct / neither. |
| *Strength comparison* | 0-3: a verdict given and justified by a named feature of each argument (**Analyze**: the comparison turns on sample size, relevance or alternative cause, not on which conclusion the writer prefers) / a verdict with general reasons / a verdict asserted. |
| *Weakness in the weaker argument* | 0-2: names a specific weakness tied to the pattern / a general doubt / none. |
| *Strengthening evidence* | 0-2: names evidence that genuinely bears on the weakness / names something merely interesting / none. |
| *Clarity* | 0-1: within the word band and organised under the four parts / not. |
