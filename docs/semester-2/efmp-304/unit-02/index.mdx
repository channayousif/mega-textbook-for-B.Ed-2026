# Recognizing and Analyzing Arguments

Unit 1 ended with a definition: an argument is a claim plus at least one reason offered to support
it. That definition is easy to state and surprisingly hard to use. Most of the writing a teacher
actually meets - a circular from the district office, a parent's letter, a textbook paragraph, a
trainer's slide - looks like it is arguing when it is only asserting, or explaining, or describing.
If you treat all of it as argument you will waste your attention. If you treat none of it as
argument you will accept things you should have questioned.

This unit is the practical half of that skill. It teaches you to look at a piece of real school
prose and say three things about it: whether it argues at all, what its conclusion is, and which
of its sentences are doing the supporting work.

## Unit learning outcomes

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. Distinguish an argument from an explanation, and say which test separates them.
2. Set out an argument in its classical form, with its premises above and its conclusion below.
3. Use the rhetorical triangle to explain why the same claim is argued differently to a head teacher, a colleague and a parent.
4. Tell an issue question from an information question, and say what each kind can and cannot settle.
5. Name the common types of argument and say what each one offers and where it typically fails.
6. Locate the premises and the conclusion in a real school document, including a premise nobody wrote down.
7. Decide, with reasons, whether a given passage argues, explains, describes or merely asserts.

## Prerequisite knowledge

This unit assumes Unit 1's basic units of reasoning: you can already tell a statement from a
question, and you know that an argument is a claim with at least one reason behind it (Unit 1,
[Topic 1.4](../unit-01/topic-04)). Those four terms are not retaught here. If "statement", "claim",
"issue" and "argument" do not yet feel steady, read Topic 1.4 again before you start.

Nothing else is assumed. No logic, no symbols, no philosophy.

## In this unit

1. [Arguing, explaining, and the shape of an argument](./topic-01) - the test that separates an argument from an explanation, and the classical form an argument takes.
2. [Who is speaking, to whom, about what](./topic-02) - the rhetorical triangle, and the difference between an issue question and an information question.
3. [Types of argument](./topic-03) - the common types, what each offers, and where each typically fails.
4. [Finding the premises, the conclusion, and the non-arguments](./topic-04) - locating the parts in real text, including the unstated premise, and recognising what is not an argument at all.

## How to use this unit

Each topic is one learning cycle: a classroom situation, the teaching, an activity, retrieval
questions, a summary, a self-check, a practicum task and a summative task. Work through them in
order, because Topic 2.4 uses the vocabulary the first three topics build.

This unit rewards practice on real text more than re-reading. Keep a school document beside you as
you read - a circular, a notice, a page of a textbook, a message from a parent. Every time the
explanation introduces a move, try it on that document before you move on. Analysing discourse you
found yourself, rather than tidy textbook examples, is what makes the skill transfer (Malone, 2003).

The unit ends with a summative assessment that draws the four topics together.
