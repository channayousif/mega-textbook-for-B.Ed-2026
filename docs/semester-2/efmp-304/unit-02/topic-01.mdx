## A real classroom situation

Grade 8 at a government boys' high school in Hyderabad has just sat its mid-term mathematics paper.
Nineteen of thirty-two pupils have failed. In the staffroom the subject teacher, Mr Soomro, says:
"The class failed because the paper was hard." The head teacher, Mr Jatoi, hears the same sentence
and takes it as a request. He replies, "So you are saying we should let them sit it again."

Mr Soomro had not said that. He had given a cause. Mr Jatoi had heard a case. The same eight words
did two completely different jobs, and neither man noticed the switch.

<Figure id="fig-U2-1" kind="table" src="/img/figures/efmp-304/unit-02/fig-U2-1.svg" alt="Table comparing an argument and an explanation across four rows: what each takes for granted, what each is trying to achieve, what question each answers, and the test that separates them." width={780} height={470} />

That switch is the most common misreading in professional life, and it is the subject of this
topic. Once you can tell a cause from a case, you can also set out the case properly, which is the
second half of the topic.

## Explanation

### Argument distinguished from explanation

An <Glossary term="Argument" /> offers reasons to make you **accept** a claim you might otherwise
doubt. An <Glossary term="Explanation" /> offers reasons to make you **understand** something both
sides already accept.

That is the whole distinction, and it turns on one question: **is the thing being supported in
dispute?**

Look again at Mr Soomro's sentence.

> "The class failed because the paper was hard."

Nobody in the staffroom doubts that the class failed. The mark sheet is on the table. So the
"because" is not trying to win agreement about the failure; it is trying to account for it. That
makes it an **explanation**. The failure is the thing explained, and the hard paper is the proposed
cause.

Now read the same material as Mr Jatoi heard it.

> "The paper was harder than the syllabus allows. A paper that goes beyond the syllabus does not
> measure what was taught. So the class should be allowed to sit it again."

Here the final sentence is genuinely in dispute. Letting a class re-sit is a decision the head can
refuse. The two sentences above it are offered to make him accept it. That makes this an
**argument**, and the thing in dispute is its conclusion.

Notice what changed. The facts did not. What changed is the **job** the sentences were doing.

**The test.** Ask: *is the supported statement something the audience already grants?*

- If yes, and the reasons are there to make it intelligible, it is an explanation.
- If no, and the reasons are there to make it acceptable, it is an argument.

A second check helps when the first is unclear. Ask what a reasonable objection would look like. To
an explanation you object, "that is not actually why it happened." To an argument you object, "your
reasons do not establish that." Those are different objections, and noticing which one you want to
make tells you what you are reading.

**Common misconception 1: "if it has 'because' in it, it is an argument."** This is the single most
expensive mistake in this unit. "Because", "since" and "as" are ordinary causal words in Urdu and in
English, and school prose is full of them. "The gate is locked because the chowkidar has gone for
lunch" argues nothing. Indicator words are **evidence** about structure, never proof of it (Malone,
2003, treats this as one of the genuinely hard problems in deciding whether a text argues at all).
The reliable test is the one above: was the supported statement in dispute?

**Common misconception 2: "an explanation is a weak argument."** It is not a weak argument; it is a
different kind of thing, judged by a different standard. A good explanation is **true and
illuminating**. A good argument is **well supported**. A district circular that explains why
examinations moved from March to April is not failing to argue. It was never trying to. Marking it
down for that is like criticising a timetable for not rhyming.

This matters professionally. Most official school prose explains rather than argues, and that is
appropriate: a circular announces a decision already taken by someone with the authority to take
it. The skill is to notice when a document that looks official is in fact arguing, because then you
are entitled to ask whether the reasons hold.

### The classical form of an argument

Once you know a passage argues, you can set it out in **classical form** (also called standard
form): every reason listed above a line, the claim they support written below it.

A <Glossary term="Premise" /> is a statement offered in support of the conclusion. A
<Glossary term="Conclusion" /> is the statement the premises are offered to support. There is no
third part. An argument is premises plus conclusion, and nothing else is required.

Here is Mr Soomro's case set out properly:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Premise 1** | The paper was harder than the syllabus allows. |
| **Premise 2** | A paper that goes beyond the syllabus does not measure what was taught. |
| **Conclusion** | The class should be allowed to sit the paper again. |

<Figure id="fig-U2-2" kind="diagram" src="/img/figures/efmp-304/unit-02/fig-U2-2.svg" alt="The classical form of an argument drawn as two premises stacked above a horizontal inference bar with the conclusion beneath it, filled in with a staffroom argument that a mathematics paper went beyond the syllabus so the class should re-sit it." width={780} height={470} />

Three things are worth noticing about that layout, because each one is a skill.

**1. The line is not decoration.** The horizontal line is the **inference**: the step from the
premises to the conclusion. Drawing it forces you to ask whether the step is any good. In this case
it is reasonable, because Premise 2 connects "beyond the syllabus" to "does not measure what was
taught", and a re-sit is the natural remedy for a measurement that failed.

**2. Order on the page is not order in the argument.** Mr Soomro could have said the conclusion
first: "They should re-sit it. The paper went beyond the syllabus." The classical form puts premises
first by convention, not because that is how people talk. You will meet this again in Topic 2.4.

**3. Setting it out changes what you can see.** Before it was written down, the argument was a
sentence you either agreed with or did not. Written down, it has joints. You can accept Premise 1
and reject Premise 2. You can accept both and still question whether a re-sit follows rather than,
say, a re-mark. This is why standard form is taught in every critical-thinking course: it moves
disagreement from "I don't buy it" to "here is the part I don't buy."

Research on teaching argument structure supports the same move. Qin (2019) found that university
students given explicit instruction in a structured model of argument, and asked to label its
elements in published writing, produced noticeably more complete arguments afterwards: they began
including opposing views and rebuttals that were "almost non-existent" in their earlier work. The
study was carried out with sixteen students in a Turkish English-as-a-foreign-language classroom, so
it is evidence about explicit structural teaching, not about Pakistani secondary schools in
particular. Still, the mechanism is the one at work here. Naming the parts is what lets a writer
notice a missing part.

### A worked example before you try it

Here is the move performed once, slowly, on a different staffroom sentence.

> "Attendance in grade 6 has dropped since the second shift started, and a pupil who misses the
> first period misses the explanation. We should move grade 6 back to the morning shift."

**Step 1 - does it argue?** The last sentence is a proposal the head could refuse. It is in
dispute. So yes, this argues.

**Step 2 - which sentence is the conclusion?** "We should move grade 6 back to the morning shift."
Everything else is offered to get you there.

**Step 3 - set it out.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Premise 1** | Attendance in grade 6 has dropped since the second shift started. |
| **Premise 2** | A pupil who misses the first period misses the explanation. |
| **Conclusion** | Grade 6 should move back to the morning shift. |

**Step 4 - look at the joints.** Premise 1 is a factual claim you could check against the register.
Premise 2 is a general claim about how lessons work. The inference also leans on something unsaid:
that the shift is what caused the drop. We will come back to those unsaid parts in Topic 2.4.

Now you try the same four steps in the activity.

## Activity: Cause or case?

Work in pairs. About 25 minutes.

Each pair takes the five sentences below. For each one, decide together whether it is an
**explanation** (a cause offered for something both sides accept) or an **argument** (reasons
offered for something in dispute). Write one sentence of justification naming the test you used.

1. "Enrolment fell this year because three families moved to Karachi."
2. "Enrolment fell this year, and the two schools that kept their enrolment both run a breakfast programme, so we should start one."
3. "The science laboratory is locked because the keys are with the storekeeper."
4. "Pupils handle apparatus more carefully when a teacher is standing beside them, and our laboratory sessions have no second adult, so we need a laboratory assistant."
5. "Homework is set on Thursdays because Friday is a half day."

Then swap papers with another pair. For each item the other pair marked as an **argument**, set it
out in classical form: premises above, a line, the conclusion below. Where you disagree about the
classification, say which of the two tests settles it.

Finish by agreeing, as a whole class, on one sentence that was genuinely ambiguous and on what extra
information would settle it.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the two parts of an argument in classical form, and state what the horizontal line between them stands for.
2. *(Understand)* In two or three sentences, explain the test that separates an argument from an explanation. Do not use the word "because" in your answer.
3. *(Understand)* A trainee says, "An explanation is just an argument that is not very convincing." Give two reasons that is wrong.
4. *(Apply)* Classify this sentence and justify your classification in one sentence: "The water cooler is empty because the pump was off all morning."
5. *(Apply)* Set out this reasoning in classical form: "Pupils copy from each other when the desks are pushed together. Copying makes the mid-term marks useless for planning. The desks should be separated during tests."

## Summary

An argument offers reasons to make you accept something in dispute; an explanation offers reasons to
make you understand something already accepted. The test is whether the supported statement is
granted by the audience, and indicator words such as "because" are clues rather than proof. Once you
know a passage argues, set it out in classical form - premises above a line, conclusion below -
because that layout turns a vague disagreement into a specific one, and shows you exactly which part
of the reasoning you want to question.

- [ ] I can state the test that separates an argument from an explanation, and apply it to a sentence I have not seen before.
- [ ] I can explain why an explanation is not a weak argument.
- [ ] I can set out a short piece of staffroom reasoning in classical form, with premises above a line and the conclusion below.
- [ ] I can say why writing an argument in classical form shows me something the original sentence hid.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find one circular, notice or written message in the school this week - a notice board item, a
message sent home, a note from the head teacher. Read it twice. The first time, list every statement
it asserts. The second time, decide for each statement whether it is argued for, explained, or
simply announced.

Write down your finding in two lines, and bring the document to your next tutorial. Most official
school prose turns out to explain and announce rather than argue; if yours argues, you have found a
genuinely useful example and should keep it.

## Summative task

Here is a passage from a letter a head teacher sent to parents of grade 9:

> "Results in grade 9 have slipped this year. The syllabus was completed late because two teachers
> were on long leave in November. Pupils who miss the revision weeks score lower in the board
> pattern. The school will therefore run extra revision classes on Saturdays, and we ask for your
> cooperation in sending pupils."

Write 150 to 200 words that (a) identifies every statement in the passage that is **explained**
rather than argued, (b) states the passage's conclusion and sets out the argument for it in
classical form, and (c) names one premise that the passage relies on but does not write down.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Separates explanation from argument* | 0-3: every explained statement correctly identified and none misclassified / most correct / one half correct / no reliable separation. |
| *Classical form* | 0-2: conclusion correctly identified and premises set out above a line / conclusion correct but premises muddled or incomplete / conclusion wrong. |
| *Reasoning about the unstated premise* | 0-3: names a premise the passage genuinely needs and explains what the argument loses without it (**Analyze**: the judgement is tied to specific wording in the passage, not to general plausibility) / names a plausible premise without explaining its role / none. |
| *Clarity* | 0-2: within the word band and organised under the three parts / unclear or over length. |
