## A real classroom situation

A government high school in Larkana has one grade 9 section of fifty-four pupils. At a staff
meeting, four teachers argue for splitting it into two sections. They do not give the same argument.

Mr Abro: "A class of fifty-four cannot be taught properly. Fifty-four pupils in one room means a
teacher cannot see every notebook in a week, and work that is never seen is work that is never
corrected. We should split it."

Mrs Shaikh: "Last year 9-A had thirty-one pupils and the pass rate was seventy-one per cent. 9-B
had fifty-two and the pass rate was forty-eight per cent. Split it."

Mr Memon: "A doctor is not asked to see fifty patients in one hour. A teacher with fifty-four pupils
is in the same position. Split it."

Miss Pathan: "If we split it, we need one more room and one more teacher period allocation. We have
the empty room next to the laboratory and Mr Abro has two free periods. So splitting it is
possible, and since the alternative is doing nothing, we should split it."

All four reach the same conclusion. All four support it differently, and so all four can fail
differently.

Knowing the type tells you which question to ask next. That is what this topic is for.

## Explanation

### Types of argument

Introductory critical thinking distinguishes a small number of recurring argument types by **what
the premises offer** (Bassham et al., 2010). The names vary between books; the patterns do not. Four
cover almost everything a teacher meets in a school.

#### 1. Argument from evidence

The premises offer observations, counts or measurements. Mrs Shaikh's argument is this type, and so
is most of what arrives from a district office or a research summary.

*What it offers:* a record of what has actually happened.

*Where it typically fails:* the evidence is real but does not establish what it is said to
establish. Mrs Shaikh's two pass rates are almost certainly accurate. They still do not show that
size caused the gap, because 9-A and 9-B differed in more than size. They had different teachers,
and sections are often formed by prior attainment in the first place. Her argument needs a premise
she has not supplied: that nothing else relevant differed. Topic 2.4 calls that an unstated premise;
Unit 3 gives it a name and a test.

A special case worth naming now: evidence from a **single** case. "I taught a class of sixty last
year and the results were fine" is evidence, and it is one observation. One observation can refute a
claim that something is impossible. It cannot establish a general pattern.

#### 2. Argument from analogy

The premises say that two things are alike in some respects, and conclude that they are alike in
another. Mr Memon's doctor comparison is this type.

*What it offers:* a judgement you already accept in a case you find easier, transferred to the case
in hand.

*Where it typically fails:* the two cases differ in exactly the respect that matters. Mr Memon's
analogy is reasonable on attention: a doctor and a teacher both have a fixed amount of it. It is
weaker on structure, because teaching is partly collective in a way consultation is not; fifty-four
pupils can all hear one explanation, where fifty patients cannot share one diagnosis. To test any
analogy, ask: **what is the relevant similarity, and is there a difference that breaks it?**

#### 3. Argument from authority

The premises say that a qualified source asserts the conclusion. Nobody in the meeting used it, but
it would have sounded like: "The district education officer says fifty is the maximum, so we should
split it."

*What it offers:* a short cut. You cannot verify everything yourself, and relying on competent
sources is reasonable and unavoidable.

*Where it typically fails:* the authority is outside its field, is not disinterested, or is being
reported rather than quoted. A textbook salesman is an authority on his catalogue, not on learning.
A trainer with a method to sell is not disinterested about it. And "they say the officer said" is not
the officer.

#### 4. Policy argument

The premises establish that an action is possible, that it will produce a result worth having, and
that the alternatives are worse. Miss Pathan's argument is this type, and most arguments in a staff
meeting are.

*What it offers:* a route from a judgement to a decision. It is the only type that can conclude in
"we should".

*Where it typically fails:* one of its three legs is missing and nobody notices. Miss Pathan
establishes that the split is **possible**. She does not establish that it is **better**, and her
last clause ("the alternative is doing nothing") quietly rules out every other option, including a
second teacher in the same room or a split for English only. A policy argument that proves
feasibility and calls it justification is the most common incomplete argument in school life.

### Classify first, judge second

There is a real temptation to skip classification and go straight to "is this convincing?" Resist
it, for a reason that is worth stating carefully.

Yu and Zenker (2022) examine how analysts decide how an argument's premises fit together, and argue
that the standard methods have the order wrong: they lean on judgements about how strong the
argument is in order to settle what its structure is, which puts, in their phrase, the cart before
the horse. Their subject is a narrower technical question than ours, but the warning transfers
exactly. If you decide an argument is weak and then describe its structure to match, you have
described your own reaction, not the argument.

So the working order is: name the type, name what that type is supposed to supply, then check
whether this instance supplies it.

Run it on Mrs Shaikh's argument. **Type:** argument from evidence. **What that type must supply:**
observations plus a reason to think they bear on the conclusion. **Does it?** It supplies the
observations and not the reason. Verdict: incomplete, and now you can say precisely which part is
missing. You got there without ever asking whether you liked her conclusion.

**Common misconception: "an argument with numbers in it is stronger than one without."** Mrs
Shaikh's argument has two percentages and is the least complete of the four. Mr Abro's has no
numbers at all and is the tightest, because his premise about unseen notebooks really does bear on
his conclusion. Numbers are a kind of premise, not a grade. The question is never "are there
figures?" but "do these premises support this conclusion?" Unit 3 turns that question into a
formal test.

A second, quieter error sits beside it: assuming an argument belongs to only one type. Mr Abro's
argument has an evidence-shaped premise and a policy conclusion. Mixed arguments are normal. Name
every type you can see in it, and test each part against its own standard.

## Activity: Four ways to argue one thing

Work in groups of four. About 30 minutes.

Take this claim: **"This school should hold parent meetings twice a term instead of once."**

Each member of the group builds an argument for it using **one** type, and nobody may use a type
somebody else has taken:

1. argument from evidence,
2. argument from analogy,
3. argument from authority,
4. policy argument.

Write three sentences each. Then pass your argument one place to the left. Your job on the argument
you receive is **not** to say whether you agree. It is to:

- name its type;
- state what that type is supposed to supply;
- name the one thing it is missing or the one point where it is weakest.

Report back as a group: which type produced the strongest argument for this particular claim, and
why that type suited this claim.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the four types of argument in this topic and, for each, state in a few words what its premises offer.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why an argument from a single observation can refute a claim but cannot establish a general pattern.
3. *(Understand)* State the two questions you ask to test an argument from analogy.
4. *(Apply)* Classify this argument and name what it is missing: "The academy next door starts at seven and their results are better, so we should start at seven."
5. *(Apply)* A head teacher argues: "We can fit a library in the store room, a library helps reading, and we have no library now, so we should build one." Name the type and say which of its three legs is the weakest as stated.

## Summary

Arguments differ by what their premises offer, and each type fails in its own way: evidence that
does not bear on the conclusion, an analogy broken by a relevant difference, an authority outside
its field or not disinterested, and a policy argument that proves feasibility and calls it
justification. Classify before you judge, because deciding how convincing an argument is before you
have described it means describing your own reaction. And numbers in an argument are premises, not
a mark of strength.

- [ ] I can test an analogy by naming the relevant similarity and looking for a difference that breaks it.
- [ ] I can explain why I should classify an argument before judging it, and why numbers do not by themselves make an argument strong.

## Try this at your practicum school

Listen for one argument in the staffroom or in a meeting this week. Write it down as closely as you
can to the words used.

Then do three things with it: name its type, name what that type is supposed to supply, and write
one sentence saying whether this instance supplied it. Do not tell the speaker. Bring the note to
your tutorial.

## Summative task

Read these two arguments about the same decision.

> **A.** "Three of the four schools in our union council that introduced a Saturday coaching class
> saw their matriculation results improve the following year. We should introduce one."
>
> **B.** "A cricket team that practises six days a week beats a team that practises five. Pupils are
> the same. We should add a Saturday class."

Write 180 to 220 words that (a) names the type of each argument, (b) states for each what that type
must supply in order to succeed, (c) judges which of the two gives the better support for the shared
conclusion, and (d) names one additional piece of information that would most change your judgement.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Classification* | 0-2: both types correctly named / one correct / neither. |
| *States what each type must supply* | 0-2: correct for both types / correct for one / generic. |
| *Comparative judgement* | 0-3: a verdict given and justified by the specific weakness of the losing argument, quoting its wording (**Analyze**: the comparison turns on a named feature of each argument, not on which conclusion the writer prefers) / a verdict with general reasons / a verdict asserted. |
| *The information that would change the judgement* | 0-2: names something that genuinely bears on the weaker premise / names something merely interesting / none. |
| *Clarity* | 0-1: within the word band and organised under the four parts / not. |
