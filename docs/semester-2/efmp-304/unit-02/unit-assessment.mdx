## Unit summary

This unit turned Unit 1's definition of an argument into a working skill. The single idea running
through all four topics is this: **an argument is a specific object with premises and a conclusion,
most school prose is not one, and you cannot judge reasoning until you can say which kind of thing
you are looking at.**

- **Topic 2.1** gave the test that separates an argument from an explanation: is the supported
  statement something the audience already grants? It also gave classical form, which turns a vague
  disagreement into a specific one by putting premises above a line and the conclusion below.
- **Topic 2.2** gave two tools for reading. The rhetorical triangle (speaker, audience, subject)
  explains why the same leave request is argued to a head teacher, traded with a colleague and
  announced to parents. The issue-question test decides whether an argument is needed at all: if
  checking settles it, no argument is required, and if weighing is unavoidable, one is.
- **Topic 2.3** named the four common types by what their premises offer, and the characteristic
  failure of each: evidence that does not bear on the conclusion, an analogy broken by a relevant
  difference, an authority outside its field or not disinterested, and a policy argument that proves
  feasibility and calls it justification.
- **Topic 2.4** gave the mechanics. Indicator words are clues, not rules; the reliable method is to
  ask what the passage is trying to get you to accept and which statements are offered in support.
  The premise carrying most of the weight is often the one nobody wrote down, and most school prose
  describes, explains, asserts or instructs rather than arguing.

Three habits should survive this unit. First, classify before you judge, because deciding how
convincing something is before describing it means describing your own reaction. Second, the quality
of an argument is a relationship between its premises and its conclusion, so who said it and whether
you like where it lands are both irrelevant. Third, recovering an unstated premise honestly, in the
weakest form that makes the argument work, is where most of the real analytical work lies. Unit 3
takes the arguments you can now find apart and gives you the tests that say whether they succeed.

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

Choose the single best answer for each question.

1. *(Remember)* What are the two parts of an argument in classical form?
   - A) A topic and an opinion
   - B) Premises and a conclusion
   - C) A cause and an effect
   - D) A question and an answer

2. *(Understand)* Which question reliably separates an argument from an explanation?
   - A) Does the passage contain the word "because"?
   - B) Is the passage longer than three sentences?
   - C) Is the supported statement something the audience already grants?
   - D) Does the passage come from an official source?

3. *(Apply)* "The staffroom fan is not working because the switch is burnt." This passage is best classified as:
   - A) An argument, because a reason is given
   - B) An explanation, because nobody disputes that the fan is not working
   - C) An assertion, because no evidence is offered
   - D) A policy argument, because it implies a repair

4. *(Remember)* The three corners of the rhetorical triangle are:
   - A) Premise, conclusion, inference
   - B) Speaker, audience, subject
   - C) Claim, evidence, warrant
   - D) Logic, emotion, credibility

5. *(Apply)* A teacher asks, "How many pupils in 7-B were absent last Monday?" This is:
   - A) An issue question, because absence is a contested topic
   - B) An information question, because the register settles it
   - C) An issue question, because reasons could be given
   - D) Neither, because it is not a statement

6. *(Understand)* An argument from analogy is tested by asking:
   - A) Whether the two cases are alike in the respect that matters, and whether a relevant difference breaks the comparison
   - B) Whether the person making it has relevant qualifications
   - C) Whether the premises contain numbers
   - D) Whether the conclusion is one most teachers would accept

7. *(Apply)* "We can fit the extra section in the store room, and doing nothing is not an option, so we should split grade 9." This argument's weakest leg as stated is that it:
   - A) Offers no evidence that anything is wrong
   - B) Establishes that the split is possible without establishing that it is better
   - C) Relies on an authority outside its field
   - D) Draws an analogy that does not hold

8. *(Remember)* Which of these is a conclusion indicator rather than a premise indicator?
   - A) Since
   - B) Given that
   - C) Therefore
   - D) As shown by

9. *(Understand)* An unstated premise is best described as:
   - A) A premise the writer deliberately concealed
   - B) A statement the argument depends on but does not state, usually because it seemed obvious to the writer
   - C) Any premise that appears after the conclusion
   - D) A conclusion that has not yet been proved

10. *(Apply)* "Corporal punishment has no place in a modern school." Standing alone, this sentence is:
    - A) An argument, because it takes a position
    - B) An explanation, because it accounts for a practice
    - C) An assertion, because it makes a disputed claim with nothing offered in support
    - D) A description, because it reports a state of affairs

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

1. *(Understand)* In two sentences, state the test that separates an argument from an explanation, and give one reason indicator words are not a reliable substitute for it.

2. *(Understand)* Explain in two or three sentences why an explanation is not a weak argument. Use one school example.

3. *(Apply)* Set out this reasoning in classical form, labelling each premise and the conclusion: "Pupils copy from each other when the desks are pushed together. Copying makes the mid-term marks useless for planning. The desks should be separated during tests."

4. *(Understand)* A trainee writes one identical paragraph about a broken water cooler and sends it to the head teacher, a colleague and the parents' committee. Using the rhetorical triangle, give two reasons this is likely to be ineffective with at least two of the three.

5. *(Analyze)* "Does streaming work?" sounds like an information question but is not. Explain in three or four sentences why, and name the judgement that has to be made before any evidence can count.

6. *(Understand)* For each of the four argument types in Topic 2.3, state in one clause what its premises offer. List all four.

7. *(Analyze)* "Three of the four schools in our union council that added a Saturday class saw results improve." Name the argument type, and state the premise this argument needs but does not supply.

8. *(Apply)* Give three premise indicator words and three conclusion indicator words, then state in one sentence the test you use when a passage contains none of them.

9. *(Analyze)* A notice reads: "Hira has missed eleven days. The van route should be restored." State the unstated premise this argument depends on, in the weakest form that makes it work, and say in one sentence why that premise is likely to be the point of disagreement.

10. *(Analyze)* Classify each of the following as argument, explanation, description or assertion, with a reason of no more than one clause each: (a) "The hall has two working fans." (b) "Results fell because the syllabus was finished late." (c) "Homework in grade 1 is pointless." (d) "The syllabus was finished late, and late completion lowers results, so we must start earlier."

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. *(Analyze)* Here is a letter from a head teacher to parents of grade 9:

   > "Results in grade 9 have slipped this year. The syllabus was completed late because two
   > teachers were on long leave in November. Pupils who miss the revision weeks score lower in the
   > board pattern. The school will therefore run extra revision classes on Saturdays, and we ask for
   > your cooperation."

   In 200 to 250 words, separate what this letter **explains** from what it **argues**. State its
   conclusion, set the argument out in classical form, and name one premise it relies on without
   writing down.

2. *(Evaluate)* A district circular directs all schools to begin a fifteen-minute morning reading
   period, and supports the direction with the single sentence "Reading improves examination
   performance." In 200 to 250 words, analyse the circular on all three corners of the rhetorical
   triangle, identify the issue question it settles without arguing for it, and judge whether a head
   teacher has been given enough to act on. Justify your judgement from the circular's own wording.

3. *(Evaluate)* Four teachers argue for splitting a grade 9 section of fifty-four pupils: one from
   the number of notebooks a teacher can see in a week, one from last year's pass rates in two
   differently sized sections, one from the comparison with a doctor's patient load, and one from the
   availability of an empty room and a free period. In 250 to 300 words, name the type of each
   argument, state what each type must supply in order to succeed, and rank the four by the quality
   of support they actually give. Your ranking must turn on named features of the arguments, not on
   whether you favour the split.

4. *(Analyze)* Take this staffroom notice:

   > "From Monday the staff register will be kept in the head teacher's office. Several colleagues
   > have been signing in after the first bell. Punctuality is a professional duty. Last month the
   > office recorded fourteen late sign-ins. All staff are requested to cooperate."

   In 250 to 300 words, number the sentences and classify each as premise, conclusion, explanation,
   description, assertion or instruction. State whether the notice contains an argument and set it
   out if it does. Then name the unstated premise it depends on and the one sentence that is asserted
   but never argued, saying what would be needed to complete it.

5. *(Create)* **Integrative.** Choose one real school document you can obtain: a circular, a notice,
   a message sent to parents, or a letter received from a parent. In 300 to 350 words, produce a full
   analysis that (a) extracts every argument the document contains, giving the premises and
   conclusion of each in classical form, (b) names the type of each argument and its characteristic
   risk, (c) identifies everything the document asserts but never argues, and (d) states, with
   reasons, whether the document should have argued for any of those assertions. Attach or quote the
   document. If the document contains no argument at all, say so and defend that finding, which is a
   complete answer rather than a failed one.

## Answers and marking guidance

This section is for self-study. Work each question before reading it.

### MCQ answer key

1. B - an argument is premises plus a conclusion and nothing else is required; A describes a topic and a position, which is not yet an argument.
2. C - the test turns on whether the supported statement is in dispute; A is the misconception the unit corrects.
3. B - nobody disputes that the fan is not working, so the "because" accounts for it rather than arguing for it.
4. B - speaker, audience and subject, with the message held at the centre; C names the elements of a structural model of argument, not the triangle.
5. B - the attendance register settles it by checking, which is the definition of an information question.
6. A - an analogy is tested by the relevant similarity and by looking for a difference that breaks it; the other options test different argument types.
7. B - this is a policy argument that establishes feasibility and treats that as justification, which the unit names as the most common incomplete argument in school life.
8. C - "therefore" introduces a conclusion; the other three introduce premises.
9. B - unstated premises are usually omitted because they seem obvious to the writer, not concealed.
10. C - a disputed claim with nothing offered for it is an assertion, and this is the non-argument most often mistaken for an argument.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. Model: ask whether the supported statement is already granted by the audience; if it is, the passage explains, and if it is not, the passage argues. Indicator words such as "because" mark causes as often as reasons. **Marks (3):** 1 - states the dispute test; 1 - states the consequence for both cases; 1 - gives a reason indicator words mislead.

2. Model: an explanation is a different kind of thing, not a defective argument. It is judged by whether it is true and illuminating, where an argument is judged by whether its premises support its conclusion. Example: a circular explaining why examinations moved from March to April was never trying to argue. **Marks (3):** 1 - names it as a different kind, not a weaker one; 1 - gives the different standard each is judged by; 1 - a school example that fits.

3. Model: P1 - pupils copy when desks are pushed together. P2 - copying makes mid-term marks useless for planning. Conclusion - the desks should be separated during tests. **Marks (3):** 1 - conclusion correctly identified; 1 - both premises listed as premises; 1 - layout places premises above the conclusion with the labels used.

4. Model: the head can grant the repair, so that audience needs an argument with a reason and a cost; the parents can grant nothing and need only to be informed; the colleague can only join, and needs a request. One paragraph shaped for all three will under-argue to the head and over-argue to the parents. **Marks (4):** 1 - names what the audience corner changes; 1 - identifies which audience needs an argument; 1 - identifies an audience that needs only information; 1 - states the consequence for the single paragraph.

5. Model: "work" has to be defined before any evidence counts, and defining it is a judgement, not a lookup: higher mean marks, a narrower spread, better attendance, or pupils' sense of themselves as learners. Different definitions make the same evidence give different answers, so weighing is unavoidable and the question is an issue question wearing factual clothes. **Marks (4):** 1 - identifies "work" as the problem word; 1 - names at least two competing definitions; 1 - states that the choice of definition changes the answer; 1 - concludes that the judgement precedes the evidence.

6. Model: argument from evidence offers observations, counts or measurements; argument from analogy offers a judgement from an easier case transferred to this one; argument from authority offers the assertion of a qualified source; a policy argument offers feasibility, a worthwhile result, and the inadequacy of the alternatives. **Marks (4):** 1 per type correctly characterised.

7. Model: argument from evidence. The missing premise is that nothing else relevant differed between the schools that added the class and those that did not, or equivalently that the Saturday class rather than some other difference produced the improvement. **Marks (3):** 1 - names the type; 2 - states a missing premise that genuinely closes the gap between the observation and the causal conclusion.

8. Model: premise indicators - because, since, given that (also as, for, the reason is). Conclusion indicators - therefore, so, thus (also hence, it follows that, we should). When none appears, ask what the passage is trying to get you to accept, then check which statements are offered in support of it. **Marks (3):** 1 - three correct premise indicators; 1 - three correct conclusion indicators; 1 - states the support test.

9. Model: a school should take reasonable steps to make attendance possible for its enrolled pupils. It is the point of disagreement because a head who refuses is not denying the eleven absences; they are rejecting or narrowing the obligation. **Marks (3):** 1 - states a premise that genuinely bridges the gap; 1 - states it in a form the writer would accept rather than a strawman; 1 - explains why it is the contested part.

10. Model: (a) description, reports a state of affairs nobody disputes; (b) explanation, gives the cause of something accepted; (c) assertion, disputed claim with nothing offered; (d) argument, two statements offered in support of "we must start earlier". **Marks (4):** 1 per item, awarded only when the reason matches the label.

### ERQ rubrics

1. Analytic rubric - *Separates explanation from argument* (0-3: every explained statement correctly identified and none misclassified) · *Classical form* (0-2: conclusion correct and premises placed above it) · *Unstated premise* (0-3, **Analyze**: names a premise the letter genuinely needs and ties the judgement to specific wording rather than general plausibility) · *Organisation and length* (0-2).

2. Analytic rubric - *Uses all three corners* (0-3: each corner named and each used to explain something about the text) · *Identifies the unargued issue question* (0-2) · *Judgement on sufficiency* (0-3, **Evaluate**: a verdict is given and defended from the circular's own wording, including what would have had to be shown) · *Organisation and length* (0-2).

3. Analytic rubric - *Classification* (0-4: all four types correctly named) · *What each type must supply* (0-4: correct for each type) · *Ranking* (0-4, **Evaluate**: the order turns on named features of each argument, and the reasoning is independent of the writer's own view of the split) · *Organisation and length* (0-2).

4. Analytic rubric - *Sentence-level classification* (0-4: every sentence classified and defensible) · *Argument identified and set out* (0-3) · *Unstated premise* (0-3, **Analyze**: derived from the gap between the stated sentences and the conclusion, in a form the author would accept) · *The unargued assertion* (0-2: named, with what would complete it) · *Organisation and length* (0-2).

5. Analytic rubric - *Completeness of extraction* (0-4: every argument in the document found, or a defended finding that there is none) · *Classical form and typing* (0-4: premises and conclusion given for each, with the type and its characteristic risk) · *What is asserted but never argued* (0-3) · *Judgement* (0-4, **Evaluate**: says which assertions should have been argued and why, reasoning from the document's audience and purpose rather than from preference) · *Use of a real document* (0-2: genuine document attached or quoted) · *Organisation and length* (0-2).
