## A real classroom situation

A letter reaches the head teacher of a government girls' school in Tando Allahyar. It is from the
father of a grade 7 pupil, written in careful Urdu and translated here.

> "Respected Madam. My daughter Hira is in 7-B. She has been absent for eleven days this term. She
> is not unwell. The school van does not come to our lane after the road work started, and the
> walk from the main road takes twenty-five minutes. My wife cannot accompany her and I leave for
> work at six. Hira is twelve years old. I request that the van route be restored."

The head reads it and feels its force immediately. But if she is going to act, she has to answer a
harder question than "is this sad?" She has to answer: **what is being asked, and what reasons have
actually been given for it?**

This topic is the mechanical half of the unit. It is also the half you will use most, because real
school text almost never arrives in classical form.

## Explanation

### Identifying premises

A premise is a statement offered in support of the conclusion. Finding them has two steps: find the
candidates, then test them.

**Step 1: the indicator words.** Certain words often introduce a premise:

> because · since · as · for · given that · the reason is · as shown by · it follows from

**Step 2: distrust them.** This is the part that matters. Indicator words are **evidence** about
structure, not proof of it. Malone (2003) treats deciding what counts as evidence of an argument's
presence as one of the genuinely unresolved problems in the field, and recommends working with real
discourse that learners find themselves rather than the tidy examples textbooks supply, precisely
because real prose does not follow the rules the indicator lists suggest.

Three ways indicator words mislead, all common in school writing:

- **"Because" marking a cause, not a reason.** "The gate is locked because the chowkidar has gone
  for lunch." Nothing is argued.
- **No indicator word at all.** Hira's father uses none. Every one of his middle sentences is a
  premise, unmarked.
- **An indicator word inside a premise.** "Since the road work began, the van has not come, so the
  route should be restored." Here "since" is temporal and "so" is the conclusion marker.

**The reliable test.** A statement is a premise if it is **offered in support of** some other
statement in the passage. So you cannot finally identify the premises until you have identified the
conclusion. In practice you move back and forth: a provisional conclusion, then a check of which
statements lean on it, then a revision.

### Identifying conclusions

The conclusion is the statement the others are offered to support. There are indicator words for it
too:

> therefore · so · thus · hence · it follows that · consequently · we should · this shows that

And the same warning applies. The dependable method is a question, not a word list:

> **What is this passage trying to get me to accept?**

Apply it to the letter. Hira's father is trying to get the head to accept that the van route should
be restored. Everything else is in service of that. His final sentence happens to be the
conclusion, but that is a convention of polite Urdu letters, not a rule of logic.

**Common misconception: "the conclusion always comes last."** It very often does not. Compare these
three, which contain the same argument:

- *Conclusion last:* "The van does not reach our lane and the walk is twenty-five minutes. The route should be restored."
- *Conclusion first:* "The route should be restored. The van does not reach our lane and the walk is twenty-five minutes."
- *Conclusion in the middle:* "The van does not reach our lane. The route should therefore be restored, since a twenty-five minute walk is not reasonable for a twelve-year-old."

A trainee who looks only at the last sentence gets the second and third wrong. Use the support test
and position stops mattering.

#### A worked analysis of the letter

Now the whole move, performed once and slowly.

**Conclusion.** "I request that the van route be restored." Reworded as a statement: *the van route
should be restored.*

**Premises, in the order given.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **P1** | Hira is in 7-B and has been absent eleven days this term. |
| **P2** | She is not unwell. |
| **P3** | The van does not come to the lane since the road work started. |
| **P4** | The walk from the main road takes twenty-five minutes. |
| **P5** | Her mother cannot accompany her and her father leaves at six. |
| **P6** | Hira is twelve years old. |
| **Conclusion** | The van route should be restored. |

**Now look at how they work together.** P2 is there to rule out an alternative explanation of the
absence, which is a job premises often do. P3, P4, P5 and P6 together establish that the walk is not
a realistic option for this child. P1 establishes that there is a cost already being paid.

**The unstated premise.** The argument needs one more thing that nobody wrote down. An
<Glossary term="Unstated premise" /> is a statement an argument depends on but does not state. Here
it is something like: *a school should take reasonable steps to make attendance possible for its
enrolled pupils.*

Without it, the premises describe a difficulty and the conclusion does not follow: the father's
circumstances, by themselves, do not generate an obligation on the school. With it, the argument is
complete and rather strong.

Notice three things about that recovery, because they generalise:

1. **The unstated premise is usually the load-bearing one.** It is also usually the one both sides
   actually disagree about. A head who refuses this request is almost certainly rejecting the
   unstated premise, or limiting it ("reasonable steps" does not extend to a van route), not denying
   that the walk is twenty-five minutes.
2. **It is left out because it is obvious to the writer.** Hira's father did not conceal it. He did
   not think it needed saying. Most unstated premises are of this kind.
3. **Recovering it is interpretation, so be honest about it.** You are adding words. State the
   premise in the weakest form that makes the argument work, and never in a form the writer would
   reject. Adding a premise nobody would accept, in order to make an argument look foolish, is the
   opposite of what this skill is for.

### What is not an argument

Most school prose is not an argument. Four non-argument kinds account for nearly all of it, and
telling them apart is the last skill in the unit.

- **Description.** Reports what is the case, with no claim in dispute. "The laboratory has four
  working microscopes and two broken ones."
- **Explanation.** Gives the cause of something already accepted. Topic 2.1 covered this. "Enrolment
  fell because three families moved to Karachi."
- **Assertion.** Makes a claim in dispute and offers nothing for it. "Streaming is unfair." This is
  the one that most often gets mistaken for an argument, because it sounds argumentative.
- **Instruction, report or announcement.** Tells you what to do or what has been decided. "Heads
  will report compliance by 15 December."

**Common misconception: "a long passage must contain an argument somewhere."** It need not, and
official prose usually does not. A three-page district circular can contain description,
explanation, instruction and assertion from first line to last, and argue nothing at all. Length is
not evidence of argument; neither is formality, nor the seniority of the author.

This has a practical edge. Saying "this circular argues nothing" is not an insult to it. A circular
announces a decision taken by someone entitled to take it; announcing is its job, and it does the
job. But the distinction tells you what you are entitled to do. You can ask an argument whether its
premises support its conclusion. You can only ask an assertion what the reasons are, and then wait
to see whether any arrive.

**A note on the hardest case.** Some passages genuinely cannot be settled, because the writer has
not made clear whether a sentence is offered as support or merely stated alongside. That is not your
failure as a reader; Malone (2003) identifies exactly this indeterminacy as one of the standing
problems of argument identification. When you meet it, say so, and say what extra information would
settle it. Recording a passage as undecidable, with a reason, is a better analysis than forcing it
into a category.

## Activity: One circular, read twice

Work in pairs. About 35 minutes.

Your tutor will give each pair a real school or district circular. If none is available, use a
notice from your practicum school's board.

**First reading: what does it assert?** Number every sentence. For each one, write down in a few
words the statement it makes. Do not evaluate anything yet. At the end, you will have a list of
everything the document puts forward.

**Second reading: is any of it argued?** Go back through your numbered list. For each statement, ask
whether any other sentence in the document is offered in support of it. Mark each sentence:

- **A** if it is a conclusion with support in the document,
- **P** if it is support for something else,
- **X** if it is asserted, described, explained or instructed without support.

Then answer together, in writing:

1. How many sentences are marked X?
2. Is there any argument in this document at all? If so, set it out in classical form.
3. Name one statement marked X that you think **should** have been argued, and say what premise would have been needed.

Finish by comparing with another pair. Most pairs find that the document is almost entirely X, and
that this is not a fault in the document. Discuss as a class why that is, and what it means for how
a teacher should read official prose.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Give three premise indicator words and three conclusion indicator words, and state in one sentence why neither list is reliable.
2. *(Understand)* State the test that identifies a conclusion, and explain why it works even when the conclusion comes first.
3. *(Understand)* Explain what an unstated premise is and why it is usually the part people actually disagree about.
4. *(Apply)* Identify the conclusion and the premises: "Hostel pupils reach the laboratory late because the hostel gate opens at eight. A pupil who arrives after the demonstration has started cannot follow it. The gate should open at half past seven."
5. *(Apply)* Classify each as argument, explanation, description or assertion: (a) "The school has two functioning fans in the hall." (b) "Corporal punishment has no place in a modern school." (c) "Results fell because the syllabus was finished late." (d) "The syllabus was finished late and late syllabus completion lowers results, so we must start earlier next year."
6. *(Apply)* Recover the unstated premise: "Hira has missed eleven days. The route should be restored."

## Summary

Premise and conclusion indicator words are useful clues and unreliable rules, so the working method
is a question rather than a word list: what is this passage trying to get me to accept, and which
statements are offered in support of it? Real school text usually gives no indicators and often puts
the conclusion first or in the middle. The premise that carries the most weight is frequently the one
nobody wrote down, and recovering it honestly, in the weakest form that makes the argument work, is
where most of the analytical work lies. Finally, most school prose describes, explains, asserts or
instructs rather than argues, and noticing that is not a criticism of it.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can find the conclusion of a passage using the support test, wherever it sits in the passage.
- [ ] I can list a passage's premises and say what job each one is doing.
- [ ] I can recover an unstated premise and state it in a form the writer would accept.
- [ ] I can classify a passage as argument, explanation, description or assertion, and say what makes the difference.
- [ ] I can recognise a passage that is genuinely undecidable and say what would settle it.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher for one written message that a parent has sent to the school this term,
or use one the head is willing to show you with names removed.

Analyse it on paper: the conclusion, the premises in order, and the unstated premise. Then ask your
cooperating teacher what they think the school's answer should be, and note whether their reasoning
accepts or rejects the unstated premise you recovered. Bring both to your tutorial.

## Summative task

Here is a notice posted in the staffroom of a government high school in Sukkur:

> "From Monday, the staff register will be kept in the head teacher's office instead of the
> staffroom. Several colleagues have been signing in after the first bell. Punctuality is a
> professional duty. Last month the office recorded fourteen late sign-ins. All staff are requested
> to cooperate."

Write 200 to 250 words that:

(a) numbers the sentences and classifies each as argument, premise, explanation, description,
assertion or instruction;

(b) states whether the notice contains an argument, and if so sets it out in classical form;

(c) names the unstated premise the notice depends on, in the weakest form that makes it work;

(d) names one sentence that is asserted but never argued, and says what would have to be added for
it to become an argument.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Sentence-level classification* | 0-3: every sentence classified and defensible / most correct / half correct / unreliable. |
| *Classical form* | 0-2: conclusion correctly identified and the supporting sentences placed above it / conclusion correct, support muddled / conclusion wrong or none attempted. |
| *Unstated premise* | 0-3: states a premise the notice genuinely needs, in a form its author would accept, and explains its role (**Analyze**: the premise is derived from the gap between the stated sentences and the conclusion, not from general knowledge about registers) / plausible premise, role unexplained / none. |
| *The unargued assertion* | 0-2: names an assertion and states specifically what premise would complete it / names one without saying what is needed / none. |
| *Clarity* | 0-2: organised under the four parts and within the word band / unclear or over length. |
