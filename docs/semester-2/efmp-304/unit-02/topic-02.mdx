## A real classroom situation

Zainab teaches grade 6 Urdu at a government girls' school in Mirpurkhas. Her mother is admitted to
a hospital in Hyderabad and she needs three days away. She has to tell three people, and she writes
three different things.

To the head teacher: "Madam, I request leave for 20 to 22 November. My mother is admitted in
Hyderabad. I have set work for grade 6 and Miss Rubina has agreed to supervise it, so no period
will be lost."

To Miss Rubina, her colleague: "Rubina, I have to go to Hyderabad for three days. Can you keep an
eye on 6-B? I will leave the work ready. I will cover your Thursday duty next week."

To the parents, in the class diary: "Grades 6-B will do written practice from the textbook on 20 to
22 November under Miss Rubina's supervision. Please check that the work is completed at home."

Same fact, same three days, three different pieces of writing. Nobody taught Zainab to do that; she
did it because she knew who she was writing to.

This topic names what she was doing, and then draws a second distinction that decides whether an

## Explanation

### The rhetorical triangle

Every piece of persuasive communication has three fixed points. The
<Glossary term="Rhetorical triangle" /> names them:

- the **speaker** (or writer), and what the audience takes them to be;
- the **audience**, and what they already believe, want and are able to grant;
- the **subject**, and what is actually true about it.

The message sits in the middle, held by all three. Move any corner and the message has to change
shape, even though the facts have not moved.

Look again at Zainab's three texts, corner by corner.

**The audience corner.** The head teacher can grant leave and nobody else can. So that text is an
**argument**: it gives the head the two things a head needs in order to say yes, which are a reason
the absence is unavoidable and an assurance that teaching will not stop. Miss Rubina cannot grant
leave; she can only agree to cover. So that text is a **request plus an offer**, and it argues
nothing. The parents can grant nothing at all; they only need to know. So that text is an
**announcement**.

**The speaker corner.** To the head, Zainab writes as a subordinate making a formal case, so she
uses "I request" and names dates. To Rubina she writes as an equal trading favours, so she offers
the Thursday duty in exchange. The relationship is doing work that the words only carry.

**The subject corner.** The subject is the same throughout: three days of absence in November. What
changes is which part of the subject each audience needs. The head needs the cover arrangement. The
parents need the date and the work. Rubina needs the class and the trade.

This is why a trainee who writes one all-purpose paragraph and sends it to everybody is usually
ineffective with all three. Rhetoric is not decoration added to content. It is the shaping of
content for the person who has to act on it.

**Where the triangle helps you as a critical thinker.** Reading, not writing, is where you will use
this most. When a claim arrives, ask the three questions: who is saying this, to whom, and about
what? A textbook publisher's claim that its series "raises results" is addressed to a purchasing
officer, not to you, and it is shaped for that audience. A trainer's claim that a method works is
addressed to teachers who have come to be convinced. Noticing the intended audience tells you
which objections the speaker has already decided not to answer.

**Common misconception: "disagreeing with the conclusion means the argument is invalid."** This one
hides in the speaker corner, and it is the most damaging habit in a staffroom. A trainee hears a
claim from someone they do not rate, or reaches a conclusion they dislike, and declares the
reasoning bad. But the quality of an argument is a relationship between its premises and its
conclusion. It is not affected by who said it, and it is not affected by whether you like where it
lands.

A worked case. Suppose the least popular member of staff argues: "Our pass rate in grade 9 English
fell for three years running. The same three years, we had no permanent English teacher. So the
vacancy is part of the cause." You may dislike the speaker. You may dread the conclusion, because
it implies a recruitment fight. Neither of those touches the argument. The only questions that
touch it are whether the pass rate really fell, whether the post really was vacant, and whether
three coinciding years is enough to support "part of the cause". Judge those, and you have judged
the argument.

The reverse error matters just as much: an argument from someone you respect, landing somewhere you
already wanted to go, still has to pass the same test.

### Issue questions and information questions

The second distinction in this topic decides whether an argument is needed at all.

An <Glossary term="Information question" /> has a single verifiable answer that can be established by
looking, counting, measuring or checking a record. "How many pupils are enrolled in 6-B?" "What date
did the circular arrive?" "Is Miss Rubina's Thursday duty in the roster?"

An <Glossary term="Issue question" /> admits more than one reasonable answer, and settling it needs
reasoning rather than retrieval. "Should 6-B be split into two sections?" "Is written practice an
adequate substitute for a taught lesson?" "Was granting the leave the right decision?"

The test is short: **can a competent person settle this by checking, or must they weigh something?**
If checking settles it, it is an information question. If weighing is unavoidable, it is an issue
question.

<Figure id="fig-U2-4" kind="table" src="/img/figures/efmp-304/unit-02/fig-U2-4.svg" alt="Table of five paired school questions, one information question and one issue question in each row, with a column for what each kind of question can settle and a column for what it cannot." width={900} height={470} />

Three practical consequences follow, and all three show up in school meetings.

**1. Arguments belong to issue questions.** You do not argue about enrolment; you open the register.
Offering an argument where a check would do wastes everyone's time and looks evasive.

**2. An information question can be settled wrongly and still be an information question.** If two
teachers disagree about the enrolment figure, they have a disagreement, not an issue. The answer
exists; somebody has it wrong. Treating that as a matter of opinion is how a school ends up with
two different figures in two different files.

**3. Issue questions get disguised as information questions, and this is where the real harm is.**
"Does streaming work?" sounds like something you could look up. It is not, because "work" has to be
defined before any evidence counts, and defining it is a judgement: higher mean marks, a narrower
spread, better attendance, pupils' sense of themselves as learners. Pick a different definition and
the same evidence gives a different answer. A question that smuggles a judgement inside a word that
looks factual is the most common way a school argument goes wrong without anyone noticing.

**Why this pairs with the triangle.** The two ideas meet in the audience corner. If your audience
thinks they are facing an information question, they will be impatient with your argument: they
want you to just tell them the answer. Part of arguing well is first establishing that there is
something here to weigh. Qin (2016) observed the same pattern in novice argumentative writers, who
tended to assert their position and omit the opposing view until they were taught to look for it;
the students in that study began supplying rebuttals only once the structure made the gap visible.
That study involved sixteen university students in a Turkish English-as-a-foreign-language class,
so treat it as evidence about explicit instruction rather than about Pakistani schools.

## Activity: Three letters, one fact

Work in groups of three. About 30 minutes.

Each group takes this situation: the school's only functioning water cooler has broken, and the
repair will cost more than the petty-cash limit allows.

Each member of the group writes **one short text** about it, on a different corner of the triangle:

1. To the **head teacher**, arguing for the repair to be approved from another budget line.
2. To a **colleague** who shares your staffroom, asking them to join you in raising it.
3. To the **parents' committee**, informing them of the situation.

Write five sentences at most. Then read all three aloud in your group and answer together:

- Which of the three is an argument, and how do you know?
- Which sentence appears in all three, and why did it survive every change of audience?
- Name one thing you said to the head teacher that you deliberately left out of the parents' note, and say why.

Finally, as a group, write down one **issue question** and one **information question** that the
water-cooler situation raises, and say which of your three texts each belongs in.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the three corners of the rhetorical triangle and say what sits at the centre.
2. *(Understand)* In two sentences, explain why the same request is argued to a head teacher but only announced to parents.
3. *(Understand)* Give the test that separates an issue question from an information question, and explain why a disagreement about a fact is not an issue.
4. *(Apply)* Classify each of these and justify each in one clause: (a) "How many periods does the grade 8 timetable allot to science?" (b) "Should grade 8 science have more periods?" (c) "Is our science teaching good?"
5. *(Apply)* A colleague you dislike argues that the morning assembly should be shortened, and gives two reasons. State what you must examine in order to judge that argument, and name one thing that is irrelevant to it.

## Summary

Every message is held by three corners: who is speaking, who is listening, and what it is about.
Move the audience corner and the shape of the message must change, which is why the same fact is
argued to a head teacher, traded with a colleague and announced to parents. Separately, a question
is either an information question, settled by checking, or an issue question, settled only by
weighing. Arguments belong to issue questions, and the most common way school reasoning goes astray
is an issue question disguised as a factual one.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can name the three corners of the rhetorical triangle and explain what changes when the audience corner moves.
- [ ] I can use the triangle on something I am reading, to work out who it was shaped for.
- [ ] I can tell an issue question from an information question, and explain why that decides whether an argument is needed.
- [ ] I can explain why my dislike of a speaker or a conclusion is irrelevant to whether an argument is any good.

## Try this at your practicum school

Sit in on, or ask about, one staff meeting or one conversation between a teacher and the head.
Write down the questions that were actually discussed, then label each one as an information
question or an issue question.

Look for one question that was treated as factual but needed a judgement first, or one that was
debated when a register or a file would have settled it. Bring your list and that one example to
your next tutorial.

## Summative task

A district office circular arrives at a government high school in Dadu. It says, in full:

> "All schools are directed to begin a morning reading period of fifteen minutes from 1 December.
> Reading improves examination performance. Heads will report compliance by 15 December."

Write 150 to 200 words that (a) analyses the circular on all three corners of the triangle, naming
the speaker, the audience and the subject, and saying what the choice of audience explains about how
it is written; (b) identifies the one issue question the circular settles without arguing for it;
and (c) states what a head teacher would need in order to judge the circular's middle sentence.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Uses all three corners* | 0-3: all three corners named and each one used to explain something about the text / two corners used / one corner named only. |
| *Identifies the unargued issue question* | 0-2: correctly names the issue question treated as settled / names a question but not the issue one / none. |
| *Judgement about the evidence needed* | 0-3: states specifically what would have to be shown, and ties it to words in the circular (**Analyze**: the demand is derived from the circular's own wording, not from general scepticism) / vague demand for "evidence" / none. |
| *Clarity* | 0-2: organised under the three parts and within the word band / unclear or over length. |
