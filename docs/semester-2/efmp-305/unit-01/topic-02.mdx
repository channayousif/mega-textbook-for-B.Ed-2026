## A real classroom situation

A primary school teacher in a government school in Sukkur attends a one-day workshop on
inclusive education. The facilitator speaks about "every child's right to learn" and shows
photographs of schools where children with and without disabilities learn together. The
teacher is sympathetic. But on Monday morning she faces a class of 56 pupils, a single
textbook, a broken blackboard and no training in how to teach a child with a hearing
loss. She thinks: "These ideas are good, but they are for schools in cities with resources.
They do not apply here."

Her doubt is honest and common. This topic examines the principles that make inclusion work,
the reasons it matters in Pakistan specifically, and why the "not for us" belief is itself
one of the misconceptions that keeps exclusion in place.

## Explanation

### Principles of inclusion

Across the international literature, several principles appear repeatedly. They are not a
checklist to complete once and forget. They are commitments a school returns to again and
again. The most widely cited set includes:

**Diversity is valued.** Difference is not a problem to be fixed. A
classroom with children of different abilities, languages and backgrounds is richer, not
weaker. This principle rejects the idea that a "good" class is a uniform one.

**All learners participate.** Every child takes part in the ordinary
lesson, the ordinary assessment and the ordinary school life. Participation is not the
same as mere presence. A child sitting silently at the back is present but not participating.

**The system adapts.** When a learner struggles, the first response is
to change the teaching, the materials or the environment - not to remove the child or to
blame the child. This is the principle that separates inclusion from integration.

**Barriers are removed.** Barriers can be physical (no ramp),
instructional (one textbook for all), attitudinal (the teacher believes the child cannot
learn) or policy-based (admission rules that exclude). Inclusion means identifying and
removing these barriers, not waiting for the child to overcome them.

**Collaboration is essential.** No single teacher builds an inclusive
classroom alone. Parents, special educators, head teachers and the wider community all play
part. This principle is the subject of Unit 6.

**Every learner can succeed.** This is not optimism. It is a
professional commitment: the teacher plans for every learner to make progress, measures that
progress, and adjusts when it does not happen. It rejects the belief that some children are
simply unable to learn.

### Why inclusion matters in Pakistan

Pakistan has one of the highest rates of out-of-school children in South Asia. UNICEF data
consistently shows that children with disabilities, girls in rural areas, children from
linguistic minorities and children in poverty are the most likely to be excluded. The
National Education Policy acknowledges that access alone is not enough: children who enrol
but cannot participate fully drop out or are pushed out.

Three reasons make inclusion especially urgent here:

1. **Scale of exclusion.** Large class sizes, limited material resources and uneven teacher
   training mean that many pupils are already being taught in ways that leave some behind.
   Inclusive practices (differentiated tasks, peer support, flexible assessment) address
   the needs of the widest possible range of learners, not only those labelled as having a
   disability.

2. **Rights and law.** Pakistan ratified the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with
   Disabilities and passed the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act in 2018. These
   commitments require schools to move toward inclusion, not as charity but as a legal and
   moral obligation. (Unit 5 examines these frameworks in detail.)

3. **Social cohesion.** Pakistani society is diverse: linguistic, ethnic, religious and
   economic differences run through every province. A school that teaches children to learn
   alongside those who are different from them is doing more than meeting individual needs.
   It is building the habits of empathy and cooperation that a diverse society requires.

### Common misconceptions about inclusion

Several beliefs block inclusion in practice. Naming them directly is more useful than
leaving them unaddressed.

**Misconception 1: "Inclusion means putting every child in the same classroom regardless of
need."** Inclusion does not mean abandoning support. It means the support comes to the
child in the ordinary classroom, or the child moves to a resource room for part of the day
while still belonging to the ordinary class. Full-time placement without any support is not
inclusion; it is neglect.

**Misconception 2: "We need more resources before we can include."** Some inclusive changes
cost almost nothing: rearranging seats so a child with hearing loss can see the teacher's
face, allowing extra time on a test, pairing a struggling reader with a stronger one.
Resource-intensive solutions (assistive devices, one-to-one aides) are part of the picture,
but waiting for them before beginning inclusion means nothing ever begins.

**Misconception 3: "Inclusion holds back the stronger pupils."** Research consistently shows
that well-implemented inclusive practices do not reduce the achievement of non-disabled
learners (Hehir et al., 2016). Differentiated teaching often benefits everyone because it
makes the teacher think more carefully about how each pupil learns.

**Misconception 4: "It is not for schools like ours."** This is the belief the Sukkur
teacher in the classroom situation above expressed. It is the most damaging misconception
because it turns a resource gap into a reason for inaction. Inclusion is a direction of
travel, not a destination you must reach before you begin.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees believe that inclusive education is only about
disability. It is not. It is about every form of difference: ability, gender, language,
poverty, ethnicity and more. Focusing only on disability narrows the principle and misses
the majority of children who are excluded for other reasons.

## Activity: Which principle is at stake?

Work in groups of three. About 25 minutes.

Each group takes one of the situations below. Identify which principle of inclusion is most
at stake, explain why, and describe one concrete action the teacher could take that respects
the principle. Present your finding to the class in two minutes.

1. A school in Tharparkar admits Hindu and Muslim children but seats them separately and
   teaches them from different Urdu readers.
2. A boy with cerebral palsy is enrolled in a government school in Karachi but cannot
   access the toilet because it is on the first floor and there is no ramp.
3. A teacher in Peshawar teaches only in Pashto. Three children in the class speak Hindko
   at home and have been silent since the term began.

Compare your group's analysis with the others. Where two groups identified different
principles for the same situation, discuss whether both can be right.

## Summary

Inclusion is built on six principles: diversity is valued, all learners participate, the
system adapts, barriers are removed, collaboration is essential, and every learner can
succeed. In Pakistan, inclusion is urgent because of the scale of exclusion, legal and
moral commitments to rights, and the need for social cohesion in a diverse society. Several
common misconceptions block it, the most damaging being the belief that it only applies to
well-resourced schools or only to disability.

## Try this at your practicum school

Visit the school and look for one barrier to inclusion - it could be physical (no ramp,
no accessible toilet), instructional (one textbook, no differentiated tasks) or attitudinal
(a teacher's statement that some children cannot learn). Write a short note describing the
barrier and one practical step that could begin to remove it. Share your note with your
supervising teacher and ask what they think.

## Summative task

A head teacher says, "Inclusive education is a good idea, but our school is not ready. We
begin when the government gives us a special educator and a resource room." Write a short
paragraph (80-120 words) responding to this statement. In your response, name one
principle of inclusion that the head teacher's statement overlooks, give one practical
step the school could take now (without waiting for new resources), and explain why
waiting for perfect conditions may never work.
