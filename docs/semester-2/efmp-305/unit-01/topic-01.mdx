## A real classroom situation

A government middle school in rural Sindh has two sections of Grade 4. In Section A, all 42
pupils sit together. In Section B, the eight pupils identified as "slow learners" are seated
together at the back, with a separate set of easier worksheets. The head teacher explains
arrangement keeps the main class moving. A new teacher asks whether those eight pupils might
join Section A for the science lesson. The head teacher replies, "They cannot keep up. They
have their own teacher for a reason."

This scene is familiar in many Pakistani schools. The head teacher is not being cruel. He
is working from a belief about how children learn and what school structure should look like.
This topic gives you the language to examine that belief precisely, and to see where it falls
on the spectrum from segregation to inclusion.

## Explanation

### What inclusive education means

**Inclusive education** is an approach in which **all** learners, regardless of
ability, background or difference, participate fully in the ordinary classroom and the ordinary
curriculum. It is not a programme for a particular group of children. It is a way of thinking
about the whole education system: the question is not "How do we fit this child into the
existing school?" but "How do we change the existing school so that every child belongs?"

UNESCO (2020) frames it as a process of responding to the diversity of learners through
increasing participation in learning, cultures and communities, and reducing exclusion from
within and beyond education. Two things matter in that definition:

1. **All means all.** The commitment is not only to children with disabilities. It extends to
   girls kept at home, children from minority linguistic groups, children affected by poverty,
   and any learner who is at risk of being left behind.
2. **The system changes, not only the child.** In an inclusive approach, the school adapts its
   teaching, its environment and its attitudes. The child is not sent away or given a separate
   curriculum as the first response.

### Segregation, integration and inclusion distinguished

These three words describe three different approaches to learner difference. Confusing them
is one of the most common mistakes in education talk, so it is worth being precise.

**Segregation** means learners with differences are educated in separate
settings - separate schools, separate classrooms, or separate sections within a school. The
assumption is that their needs are so different that the ordinary classroom cannot meet them.
Special schools for the visually impaired, separate "slow learner" sections within a mainstream
school, and home-based education for children with severe disabilities are all forms of
segregation.

**Integration** means learners with differences are placed in the ordinary
school, but the expectation is that they must adapt to what the school already offers. The
school changes little. A child in a wheelchair is admitted to the local school, but no ramp is
built, no seating is rearranged, and the same textbook is used without modification. The
burden of fitting in falls on the child, not on the system.

**Inclusion** goes further. It means the school itself changes: teaching
methods, classroom arrangements, assessment practices and attitudes are all adapted so that
every learner can participate. A child with a learning difficulty joins the ordinary class,
and the teacher prepares material at more than one level, uses peer support, and adjusts the
pace. The child belongs; the system adjusts.

### Equity and equality

Two more terms appear whenever inclusion is discussed, and they are often confused.

**Equality** means giving every learner the same treatment: the same textbook,
the same seat, the same exam. It sounds fair, but it is not always fair. Giving the same
worksheet to a child with dyslexia and to a child without it treats them equally but produces
very different results.

**Equity** means giving each learner what they need to participate and
succeed. This may mean different resources for different pupils: extra time in an exam, a
seat near the front for a child with hearing difficulty, a bilingual glossary for a child
who speaks a minority language at home. Equity is the principle that drives inclusion.

### The inclusion spectrum

Most schools are not fully segregated or fully inclusive. They sit somewhere on a spectrum.
A school that runs a special education unit but also sends some of those pupils into the
ordinary classroom for art and physical education is moving from segregation towards
integration. A school that admits all children and trains its teachers in differentiated
teaching is moving from integration towards inclusion. The direction matters more than the
current position.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees use "inclusive education" and "integrating children
with disabilities" as if they mean the same thing. They do not. Integration places the child
in the ordinary school but leaves the school largely unchanged. Inclusion changes the school
itself. A child sitting in the back of a mainstream classroom with no support is integrated,
not included. Recognising the difference is the first step toward real inclusive practice.

## Activity: Where does this school stand?

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Read each of the three descriptions below. For each one, decide whether it shows segregation,
integration or inclusion. Write one sentence explaining your reasoning, citing a specific
detail from the description.

1. A private school in Hyderabad admits children with mild disabilities. They sit in the
   ordinary classroom but follow a separate, simplified curriculum taught by a visiting
   special educator twice a week.
2. A government girls' school in Larkana has no separate sections. A child who uses a
   wheelchair is enrolled in Grade 3. The classroom is on the ground floor near the door,
   the desk is adjusted to her height, and the teacher checks that she can reach the
   materials.
3. A special school in Karachi educates children with intellectual disabilities in its own
   building with its own teachers. Pupils do not attend any mainstream school.

Compare your decisions with another pair. Where you disagree, explain your reasoning using
the definitions from this topic.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* In your own words, define inclusive education in one sentence. Do not use
   the word "special" in your definition.
2. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between integration and inclusion in two sentences.
   Use a concrete school example in your explanation.
3. *(Apply)* A school places all "weak" pupils in one section and teaches them from a
   shorter textbook. Using the segregation-integration-inclusion framework, name which
   approach this shows and give two reasons for your answer.

## Summary

Inclusive education is a way of thinking about the whole school system: all learners
participate fully, and the system adapts to them. It is not the same as segregation
(separate settings) or integration (the child adapts to an unchanged school). Equality
gives everyone the same treatment; equity gives each learner what they need. Most schools
fall somewhere on the spectrum between segregation and inclusion, and the direction of
travel matters.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your supervising teacher whether the school has any pupils who learn in a separate
section or with a separate curriculum. If so, find out: what is the reason given? What
would need to change for those pupils to learn in the ordinary classroom with support?
Write a short note (no more than one page) summarising what you found.

## Summative task

A parent tells you, "My son is in the slow section. At least he is in the same school as the
other children." Using the concepts from this topic, write a short paragraph (80-120 words)
evaluating whether this arrangement is integration or inclusion. State which it is, give
two reasons based on the definitions, and name one thing the school could do to move it
toward genuine inclusion.
