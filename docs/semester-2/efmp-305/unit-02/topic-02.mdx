## A real classroom situation

A teacher in a private school in Islamabad keeps a list of "weak pupils" on the inside cover
of her planner. The list has been there since September. By June, the pupils on it have
stopped putting up their hands. They have started to believe the label. One of them, a boy
named Danish, has begun to say "I am stupid" when he cannot answer. The teacher thinks she
is being organised. This topic examines what her list is actually doing, and what she could
do instead.

## Explanation

### Identifying individual needs

Identifying a pupil's individual needs does not require a clinical diagnosis. It requires
systematic observation and simple assessment. The goal is not to label the pupil but to
understand what the pupil can do, what they find difficult, and what change might help.

**Observation** is the first and most important tool. A teacher who watches carefully learns
more than a teacher who tests formally. Useful observations include:

- What can the pupil do independently?
- What can the pupil do with a small amount of help?
- When does the pupil struggle most: at the start of a task, in the middle, or at the end?
- Does the pupil's difficulty change with the subject, the time of day, or the seating position?
- How does the pupil respond to different modalities: hearing, seeing, doing?

Keep a simple record: a few lines after each lesson noting what you noticed about two or three
pupils. Over a week, patterns emerge.

**Informal assessment** goes one step further. It means giving the pupil a small task at the
edge of their ability and watching how they manage. Useful techniques include:

- **Reading aloud** for one minute and noting the errors (gives a rough sense of fluency).
- **Asking the pupil to explain their reasoning** (reveals understanding that a written test may miss).
- **Offering two ways to show the same understanding** (written or oral; drawing or speaking).
- **Checking prior knowledge** before a new topic (what does the pupil already know about this?).

None of these require special training. They require attention and a simple record.

**When to refer.** Observation and informal assessment are the teacher's tools. If a pattern
suggests a disability or a significant learning difficulty that the teacher's adaptations do
not address, the next step is a referral: to the head teacher, to a learning support
coordinator if the school has one, or to the parents with a request for professional
assessment. The teacher's job is to identify and support, not to diagnose.

### Labelling and stigma

**Labelling** is the act of attaching a fixed description to a pupil: "slow",
"weak", "naughty", "special". Labels are sticky. Once attached, they are hard to remove,
and they shape how every subsequent teacher sees the pupil.

**How labelling works:**

1. A teacher notices a pattern (the pupil reads slowly).
2. The teacher attaches a label ("slow reader").
3. The teacher's expectations drop (does not ask the pupil to read aloud; gives easier work).
4. The pupil internalises the label ("I am a slow reader; I cannot improve").
5. The pupil's effort and confidence drop, confirming the label.

The label becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy. The pupil who was "slow" becomes slower because
the label removed the expectation of progress.

**Stigma** is the social cost of the label. The pupil is teased by peers, avoided in group
work, or treated as less capable by subsequent teachers. In Pakistani schools, stigma
attaches quickly and lasts. A pupil labelled "weak" in Grade 3 may carry the label to Grade
8, even after the original difficulty has been resolved.

### Promoting respect for diversity

Respect is not a slogan on the wall. It is a set of daily practices:

**Use person-first language.** Say "a pupil with a hearing impairment" not "a deaf pupil".
Say "a pupil who finds reading difficult" not "a slow reader". Person-first language keeps
the person before the condition.

**Make diversity ordinary.** Include examples from different languages, different family
structures and different abilities in your teaching materials naturally, not as a special
lesson. When a Maths word problem mentions a girl who is an engineer and a boy who is a
teacher, you are normalising diversity without a lecture.

**Respond to behaviour, not to label.** When a pupil is disruptive, ask "What is happening
for this pupil right now?" not "What else can you expect from a naughty child?" The first
question leads to a response; the second leads to resignation.

**Create mixed-ability partnerships.** Pair a strong reader with a struggling reader for a
shared task where both have something to contribute. The stronger pupil learns patience
and explanation; the struggling pupil learns from a peer. Neither is labelled.

**Speak to parents about what the pupil can do.** When a teacher tells a parent "Your child
is weak", the parent hears "Your child is failing". When the teacher says "Your child
understands the concepts but needs more practice with written work", the parent hears a
specific, addressable message.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees believe that "being kind to the pupil with a
disability is enough". It is not. Kindness without high expectations is pity, and pity is
itself a form of exclusion. Respect means expecting every pupil to learn and providing the
support that makes learning possible.

## Activity: What is the label doing?

Work in groups of three. About 20 minutes.

Read the case of Danish from the classroom situation above. Then discuss:

1. What label has been attached to Danish? What is the evidence for it?
2. How has the label affected Danish's own view of himself?
3. What has the teacher done that reinforced the label (even though she did not mean to)?
4. What could the teacher do differently, starting tomorrow, to break the cycle?

Write four bullet points (one per question) on a sheet of paper. Be ready to share one
point with the class.

## Summary

Individual needs are identified through systematic observation and simple informal
assessment, not through labelling. Labelling creates a self-fulfilling cycle: the label
lowers expectations, the pupil internalises it, and performance drops. Respect is built
through daily practices: person-first language, mixed-ability partnerships, responding to
behaviour rather than labels, and speaking to parents about what pupils can do.

## Try this at your practicum school

Choose one pupil who seems to struggle. Spend three days observing them without labelling
them. Note: what can they do? When do they struggle? What helps? Write a short note (no
more than one page) describing what you learned about the pupil's needs, and one change you
would make to help them.

## Summative task

A new teacher writes on a pupil's report card: "Bilal is a slow learner who cannot keep up
with the class." Rewrite this comment (80-120 words) in a way that describes Bilal's needs
precisely, avoids labelling, and gives the parents something they can act on. Then explain
in two sentences why your version is more useful than the original.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
