## Explanation

### Three broad categories of diversity

**Learner diversity** is the range of differences among pupils in a classroom.
These differences fall into three broad categories:

**1. Disabilities** - sensory, physical or intellectual conditions that affect how a pupil
perceives the world, moves through it, or processes information.

**2. Learning difficulties** - specific challenges with particular academic skills (reading,
writing, mathematics, attention) that are not explained by general intelligence or lack of
teaching.

**3. Social differences** - differences in gender, language, ethnicity, religion, family
income or caste that affect a pupil's experience of school and the expectations others have
of them.

These categories overlap. A child from a poor rural family (social difference) may also have
an untreated hearing loss (disability) and a reading delay (learning difficulty). The
categories are a thinking tool, not a set of boxes.

### Disabilities: sensory, physical, intellectual

**Sensory disabilities** affect the senses. The most common in Pakistani classrooms are:

- **Visual impairment**: the pupil cannot see the board, the textbook or the demonstration.
  Ranges from mild (needs to sit near the front) to severe (needs Braille or auditory
  materials). Many cases in Pakistan go undetected because vision screening is rare in
  government schools (UNESCO, 2020).
- **Hearing impairment**: the pupil cannot hear the teacher clearly, especially in a noisy
  classroom with hard walls. May be partial (hears some frequencies) or complete. A pupil
  who "does not pay attention" may in fact not be able to hear.

**Physical disabilities** affect movement or the body. Cerebral palsy, limb differences,
polio-related impairments and conditions requiring a wheelchair or crutches are the most
common in Pakistani schools. The classroom barrier is often physical: steps, narrow
corridors, high shelves, desks that do not accommodate a wheelchair.

**Intellectual disabilities** affect cognitive functioning. The pupil learns more slowly,
needs more repetition, and may struggle with abstract reasoning. The range is wide: a
pupil with a mild intellectual disability may learn to read with extra time and support;
a pupil with a profound intellectual disability needs a highly individualised approach.

### Impairment, disability and handicap distinguished

These three words are often used interchangeably, but they describe different things. The
distinction matters because it changes what the teacher does.

**Impairment** is the medical condition itself: the hearing loss, the visual deficit, the
limb difference. The teacher does not diagnose impairment; a medical professional does.

**Disability** is the functional limitation the impairment creates: the pupil cannot hear
the teacher, cannot read the textbook, cannot reach the classroom. The teacher observes and
responds to disability.

**Handicap** is the social disadvantage that results when the environment does not
accommodate the disability: the pupil is excluded from learning, from play, from
participation. The teacher's job is to remove the handicap by changing the environment.

A pupil who cannot hear (impairment) and cannot follow the lesson (disability) is
handicapped only if the teacher does nothing about it. If the teacher seats the pupil near
the front, faces the class when speaking and writes key points on the board, the handicap
is reduced or removed.

### Learning difficulties

Learning difficulties are specific challenges with particular academic skills. The most
common in Pakistani classrooms include:

- **Dyslexia**: difficulty with reading and decoding text. The pupil may reverse letters,
  read very slowly, or be unable to sound out unfamiliar words despite adequate teaching
  and normal intelligence.
- **Dyscalculia**: difficulty with number concepts and arithmetic. The pupil may struggle
  to understand place value, memorise multiplication tables or follow multi-step problems.
- **ADHD (Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder)**: difficulty sustaining attention,
  controlling impulses and regulating activity level. The pupil may be restless, distractible
  and impulsive, and may be labelled "naughty" when the underlying issue is neurological.

These conditions are not the result of laziness, poor teaching or low intelligence. They are
neurological differences that require specific classroom responses.

### Social differences

Social differences shape a pupil's experience of school even when no disability or learning
difficulty is present:

- **Language**: a pupil who speaks Pashto at home and is taught in Urdu faces a barrier that
  has nothing to do with ability. In Sindh, Balochistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, this is the
  most common form of diversity in the classroom.
- **Gender**: in some communities, girls are withdrawn from school at puberty or given less
  attention in mixed classrooms. In others, boys are expected to be tough and are punished
  for showing vulnerability.
- **Poverty**: a pupil who comes hungry, who has no quiet place to study at home, whose
  parents cannot read, carries a set of disadvantages that affect learning but are invisible
  in the classroom.
- **Ethnicity and caste**: in parts of rural Sindh, pupils from lower-caste or minority
  backgrounds face exclusion and low expectations from teachers and peers.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees believe that "diverse classroom" means "classroom
with a child in a wheelchair". It does not. Every classroom in Pakistan is diverse. The
diversity is just more visible in some than in others.

## Activity: Sort and examine

Work in pairs. About 25 minutes.

Below are eight short descriptions of pupils. For each one, decide which category of
diversity most applies (disability, learning difficulty or social difference). Then state
whether the pupil's main barrier is the impairment/disability itself or the handicap created
by the environment.

1. A boy in Grade 3 cannot read Urdu though his spoken Urdu is good and he understands
   stories read aloud.
2. A girl in Grade 5 sits in the back row and copies from her neighbour's notebook because
   she cannot see the board.
3. A boy who speaks only Seraiki is in a school where all teaching is in Urdu.
4. A girl with polio in her right leg cannot reach the classroom on the first floor.
5. A boy in Grade 4 is restless, leaves his seat often and cannot finish written work in the
   time given.
6. A girl from a landless family misses school every harvest season and returns having
   forgotten much of what she learned.
7. A boy in Grade 2 cannot hold a pencil properly and his handwriting is illegible.
8. A girl is quiet in class but her written work shows understanding; she asks to give
   oral answers instead of written ones.

Compare your answers with another pair. For any pupil where you disagree on the category,
discuss whether more than one category might apply.

## Summary

Learner diversity falls into three broad categories: disability (sensory, physical,
intellectual), learning difficulty (dyslexia, dyscalculia, ADHD) and social difference
(language, gender, poverty, ethnicity). Impairment is the medical condition; disability is
the functional limitation; handicap is the social disadvantage the environment creates. Every
Pakistani classroom contains all three categories of diversity; the teacher's task is to see
them clearly and respond to the handicap, not to blame the child.

## Try this at your practicum school

Walk through the school and note one example of each type of barrier: a physical barrier
(steps, narrow doorways), a sensory barrier (no visual aids, poor lighting), and a social
barrier (language mismatch, gender segregation). Write a short note on what you found and
one practical change for each.

## Summative task

A parent says, "My daughter is slow. She cannot learn. Put her in the special school."
Using the concepts from this topic, write a short paragraph (80-120 words) responding to this
statement. In your response: name one category of diversity that might explain the daughter's
difficulty, distinguish the impairment from the handicap, and suggest one thing the ordinary
school could do before considering a special school.
