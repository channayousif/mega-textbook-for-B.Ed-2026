## A real classroom situation

Government Boys Primary School, a village in Taluka Tando Muhammad Khan. Monday morning, the
second week of the session. Four classrooms, two of them in use, 212 pupils on the register and
about 160 present. The head teacher, Mr Soomro, teaches Grade 5 and runs the school.

Bilal's father has come to enrol his son. Bilal is seven. He was born hearing and lost most of his
hearing after an illness at two. He has no hearing aid. At home he communicates with about forty
signs the family invented, points, and reads faces very well. He has never been to school.

Mr Soomro is not unkind. He gives them twenty minutes, which is more than many head teachers
would. He says four things.

1. "There is a special school for such children in Hyderabad. That is the proper place."
2. "I have no teacher here who knows the sign language."
3. "In a class of fifty-five, he will sit and understand nothing. It is not fair to the boy."
4. "Bring me an order from the Education Officer and I will admit him."

Bilal's father has heard about something. A relative in Karachi told him that "there is now a law
for disabled persons, from the Assembly". He says this. Mr Soomro answers, honestly, that nobody
has sent him any such law, that he has received no circular about it, and that he has been a head
teacher for nineteen years and has never had a deaf child on his register.

Every statement in that conversation is either true or sincerely believed. Nobody in it is a
villain. And yet a seven-year-old is not going to school.

Hold the conversation in mind. We will return to it three times. In this topic we ask the first
question a trainee usually asks, which is: **what does the government's own policy say about
this?**

## Explanation

### What a national education policy is, and what it is not

A **national education policy** is a statement by a government of what it intends the education
system to become. It sets goals, names priorities, and commits the government to actions. It is
written to guide the people who write budgets, design curricula, build schools and train teachers.

It is not a law. Nobody is prosecuted for breaching a policy. No parent can take a policy to a
court and ask for an order. A policy does not create a right that a named child holds against a
named school.

This distinction sounds academic until you are standing in Mr Soomro's office. His fourth statement
("bring me an order") is a request for something with authority behind it. A photocopy of a policy
paragraph is not that. An Act of the Provincial Assembly, which Topic 5.2 turns to, is closer to
it.

So why teach the policy at all? Three reasons, and they are not ceremonial.

- The policy is the document the education department itself works from. Officers, trainers and
  curriculum writers were given this text, not the Act. If you want a district officer to act, the
  policy is the vocabulary he already recognises.
- A policy reveals how a system was thinking. The words a government chose in 2009 tell you what
  it understood inclusion to be, and that understanding is still sitting in many of the people who
  were trained on it.
- Where a policy and an Act point the same way, you can cite both. Where a policy is silent and an
  Act is not, the silence is useful information: it tells you where a teacher will get agreement
  in principle and no mechanism.

#### The misconception: a policy statement is the same as a legal duty

This is the first of four misconceptions this unit addresses, and the one that wastes most of a
young teacher's energy. Trainees who learn that "the National Education Policy promotes inclusive
education" often believe they have learned that schools are **required** to include. They then
quote the policy at a head teacher, are brushed aside, and conclude that the law means nothing in
Pakistan.

The policy means what a policy means. The *requirement* is somewhere else. Knowing which document
is which is not pedantry: it is the difference between an argument that can be deflected and a
request that cannot.

### What the National Education Policy 2009 actually says

The relevant edition is the **National Education Policy 2009**, revised 1 August 2009, issued by
the Ministry of Education. A later and much shorter document, the *National Education Policy
Framework 2018*, exists and is sometimes what people mean when they say "the policy"; it is named
here for currency and is not the document this topic analyses. The course guide's topic is the 2009
policy, and so is this.

The 2009 policy's inclusion content sits in section 2.4, "Overarching priorities: widening access
and raising quality", in a numbered list of system-level policy actions. Two of them bear directly
on this course. They are quoted in full because their brevity is the point:

> 4. To achieve the commitments of Government of Pakistan towards Education for All (EFA) and the
> MDGs, inclusive and child-friendly education shall be promoted.
>
> 5. Special measures shall be adopted to ensure inclusion of special persons in mainstream
> education as well as in literacy and TVE programmes.
>
> (National Education Policy 2009, s. 2.4, Policy Actions 4 and 5)

The action immediately before them is also worth having:

> 3. The plans shall also promote equity in education with the aim of eliminating social exclusion
> and promoting national cohesion. Greater opportunities shall be provided to marginalised groups
> of society, particularly girls.
>
> (National Education Policy 2009, s. 2.4, Policy Action 3)

Read Action 5 again. There are two things in it that a trainee should notice.

First, **"inclusion of special persons in mainstream education"**. This is a genuine commitment to
mainstreaming, made by the federal government in 2009, and it is worth knowing that it exists. A
head teacher who says "the proper place for such children is a special school" is not stating
government policy. He is stating a practice that government policy, even in 2009, had already set
itself against.

Second, **"special persons"**. That phrase has aged badly, and the ageing is instructive rather
than embarrassing. Unit 2 taught you person-first language and the move away from labels that
define a person by a condition. "Special persons" was the respectful term in Pakistani official
writing in 2009. The Sindh Act of 2018 says "persons with disabilities". The vocabulary moved in
nine years, which is a useful reminder that terminology is a practice and not a possession, and
that you should expect your own current phrasing to be corrected by somebody in 2035.

The policy also, in its own annex on equity in education, concedes the problem in unusually blunt
terms:

> It is common knowledge, as well as a proven outcome of many studies that discrimination exists
> in the education system in various forms. The inequity has been the result of poor implementation
> and social customs.
>
> (National Education Policy 2009, Annex, s. B, para 9)

"Poor implementation and social customs." Keep that phrase; Topic 5.3 is largely about the second
half of it.

### What the policy does not say, and why the silence matters

This is where a careful reading pays. The National Education Policy 2009 is seventy-one pages long.
Its treatment of learners with disabilities is the two sentences above.

Specifically, and a reviewer can check every item of this list against the policy text:

- There is **no definition** of inclusive education anywhere in it. The phrase appears in Action 4
  and is not explained.
- There is **no chapter** on inclusive or special education. Other sub-sectors get chapters;
  this does not.
- There is **no duty on a school**. Both relevant actions are addressed to governments, not to
  institutions, and neither names an entitlement a particular child holds.
- There is **no disability data**. The policy's own equity annex disaggregates by gender, by
  rural and urban, and by province. Disability is not disaggregated anywhere in it, which is
  consistent with Unit 2's point that a child who is not counted is a child nobody plans for.
- There is **no accountability mechanism**. Nothing says who checks, how often, what evidence
  counts, or what follows if nothing happens.
- There is **no mention of accommodation**, reasonable or otherwise, and no mention of
  accessibility of school buildings in the inclusion actions.

Now put that beside Mr Soomro's four statements. The policy does contradict his first statement.
It does not help at all with his second, third or fourth, because it creates no training
entitlement he can claim, no class-size relief, and no instrument his father can bring him.

This is what a **policy-to-practice gap** looks like at its origin. It is not only that
implementation failed. Part of the gap was built into the document: a commitment with no
definition, no duty, no data and no mechanism will not survive contact with a head teacher who has
fifty-five children in a room and no training.

Pakistan is not unusual in this. UNESCO's *Global education monitoring report 2020: Inclusion and
education: All means all* takes inclusion as its whole subject precisely because the distance
between what education systems declare and what they deliver is a worldwide pattern rather than a
local failing. That is worth knowing before you conclude that your own system is uniquely broken;
it is not worth knowing as an excuse, which is why the rest of this unit is about one province's
law and one teacher's decisions.

#### Reading a policy for what it commits and for what it leaves open

Make this a habit, because you will meet many policy documents in your career and most of them
reward the same four questions.

1. **Who is addressed?** "Governments shall", "provinces shall", "schools shall", "teachers
   shall". The addressee tells you whose desk the obligation lands on. In the 2009 policy's
   inclusion actions, the addressee is a government.
2. **What verb?** "Shall be promoted" is softer than "shall provide", which is softer than "shall
   not be denied". Weak verbs are not dishonest; they are a signal about how much the drafter was
   prepared to be held to.
3. **Is there a mechanism?** A target, a date, a budget line, a reporting duty, a named officer, a
   penalty. A commitment with no mechanism is an intention.
4. **What is absent?** The hardest and most useful question. Look for the definition, the data,
   the complaint route. Absence is rarely accidental.

Apply those four questions to Action 5. Addressee: government. Verb: "shall be adopted", passive,
with no actor named for the adopting. Mechanism: none. Absent: the definition, the data and the
route. That is a two-minute analysis you could do in an examination, and it is worth more than
memorising the sentence.

### The 2009 policy in its sequence

A policy does not stand alone, and Figure 5.1 puts this one in order. Four points about the
sequence matter for a Sindh teacher.

**Before 2009, the framing was welfare and employment.** The Disabled Persons (Employment and
Rehabilitation) Ordinance, 1981 was the main Pakistani instrument for decades, and it is built
around employment quotas and rehabilitation rather than schooling. It created no school duty. If
you hear an older colleague say "there has always been a law", this is usually the law meant, and
it is not an education law.

**In 2006 the international standard changed.** The UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with
Disabilities was adopted in 2006, and its Article 24, which Unit 1 taught you, requires states to
ensure an inclusive education system at all levels. Pakistan ratified the Convention on 25 October
2011. Ratification is a commitment by the state to bring its own law into line; the Sindh Act's
preamble recites exactly this, naming the Convention, the ratification date, and the fact that
disability is a provincial subject under the Constitution.

**In 2010 the 18th Amendment moved the desk.** Education and the welfare of persons with
disabilities became provincial subjects. This is the single most practically important fact in this
topic. It means that after 2010 the instrument that governs your school is a **provincial** one,
and it explains why a federal Act passed in 2020 does not reach Tando Muhammad Khan. It also
explains why no federal circular ever arrived on Mr Soomro's desk: by 2018 the federal government
was not the one with the duty.

**In 2018 Sindh legislated.** The Sindh Empowerment of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2018 was
passed by the Provincial Assembly on 24 May 2018, assented to by the Governor on 7 June, and
notified on 11 June. Its section 9 is titled "Equity in education" and it is the subject of
Topic 5.2.

So the honest summary of Pakistan's position is this. The 2009 policy is a federal statement of
intent, made before devolution, with no definition and no mechanism. The duty that actually binds
a Sindh school was created nine years later by the province. A teacher who knows only the policy
knows the weaker half of the position.

### Worked example: the policy read against one school

Take the 2009 policy's three relevant actions and audit Mr Soomro's school against them, as a
district officer might. This is the kind of reading the topic is teaching, and it is deliberately
uncomfortable in both directions.

**Action 3, equity and the elimination of social exclusion, greater opportunities for marginalised
groups.** The school enrols girls and boys; the Grade 1 register has 31 girls and 36 boys, which
is better than the taluka average. On gender, something has happened. On disability, the register
records no pupil with a disability at all, in a catchment of about 1,400 households. Unit 2 told
you what a zero like that means: it is not an absence of children, it is an absence of counting.
Verdict: partial, and the part that failed is the part this course is about.

**Action 4, inclusive and child-friendly education shall be promoted.** Promoted by whom, to whom?
No circular has reached this school. No in-service training on inclusion has been offered to its
four teachers in nineteen years. Verdict: not implemented at this school, and the failure is above
the head teacher, not inside his office.

**Action 5, special measures to ensure inclusion of special persons in mainstream education.** No
special measure of any kind is visible: no identification, no accommodation, no referral route
except "the special school in Hyderabad", which is a two-hour journey the family cannot make
daily. Verdict: not implemented, and note what is being substituted for inclusion, which is
segregation framed as kindness.

Now the uncomfortable second direction. What would the 2009 policy, on its own terms, have
**required** Mr Soomro to do differently this morning?

Nothing enforceable. It would have told his department to promote inclusive education and adopt
special measures. It gives him no duty, no definition of what inclusion would mean in his Grade 1,
no training, no accommodation budget and no answer to his class size. A trainee who walks into that
office with only the policy in her hand is carrying a document that agrees with her and commits
him to nothing.

That is a real finding, and it is the reason the unit has a Topic 5.2. It is also the reason
Topic 5.3 exists: because even after Topic 5.2 arms you with an Act, Mr Soomro's second and third
statements are still standing, and they are about capacity and belief rather than about law.

### What the policy still gives you

Do not leave this topic with the conclusion that the policy is worthless. Three uses survive, and
all three are things you can do.

1. **It legitimises the conversation.** "Government policy since 2009 has been to include special
   persons in mainstream education" is a true sentence, it is federal, and it is the sentence that
   stops "such children belong elsewhere" from being presented as official wisdom.
2. **It gives the department its own words back.** A request written in the vocabulary of equity,
   social exclusion and Education for All is a request an education officer has been trained to
   recognise. Pair the policy's language with the Act's duty and you have both legitimacy and
   leverage.
3. **It documents the gap for you.** When you argue later for training or for a resource, the
   policy is evidence that the system has promised this for over fifteen years. A promise on record
   is harder to deny than a need you merely assert.

## Activity: The four-question policy audit

Work in pairs. About forty minutes. You need the quoted passages in this topic and nothing else.

**Part 1 - the audit (15 minutes).** Take Policy Action 4 and Policy Action 5 as quoted above.
For each, answer the four reading questions on paper: who is addressed, what verb, what mechanism,
what is absent. Write one line per question. Do not consult the Act yet.

**Part 2 - the same questions on a real school (15 minutes).** Use your own practicum school, or
the one you attended as a child if you have no placement yet. Answer three questions:

1. How many pupils with a disability are on its register? If the answer is zero or unknown, write
   that down as a finding rather than as missing data.
2. Has any teacher there received training on inclusion? Ask, if you can.
3. If a father arrived tomorrow with a deaf seven-year-old, what would actually happen, step by
   step, in that school? Write the steps, not the ideal.

**Part 3 - the one-page note (10 minutes).** Write a one-page note to a district education
officer, in the policy's own vocabulary, asking for one specific thing for that school. Not a
complaint and not a list. One thing, named, with the policy action it serves and the evidence that
it is absent.

Swap notes with another pair. Their job is to find anything in your note that a busy officer could
deflect in one sentence, and to say what he would say. This is the most useful fifteen seconds of
the activity: most first drafts can be deflected, and knowing how is what makes the second draft
work.

## Summary

A **national education policy** states what a government intends. It is not a law: it creates no
right a named child holds against a named school and no duty a parent can enforce. Confusing the
two is the first misconception of this unit, and it is the reason trainees quote policy at head
teachers and get nowhere.

The **National Education Policy 2009** commits Pakistan, in section 2.4, to two things that matter
here: that "inclusive and child-friendly education shall be promoted" (Action 4), and that
"special measures shall be adopted to ensure inclusion of special persons in mainstream education"
(Action 5). Action 3 adds equity and the elimination of social exclusion, with particular reference
to girls. The policy's own equity annex concedes that discrimination exists in the education system
and attributes the inequity to "poor implementation and social customs". The dated phrase "special
persons" marks the document's period rather than discrediting it.

Its **silences** are as important as its commitments. In seventy-one pages the policy gives no
definition of inclusive education, no chapter on it, no duty on any school, no disability
disaggregation in its equity data, no accountability mechanism and no mention of accommodation.
A commitment with no definition, no duty, no data and no mechanism does not survive contact with a
head teacher who has fifty-five children and no training. Part of the policy-to-practice gap was
built into the document.

Read any policy with **four questions**: who is addressed, what verb is used, what mechanism
exists, and what is absent. On Action 5 the answers are government, a passive "shall be adopted",
none, and the definition, the data and the complaint route.

The **sequence** matters. The 1981 Ordinance framed disability as employment and welfare and
created no school duty. The UNCRPD was adopted in 2006 and ratified by Pakistan on 25 October
2011. The 18th Amendment in 2010 made education and the welfare of persons with disabilities
provincial subjects, which is why the instrument that binds a Sindh school is the Sindh Act of
2018 and why the federal Act of 2020 reaches only Islamabad.

The policy still has three uses: it legitimises the conversation, it gives the department its own
vocabulary back, and it documents on the record that the system has promised this for over fifteen
years.

## Try this at your practicum school

Two tasks. Write up each in half a page.

1. **The register count.** Ask, politely and of the right person, how many pupils with a
   disability are on the school's register, and how the school knows. Record the number, the
   source of the number, and whether anyone was able to answer. If nobody can, record that; it is
   the finding, and it is the same finding the 2009 policy's own missing data predicts.
2. **The hypothetical.** Ask one teacher and, if you can, the head teacher: "If a parent brought a
   deaf child of seven to enrol next week, what would happen?" Write their answers in their own
   words, without arguing. Then write what the 2009 policy would and would not have required, and
   leave a blank line for what the Sindh Act requires, which you will fill in after Topic 5.2.

Bring both to the next session. The second task is the one to do carefully, because in the next
topic you will come back to the same answers with an Act in your hand, and the contrast is the
teaching.

## Summative task

**Task.** Write a **policy analysis note** of no more than two pages on the inclusion content of
the National Education Policy 2009, addressed to a fellow trainee who has not read it.

Your note must contain:

1. The two policy actions that bear on inclusion, quoted accurately, with the section identified.
2. The four-question reading applied to one of them.
3. A list of at least four silences, each with a one-line statement of what its absence costs in
   a school office.
4. One paragraph placing the 2009 policy in the sequence, and explaining the effect of the 18th
   Amendment on which document binds a Sindh school.
5. A closing paragraph on what the policy is still useful for, naming at least two uses.

**Mini-rubric** (20 marks)

| Criterion | Marks | What a strong response does |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy of the policy content | 4 | Quotes or paraphrases the two actions correctly and attributes them to the right section and edition. Does not attribute to the policy anything it does not contain. |
| The four-question reading | 4 | All four questions answered with reference to the actual wording, including the passive construction and the absent actor. |
| Analysis of silence *(Analyze)* | 5 | Four or more silences named precisely, each tied to a concrete consequence in a real school rather than to an abstract shortcoming. |
| Devolution and sequence | 4 | The 18th Amendment's effect is stated correctly and used to explain which instrument binds the reader's own school. |
| Balance | 3 | The note neither dismisses the policy nor overstates it. The three remaining uses are named without turning into consolation. |

A note that ends "therefore the policy is useless" loses the balance marks even if everything
before it is correct. The policy is weak, and a professional says precisely how weak and then says
what she will do with it anyway.
