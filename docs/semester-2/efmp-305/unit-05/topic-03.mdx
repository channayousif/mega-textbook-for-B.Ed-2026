## A real classroom situation

Different school. Government Girls Higher Secondary School in a small town in Sindh, a town with
electricity most of the day and a bus route. Miss Farzana teaches Grade 7 English. Seventy-one
girls on the register, about sixty present, four long benches deep.

Miss Farzana has done a two-day departmental workshop on inclusive education. She can define it.
She agrees with it. Asked directly, she says: "Of course every child has the right to education. I
have no objection to any child."

Three facts about her classroom.

Rukhsana, who has a moderate hearing loss, sits in the fourth row because that is where the
register put her in April and the register has not been changed. Miss Farzana knows about the
hearing loss. She has not moved her, because moving one girl means a conversation with the class
teacher who made the seating plan, and because "she manages".

Sidra, who reads about three years below her grade, is never asked to read aloud. Miss Farzana
regards this as a kindness, and in one sense it is: Sidra was laughed at in Grade 5 and has not
volunteered since. The effect, after eighteen months, is that Sidra has not read aloud in front of
anyone and nobody knows what she can now do.

Nasreen, who has a visible physical disability and uses a stick, was not entered for the
inter-school debate, although she is one of the three best speakers in the class. Miss Farzana did
not decide to exclude her. She filled in the form at the end of a long day and wrote three names,
and Nasreen's was not among them. When asked afterwards she was genuinely dismayed.

Now the question this topic exists to ask. **Miss Farzana has a favourable attitude to inclusion.
Does she?**

## Explanation

### Attitude is not a feeling, it is a hundred decisions

Ask any staff room in Pakistan whether children with disabilities have a right to education and
almost every hand goes up. Attitude surveys in Pakistan find broadly favourable general attitudes
too. And yet Rukhsana is in the fourth row.

The trouble is that we measure attitude by asking, and attitude operates by deciding. The things
that determine whether a pupil is included are not opinions; they are:

- who gets called on, and who is reliably skipped;
- who is seated where, and whether anybody revisits it;
- who is entered for the debate, the sports day, the scholarship form;
- what is expected, which is the big one, and which shows in what you accept as her finished work;
- who gets the five minutes after the bell;
- whose parents get spoken to, and in what tone.

Each of those is small. Each is made in a hurry, at the end of a long day, in a class of
seventy-one. None of them feels like a decision about inclusion.

So the working definition for this topic: **a teacher's attitude is the pattern of her small
decisions about a pupil, not the sentence she would write on a survey.** You can be in favour of
inclusion and not practise it, which is Miss Farzana's position and is a much more common position
than opposition.

#### The misconception: inclusion is the government's job, not the teacher's

This is the fourth and last misconception of the unit, and it is the one that survives everything
Topic 5.2 established.

It has a strong-sounding version: the duties in section 9 are addressed to the Government, the
posts and the buildings and the training are not in a teacher's gift, and therefore inclusion is
something that will arrive, or fail to arrive, from above.

Here is why it does not hold. Of the six decisions listed above, the Government makes none. A
signing teacher for Bilal requires a sanctioned post. Moving Rukhsana to the front requires one
conversation. Entering Nasreen for the debate requires remembering her name while holding a pen.
The part of inclusion that the state controls is the expensive part. The part that decides whether a
pupil who **is** in school participates in it is almost entirely in the hands of the person
standing in front of the class.

That is not a reason to let the state off. Topic 5.2 taught you to write the escalation. It is a
reason to notice that the teacher-sized portion of the problem is large, cheap, and nobody else's.

### What Pakistani teachers themselves report

It is worth knowing what the research literature in Pakistan finds, both because it corrects a
caricature and because it tells you what your own staff room is likely to say.

Parveen, Batool and Arif (2022) surveyed 191 pre-service teachers enrolled on teacher-training
programmes at a university in Lahore, using two established instruments: Attitudes Toward Inclusive
Education Scale and Concerns about Inclusive Education Scale. Three findings from their abstract
are directly useful to you.

- Attitudes were **generally favourable** toward including pupils with several kinds of difficulty,
  including pupils who have trouble expressing themselves, pupils performing two or more academic
  years below grade level, and pupils needing instruction in self-help and daily-living skills.
- Attitudes were **least favourable** toward including pupils with visual impairment and pupils
  requiring individualised functional reading and mathematics.
- The **concerns** respondents expressed most strongly were a shortage of time, the anticipated
  dislike of parents of children without disabilities, and inappropriate school infrastructure.

Read those three together. The pattern is not hostility. It is a workforce that accepts the
principle, becomes much less confident where it feels unqualified, and is worried about time,
buildings and other parents. Those are the real obstacles to name in a staff room, and two of the
three are not about the child at all.

Virani and Ali (2022) took a different approach: an exploratory qualitative study with fifteen
purposively selected primary school teachers in Pakistan, five of them with pupils with special
needs on their registers. Their abstract reports that negative attitudes among the public, and
particularly among teachers and parents, can be considered among the most significant deterrents to
inclusive education, and that successful inclusion requires proper comprehension of diverse
learning needs, responsible management and a stronger parent-teacher relationship.

Anis, Waqar and Muhammad (2025) report something else worth knowing. They developed and validated
an instrument for measuring Pakistani elementary teachers' attitudes to inclusive education with
300 teachers from public and private schools, and the factor structure they recovered had four
dimensions: professional knowledge and preparedness, attitudes and beliefs, resource availability
and support, and collaboration and development. Each accounted for roughly equal variance. Whatever
else that tells us, it says that in the Pakistani context "attitude" does not come apart from
preparedness, resources and collaboration; they travel together.

**How to use this evidence honestly.** All three are Pakistani studies and all three are open
access. None of them was read here beyond its abstract, which is recorded in the unit's sources
file, so none of them is cited in this unit for a number, a strength of effect, or a claim about
how widespread anything is nationally. Parveen and colleagues studied pre-service teachers at one
university in one city; Virani and Ali interviewed fifteen teachers. These are indications of
pattern, not measurements of a country. Use them in an argument the way this topic does: as what
teachers report, labelled as such.

### Why attitude has such leverage: the cycle

Figure 5.5 draws the mechanism. It is worth walking through with Sidra, the girl who is never asked
to read aloud, because the cycle is the reason a kindness becomes an exclusion.

**Belief.** Miss Farzana expects that Sidra cannot read aloud at Grade 7 level without
embarrassment. In April this was probably accurate.

**Decision.** She does not call on Sidra. The decision takes no time and feels humane.

**Experience.** Sidra is in a class where she is never asked. Over eighteen months this is not
neutral: it is a daily, repeated signal about what she is for in this room.

**Behaviour.** Sidra stops offering. She also stops practising, because reading aloud in class was
the only place she ever did it.

**Evidence.** Sidra, asked in December, reads worse relative to her peers than she did in April,
and visibly dreads being asked.

**Back to belief.** Miss Farzana's expectation is now confirmed by evidence she produced. And she
is not lying when she says Sidra cannot read aloud.

That is the attitude-action cycle, and its cruelty is that it requires no ill will at any point. It
is also why low expectation is a more dangerous attitude than open objection: objection can be
argued with, and expectation hides inside what looks like evidence.

Two interventions break it, and Figure 5.5 marks where each enters.

**A declared duty enters at the decision.** This is what a law can reach. Nothing in the Sindh Act
can make Miss Farzana believe in Sidra. But a school rule that every pupil reads aloud at least
once a fortnight, or a seating policy that is reviewed each term against named needs, changes the
decision without waiting for the belief. Duties and procedures are how a system gets past attitudes
it cannot directly change, which is why Topic 5.2's audit matters even in a school full of
goodwill.

**Contact with a pupil succeeding enters at the evidence.** This is what changes belief, and in
practice almost nothing else does. A teacher who has taught one deaf child well stops being the
teacher who says a deaf child cannot be taught here. This is the single strongest argument for
admitting Bilal rather than redirecting him: he is the evidence the school needs, and the school
cannot acquire that evidence any other way.

#### What a teacher can honestly be asked to do about her own attitude

Not "be more positive". That instruction has never changed anyone's behaviour, and trainees are
right to be irritated by it.

Four things are actually actionable.

1. **Audit the decisions, not the feelings.** For one week, keep a tally of who you call on. Then
   look at whether the pupil you would describe as "needing support" appears in it. This is Unit
   4's participation tally, used on yourself for a different purpose.
2. **Separate the two questions you are actually asking.** "Should this child be here?" and "am I
   equipped to teach this child?" are different questions, and the second one is often answered as
   though it were the first. Doubt about your own preparation is legitimate, is widely shared by
   Pakistani teachers, and is not a reason to exclude a child. Say the second out loud so that it
   stops masquerading as the first.
3. **Name the reservation.** Write down, in one sentence, the thing about inclusion you are not
   convinced by. Everyone has one. An unnamed reservation operates at the level of decisions and
   cannot be examined; a named one can be tested against what happened in your own class.
4. **Choose one pupil and change one decision.** Move Rukhsana. Ask Sidra to read two sentences,
   prepared in advance so the first time is not a failure. Put Nasreen's name on the form. Then
   watch what happens, because the evidence you collect is the only thing that will move the
   belief.

### The policy-to-practice gap: analyze it, do not deplore it

"There are good policies but no implementation" is the most common sentence in Pakistani education
discussion, and as analysis it is worthless. It names no cause, no level and no action.

Ainscow (2020) frames the global version of the problem usefully: there is increased interest in
making education systems more inclusive and equitable, but, in his words, the field "remains
confused as to the actions needed in order to move policy and practice forward". His argument from
international experience is that the moves that matter are as much about school culture,
collaboration and the use of existing resources as about new money.

UNESCO's *Global education monitoring report 2020*, whose entire subject is inclusion in
education, exists because this gap is a global one: systems across the world have adopted inclusive
commitments faster than they have changed what happens in classrooms. The usefulness of that for
you is narrow but real. It means the gap is a normal object of analysis with known causes, rather
than evidence that Pakistani education is beyond help.

So replace deploring with a method. A gap has **four levels**, and every real gap can be sorted
into them.

**Level 1, the document.** What the instrument actually says, and what it omits. Topic 5.1 showed
the 2009 policy committing to inclusion with no definition, no duty, no data and no mechanism. A
gap that originates here cannot be closed by better implementation of the same words.

**Level 2, the system.** Posts, budgets, training, data, buildings, examinations. The three-yearly
survey the Sindh Act requires; the teachers qualified in sign language it obliges the Government to
employ; the materials it promises free to age eighteen. When these are absent, the gap is systemic
and the teacher-level response is a written record, not a cure.

**Level 3, the school.** The head teacher's decisions, the register, the seating policy, the
admissions practice, whether anybody monitors participation. This level is where a surprising
amount of the gap lives, and it is the level a trainee most often overlooks because it is neither
glamorous nor distant.

**Level 4, the classroom.** The hundred small decisions. Expectation. Who is asked. What is
accepted as finished work.

The use of the method is simple: **for any gap, say which level it is on, and then say what the
action is at that level.** A gap on level 2 does not get solved by a teacher trying harder, and a
gap on level 4 does not get solved by waiting for a circular. Most real gaps sit on more than one
level, and the honest analysis separates them instead of blaming whichever level is furthest away.

### Worked example: Miss Farzana's class, analysed and acted on

Take the three facts from the start of this topic and run the method.

**Rukhsana in the fourth row.** Level 4, with a touch of level 3. The cause at level 4 is that
nobody owns the seating plan after April; at level 3, that the school has no termly seating review
against named needs. The action: move her this week, to the second row on the side of her better
ear, and tell the class teacher why in one sentence. Cost: nothing. Time: four minutes. The level 3
action is a one-line addition to the school's start-of-term routine, which is the head teacher's
decision and worth asking for.

**Sidra never reading aloud.** Level 4 entirely, and it is the attitude-action cycle. The action:
two sentences, given to her the day before so that the first attempt is prepared and cannot
humiliate her, once a fortnight, and a written note of what she manages each time. The note is
there because Miss Farzana's own belief is the thing that has to change, and only evidence will do
it. If after a term there is no change, that is information too, and it belongs in a referral under
duty six of section 9, early detection of specific learning disabilities.

**Nasreen not entered for the debate.** Level 4, cause is nothing more than a form filled at speed.
The action is procedural and takes ten seconds: keep the class register beside any form that
selects pupils, and read it. Procedures beat intentions, which is the same lesson the Act teaches
at a larger scale.

Now the part a trainee must not skip. What in Miss Farzana's situation is **not** level 4?

Seventy-one girls in a class is a system and school matter, and it is real: the shortage-of-time
concern that Parveen and colleagues found teachers naming most strongly is a true description of
her week. The absence of any in-service training beyond two days is level 2. The fourth row
existing at all, in a room four benches deep, is level 3. Miss Farzana cannot fix any of those by
Monday.

What she can do by Monday is move one girl, prepare two sentences for another, and put a third
girl's name on a form. Three decisions, no money, about fifteen minutes in total. That is not the
whole of inclusion. It is the whole of the part that belongs to her, and it is the part that
decides what these three girls' year is like.

### What this unit asks of you, in the end

You now know four things a trainee teacher in Sindh mostly does not know.

1. That the National Education Policy 2009 commits the state to inclusion and gives you no duty to
   cite, so you should not rest an argument on it alone.
2. That the Sindh Empowerment of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2018 places ten specific duties on
   your school, including admission without discrimination and reasonable accommodation according
   to the individual's requirements.
3. That reasonable accommodation is measured against the pupil's need and not against the budget,
   that its denial is discrimination in the Act's own words, and that most accommodations are free.
4. That the portion of the gap that belongs to you is made of small decisions, is nearly free, and
   is not waiting for anyone's permission.

Four things move a case like Bilal's, and three of them cost nothing: an accurate citation of the
right Act, a written request that names the pupil and the clause, and a school audit that produces
evidence rather than opinion. The fourth is money – a resourced provision the school cannot supply
from its own means, such as a sanctioned post or a trained specialist – and it is the one you
escalate.

Do not leave this unit believing either of the two comfortable conclusions. Not "the law has solved
this", because Bilal's father was refused with the Act already in force for three years. And not
"the law changes nothing here", because the refusal was unlawful, and the only reason nobody said so
is that nobody in the room knew. You now know.

## Activity: The reservation and the gap analysis

Work individually for the first part. This activity does not work if you are performing for a
group.

**Part 1 - the reservation (10 minutes, private).** Write one sentence: the thing about inclusive
education you are **not** convinced by. Not a safe sentence. The real one. Examples of real ones
from previous cohorts: "I do not believe a deaf child can learn in a class of sixty and I think we
are pretending." "I think it is unfair to the other fifty-nine." "I am afraid of a child having a
fit and me not knowing what to do." "I think parents of children with disabilities expect too much
of one teacher."

Underneath, write two things: what would have to happen for you to change your mind, and whether
the reservation is about the child, about your own preparation, or about the conditions you work
in.

Nobody collects this. The point is that an unnamed reservation acts on your decisions and cannot be
examined.

**Part 2 - pair exchange (10 minutes).** With one partner you trust, exchange only the **third**
answer: child, preparation, or conditions. Count across the pair. Across a whole cohort, the great
majority of honest reservations turn out to be about preparation or conditions rather than about
the child, and seeing that is worth more than being told it.

**Part 3 - the gap analysis (25 minutes, in threes).** Choose one real gap from a school one of you
knows. Write:

1. The gap, in one sentence, concretely. Not "lack of facilities".
2. Its level or levels, with the cause at each.
3. The action at each level, and who owns it.
4. The one action available to a classroom teacher this week, with its cost.
5. What evidence would tell you in three months whether anything had changed.

Present point 4 only to the room, in one minute, and let the room challenge whether it is really
free and really available this week. Half of all first drafts fail that challenge, which is the
most useful finding of the session.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State the working definition of a teacher's attitude used in this topic.

2. *(Understand)* Explain the attitude-action cycle in your own words, using a pupil who is never
   asked to read aloud, and say at which two points it can be interrupted.

3. *(Understand)* Parveen and colleagues (2022) found pre-service teachers' attitudes generally
   favourable but least favourable toward two groups. Name the two, and explain what their
   particular reluctance suggests about what the workforce needs.

4. *(Understand)* Explain why low expectation is harder to correct than open objection.

5. *(Apply)* Name the four levels of a policy-to-practice gap, and assign each of these to a level:
   no sanctioned post for a signing teacher; a seating plan nobody revisits; a policy that defines
   nothing; a school that counts enrolment but not participation.

6. *(Apply)* A teacher says, "Inclusion is the government's job." Answer her in four sentences, in
   a way that concedes what is true and names three decisions that are hers.

7. *(Analyze)* Take Miss Farzana's three facts. For each, identify the level, the cause at that
   level, and the free action available this week. Then state the two things in her situation that
   are not within her power.

8. *(Analyze)* Why is admitting one pupil with a disability the fastest way to change a staff's
   beliefs about whether such a pupil can be taught there? Analyze the mechanism, and state one
   risk of the argument if the admission is not supported.

9. *(Evaluate)* "A teacher with a favourable attitude and no training does less harm than a teacher
   with training and a poor attitude." Evaluate this claim with reference to the cycle and to what
   Pakistani teachers report about their own preparedness.

## Summary

A teacher's **attitude** is not what she would write on a survey. It is the pattern of her small
decisions: who is called on, who is seated where, who is entered for the debate, what is expected,
whose parents are spoken to and how. You can be sincerely in favour of inclusion and not practise
it, and that position is far more common than opposition.

Pakistani evidence supports a more useful picture than hostility. Parveen and colleagues (2022),
surveying 191 pre-service teachers in Lahore, found generally favourable attitudes, markedly less
favourable attitudes toward pupils with visual impairment and pupils needing individualised
functional reading and mathematics, and strongest concerns about shortage of time, the anticipated
dislike of other parents, and inappropriate infrastructure. Virani and Ali (2022), interviewing
fifteen primary teachers, report attitudes among teachers and parents as among the most significant
deterrents, and name comprehension of diverse needs, responsible management and the parent-teacher
relationship as what successful inclusion requires. Anis and colleagues (2025) recovered four
roughly equal dimensions in Pakistani elementary teachers' attitudes: professional knowledge and
preparedness, attitudes and beliefs, resource availability and support, and collaboration and
development. All three are cited here from their abstracts for pattern, never for a number or for
national prevalence.

The **attitude-action cycle** explains the leverage. A belief about what a pupil can do produces a
decision, the decision produces an experience, the experience produces behaviour, and the behaviour
becomes the evidence that confirms the belief. No ill will is required at any point, which is why
low expectation is more dangerous than objection: it hides inside what looks like evidence. Two
things break the cycle. A **declared duty or procedure** enters at the decision, which is what a
law or a school rule can reach without changing anyone's mind. **Contact with a pupil succeeding**
enters at the evidence, which is the only thing that reliably changes the belief, and is the
strongest practical argument for admitting the child rather than redirecting her.

Four things a teacher can honestly be asked to do about her own attitude: audit the decisions
rather than the feelings; separate "should this child be here?" from "am I equipped to teach this
child?", because the second is legitimate, widely shared, and not a reason to exclude; name the
reservation so it can be examined; and change one decision for one pupil and watch what happens.

"Good policies, no implementation" is not analysis. A **policy-to-practice gap** has four levels:
the **document** (what it says and omits), the **system** (posts, budgets, training, data,
buildings, examinations), the **school** (admission practice, register, seating policy,
monitoring), and the **classroom** (the hundred decisions and, above all, expectation). For any
gap, name the level, then name the action at that level. A level 2 gap is not closed by a teacher
trying harder; a level 4 gap is not closed by waiting for a circular. Ainscow (2020) argues from
international experience that moving policy to practice is as much about school culture,
collaboration and the use of existing resources as about new money.

In Miss Farzana's class, three of the gaps are level 4 and cost nothing: move Rukhsana this week,
give Sidra two prepared sentences every fortnight, keep the register beside the entry form. Two
things are not hers: seventy-one pupils in a room and the absence of training. Naming both honestly
is what separates analysis from either complaint or guilt.

Four things move a case: an accurate citation of the right Act, a written request naming the pupil
and the clause, an audit that produces evidence rather than opinion, and a resourced provision the
school cannot supply from its own means. Three of the four are free; the fourth is money and the
one you escalate.

## Try this at your practicum school

Three tasks. Half a page each, and the third one matters most.

1. **The call-on tally, on yourself.** Teach one lesson and have someone tally which pupils you
   call on, or tally it yourself immediately afterwards from memory and mark it as such. Then look
   for the pupil you would describe as needing support. Write what you find, including if the
   finding is comfortable.
2. **The staff room question.** Ask two teachers, separately and without arguing: "What worries you
   most about having a child with a disability in your class?" Write their answers in their own
   words. Then sort each answer into child, preparation or conditions. Compare with what Parveen
   and colleagues report.
3. **One decision.** Choose one pupil and change one decision for her this week: a seat, a question
   asked, a name on a form, an expectation raised. Record what you did, what happened, and what the
   pupil did differently if anything. One week is not enough for a result, which is why you also
   record what you will watch for over the term.

## Summative task

**Task.** Write a **gap analysis and action plan** of no more than three pages on one real
policy-to-practice gap in a school you know.

Your plan must contain:

1. The gap stated concretely in one sentence, with the evidence that it exists.
2. A four-level analysis: which levels the gap sits on, and the cause at each. Levels that do not
   apply are to be stated as not applying rather than padded.
3. The action at each applicable level, with the owner named. Where the owner is above you, the
   written request you would send, including the provision you would cite.
4. The teacher-level action available to you this week, with its cost in rupees and in minutes, and
   the evidence you would collect over one term.
5. An honest paragraph on your own attitude as a factor: the reservation you named in the activity,
   whether it is about the child, your preparation or your conditions, and what would change it.
6. One paragraph evaluating what you can and cannot claim about this gap from the evidence you
   have, including the limits of the Pakistani studies cited in this topic if you use them.

**Mini-rubric** (25 marks)

| Criterion | Marks | What a strong response does |
|---|---|---|
| Concreteness of the gap | 4 | A specific, observable gap with evidence, not "lack of facilities" or "lack of awareness". |
| Four-level analysis *(Analyze)* | 6 | Levels correctly assigned with a distinct cause at each; multi-level gaps separated rather than collapsed; inapplicable levels stated as such. No level is blamed because it is distant. |
| Action and ownership *(Apply)* | 5 | Each action sits at the level that can execute it. The escalation names a pupil and a clause. Nothing systemic is assigned to a teacher's willpower. |
| The teacher-level action | 4 | Available this week, costed in money and minutes, with named evidence to be collected over a term. Survives the "is it really free and really this week?" test. |
| Self-account *(Evaluate)* | 4 | An honest reservation, correctly classified, with a stated condition for change. Neither performed humility nor a denial of having any reservation. |
| Evidential honesty | 2 | States what the cited studies can and cannot support, including their scope, and distinguishes what was observed from what was inferred. |

The criterion that separates a professional from a complainer is the third. Anyone can list what is
wrong with Pakistani education. The person who can say which level each item belongs to, and then
do the one thing on her own level this week, is the person whose pupils are different at the end of
the year.
