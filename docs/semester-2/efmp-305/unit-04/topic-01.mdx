## A real classroom situation

Room 4, Government Girls Primary School, Tando Allahyar. Grade 3. Fifty-eight pupils on fifteen
wooden benches, five rows of three, the benches too heavy for one person to shift. One door at the
front left, with a six-inch step up into the room. Three windows along the left wall, which face
the morning sun. A blackboard, a teacher's table across the front-left corner, and a ceiling fan
that works when there is power.

Miss Nasreen has taught this class since April. Last week the headmistress stood at the door for
ten minutes and told her afterwards that Room 4 was the best-managed class in the school. Nobody
was talking. Nobody was out of their seat. Every pupil had her copy open.

Here is the same ten minutes from inside the room.

- **Rameen**, who had polio as an infant and walks with one crutch, was in her seat. She had been
  in it since 8:05, because getting out of the middle of the third row means asking two girls to
  stand, and she has learned not to ask. She did not go to the water cooler at break. She has
  stopped drinking water at school.
- **Shabana** had her copy open at the right page. She could not read the board. From the third
  row she sees the writing as grey smudges, and at nine in the morning the sun comes through the
  left window straight onto the board's left half. She copies from the girl beside her, which
  looks like diligence and is actually guesswork.
- **Komal**, who hears well on the right side and poorly on the left, sits with her good ear to
  the wall and her poor ear to the room. She catches about half of what Miss Nasreen says while
  Miss Nasreen is facing the blackboard, which is most of the time.
- **Hina** was in her seat for those ten minutes. She is in her seat for about twelve minutes of
  any forty-minute period, after which she stands up, walks to the door and looks out. Miss
  Nasreen has written her name on the board four times this month. It has not changed anything.
- **Zoya** was silent. Zoya has been silent since the first week of term. She has never answered a
  question out loud in Room 4, and no adult in the school has noticed, because a silent pupil in a
  silent class is invisible.
- **Saba** speaks Sindhi at home and is still building Urdu. She was copying the date.

Fifty-eight pupils sat quietly. Perhaps thirty of them were learning. The headmistress was not
wrong about what she saw; she was wrong about what it meant. A quiet class is easy to see from the
door and tells you almost nothing about who in the room has access to the lesson.

Miss Nasreen's room is not badly managed. It is managed for the wrong outcome. She has been
measuring quiet, which is cheap to measure, instead of measuring access and participation, which
take a tally sheet and ten minutes. This topic is about what to measure instead, and what to
change on Monday.

## Explanation

### What classroom management is for

**Classroom management** is the set of arrangements, routines and responses that make learning
possible for every pupil in the room. That is the whole definition, and the last three words are
the ones that make it an inclusion topic.

Notice what the definition does not say. It does not mention quiet, obedience, or control. Those
can be means to the end, and often are. They are not the end, and a teacher who confuses the means
with the end will reliably exclude the pupils who need to move, need to ask, or need more time.

#### The misconception: management means keeping everyone quiet

This is the most expensive misconception in the unit, so name it properly.

Silence has three attractions. It is visible from the doorway, so it earns praise. It is easy to
produce with a loud voice and a stick. And it feels like teaching, because the teacher is the only
one making a noise.

What it costs is specific:

- The pupil who has not understood cannot ask, because asking is talking.
- The pupil who learns by saying things aloud (most eight-year-olds) cannot learn.
- The pupil who physically cannot sit still for forty minutes is turned into a discipline case
  rather than a planning problem.
- The teacher gets no information. In a silent room you cannot tell the difference between a pupil
  who has understood and a pupil who has given up. Shabana looks exactly like a diligent learner.

The repair is not noise. It is **the right noise at the right time**, which means distinguishing
two things that sound similar from the corridor:

- **Productive noise** - fifty-eight pupils telling a partner the answer for ninety seconds, on
  your signal, about the lesson, stopping on your signal.
- **Disruptive noise** - talk that is not about the lesson, that nobody asked for, that does not
  stop.

The first is a management achievement. The second is a management failure. A teacher who cannot
tell them apart will suppress both, and will lose the first one permanently.

#### Four things management actually does

Figure 4.1 names them. They are worth learning as a checklist, because when a lesson goes wrong
one of the four is almost always the cause.

1. **Predictability.** Pupils know what happens next, what the signal means, and what follows a
   particular behaviour. Predictability is the single largest kindness you can offer an anxious
   pupil, a pupil with an intellectual disability, or a pupil whose home is chaotic.
2. **Access.** Every pupil can see the board, hear the instruction, reach her seat, and use the
   material. This is Topic 4.2's subject, but it belongs in the management list because access is
   a management decision, not a building decision, four times out of five.
3. **Participation.** Every pupil is called on, not only those who raise a hand. Participation does
   not happen by itself; left alone, it concentrates in four or five pupils, and this topic shows
   you the tally that proves it.
4. **Response.** What you do when behaviour goes wrong. Inclusive response reads the behaviour as
   information and changes the environment first.

Hold on to the fourth one. It is where this topic turns from description into a method.

### Positive behaviour support: teaching the behaviour you want

**Positive behaviour support** (PBS) is an approach to behaviour that does three things in order:
it works out what the behaviour is **for**, it changes the **environment** that provokes it, and it
**teaches** the pupil the behaviour you want instead. Punishment is not the first move and often
not a move at all.

Iriyani, Santosa and Zuhaery (2025) describe PBS being used in an inclusive school precisely this
way: as a planned, taught, whole-environment approach rather than a reaction after the event. Read
their account as one school's experience, not as proof: it is a small qualitative study in an
Indonesian setting, so it tells you what the approach looks like when a staff takes it seriously,
not how well it would transfer to a class of fifty-eight in Sindh.

#### Behaviour is a message, not a character

Take Hina, who stands up at minute twelve.

The traditional reading is defiance, and the traditional response is her name on the board. Four
names on the board have produced no change, which is itself a finding: if a consequence has been
applied four times and the behaviour is identical, the consequence is not addressing the cause.

The PBS reading asks what standing up achieves for Hina. The likely answers are physical - she
cannot sustain forty minutes of stillness - or escape - the task at minute twelve is copying, she
cannot keep up, and leaving is easier than failing in public.

Each reading suggests a different change to the room, and neither needs a punishment:

- If the function is movement, **give her a legitimate reason to move**. Hina distributes the
  slates at minute ten and collects them at minute thirty. She is now the pupil who moves with
  permission rather than the pupil who defies. Cost: nothing.
- If the function is escape, **shorten the task** and check at the point she usually leaves. Three
  sums, then a tick from you, then three more. Cost: nothing, except that you must walk to her
  bench twice.

You will often not know which function is operating. Then you test one for a week and watch. That
is the whole method: a guess, a change, and an observation.

#### A positive behaviour routine for Grade 3, fifty-eight pupils

Here is the worked example. This is Miss Nasreen's routine after she rebuilt it. Read it as a
sequence you could copy on Monday, and note that nothing in it costs money.

**1. Three rules, stated as what to do.** Not "no talking", "no fighting", "no leaving your seat",
which name the behaviour you do not want and leave the pupil to guess the rest. Instead:

- We listen when someone else is speaking.
- We ask when we do not understand.
- We move when the teacher's hand goes up.

Three is the maximum a Grade 3 class will hold. The second rule is the inclusion rule: it makes
asking legal, which in most classrooms it quietly is not.

**2. Teach the rules like content, not like a notice.** Miss Nasreen spent one full period on
them. For each rule the class saw it done correctly, saw it done incorrectly (she played the part
of the pupil who interrupts), and practised it twice. Then she displayed them where she could point
at them without stopping the lesson. Cost: one sheet of chart paper, about Rs 40, and one period.

**3. One signal, used for everything.** Her raised hand means stop, look, silence. She taught it,
practised it eight times in that first period, and now uses it instead of raising her voice. A
signal beats a shout for three reasons: Komal can see it and cannot reliably hear it; it costs the
teacher no voice, which matters by the fifth period; and it is unambiguous, where a shout competes
with the other noise in the room.

**4. An entry routine.** The class knows that on arrival they sit, open the copy, and copy the four
sums already on the board. This gives Miss Nasreen the first six minutes of every period free to
walk the room, which is when she checks on Rameen, Shabana and Hina without any of them being
singled out. Cost: nothing, and it buys her thirty minutes a week.

**5. A transition routine.** Hand up, count to five aloud, next activity. Transitions are where
large classes lose time and where pupils with attention or processing difficulties lose the thread
entirely, because the instruction is given once, into noise, while everybody is moving.

**6. Response, in three steps, same every time.** Proximity first: she walks and stands beside the
pupil, saying nothing. Then a private reminder of the rule, at the bench, in two words. Then the
planned consequence, which is a task completed at break with her, not a humiliation in front of
fifty-seven classmates. The order matters more than the content. Pupils who know the order can
predict you, and predictability is what makes a classroom feel safe to a pupil who is struggling.

**7. Specific praise, four times a period, spread deliberately.** Not "good girl" but "you asked a
question when you did not understand - that is rule two". Four is a quota she keeps on a corner of
the register, because unmonitored praise goes to the same confident pupils, and the pupil who needs
it most is the one who gets it least.

#### What PBS is not

Three clarifications, because this approach is easy to caricature.

- It is **not** the absence of consequences. There is a planned consequence in step six. What is
  absent is the unplanned, public, escalating consequence that teaches a pupil she is a problem.
- It is **not** softness. Teaching a behaviour is more demanding than punishing its absence, and it
  takes longer.
- It is **not** a programme you need money or a trained specialist for. Everything in the routine
  above was produced by one teacher with chart paper. The research literature describes PBS as a
  whole-school system, and at that scale it does need training and leadership; what you can do in
  Room 4 alone is a smaller version, and it is worth doing.

That last point connects to something Pakistani teachers say consistently. Batool, Bukhari, Akhtar
and Naseer (2023) surveyed government primary school teachers in Multan district about what gets
in the way of an inclusive classroom culture, and the hurdles the teachers named were insufficient
training, limited resources and an absence of support services. Those are real and they are not
yours to fix alone. They are also not an argument for doing nothing, because the routine above
needed none of the three.

### Arranging a fixed-bench classroom for participation

Now the room itself. You have fifteen benches you cannot move, fifty-eight pupils, and one door.
Here is what you can still change.

#### What can and cannot change

**Cannot change this term:** the number of benches, their position if they are bolted or too
heavy, the single door, the window positions, the blackboard, the power supply.

**Can change by Monday, for nothing:**

- **Who sits where.** This is the largest lever in the room and it is entirely yours.
- **How many sit on a bench.** Three to a bench is the default. Two on one bench, with the third
  pupil moved elsewhere, creates the space a pupil on crutches needs to get in and out.
- **Which way pupils face for part of the lesson.** Benches are fixed; shoulders are not. Grade 3
  pupils can turn and sit facing the bench behind for a pair task, then turn back on your signal.
- **Where the teacher's table is.** Miss Nasreen's table blocked the front-left corner, which is
  also the doorway, which is also Rameen's route. Moving it to the right wall took four minutes.
- **The aisle.** One clear route from door to back, wide enough for a crutch, kept clear of bags.
  Mark it with chalk if the class keeps filling it.
- **Where you stand.** Discussed below, and more powerful than it sounds.
- **The seating cycle.** Who sits at the front this fortnight, and who sits there next.

#### The misconception: put the disabled child at the front

Almost every teacher offered a pupil with a disability reaches for the same answer: front row,
beside the teacher's table. Sometimes that is right. Often it is wrong, and it is always worth one
question first: **what is the barrier, and does this seat remove it?**

Work through the four pupils in Room 4.

- **Shabana (low vision).** The front helps - but front **left** is the worst seat in this room,
  because the morning sun comes through the left windows and the glare lands on the board's left
  half, exactly where a pupil in the left-front seat is looking. Front **centre or right**, with
  the board washed and the print enlarged, is the answer. The seat alone does not solve it;
  Topic 4.2 finishes the job.
- **Komal (hearing loss on the left).** The front helps only if she can see your face, and if her
  **good ear** faces the room rather than the wall. That means moving her to the other side of the
  bench, which costs nothing and which nobody had thought of. It also means you stop talking while
  facing the blackboard.
- **Rameen (uses a crutch).** The front is useful because it is near the door, and the aisle seat
  is essential, since the barrier is not distance but getting in and out. But front-left in Room 4
  puts her at the door, which in a class of fifty-eight is the busiest, most jostled square metre
  in the room, and her crutch is the thing most likely to be knocked. **Aisle seat, second row,
  with a two-pupil bench** is better than the obvious answer.
- **Hina (does not stay seated).** The front is actively unhelpful if she finds being watched
  intolerable, and useful if what she needs is frequent contact with you. Either way the real
  change was the movement job, not the seat.

Two further costs of the reflex, both real:

- A pupil permanently installed at the front is marked out. In Grade 3 that has social
  consequences, and Unit 2's work on stigma applies directly.
- A permanent front seat removes her from her friends, and friends are the main reason a child with
  a disability stays in school.

So: diagnose the barrier, then choose the seat. And then review it, because the sun moves across
the year, pupils grow, and eyesight changes.

#### Participation is concentrated unless you spread it

Here is the finding that embarrasses most teachers who test it, including experienced ones. Take a
page of your register, write every pupil's number down the margin, and for one lesson make a tick
beside the number of every pupil who speaks to the class. Not whispers to a neighbour: speaks, is
heard, is answered.

In a class of fifty-eight, a typical forty-minute lesson produces somewhere between twenty and
forty ticks. They are almost never spread across thirty pupils. They cluster on four to eight, and
those pupils sit in a predictable place: the front and the middle of the room.

That the front and middle seats carry more participation is not folklore. Pham, Le and Duong
(2025) measured participation by seating zone in a language class and found it highest at the front
and lowest at the back, with the row-and-column arrangement itself shaping how much each zone
contributed. Note the scope before you quote it: forty-one university students in Vietnam, learning
English, in a presentation class. That is not Grade 3 in Tando Allahyar, and it is not evidence
about children. What transfers is the mechanism - a fixed row-and-column room distributes
participation unevenly, and the back is the cheapest place to disappear - not the numbers.

Three repairs, in increasing order of effort:

1. **Stop calling on hands.** Hands select for confidence, language fluency and extroversion, which
   means they select against exactly the pupils this course is about. Use numbers instead: pupils'
   register numbers on lolly sticks or folded paper slips in a cup, drawn blind. Cost: a cup you
   have and thirty sticks at about Rs 30, or free with paper slips. This single change does more
   for participation equity than anything else in this topic.
2. **Put talk before the question.** "Tell your partner what you think the answer is. Ninety
   seconds." Then draw a number. Now the pupil who is called has already said the sentence once,
   to one person, which is the difference between an answer and a silence for Zoya, for Saba, and
   for most of the class.
3. **Rotate the seating.** A fortnightly cycle in which every bench moves one row forward and the
   front row goes to the back means every pupil spends time in the high-participation zone, and
   your low-vision and hearing-loss placements are handled as exceptions that stay put. Announce
   the cycle so it is not read as punishment.

#### Where you stand, and who gets spoken to

Teachers have a home position. In most Pakistani classrooms it is front and centre, within a metre
of the blackboard, and in a forty-minute period the teacher spends perhaps thirty-two minutes there.

Draw your room, mark your home position, and shade everywhere you went. You will usually find a
dead zone: the back corners, and the far side of the room from the door. The pupils in the dead
zone are spoken to least, checked least, and praised least. In Room 4 the dead zone was the back
right, where Zoya sits.

The fix costs nothing and needs no equipment: during the entry routine and during any written task,
walk the whole room on a fixed route, so that you pass every bench once. If you cannot walk the
room because the aisles are full of bags, that is itself the finding, and clearing them is Monday's
job.

One extra return on walking: you see the lesson from the pupils' seats. Miss Nasreen discovered the
glare on the board by sitting in Shabana's place for thirty seconds, which no amount of planning
from the front would have revealed. Universal Design for Learning calls the deliberate provision of
different ways in "multiple means of engagement and representation" (CAST, 2024); in a fixed-bench
room, half of it is simply knowing what the room looks like from fifty-eight different chairs.

### The cost of all of this, honestly

Set out plainly, because a method whose cost is hidden will be abandoned in week three.

- Teaching the three rules and the signal: one period, once.
- The seating plan: about forty minutes at a table with the register and a sheet of paper, plus
  fifteen minutes of actual moving.
- The participation tally: no extra time. You hold the register anyway.
- The walking route: no extra time. You are in the room either way.
- Materials: one sheet of chart paper for the rules, about Rs 40. Thirty lolly sticks, about Rs 30,
  or free if you use paper slips.
- Recurring cost after the first fortnight: close to zero, and you get the entry-routine minutes
  back every single period.

What it does cost is attention. For the first two weeks you will be running a routine that is not
yet habit, in a class that is testing whether you mean it. That is the real price, and it is paid
in the first fortnight or not at all.

## Activity: The participation tally and the re-seating plan

Work in pairs. About forty-five minutes.

**Part 1 - the tally (15 minutes).** Your tutor will play a Grade 3 lesson while the group acts as
the class, or you will use a recording or a transcript supplied in session. One of you keeps the
register-number tally described above: a tick for every pupil who speaks to the class. The other
notes where the teacher stood, in a sketch of the room, every two minutes.

**Part 2 - read the evidence (10 minutes).** Together, answer three questions on paper:

1. How many distinct pupils spoke, out of the total?
2. Where did they sit?
3. Which part of the room did the teacher never visit, and who was in it?

**Part 3 - the plan (20 minutes).** You now have Room 4. Fifteen benches, five rows of three, door
front left, windows left, teacher's table front left, morning sun. Your pupils include Rameen
(crutch), Shabana (low vision), Komal (hearing loss on the left), Hina (leaves her seat at minute
twelve), Zoya (does not speak), Saba (building Urdu).

Draw the room. Place all six pupils. Beside each placement write **one line**: the barrier, and how
this seat removes it. Then write the two changes you would make to the room itself and the one
routine you would teach first.

Swap plans with another pair. Their job is to find one placement that was made for the pupil's
label rather than for her barrier. Mark each other honestly; this is the error the whole topic is
built to prevent.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State the four things classroom management does in an inclusive classroom, in the
   order Figure 4.1 gives them.

2. *(Remember)* Positive behaviour support does three things, in order. Name them.

3. *(Understand)* Explain why a silent classroom gives a teacher less information than a noisy one,
   using one of the six pupils in Room 4 in your answer.

4. *(Understand)* Miss Nasreen's second rule is "we ask when we do not understand". Explain why
   this is the inclusion rule of the three, and what it makes legal that most classrooms do not.

5. *(Apply)* Hina's name has been written on the board four times with no change in her behaviour.
   State what that fact tells you, and give the two possible functions of her behaviour together
   with the change you would try for each.

6. *(Apply)* A pupil with low vision is moved to the front-left seat and still cannot read the
   board. Give two reasons this might be happening in Room 4, and one free change for each.

7. *(Apply)* Your tally for one lesson shows nineteen ticks across six pupils in a class of
   fifty-five. Describe exactly what you would say and do for the next question you ask, so that
   the pupil who answers is not one of the six.

8. *(Analyze)* A head teacher says, "Your class is noisier than it was last term, so your management
   has got worse." Identify the assumption in this judgement, and state what evidence you would
   bring to answer it.

## Summary

Classroom management is the arrangements, routines and responses that make learning possible for
**every** pupil. Silence measures none of that; it is visible from the doorway, which is why it
gets praised, and it hides the pupil who cannot see, cannot hear, or has given up.

Management does four things: it makes the day **predictable**, it secures **access**, it spreads
**participation**, and it **responds** to behaviour. Inclusive response is positive behaviour
support: read what the behaviour is for, change the environment that provokes it, and teach the
behaviour you want. A consequence applied four times without effect is information about the
consequence, not about the pupil.

A workable routine for fifty-eight pupils is three rules stated as what to do, taught like content;
one signal; an entry routine; a transition routine; a three-step response used identically every
time; and four pieces of specific praise, deliberately spread. Total cost, about Rs 40 and one
period.

In a fixed-bench room you cannot move the benches, but you choose who sits where, how many to a
bench, which way pupils face for part of a lesson, where your table goes, where the aisle runs,
where you stand, and when seats rotate. Choose each seat from the pupil's **barrier**, never from
her label: the front row is the right answer surprisingly often and the wrong answer often enough
to be worth checking every time.

Participation concentrates in four to eight pupils at the front and middle unless you spread it
deliberately. Draw numbers instead of taking hands, put ninety seconds of partner talk before the
question, and rotate the rows. Walk a fixed route that passes every bench, and sit in your pupils'
seats occasionally, because the room looks different from there.

## Try this at your practicum school

In your first week, do three things and write them up in half a page each.

1. **The tally.** Take one lesson, taught by your mentor or by you, and tally which pupils speak to
   the class. Record the total number of pupils, the number who spoke, and where they sat.
2. **The seat test.** Choose two seats at the extremes of the room - the back corner furthest from
   the door, and the seat nearest the window. Sit in each for thirty seconds while someone writes
   on the board in normal chalk and normal handwriting. Record what you could and could not read,
   and the time of day.
3. **One change.** Agree one free change with your mentor and make it: a moved table, a cleared
   aisle, a swapped seat, or drawing numbers instead of taking hands for one lesson. Record what
   happened, including anything that went worse.

Bring all three to the next session. The seat test is the one that changes people's minds, so do
not skip it on the grounds that you can imagine the result.

## Summative task

**Task.** Choose a real classroom you have taught in or observed, with at least thirty pupils.
Produce a **management and seating plan** of no more than three pages containing:

1. A scale sketch of the room marking benches, door, windows, board, your table, the aisle, and the
   position of the sun in the morning and the afternoon.
2. A seating plan placing at least five named pupils (use initials), each with a one-line
   justification naming the **barrier** the placement removes.
3. Your three rules, stated as what to do, with a note on how you would teach them in one period.
4. A three-step response sequence and the planned consequence at step three.
5. One participation-spreading procedure, with the exact words you would use.
6. A costing in rupees, itemised, including anything that costs nothing.

| Barrier-based placement *(Analyze)* | 6 | Every placement is argued from a diagnosed barrier, and at least one placement departs from the obvious label-driven answer with a stated reason. Trade-offs are acknowledged where two pupils compete for the same seat. |
| Routine quality | 4 | Rules are positively stated and teachable; the response sequence is identical every time and ends in a planned, private consequence. |
| Participation procedure | 3 | Specific enough to execute, with the teacher's actual words, and it does not rely on hands. |
| Honest costing | 3 | Itemised in rupees, includes the time cost, and claims nothing free that is not. |

The sixth criterion of a good plan is one the rubric cannot mark: whether you would still be
running it in week six. Write the plan you would actually keep.
