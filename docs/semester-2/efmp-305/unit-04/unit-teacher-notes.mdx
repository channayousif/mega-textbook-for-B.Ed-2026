# Teacher notes: Unit 4

These notes are for the teacher educator delivering this unit to B.Ed trainees. They are not part
of the learner-facing material.

## What this unit is really teaching

The content is management, seating, accessibility and aids. The capability is **noticing**.

A trainee who leaves this unit able to list the four layers of accessibility has learned a
framework. A trainee who walks into any classroom and sees, within a minute, that the glare lands
on the left half of the board, that the aisle is forty centimetres wide and full of bags, and that
the pupil in the back corner has not been spoken to all morning, has learned the unit.

Weight your teaching accordingly. The frameworks take twenty minutes. The noticing is built almost
entirely by making trainees walk, sit and measure, which is why both activities are physical and
why neither can be replaced by discussion.

## Sequencing across the two weeks

The guide allocates Weeks 8 and 9. A workable division:

- **Week 8 - Topic 4.1.** Open with the headmistress at the door. Do not define classroom
  management first. Read the two accounts of the same ten minutes, then ask the group what the
  headmistress got right. They will arrive at the observation-versus-inference distinction
  themselves, which is worth more than being given it. Then the participation tally, which should
  be run on **your own** teaching if you are willing; a tutor who shows the group her own lopsided
  tally sets a tone no lecture can.
- **Week 9 - Topic 4.2.** Open with Rameen's journey from the gate, then send the group out to walk
  a real route in your own institution before you teach anything about layers. Keep the last
  session for the making activity, because it needs the full fifty minutes and a table.

If you have only one week, cut the second half of Topic 4.1's seating material and set it as
reading, but do not cut either activity. Trainees who have not walked a route and not made an aid
will write plans that sound like policy documents.

## Materials to arrange in advance

For the Topic 4.2 making activity, per group of three: card offcuts or a cut-up carton, newspaper,
a metre of string, ten bottle caps, a cloth scrap, a rubber band, tape, one thick marker, a cutter
or scissors, a brick if you can get one, and a hard-cover register. One bottle of white glue serves
the whole room. Total cost for a group of thirty trainees is under Rs 700, most of it the markers,
and the markers survive the year.

Two things to have ready for Topic 4.1: a sheet of chart paper so the three rules can be written in
front of the group, and thirty lolly sticks or paper slips in a cup, so that drawing numbers is
demonstrated rather than described.

Set the room before the session where you can. If your own teaching room has movable furniture,
push it into five rows of three benches for the duration of Topic 4.1, because trainees argue
differently about fixed benches while sitting on them.

## Where trainees reliably go wrong

**1. They audit the room and forget the other three layers.** The route, the material and the
routine are where most of the free wins are, and the room is the only layer trainees instinctively
look at. The fix is procedural: insist that every walk write-up has four headings, and refuse a
submission with three.

**2. They seat by label.** Expect every first draft of a seating plan to put every pupil with a
disability in the front row. Do not correct it from the front of the class. Use the peer-swap step
in the Topic 4.1 activity, where another pair has to find the label-driven placement; trainees
accept the finding from a peer and argue with a tutor.

**3. They confuse an aid with a display.** The second most common first draft is a beautiful chart
that no lesson refers to. Apply the test out loud, every time, in the same words: can a pupil who
did not get the idea from the explanation get it from this?

**4. They invent prices.** A plan costed at "approximately Rs 5,000" with no itemisation is worth
no marks and is a habit that will later embarrass them in front of a head teacher. Require local
prices or the words "to be quoted". Ask one trainee each session to telephone a stationery shop
during the break and report the real price of chart paper and a marker; the figures in this unit are
indicative and will drift.

**5. They improvise what they must not.** Every cohort produces at least one trainee who wants to
build a ramp out of bricks and mud, or to adjust a hearing aid. Address it explicitly and early, in
Topic 4.2's "what not to improvise" terms. The line is weight-bearing and prescribed; everything
else is fair game.

**6. They treat the escalation list as failure.** A trainee who writes "not by me" feels she has
given up. Reframe it: the third list is the only mechanism by which a school council ever funds a
ramp, and writing it is an act, not an excuse. Mark the written request as carefully as the free
changes.

## Running the activities with large trainee groups

**The participation tally** scales without difficulty. With more than forty trainees, use a
recording or a transcript rather than a live demonstration, and have half the group tally while the
other half tracks the teacher's position; comparing the two maps is where the dead zone becomes
obvious.

**The accessibility walk** does not scale as a single group. Thirty trainees walking one route
produces nothing. Send them in pairs on four different routes - the main gate, the far classroom
block, the toilets, and the route to wherever your institution has a step - with a fifteen-minute
limit and a fixed sheet to complete. Then pool the findings on the board. The pooled list is always
longer than any individual expects, which is the lesson.

**The making activity** needs a table per group and a tutor willing to let trainees make something
poor. Resist improving their aids. The peer-test step at the end, where another group has to work
out the barrier from the aid alone, does the teaching.

## Marking the summative tasks

For the Topic 4.1 plan, mark the **justifications** rather than the plan. A seating plan with no
label-driven placements and no stated trade-offs is usually a plan that was never tested against a
real room; ask where two pupils competed for the same seat and what the trainee did about it.

For the Topic 4.2 plan and for ERQ 3, the two criteria that separate real work from paper are the
**timed route** and the **itemised costing**. A route described without a time was not walked. A
costing without a time column has not counted the teacher's own hours, which is the cost that
actually determines whether a plan survives to week six.

For ERQ 5, the strongest answers are the ones that decline the invitation to conclude that inclusion
is cheap. A trainee who can hold both halves - that the free end is real and worth starting this
week, and that it will not reach a pupil who is blind or who has no speech - has understood the
unit's economics better than one who has simply agreed with Miss Nasreen.

  home, and the communication board in Topic 4.2 is explicitly a joint piece of work with parents.

## Practical work

The course guide lists no assessed practical component for this course, so nothing here is set as
one. What the unit does contain is three pieces of placement work in Topic 4.1 and four in
Topic 4.2. Treat them as the practical spine of the unit and ask for the artefacts: the tally
sheet, the obstacle list with its times, the aid itself, and the written request. Artefacts are
harder to fabricate than reflections, and in this unit they are also the thing the trainee keeps.
