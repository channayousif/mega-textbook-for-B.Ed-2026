## Unit summary

Unit 3 changed the lesson. This unit changed the room, and the argument of both topics was the
same: most of what excludes a pupil is a decision somebody made, and most of those decisions are
free to make differently.

**Topic 4.1** defined **classroom management** as the arrangements, routines and responses that
make learning possible for **every** pupil, and spent its first pages dismantling the measure most
schools actually use. A silent class is visible from the doorway, which is why it gets praised, and
it hides Shabana copying from her neighbour, Zoya who has not spoken since March, and Rameen who no
longer drinks water at school. Management does four things: predictability, access, participation
and response. Inclusive response is **positive behaviour support** - work out what the behaviour is
for, change the environment that provokes it, then teach the behaviour you want. A consequence
applied four times without effect is information about the consequence.

The topic then turned to the fixed-bench room. You cannot move the benches; you choose who sits
where, how many to a bench, which way pupils face for part of a lesson, where your table goes,
where the aisle runs, where you stand, and when seats rotate. Every seat is chosen from the pupil's
**barrier**, never from her label, which is why "put the disabled child at the front" is sometimes
right and often wrong: front-left was the worst seat in Room 4 for a low-vision pupil because of
the glare, and the busiest square metre in the room for a pupil on crutches. Participation
concentrates in four to eight pupils unless you spread it, so draw register numbers instead of
taking hands, put ninety seconds of partner talk before the question, and rotate the rows.

**Topic 4.2** defined **accessibility** as being able to get to the learning, get into it and use
it, and audited it in four layers - the **route**, the **room**, the **material** and the
**routine**. Ramps belong to one part of one layer; believing that accessibility **is** ramps is
what persuades a teacher that the problem needs a budget she does not have. The accessibility walk
takes fifteen minutes and ends in three sorted lists: this week by me, this term with the head
teacher, and not by me, written down with the pupil and the barrier named.

The free changes are the ones that get ignored: a washed board, controlled glare, a seat chosen for
ear and eye, a brick under the feet, a register propped on a brick as a slant board, and an
evacuation in which the pupil who moves slowly leaves first. A **teaching aid** carries the same
content through another sense, which rules out decoration; your voice, your body, objects, hand
drawn large print and touch are five channels and four are free. **Assistive technology** runs from
a rolled-cloth pencil grip at nothing to a prescribed hearing aid, and the cheap end removes most
of the barrier for most pupils. Four pupils in Room 4 moved from partial access to access for about
Rs 320 and two hours of making. Nothing weight-bearing and nothing prescribed is ever improvised.

Unit 5 turns from the room to the law and the policy that stand behind it, and asks what a pupil is
actually entitled to in Pakistan.

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

Choose one answer. Each question carries 1 mark.

1. *(Remember)* Which definition of classroom management does this unit use?
   - A) The techniques a teacher uses to keep a class quiet and seated
   - B) The arrangements, routines and responses that make learning possible for every pupil
   - C) The school's rules on discipline and punishment
   - D) The timetable and the seating register

2. *(Remember)* Positive behaviour support does three things. In what order?
   - A) Punish the behaviour, warn the pupil, inform the parents
   - B) Identify what the behaviour is for, change the environment, teach the behaviour you want
   - C) Teach the rule, display the rule, enforce the rule
   - D) Record the behaviour, refer the pupil, await assessment

3. *(Understand)* Why is a silent classroom a poor measure of inclusive management?
   - A) Because pupils are supposed to enjoy school
   - B) Because silence is hard to maintain in a large class
   - C) Because it is visible from the doorway and tells you nothing about who has access to the
     lesson
   - D) Because talking is how all learning happens

4. *(Understand)* A pupil leaves her seat at the same minute in every lesson and her name has been
   written on the board four times with no change. What does the unit say this fact tells you?
   - A) That the pupil is deliberately defiant
   - B) That the consequence is not addressing the cause of the behaviour
   - C) That the parents must be called in
   - D) That the pupil needs a medical referral before anything else

5. *(Apply)* Your participation tally shows seventeen ticks across five pupils in a class of
   fifty-two. Which change does this unit put first?
   - A) Ask more questions in the lesson
   - B) Praise the five pupils who answer so that others copy them
   - C) Draw register numbers instead of taking hands, after ninety seconds of partner talk
   - D) Move the five confident pupils to the back

6. *(Remember)* What are the four layers of accessibility in this unit, in order?
   - A) Building, furniture, equipment, staff
   - B) Route, room, material, routine
   - C) Policy, school, classroom, pupil
   - D) Seeing, hearing, moving, speaking

7. *(Understand)* A pupil has an impairment in one leg. According to the environment-interaction
   account used in this unit, what makes it a disability at her school?
   - A) The severity of the impairment itself
   - B) Her family's decision to send her to a mainstream school
   - C) The step, the narrow aisle and the middle seat she must cross to reach the lesson
   - D) The absence of a medical certificate

8. *(Understand)* Which of these fails this unit's test for a teaching aid?
   - A) A tactile number line made from card and bottle caps
   - B) Eight sums copied onto chart paper in 3 cm letters
   - C) A laminated science poster on the back wall that no lesson refers to
   - D) The teacher reading aloud everything she writes on the board

9. *(Apply)* A pupil cannot grip a pencil for more than five minutes and another pupil's feet do
   not reach the floor. What does the unit recommend, and at what cost?
   - A) A referral to the district for assistive devices; cost unknown
   - B) A grip rolled from a strip of cloth and a brick or folded sack as a footrest; no cost
   - C) Specialised classroom furniture from the school budget; several thousand rupees
   - D) Reduced written work for both pupils; no cost

10. *(Apply)* Your class has one inward-opening door, fifty-eight pupils, and a pupil who walks
    with a crutch. What is the evacuation arrangement this unit recommends?
    - A) She waits until the room is clear, so she is not knocked over
    - B) She leaves first, with two named partners who have rehearsed not running
    - C) The teacher carries her out
    - D) She sits nearest the door all year so the question does not arise

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

Answer each question concisely, using the unit's terminology. Marks are shown.

1. *(Understand)* State the four things classroom management does in an inclusive classroom, and
   explain why "access" belongs on a management list rather than only on a building list.
   (4 marks)

2. *(Understand)* Distinguish productive noise from disruptive noise, and explain what a teacher
   loses when she suppresses both. (4 marks)

3. *(Understand)* Miss Nasreen's three rules are stated as what to do rather than what not to do.
   Explain why, and identify which of her three rules is the inclusion rule and what it makes
   legal. (4 marks)

4. *(Apply)* A Grade 3 pupil stands up and walks to the door at about minute twelve of every
   lesson. Give the two possible functions of this behaviour, and for each give the change to the
   environment you would try, with its cost. (5 marks)

5. *(Analyze)* A head teacher praises a trainee for a class in which "nobody made a sound for forty
   minutes". Analyse what the head teacher observed, what she inferred, and what evidence the
   trainee could bring to show whether the lesson included every pupil. (5 marks)

6. *(Understand)* Explain what the accessibility walk asks you to do that auditing the room from
   the front cannot, and name the two parts of the route that teachers most often fail to check.
   (4 marks)

7. *(Apply)* A pupil with low vision has been moved to the front and still cannot read the board.
   Give three free changes, in the order you would try them, and justify the order. (5 marks)

8. *(Apply)* Write the four-sentence written request you would hand to a head teacher about a
   broken slab over an open drain on the route a named pupil uses. Name the pupil and the barrier
   rather than the solution. (5 marks)

9. *(Analyze)* Teacher A spends Rs 300 on a laminated chart for the wall. Teacher B spends Rs 300 on
   chart paper, a marker and glue, and makes a tactile number line and a large-print sum chart.
   Analyse the difference using this unit's test for a teaching aid, and say what each teacher's
   pupils can now do that they could not before. (5 marks)

10. *(Analyze)* A school reports that it has built a ramp at the front gate and describes itself as
    accessible. Analyse this claim across the four layers, identifying what has genuinely changed
    for a pupil who uses a wheelchair and what has not. (5 marks)

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

Answer in continuous prose. Each question carries 10 marks and has a band rubric below.

1. *(Analyze)* Room 4 was described twice in this unit: once as the headmistress saw it from the
   door, and once from inside, through six named pupils. Analyse the gap between the two accounts.
   For four of the six pupils, identify what the headmistress could not see, the feature of the
   environment responsible, and whether the repair belongs to the teacher, the head teacher or the
   school council. Conclude by stating what the school should measure instead of silence.

2. *(Evaluate)* "Everything in this unit is for schools that have something. In a class of sixty
   with no electricity, no budget and no training, a teacher can change nothing that matters."
   Evaluate this claim. Take the objection seriously, use at least four specific changes from the
   unit with their real costs, and reach a judgement you defend. You may draw on what Pakistani
   teachers themselves report as hurdles.

3. *(Create)* Design a complete inclusive environment plan for one real classroom you know, with at
   least thirty pupils. Include a sketch of the room marking light and routes, a seating plan with
   at least four named pupils each justified by a barrier, one positive behaviour routine, the
   findings of an accessibility walk across all four layers, two aids or assistive solutions you
   would make, a costing in rupees that separates what you spend from what you escalate, and an
   evacuation arrangement. Justify your three most important decisions.

4. *(Analyze)* A trainee returns from practicum having seated every pupil with a disability in the
   front row, beside the teacher's table, for the whole term. Analyse this decision: what it gets
   right, what it gets wrong for at least three different barriers, what it costs the pupils
   socially, and what question the trainee should have asked before each placement.

5. *(Evaluate)* Miss Nasreen's four pupils moved from partial access to access for about Rs 320 and
   two hours of making. Evaluate whether that figure is a fair account of what inclusion in a
   government primary school costs. Identify what the figure includes, what it leaves out, what it
   would be dishonest to generalise from it, and what you would tell a new teacher who read it and
   concluded that inclusion is cheap.

## Answers and marking guidance

### MCQ answer key

1. B  2. B  3. C  4. B  5. C  6. B  7. C  8. C  9. B  10. B

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. Management makes the day **predictable**, secures **access**, spreads **participation**, and
   provides a consistent **response** to behaviour. Access belongs on the management list because
   four times out of five the thing blocking access is a decision the teacher owns - the seat, the
   aisle, the bags, the glare, the print size, which way she faces when she speaks - rather than
   the building. (1 mark per element; 1 mark for the reason, which must name at least one
   teacher-owned decision.)

2. Productive noise is on your signal, about the lesson, between pupils, and it stops on your
   signal. Disruptive noise is unasked for, not about the lesson, and does not stop. A teacher who
   suppresses both loses her only cheap source of information: in a silent room she cannot
   distinguish a pupil who has understood from one who has given up, and the pupil who needs to ask
   cannot ask. (2 marks for the distinction with a criterion each; 2 marks for the loss, one of
   which must be about information rather than only about kindness.)

3. Rules stated as prohibitions name the behaviour you do not want and leave the pupil to guess
   what is wanted; stated as actions, they are teachable and can be praised specifically. The
   inclusion rule is "we ask when we do not understand", because asking is talking and most
   classrooms have quietly made talking illegal, which disables exactly the pupil who has missed
   something. (2 marks for the reason; 1 mark for identifying the rule; 1 mark for what it makes
   legal.)

4. Function one, movement: she cannot sustain forty minutes of stillness, so give her a legitimate
   movement job - distributing and collecting slates at minute ten and minute thirty - at no cost.
   Function two, escape: the task at minute twelve has become impossible, so shorten it into three
   sums and a check from you, which costs nothing but two walks to her bench. A full answer notes
   that you cannot know which function is operating and so test one for a week and observe.
   (2 marks per function and change; 1 mark for the testing point.)

5. The head teacher observed silence and stillness, which are real observations. She inferred
   access, participation and learning, which do not follow from them. The trainee can bring a
   participation tally showing how many distinct pupils spoke and where they sat, a note of the dead
   zone her walking route revealed, and the results of sitting in two extreme seats to test whether
   the board is readable and the voice audible. A strong answer says explicitly that the two
   accounts are not in conflict: the room was quiet **and** several pupils had no access.
   (2 marks for separating observation from inference; 3 marks for the evidence, at least two
   items specific.)

6. The walk requires you to meet the room at the pupil's speed, from her seat, with her material,
   rather than from the one position in the room where everything works. The two most-missed parts
   of the route are the way to the water point and the way to the toilet, which is why some pupils
   stop drinking at school and some stop attending after a few years. (2 marks for the method and
   why the front view fails; 1 mark each for water and toilet.)

7. In order: wash the board properly and let it dry, because a grey film halves the contrast and
   costs nothing; check and control the glare, writing on the unlit half of the board or screening
   the window with a cloth, because the seat is irrelevant if the surface is washed out; then write
   larger, in a bigger hand with double spacing, and read aloud everything you write. The order runs
   from the change that affects the whole class and costs nothing, through the change that affects
   the board surface, to the change that depends on the teacher's habit and takes longest to
   establish. (1 mark per change with its reason; 2 marks for a defensible ordering principle.)

8. A full-credit answer is four sentences, names the pupil and her class, states the barrier as what
   she cannot do, gives the location precisely, and asks for a decision rather than describing a
   solution. For example: "Rameen of Grade 3 walks with a crutch and crosses the open drain outside
   the Room 4 veranda every morning, where the concrete slab has been broken since last year. The
   gap is about fifteen centimetres wide and she steps over it unaided, with pupils waiting behind
   her. This adds several minutes to her arrival and is the part of her route I consider unsafe.
   Please advise whether the school council can have the slab replaced, and what quotation you would
   like me to obtain." (2 marks for naming pupil and barrier; 1 mark for precision of location;
   1 mark for asking for a decision; 1 mark for length discipline.)

9. The test is whether the aid carries the content of a lesson through another sense or form.
   Teacher A's chart carries content that no lesson refers to, cannot be changed, and is read by
   nobody; her pupils can do nothing new. Teacher B's two aids each carry a specific piece of
   current content: her low-vision pupil can now read the day's sums, and her whole class can feel
   order and distance on a number line, which is what place value needs and what a board drawing
   does not give. A strong answer also notes that B's spend is recoverable and correctable while
   A's is laminated and fixed. (2 marks for applying the test; 2 marks for what each teacher's
   pupils can now do; 1 mark for a further difference such as reusability or correctability.)

10. The ramp changes part of layer one, the route, and only the part at the gate. A full answer
    tracks the rest: the route from gate to classroom may still have a step, a drain or a surface a
    wheelchair cannot cross; the room may still have a forty-centimetre aisle and no reachable seat;
    the material may still be print she cannot read or instructions she cannot follow; and the
    routine may still give her no permitted way to ask for help or extra time. The school's claim
    is not false, it is partial, and the honest version names which layer has been addressed.
    (2 marks for locating the ramp correctly in the four layers; 1 mark per remaining layer
    analysed.)

### ERQ rubrics

Each ERQ is marked in four bands. Award the band that best fits the whole response; a response need
not meet every descriptor in a band.

**ERQ 1 - the two accounts of Room 4**

- **Band 4 (9-10).** The gap is analysed as a difference between what is visible from a doorway and
  what is true from a seat, not as a criticism of the headmistress. Four pupils are treated
  individually, with the environmental feature correctly identified in each case and the repair
  assigned to the right level. The conclusion proposes something measurable in place of silence - a
  participation tally, a timed route, a seat test - and says who would collect it.
- **Band 3 (7-8).** Four pupils analysed, most environmental features correct, repairs mostly
  assigned to a plausible level, and a measurable alternative proposed.
- **Band 2 (4-6).** Pupils described rather than analysed, or their difficulties located in the
  pupils themselves ("she is weak", "she is shy") rather than in the room. Repairs are listed
  without an owner. The conclusion restates that silence is bad without offering a measure.
- **Band 1 (1-3).** The vignette is retold. No distinction between observation and inference.

**ERQ 2 - evaluating the "a teacher can change nothing" claim**

- **Band 4 (9-10).** The objection's legitimate core is conceded explicitly: training, resources and
  support services genuinely are missing, and Pakistani teachers themselves report exactly that.
  At least four changes are then named with their real costs, correctly distinguishing the free ones
  from those needing Rs 40 to Rs 320, and at least one item is correctly placed outside the
  teacher's power. A clear judgement is reached and defended, and the answer does not resolve into
  either despair or slogan. Evidence is used carefully: Batool and colleagues (2023) cited for what
  teachers report rather than for how common it is, and Cote (2021) for the systemic financing
  picture rather than for anything about one classroom.
- **Band 3 (7-8).** The objection is addressed, four changes named with costs, a defended judgement
  reached. Evidence may be used loosely or without its scope stated.
- **Band 2 (4-6).** The claim is rejected by assertion or accepted uncritically. Changes are named
  without costs, or costs are invented. No distinction between what the teacher owns and what she
  must escalate.
- **Band 1 (1-3).** General remarks about resources and government. No specific changes, no costs,
  no judgement.

**ERQ 3 - design an inclusive environment plan**

- **Band 4 (9-10).** Every element present and internally consistent: the sketch is from a real room
  with light and routes marked and used in later decisions; four named pupils each placed by a
  diagnosed barrier, with at least one placement departing from the obvious label-driven answer;
  one teachable behaviour routine; a four-layer walk with real distances and times; two aids that
  serve the named pupil and the whole class; a costing that is local, itemised, includes time, and
  separates spend from escalation; a rehearsed evacuation in which the slowest pupil leaves first.
  The three justified decisions are genuine trade-offs rather than restatements.
- **Band 3 (7-8).** All elements present, most decisions justified, costing itemised, evacuation
  correct, aids appropriate. Some trade-offs unexamined.
- **Band 2 (4-6).** Elements present but generic, or the plan describes an ideal classroom rather
  than a real one. Placements made by label. Costs absent, guessed, or unaffordable. Evacuation
  places the slowest pupil last. No separation of spend from escalation.
- **Band 1 (1-3).** A list of recommendations with no room behind it.

**ERQ 4 - the front-row reflex**

- **Band 4 (9-10).** What the reflex gets right is granted honestly: proximity does help several
  barriers, and it is better than ignoring the pupil. The analysis then takes at least three
  distinct barriers and shows why the same seat serves them differently, with the Room 4 specifics
  or equivalents - glare on the left half of the board for a low-vision pupil, the door as the
  busiest square metre for a pupil on crutches, the good ear facing the wall for a pupil with
  hearing loss. The social cost is argued rather than asserted, connecting to Unit 2 on stigma and
  to the role of friendship in keeping a child in school. The question named is the right one: what
  is the barrier, and does this seat remove it?
- **Band 3 (7-8).** Three barriers distinguished, social cost noted, the diagnostic question named.
- **Band 2 (4-6).** The practice is condemned or defended as a whole, with one barrier considered.
  The social cost is asserted. The question is implied rather than stated.
- **Band 1 (1-3).** Agreement or disagreement with no analysis of barriers.

**ERQ 5 - evaluating the Rs 320 figure**

- **Band 4 (9-10).** The figure is unpacked accurately: it covers materials for four named pupils
  over a term and excludes the teacher's two hours, her skill, the escalated ramp and drain slab,
  anything prescribed or weight-bearing, and all recurring costs such as photocopies. The answer
  identifies what cannot be generalised - one room, four pupils, four specific barriers, one town's
  prices, one teacher who had already read this unit - and says what would raise the figure sharply:
  a pupil who is blind, a pupil with no speech, a pupil needing a hearing aid, or a route requiring
  construction. The advice to a new teacher is balanced and practical: the cheap end is real and
  worth starting with this week, and "inclusion is cheap" is a claim that will fail the first time
  it meets a barrier the free end cannot reach.
- **Band 3 (7-8).** Inclusions and exclusions mostly identified, limits on generalisation named,
  sensible advice given.
- **Band 2 (4-6).** The figure is accepted or dismissed without unpacking. Limits are gestured at.
  Advice is a slogan in either direction.
- **Band 1 (1-3).** Restates the cost without evaluating it.
