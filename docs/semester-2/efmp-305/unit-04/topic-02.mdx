## A real classroom situation

Follow Rameen from the school gate to her bench. She is eight years old, she had polio as an
infant, and she walks with one crutch under her left arm. Room 4 is forty metres from the gate.

- **Gate to veranda, 25 metres.** Compacted earth, dry today. In July it is mud, and for three days
  after rain it is a set of ruts her crutch sinks into. She arrives late in the monsoon and is
  marked late.
- **The drain crossing, 3 metres from the veranda.** A concrete slab over the open drain has been
  broken since last year. The gap is about fifteen centimetres. She steps across it, which she can
  do, and which she thinks about every morning.
- **The veranda step.** One step, about six inches, no handrail. She manages it by putting the
  crutch up first and pulling. Two other girls usually wait behind her.
- **The door.** It opens inward, into the room, and there is a second small step at the threshold.
  Fifty-seven other pupils are coming through the same door in the same four minutes.
- **The aisle.** About forty centimetres wide between the benches, and this morning there are six
  school bags in it.
- **Her bench.** Third row, middle seat. To sit down she must ask the two girls on the outside to
  stand. To leave she must ask again.

Total time from gate to seated: about seven minutes on a dry day. The lesson starts at 8:00.

Then there is the rest of the day. The water cooler is at the far end of the veranda, forty metres
back. The toilet is further, and the last three metres are uneven. Rameen does not go to either.
She drinks nothing at school and goes home at 1:30 having not used a toilet for six hours. Her
teachers believe she is not thirsty.

Now the others in the same room. Shabana cannot read the board from row three and will not say so.
Komal misses half the instructions because they are delivered to the blackboard. Saba has no Urdu
word for "photosynthesis" and no picture to attach it to. None of this is anybody's fault and all
of it is somebody's responsibility.

## Explanation

### What accessibility actually means

**Accessibility** means that a pupil can get to the learning, get into it, and use it. If any one
of the three fails, she is excluded, however welcoming the school's language is.

Unit 2 established the framing this rests on. The World Health Organization and World Bank (2011)
present disability as arising from the interaction between a person's impairment and the
environment they meet, which is why the same impairment produces very different exclusion in two
different schools. Rameen's leg is the same leg in both. The six-inch step, the forty-centimetre
aisle and the middle seat are what turn it into a disability at Government Girls Primary School,
Tando Allahyar, and all three are things somebody chose.

#### The misconception: accessibility is only about ramps

Ramps matter. A ramp over the veranda step would take about ten minutes off Rameen's morning in
total, across the several crossings of that step in a day, and remove the one part of the journey
she is afraid of. But if you believe accessibility **is** ramps,
two things follow and both are bad: you conclude that accessibility is a construction budget you do
not have, and you therefore do nothing at all, when most of what excludes your pupils costs
nothing.

Kurniawati and colleagues (2026) make the point from the other direction. Studying how Universal
Design for Learning was actually enacted after staff training, they argue that providing physical
access is not sufficient on its own, and that the gap between getting learners in the door and
getting them to succeed sits in teaching practice. Their setting is Indonesian universities, not
Pakistani primary schools, so treat the claim as a warning about where attention goes, not as data
about Grade 3. The warning is worth having: a school can build the ramp and still teach to the
middle.

So think of accessibility in **four layers**. Most teachers only ever audit the second.

1. **The route** - gate to seat, seat to water, seat to toilet, seat to safety in an emergency.
2. **The room** - aisle width, seat position, what can be seen, heard and reached from that seat.
3. **The material** - print size, contrast, language, the number of steps in an instruction.
4. **The routine** - time allowed, how instructions are given, who is allowed to ask, how help is
   requested without humiliation.

Layers 3 and 4 are free. Layer 2 is nearly free. Layer 1 sometimes costs money, and that part you
escalate rather than solve.

### The accessibility walk: a method you can run in fifteen minutes

Do not audit the room from the front of it. Walk it, in this order, with a sheet of paper. This is
the single most useful thing in this unit.

**Step 1 - walk the route in (4 minutes).** Start at the gate. Walk to the bench of the pupil you
are most worried about, at the speed she walks, and write down every place where you had to step
up, step over, turn sideways, or wait. Then walk to the water point and to the toilet. Those two
are the ones nobody checks, and they are the reason some pupils with disabilities stop attending
after Grade 4.

**Step 2 - sit in four seats (4 minutes).** The front centre, the back corner furthest from the
door, the seat nearest the window, and the seat of the pupil you are worried about. In each, for
thirty seconds: can you read ordinary chalk handwriting on the board? Can you hear a normal
speaking voice from the front? Is there glare? What time is it, because the answer changes by
mid-morning.

**Step 3 - try the material as the pupil meets it (4 minutes).** Hold the textbook at the distance
your low-vision pupil holds it. Read one instruction aloud once, at normal speed, facing away from
the room, and ask yourself what a pupil with hearing loss in one ear got. Read one paragraph and
count how many words in it would be new to a pupil whose home language is Sindhi.

**Step 4 - write three lists (3 minutes).** Not one list. Three, because a single list of problems
is paralysing and a sorted list is a plan:

- **This week, free, by me.** Seats, the aisle, the table, where you stand, the board washed, the
  print enlarged by hand, instructions given facing the class.
- **This term, cheap, with the head teacher.** A bench removed from a row, a timetable swap to a
  ground-floor room, a cupboard moved, Rs 200 of chart paper and markers.
- **Not by me.** The ramp, the drain slab, the toilet rail, the handrail. These go in a written
  note to the head teacher, with a cost estimate and the name of the pupil affected.

Writing the third list is not passing the problem on. It is the only way the third list ever gets
done, because a school council cannot fund what nobody has asked for in writing.

### Light, noise, reach and safety: the free changes

Four physical factors that cost nothing and are almost always ignored.

**Light.** A blackboard that has not been washed properly for a month has a grey film on it, and
the contrast between chalk and board may be half what it should be. For Shabana that is the
difference between reading and guessing. A bucket of water and a cloth once a week, done properly
and allowed to dry, is the cheapest low-vision aid in this unit. Then the glare: note where the sun
falls on the board at 8:30, at 10:00 and at 11:30, and write on the half of the board that is not
lit. If the glare is unavoidable, hang an old dupatta or a sheet of newspaper over the top half of
that window; the room stays bright and the board becomes readable. Cost: nothing.

**Noise.** Komal's bench is under a window that faces the road. Moving her across the room cost
nothing and gained her perhaps a third of the instructions. Then the teacher's own habits, which
matter more: do not talk while facing the board, do not give an instruction during a transition,
and give the important instruction twice - once spoken, once written. For a pupil with any hearing
loss, seeing your face is not a politeness; it is half the message.

**Reach and posture.** A Grade 3 bench is built for an average Grade 3 body. A small pupil whose
feet do not reach the floor cannot write steadily, and a pupil with cerebral palsy or low muscle
tone may be using all her effort to stay upright rather than to write. Two free fixes: a brick or a
folded sack under the feet, and a folded shawl behind the back to bring her closer to the desk. If
her arm cannot cross the desk comfortably, a hard-cover register propped on a brick gives her a
sloped writing surface at the right angle. Cost: nothing, from things already in the room.

**Safety, which almost nobody plans.** Fifty-eight pupils, one door that opens inward, and a pupil
who cannot move quickly. In an earthquake drill or a fire, the honest default outcome is that
Rameen is last out or not out at all. The plan is simple, free and must be rehearsed: two named
pupils are her partners, they know they do not run, and she leaves **first**, not last, because
fifty-seven pupils behind her in a doorway is the hazard. Rehearse it twice a term. Write the two
names in the register. If the door opens inward and the school can change it, that goes on list
three.

### Teaching aids for diverse learners

A **teaching aid** is any object, picture, sound or action that carries the content of the lesson
through a different sense or in a different form from your speech and the textbook.

That definition contains the only rule you need: an aid must **carry the content**. A chart that
makes the wall look busy, a laminated poster nobody reads, a model that stays in the cupboard
because it might break - these are decoration. The test is whether a pupil who did not get the idea
from your explanation can get it from the aid.

This is Universal Design for Learning's "multiple means of representation" (CAST, 2024) in its most
practical form. In a room with a blackboard, chalk and no electricity for half the day, you have
five channels available, and four of them are free:

- **Your voice** - reading aloud everything you write, and saying each key term twice.
- **Your body** - gesture, demonstration, acting the process out.
- **Objects** - stones, bottle caps, sticks, bangles, leaves, a water jug.
- **Pictures and large print** - drawn by hand with a marker.
- **Touch** - raised, textured, or cut-out shapes a pupil can feel.

#### Worked example 1: the tactile number line

Grade 3 is learning place value. Shabana cannot see a number line drawn on the board, and Saba has
the arithmetic but not the Urdu. One aid serves both, and every other pupil in the room.

**Materials.** The side panel of a biscuit carton, free from the shop or the school store. Eleven
bottle caps, free. A metre of jute string, free from a sack. White glue, about Rs 60 for a bottle
that will make twenty of these. One thick black marker, about Rs 50. Total outlay about Rs 110,
most of which you keep.

**Making it, about twenty minutes.**

1. Cut the card into a strip about 60 cm by 10 cm.
2. Rule eleven marks along it. Write 0 to 10 in numerals about 4 cm high in thick black marker.
3. Glue one bottle cap at each mark, so the line can be read with a fingertip.
4. Glue a short length of knotted string between 5 and 6, so the halfway point can be felt.

**What it does.** Shabana counts by touch and reads 4 cm numerals at close range. Saba attaches the
Urdu and Sindhi number words to a physical position rather than to a sound she has not caught. The
whole class gets a concrete representation of order and distance, which is exactly what place value
needs and what a drawing on the board does not give. And because it is on the back shelf, pupils
use it in pairs without being told to.

**What it does not do.** It does not teach addition on its own, and it is useless for numbers above
ten. Build the second one to 20 only if the class needs it; do not laminate it, because you will
want to change it.

#### Worked example 2: enlarged print, two ways

Shabana reads 24-point print at about 20 cm. Her textbook is set at about 12 point. You have two
honest options.

**Option A, the photocopier.** If there is a shop near the school, an A4 page enlarged to A3 costs
about Rs 10 to Rs 20 depending on the town, and the school's own machine, where there is one, is
cheaper but frequently out of toner. For a page of sums, this is the fastest route. For a whole
textbook it is unaffordable, so choose: enlarge the **assessment** papers and the pages the pupil
must read alone, and handle the rest in class by other means.

**Option B, the marker and the chart paper.** A sheet of chart paper costs about Rs 30 to Rs 40. In
ten minutes you can copy the eight sums or the key paragraph onto it in 3 cm letters. Two
advantages over the photocopy: you choose what goes on it, so you can cut the sixty-word paragraph
to the twelve words that carry the idea; and it is reusable for the whole class next year. One
disadvantage: your handwriting is now the font, so write slowly and leave double spacing.

**The free option that teachers forget.** Write it on the board in large letters and seat her in
front of it. Most of what a low-vision pupil needs in Grade 3 is not a document; it is a bigger
board hand and a closer seat.

#### What to avoid

- **Aids that need electricity** in a school with load-shedding, unless there is a plan for the
  hours there is no power.
- **Laminating anything** you will want to correct.
- **One aid for one pupil, used once.** Build aids that serve the whole class and happen to be
  essential for one pupil. That is both cheaper and kinder, because nothing in it marks her out.
- **Borrowing the science kit** and keeping it in the cupboard. An aid in a cupboard is not an aid.

### Low-cost and no-cost assistive solutions

**Assistive technology** means any product or system that helps a person do something they could
not otherwise do, or could not do safely or independently. The World Health Organization and World
Bank (2011) treat access to appropriate assistive products as a precondition for participation, and
make the point this section rests on: simple, cheap products do most of the work.

#### The misconception: assistive technology is always expensive and electronic

The word "technology" is doing the damage. Think of a spectrum instead.

- **No-tech.** A rolled strip of cloth taped around a pencil for a pupil who cannot grip. A hard
  register propped on a brick as a slant board. A cardboard window cut to show one line of text at
  a time. A folded shawl as a seat cushion. Cost: Rs 0, from the room.
- **Low-tech.** A hand-drawn communication board of twelve pictures for a pupil with no speech. A
  tactile number line. Large-print exercise books, which are an ordinary Rs 40 copy re-ruled every
  third line with a marker. A magnifying glass from the stationery shop. Cost: Rs 0 to Rs 400.
- **High-tech.** A donated Android phone with the built-in screen reader and magnifier switched on
  in the accessibility settings. A hearing aid, which is prescribed and fitted, not improvised.
  Cost: Rs 0 if donated, otherwise beyond a teacher's pocket and squarely a referral.

The pattern is the same everywhere: the cheap end of the spectrum removes most of the barrier for
most pupils, and you can build it yourself this week.

#### Worked example 3: four solutions for Room 4 in one afternoon

This is the costed version of the plan Miss Nasreen actually built, and it is the model for this
topic's summative task.

**For Shabana (low vision).** Front-centre seat, free. Board washed weekly, free. Key sums copied
onto chart paper in 3 cm letters, Rs 70 for paper and marker, ten minutes a day. A reading window
cut from a card offcut, free, three minutes. Assessment papers enlarged at the shop, about Rs 15
per paper, perhaps six papers a term, so Rs 90 a term. **Term total: about Rs 160.**

**For Rameen (uses a crutch).** Aisle seat, row two, free. That bench carries two pupils instead of
three, which means one pupil moves to row five, free. Bags stored under benches and a chalk line
marking the route, free. Two named evacuation partners, rehearsed twice a term, free. A written
request to the head teacher for a ramp over the veranda step and a slab over the drain, with the
mason's quote attached. **Teacher's cost: Rs 0. Escalated cost: a few thousand rupees, and not
yours to find.**

**For Komal (hearing loss, left ear).** Seat turned so the right ear faces the room, free. Teacher
faces the class to speak, free. Every important instruction also written on the board's right half,
free. A referral note to the parents suggesting a hearing check at the nearest BHU or district
hospital, free. **Total: Rs 0.**

**For Saba (building Urdu) and the whole class.** The tactile number line, Rs 110, twenty minutes.
Twelve key terms written in Urdu and Sindhi on a strip above the board, Rs 40. **Total: Rs 150, and
it serves fifty-eight pupils.**

**Whole afternoon's spend: about Rs 320, plus roughly two hours of making.** Four pupils move from
partial access to access. Nothing on that list needed a budget, a specialist, or permission.

#### What not to improvise

Say this plainly, because enthusiasm after a unit like this can do harm.

- **Nothing that bears a pupil's weight.** Ramps, handrails, steps, standing frames and seating
  supports are engineering, not craft. A ramp that is too steep or a rail that pulls out of the
  plaster is more dangerous than the step was. Request these; do not build them.
- **Nothing prescribed.** Do not adjust a hearing aid, a brace, a splint or spectacles, and do not
  advise a family to stop using one. Your job is to notice, to write it down, and to refer.
- **Nothing that singles a pupil out without her agreement.** An eight-year-old has a view about
  whether she wants a special chair at the front. Ask her. Her cooperation is worth more than the
  aid.
- **Nothing you have promised but not priced.** Quote first, promise afterwards. A family told
  there will be a ramp in January, who then see no ramp, will trust the school less than before you
  spoke.

### Who decides, who pays, and how you ask

Most of what this topic recommends is yours to do alone. Some is not, and the difference matters
because it determines whether you are empowered or stuck.

| Level | What it can change | How you access it |
|---|---|---|
| You, this week | Seats, aisle, bags, light, board hygiene, print size, how instructions are given, aids you make, evacuation partners | Do it. Tell the class why. |
| Head teacher, this term | A bench removed, a room swap to the ground floor, a timetable change, a small stationery allocation, a cupboard moved | A short written request naming the pupil and the barrier |
| School council or management committee | A ramp, a drain slab, a handrail, a toilet modification, a water point nearer the room | A written request with a quote and the names of pupils affected; ask the head teacher to table it |
| District or department | Assistive devices, specialist assessment, a resource teacher, building works | A referral through the head teacher, in writing, kept on file |

Two habits make this work. **Write it down**, because a verbal request to a busy head teacher
disappears and a written one accumulates. And **name the pupil and the barrier**, not the solution:
"Rameen, Grade 3, uses a crutch, cannot enter the veranda without help, loses ten minutes of every
lesson" gets further than "we need a ramp".

The honest picture beyond the school gate is harder. Cote (2021), writing about low- and
middle-income countries, describes how weak social protection and financing systems leave assistive
products out of reach for most of the people who need them. That is a policy-level analysis, not a
classroom one, and it is cited here for one purpose: so that you do not conclude from your own
frustration that your school is uniquely badly run. The gap is systemic, which is exactly why the
free end of the spectrum matters so much to the pupil in front of you this year.

And the teachers' own account, from Pakistan: Batool and colleagues (2023) found government primary
teachers in Multan district naming insufficient training, limited resources and missing support
services as the hurdles to an inclusive classroom culture. Read that as a description of the
conditions you will work in, and notice that it does not describe the conditions as hopeless.

## Activity: Build one aid and cost it

Work in groups of three. About fifty minutes. You will need whatever is in the room: card,
newspaper, string, bottle caps, bricks, cloth, markers, tape, a cutter.

**Part 1 - draw the learner (5 minutes).** Your tutor will give each group one learner profile from
Room 4 or a comparable one. Write, in two lines, the barrier in the pupil's terms: what she cannot
currently do in the lesson.

**Part 2 - build (25 minutes).** Make a working aid or assistive solution for that barrier out of
what is on the table. It must be usable, not a model of a usable thing. If your idea needs
something you do not have, substitute and note the substitution.

**Part 3 - cost it (10 minutes).** On a card, write: the materials, the real price of each in
rupees in your own town, the time to make it, who could make it, and how long it will survive in a
class of fifty-eight. Guessing is not allowed in the price column; if you do not know, write
"to be quoted".

**Part 4 - the test (10 minutes).** Hand your aid to another group with the profile card removed.
Their job is to work out, from the aid alone, what barrier it removes, and then to find one way a
pupil in a class of fifty-eight would break it, lose it, or be embarrassed by it. Fix one of the
three before the session ends.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the four layers of accessibility in the order this topic gives them, and state
   which two are free.

2. *(Remember)* Define assistive technology in one sentence, and give one no-tech, one low-tech and
   one high-tech example.

3. *(Understand)* Explain why Rameen's impairment produces a disability in Room 4 but might not in
   another school, using the environment-interaction idea from Unit 2.

4. *(Understand)* A teaching aid must carry the content. Explain what this rules out, and give one
   example of a popular classroom display that fails the test.

5. *(Apply)* A pupil with low vision has been moved to the front and still cannot read the board.
   Give three free changes you would make, in the order you would try them, and say why that order.

6. *(Apply)* Your Grade 4 class has a pupil who cannot grip a pencil for more than five minutes and
   a pupil whose feet do not reach the floor. Describe the solution for each, the material it is
   made from, and its cost.

7. *(Apply)* Write the written request you would hand your head teacher about the broken drain
   slab. Four sentences maximum, naming the pupil and the barrier rather than the solution.

8. *(Analyze)* A school proudly reports that it has built a ramp at the front gate. Analyse what
   this does and does not achieve for a pupil who uses a wheelchair, using the four layers.

9. *(Analyze)* Two teachers spend Rs 300 each. Teacher A buys a laminated science chart for the
   wall. Teacher B buys chart paper, a marker and glue, and makes a tactile number line and a
   large-print sum chart. Analyse the difference in what each purchase bought, in terms of this
   topic's test for a teaching aid.

## Summary

Accessibility means a pupil can get to the learning, get into it and use it. Disability arises in
the interaction between an impairment and the environment (WHO and World Bank, 2011), which is why
the same pupil is excluded in one room and included in another.

Audit it in four layers: the **route** (gate to seat, seat to water, seat to toilet, seat to
safety), the **room** (aisle, seat, what can be seen, heard and reached), the **material** (print
size, contrast, language, steps in an instruction) and the **routine** (time, how instructions are
given, whether asking is allowed). Layers three and four are free; layer two is nearly free; parts
of layer one must be escalated in writing.

Run the accessibility walk rather than auditing from the front: walk the route at the pupil's
speed, sit in four seats, meet the material as she meets it, and sort the findings into three lists
- this week by me, this term with the head teacher, and not by me.

The free changes that get ignored are a washed board, a controlled glare, a seat chosen for ear and
eye rather than for label, a brick under the feet, a sloped writing surface, and a rehearsed
evacuation in which the pupil who moves slowly goes first.

A teaching aid carries the same content through another sense. Your voice, your body, objects, hand
drawn large print and touch are five channels and four are free. The tactile number line costs
about Rs 110 and serves the whole class; enlarged print costs Rs 70 by hand or Rs 10 to Rs 20 a
page at the shop.

Assistive technology is a spectrum from a rolled-cloth pencil grip at Rs 0 to a prescribed hearing
aid. The cheap end removes most of the barrier for most pupils. Do not improvise anything that
bears weight or that was prescribed, do not single a pupil out without asking her, and do not
promise what you have not priced.

Know which level owns each change - you, the head teacher, the school council, the district - and
ask in writing, naming the pupil and the barrier rather than the solution.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can state the four layers of accessibility and sort a list of classroom problems into them.
- [ ] I can run a fifteen-minute accessibility walk and produce the three sorted lists from it.
- [ ] I can name five free physical changes to a classroom and say which pupil each one serves.
- [ ] I can apply the test for a teaching aid and reject an aid that only decorates.
- [ ] I can make at least two assistive solutions from materials already in a government school
      classroom, and cost them in rupees.
- [ ] I can say what I must not improvise, and why.
- [ ] I can write a four-sentence request to a head teacher that names a pupil and a barrier.
- [ ] I can plan an evacuation for a class of fifty-plus containing a pupil who moves slowly.

## Try this at your practicum school

1. **Walk one route.** Choose the pupil in your class whose access you are least sure about. Walk
   her route from the gate to her seat, then to the water point, then to the toilet, at her pace.
   Write down every obstacle and time the journey.
2. **Make one aid.** From materials costing under Rs 150, make one aid that serves a named pupil and
   the whole class. Use it in a lesson. Record what happened, including whether the pupils used it
   without being told to.
3. **Write one request.** Draft the four-sentence written request for the one thing on your list
   that is not yours to fix. Show it to your mentor. If your mentor agrees, hand it in.
4. **Rehearse one exit.** With your mentor's permission, walk the evacuation route with the class
   once, with the slowest-moving pupil leaving first and two named partners.

Bring the obstacle list, the aid itself, the request and a note on the drill to the next session.

## Summative task

**Task.** Produce a **costed accessibility and aids plan** for one real classroom, of no more than
four pages, containing:

1. The findings of an accessibility walk, written as four short sections matching the four layers,
   with the route timed.
2. Three named pupils (initials only) with the specific barrier each meets, stated as what she
   cannot currently do.
3. For each pupil, the solutions you propose, each with the material, the making time, the rupee
   cost and the level that owns it. At least one solution per pupil must cost nothing.
4. One aid you have actually made, described, with a note on how it survived use.
5. A single costed table for the whole plan, separating what you spend from what you escalate.
6. The four-sentence written request for the item that is not yours to fix.
7. An evacuation plan of five lines.

**Mini-rubric** (25 marks)

| Criterion | Marks | What a strong response does |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence from the walk | 5 | Findings are observed, not imagined: real distances, real times, the time of day for light, the state of the board. All four layers are covered, not only the room. |
| Barriers stated as capability *(Analyze)* | 6 | Each barrier is written as what the pupil cannot currently do, traced to a feature of the environment rather than to her condition, and the analysis distinguishes the barrier from the impairment. At least one finding is one a casual observer would have missed. |
| Solution fit | 5 | Each solution removes the barrier named, not a different one. At least one per pupil is free. The aid made is usable and serves more than one pupil. |
| Honest costing | 5 | Rupee prices are local and current, time is included, and anything unpriced is marked "to be quoted" rather than guessed. Spend and escalation are separated. |
| Ownership and escalation | 4 | Each item is assigned to the right level, and the written request names the pupil and the barrier rather than the solution. Nothing weight-bearing or prescribed is improvised. |

A plan that spends Rs 4,000 you do not have will score less than a plan that spends Rs 300 you do.
The discipline is the point.
