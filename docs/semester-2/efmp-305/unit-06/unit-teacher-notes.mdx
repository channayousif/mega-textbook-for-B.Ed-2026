# Teacher notes: Unit 6

For the teacher educator delivering this unit to B.Ed trainees. Not part of the learner-facing
material.

## What this unit is really teaching

The content is culture, parents, specialists and barriers. The capability is **doing something
specific, cheaply, without permission, and knowing honestly what it will not achieve.**

This is the closing unit of the course, and it carries a particular risk. A unit on collaboration
invites sentiment, and sentiment is the enemy of everything here. A trainee who leaves saying that
teachers should care more about parents has learned nothing assessable. A trainee who can say "I
would ask the pupil who looks after her, send her cousin with a message, go to the house on Thursday
with a female colleague, speak Sindhi, open with the long division, and ask these four questions" has
learned the unit.

Say that to the group in the first session. Then hold to it: every time a discussion drifts towards
attitude, ask what the person would do on Monday and what it costs.

## Sequencing across the three weeks

Weeks 14 to 16. Note for the record that the course guide's Unit 6 header says two weeks while
listing three; `D-2026-0047` resolved this as a slip within the guide, and the unit is taught as
three weeks.

**Week 14, Topic 6.1.** The charter session is the practical centre. Run it twice: once with the
trainees as themselves writing a charter for the training group, which takes forty minutes and is
unexpectedly revealing, and once as the Part 1 and Part 2 activity for a school class. Do not skip
Part 2, the breaking of the charter. It is where the learning is, and groups that are only allowed to
build charters produce decorative work.

**Week 15, Topic 6.2.** The role-play needs a full session and should not be compressed into the end
of a lecture. Set the reading of the topic as preparation so the session is spent practising rather
than explaining.

**Week 16, Topic 6.3.** Design work in threes, then the doubling test. Reserve the last hour of the
course for the Monday list: each trainee commits aloud to one of the eight items, naming the school
they will be in and the week. Write the commitments down and, if you teach this cohort again, open
with them.

## Running the parent role-play without caricature

This is the one activity in the course that can do harm if it is run badly, and there are three ways
it goes wrong.

**Caricature.** A trainee playing a poor rural mother will, left alone, play her as helpless,
confused or comic. Stop this immediately and firmly the first time it appears. The briefs are written
to prevent it: every family member in them is competent, has a reason for her position, and knows
something the teacher does not. Tell the group before they start that the family member in each brief
is the most knowledgeable person in the room about the child, because that is true.

**Performed agreeableness.** Both parties try to make the meeting succeed and nothing is learned. The
briefs each contain an obstacle and the family member must hold it. The instruction is explicit in the
activity; repeat it verbally, because trainees do not believe it.

**The rescue.** The teacher-player solves everything in ten minutes and the family is grateful. Cut
this off by keeping strictly to the ten-minute limit and by running Part 2, where the family member
reports what the teacher failed to obtain. Most first attempts miss two of the four questions
entirely, which is the single most useful finding of the session and will not emerge if Part 2 is
dropped.

A point on composition: where your group includes trainees from the communities being depicted, they
will often play the family briefs with much greater accuracy than the briefs require. Let them, and
let the group notice the difference.

## Where trainees reliably go wrong

Five errors, in order of frequency.

1. **Collapsing collaboration into communication.** Trainees will describe a shared support plan as
   "keeping parents informed". Push back on the specific point: informing is the tokenism zone of the
   participation ladder, and the four questions exist because the information has to travel the other
   way first.

2. **Producing an IEP form.** Having met the term, trainees will design a two-page document with
   boxes. Ask who will read it in March. The unit's position must be taught carefully: the IEP is a
   real international instrument and the term is worth knowing, Pakistani schools are not generally
   required to produce one, and a one-page working document that three people use beats a long form in
   a cupboard. Do not let this slide into "paperwork does not matter"; the one page matters a great
   deal, and the reason is that the teacher transfers.

3. **Treating the specialist as the solution.** Trainees who have met a special educator will
   reorganise everything around the monthly visit. Compare the session with the actual timetable rather than claiming a fixed percentage.
   Ask what happens on the other school days
   and keep asking until the answer is specific.

4. **The permanent buddy.** This is the most common practical recommendation in Pakistani teacher
   training and trainees arrive with it. Expect resistance when you call it a failure, because it is
   produced by kindness. The three-children framing works better than argument: name what happens to
   the supported pupil, to the helper and to the rest of the class, then introduce the five rules as a
   repair rather than a rebuke.

5. **Either despair or inflation about class size.** Half the group will say inclusion is impossible at
   eighty; the other half will produce plans that need a class of twenty-five. Both are refusals to
   engage. The design-versus-attention distinction is the lever, and the doubling test is the exercise
   that settles it. Master Imtiaz's thirty-five seconds should be on the board for the whole of week 16.

## A caution on home visits

Topic 6.2 recommends visiting families, and in most of rural Sindh a visit by a female teacher is
ordinary and welcome. It is not universally so, and a trainee on placement is a guest in somebody
else's school and somebody else's village.

Teach three conditions explicitly, because a trainee who gets this wrong can damage a relationship
that is not hers to risk: the class teacher's and head teacher's permission first, always; a second
person, normally a female colleague, where that is what local propriety requires; and never a visit
arranged around an incident or a complaint. Where a visit is not possible, the messenger route is not
a lesser substitute but the normal alternative, and the four questions work as well on a veranda or
over a borrowed telephone.

## Marking the summative tasks

Three tasks, and the same failure mode in all three: pleasant, general work that no one could act on.

For the **culture plan** (Topic 6.1), the discriminating criterion is the diagnosis of the most
damaging marker. Require the counts. A plan built on "the class seems friendly" has not been done, and
should be returned rather than marked down.

For the **home-school plan** (Topic 6.2), look first at whether the trainee established who raises the
child by asking, and whether the four questions are phrased in the language they would really be
spoken in. A plan with the questions in textbook English has not been thought through.

For the **inclusion action plan** (Topic 6.3), the discriminating criterion is the honest evaluation at
the end. A plan that claims the scheme will transform the class is weaker than one that says what will
still be true in March.

A general note on marking the whole unit: reward costed, specific, modest work and mark down fluent
generality, however well written. This is the standard the unit assessment's rubrics are built on, and
trainees should be told so before they start.

## Practical work

The course guide carries no "Suggested Practical Activities" section, so nothing is invented here
beyond what the unit's own topics supply. The three practicum sets in the topics are the practical
work, and the following are worth prioritising if placement time is short.

- **Ask one child who looks after her**, then check the register. Two minutes, and it reliably
  produces a discrepancy in a good proportion of cases. If your trainees do one thing from this unit,
  make it this.
- **Twenty minutes with last year's teacher.** The highest-value, lowest-cost collaboration in the
  course, and the one trainees are most surprised to find nobody does.
- **One non-literate communication channel**, established and used twice.
- **Number one on the Monday list**: move one child, for a stated reason, with the class teacher
  informed. Require the half-page afterwards.

Collect the Monday-list commitments at the end of week 16 and follow them up during placement
supervision. A commitment nobody asks about is a commitment that was made to the room.
