## A real classroom situation

Government Primary School, Goth Allah Dino, taluka Johi, district Dadu. A village of about three
hundred households, two hours from Dadu town on a road that is a problem in the rains. Four
teachers, two hundred and thirty-one children on the register, Grades 1 to 5.

Miss Hameeda has Grade 4: sixty-two names, between forty and fifty present depending on the week
and the harvest.

Sahiba is nine. She has had a hearing loss since an illness at three and nobody has ever measured
it. She hears better on one side than the other, which Miss Hameeda does not know. She has no
hearing aid; the family cannot buy one and has not been told that anybody might. She has been in
this school since Grade 1 and has moved up every year.

Nobody in Grade 4 is cruel to Sahiba. That is true, and it is why the problem has gone unexamined
for three years. No bullying, no name-calling. Asked whether Sahiba should be in the school, the
class would look puzzled: of course she should, she is from the village.

Here is what there is instead.

At lunch she eats with her cousin Rabia, who is seven and in another grade, because the girls in her
own class have eaten in the same group of five since Grade 2 and nobody has ever moved.

When Miss Hameeda says "make groups of four", three groups form at speed and a fourth assembles out
of whoever is left. Sahiba is in the slow group every time. No child decided this. The fast groups
are made of friends, friendship is made of conversation, and conversation is what Sahiba finds
hardest.

At games, teams are picked by two captains. Sahiba goes eleventh or twelfth of twelve. Again no
cruelty: the captains want to win and she does not catch a shouted instruction, so she is a poor
pick - and she is a poor pick partly because she is always picked last and so never learns the game.

Miss Hameeda has noticed all of it. Four times this year she has said to the class, in Sindhi and
with real feeling, "Sahiba is your sister, you must be kind to her." Each time, for about two days,
two or three girls are kind to her in a way that embarrasses everybody. Then it stops, the groups of
five reassemble, and nothing has changed.

The question this topic answers is not why these children are unkind. They are not unkind. **It is
why four sincere appeals to a class's kindness produced no change at all, and what Miss Hameeda
should have done instead.**

## Explanation

### Culture is what reliably happens, not what is displayed

Walk into a classroom in Sindh and you may see a chart saying "We Respect Everyone" in careful
letters. The chart tells you nothing. It tells you somebody had coloured paper.

A classroom culture is the set of things that **reliably happen** in that room: a pattern of events,
not a set of values. The test is behavioural. If you watched four lessons without being told
anything, what could you predict?

That is where the work is. You cannot change a culture by announcing a value. You change what
reliably happens, and the announcement at best explains to the children why.

The standard a culture has to be judged against is the one Unit 1 took from Ainscow (2020): not the
absence of unkindness, but **presence, participation and achievement** for every learner. A class can
be entirely free of cruelty and still fail all three, which is Grade 4's position.

Four markers describe most of a classroom's culture, and all four can be counted in one lesson.

**Who speaks.** Not how many hands go up. Who is actually asked, by name, and how long the teacher
waits. In most rooms a small group of confident pupils carries the lesson and the rest are audience.
A culture is supportive when being asked is universal and ordinary, and when a pupil can say "I did
not understand" at no cost.

**Who is chosen.** For groups, for teams, for the form that goes to the taluka office, for the one
trip of the year. Choosing is where a class's real hierarchy shows, and it is almost always done at
speed and without thought, which is why it reproduces itself.

**Who is helped, and how.** Not whether help exists, but whether it is a bounded task or a standing
charge over a person, whether the same child always gives it and the same child always receives it,
and whether anyone has checked that it is wanted.

**What happens when someone fails.** The highest-leverage marker and the least noticed. Where a
wrong answer is a small event and a second attempt is normal, a child with any difficulty can afford
to participate. Where it brings laughter or a sharp word, her rational strategy is silence, and she
will adopt it within a fortnight.

Now score Grade 4 for Sahiba. Who speaks: almost never asked, because asking her takes longer and
the lesson is behind. Who is chosen: last, by a mechanism nobody designed. Who is helped: nobody
helps her and nobody was ever given the job. What happens when someone fails: unknown, because she
has not attempted anything in front of the class for two years.

Four markers, four answers, and not one is about kindness. **Miss Hameeda was addressing a feeling,
and the problem was a set of routines.**

Unit 2 gave you stigma and labelling, and this is where they live: stigma is less about what children
say than about what reliably happens to a classmate, which the class reads as information about what
she is for. Unit 4 built the room. This topic builds the social layer on top, and the two fail
together. An accessible room with a hierarchy in it is a room a child can enter and not join.

#### The misconception: empathy is a personality trait that cannot be taught

Tell a staff room that a unit on inclusion will spend a week on empathy and somebody will say, with
no hostility, that you either have it or you do not. A teacher can insist on behaviour; she cannot
install a feeling.

Half of that is right and worth keeping: **you cannot install a feeling, and instructing a class to
feel something does not work.** Miss Hameeda proved it four times.

The wrong half is the conclusion. Empathy is not a fixed quantity a child arrives with; it varies
with circumstances, develops with age, and responds to what a child gets to practise. And for a
teacher with sixty children, it is not even the thing to work on directly.

Fu, Xiao, Yin and Zhou (2022) studied 392 typically developing pupils aged ten to seventeen in
Chinese primary and middle schools, measuring the inclusive climate of the classroom, the pupils'
own empathy, and their attitudes toward classmates with disabilities. Three findings from their
abstract matter here.

- Pupils held, on average, a **neutral** attitude toward classmates with disabilities. Not hostile,
  not welcoming. Neutral, which is exactly the state of Miss Hameeda's Grade 4.
- There were positive relationships among inclusive climate, empathy and attitude toward classmates
  with disabilities.
- Empathy played a **partial mediating role** between the inclusive climate and peers' attitudes.

The third suggests a useful question for teaching: could changing classroom routines help pupils
practise perspective-taking and participation? A partial mediator is a variable that accounts for
part of a statistical relationship. It does not establish which factor causes another. A teacher
can change routines and observe what follows; she cannot promise a change in feelings.

**What this evidence cannot carry.** One study, of Chinese schoolchildren, in schools nothing like
Goth Allah Dino. It is correlational with a mediation analysis, which is not the same as showing
that changing a climate causes a change in attitudes. Only the abstract was read for this unit,
which the sources file records. So it is cited for the **shape of the relationship** and the neutral
baseline: no number, no effect size, no claim about how Pakistani children work. What makes the
shape worth teaching is that it agrees with what this course has already shown about routines
beating exhortation, and that it points the teacher at the one link she owns.

So: empathy cannot be ordered. But a teacher is not in the business of raising it directly. She
builds a climate, out of countable, unromantic routines that work whether or not any individual
child's heart is in it.

### Three moves that build climate in a class of sixty

All three have to survive a tired teacher, forty minutes, sixty children, no money and no training
day.

**Move one: make perspective-taking a task, not an appeal.** "Imagine how Sahiba feels" asks a
nine-year-old to do something she cannot do on demand and gives her nothing to produce. Replace it
with a task that has an output.

The teacher gives an instruction in the normal way, except that she turns her back and says it
quietly, so nobody catches more than a word. She asks the class to write what they think she asked.
The answers are wrong and various and the class finds this funny. Then the second question: "What
should I have done so that you could have followed it?" In about ninety seconds the class generates
the list - face us, say it louder, write the key word on the board, say it twice, let us ask each
other.

That is the accommodation list for Sahiba, and the class produced it. Nobody mentioned Sahiba and
nobody was asked to feel anything. The next time Miss Hameeda talks while facing the board, three
children will tell her, because the idea is now theirs.

The principle generalises: the activity works when it puts the class in the position for sixty
seconds and then asks them to produce the fix. It fails when it asks them to imagine a feeling and
report it.

**Move two: structured contact with a shared purpose.** Children do not become friends by being told
to. They become friends doing something together that needs both of them. That is the mechanism
behind every cooperative structure in Unit 3, which makes those structures a culture tool as much as
a teaching one.

The key word is **structured**. Unstructured contact is what Grade 4 already has, and it reproduces
the existing hierarchy: that is what the groups of five are. Structured means the teacher assigns the
pairs, gives each member a role the other cannot do, and keeps the task short enough to finish.

Unit 3's jigsaw is the strongest version because it makes each member necessary by design. Notice
what that does for Sahiba: she holds a piece of information the others need, so they must get it from
her, so they must work out how to communicate with her. That is the whole lesson, and nobody had to
be told to be kind.

**Move three: name and correct in one sentence, immediately.** When something happens that the
culture should not contain - a laugh at a wrong answer, a comment about Sahiba's speech, a group that
has clearly excluded somebody - the response that works is short, specific and unemotional. "In this
class we do not laugh at a wrong answer. Try again, Zahida." Seven seconds. No lecture, no assembly,
no appeal to kindness, and no shaming of the child who laughed.

Long responses fail for a reason worth knowing. A five-minute speech about respect makes the incident
important, makes the offending child the centre of attention, and teaches the class that this subject
produces drama. A seven-second correction teaches that this is simply not what happens here, which is
what a culture is.

### The teacher is the curriculum here

Unit 5 drew the attitude-action cycle: a teacher's belief about what a pupil can do produces her small
decisions, the decisions produce the pupil's experience, the experience produces the pupil's
behaviour, and the behaviour becomes the evidence that confirms the belief.

That cycle has a second loop Unit 5 did not draw. **Thirty other children are watching the
decisions.**

They notice who is asked and who is skipped, whose answer the teacher waits for, who gets the five
minutes after the bell, and whether she speaks to Sahiba in her ordinary voice or in the soft voice
adults use for children they have decided are fragile. They notice, accurately, whether she expects
anything of her.

Then they copy it - not consciously, but because a teacher's treatment of a pupil is the most
authoritative information in the room about that pupil's status. A class will not value a child their
teacher visibly expects nothing of, however often they are told to be kind to her.

That is uncomfortable and it is also the best news in the topic, because the lever is small and it is
in your hand. Asking Sahiba a question she can answer, in your ordinary voice, in front of everybody,
three times a week, does more for her standing than a term of assemblies. Topic 6.3 returns to this
point when the class has eighty children in it and the ninety seconds is all there is.

### The class charter: a culture the children wrote

A charter is the culture put in writing by the people who have to live in it. It is not a list of
school rules, and it is not the teacher's rules with the children's signatures underneath.

How to make one that works, in about forty minutes.

1. **Ask the right question.** Not "what are our rules?" but "what do we want to be true about this
   class?" and then "what do we want never to happen here?" The second question gets the real
   material.
2. **Collect in the home language.** In Goth Allah Dino that is Sindhi. A charter in a language the
   children do not use among themselves is a wall chart.
3. **Keep the children's words.** If a nine-year-old says "nobody should be left standing when groups
   are made", that is the rule. Tidying it into "We include everyone" destroys it: the child's version
   names an event, yours names a value.
4. **Six rules at most**, in positive form where you can. "We ask before we help" beats "do not grab
   her book".
5. **Everybody signs**, including the teacher, and including the children who cannot yet write their
   names, with a thumb mark.
6. **Review it.** The step always skipped, and the one that separates a charter from a poster. Five
   minutes a month: which rule did we keep, which did we break, does any rule need to change? An
   unreviewed charter is dead in three weeks and the class knows it.

A charter will not stop a determined bully and is no substitute for a teacher dealing with one. What
it does is give the class words for the ordinary, undramatic, nobody-is-cruel exclusion that Grade 4
actually has.

### The buddy system, and the way it reliably goes wrong

A buddy system is the most commonly recommended inclusion practice in Pakistani teacher training and
the most commonly botched. The botched version is worth studying because good intentions produce it
and it damages two children.

The teacher identifies the kindest, most capable girl in the class - in Grade 4, Nasreen - and asks
her to look after Sahiba. Nasreen is nine and wants to please her teacher, so she says yes. No end
date, no definition, no review. Six months later, three things have happened.

**Sahiba has a carer instead of friends.** Her social world has one person in it, assigned by an
adult. She is more visibly different than before, because she is the only child in the room with a
minder.

**Nasreen has lost something.** She has been removed from the ordinary business of being nine. She
sits where Sahiba sits, works with whom Sahiba works, and carries a duty of care that is an adult's
job. Her own friendships have thinned, and she will not complain, because she has been praised for
this for six months.

**The rest of the class has been let off.** Sahiba's inclusion has a named owner who is not them. That
is exactly what Unit 1 called integration: the child is in the room, there is an arrangement about
her, and the room has not changed.

Five rules separate a buddy system from a minder.

1. **Rotate, on a written rota.** Everybody takes a turn, including the children you would not have
   chosen. The rota is on the wall, so rotation is a fact rather than a favour.
2. **Give a task, not a charge.** "Tell Sahiba what page we are on and check she has found it."
   "Bring Sahiba into your group for the jigsaw and give her the third card." A task finishes; a charge
   over a person does not.
3. **Time-box it.** A week is plenty, and two buddies a week, so neither carries it alone or for long.
4. **Train them once, for fifteen minutes.** Children are not born knowing how to help without taking
   over. Teach the ask-before-you-help rule, teach that a refusal is accepted, teach the three things a
   buddy does and the one thing a buddy never does - her work.
5. **Let her be the buddy.** The rule that converts the scheme. Sahiba takes her turn on the rota like
   everyone else and is somebody else's buddy for a week. If the supported child never appears in the
   helping column, the rota is a label with a timetable.

The fifth rule matches what the UNICEF booklet on parent, family and community participation says
about adult helpers, and the logic is identical. It sets an "only-as-much-as-needed" principle so that
support is not imposed on those who do not need or want it, and it advises that parent aides in
classrooms be treated as "available teacher support rather than assigned to individual students"
(Meresman, 2014). Attach a helper to a person and you have built a dependency. Attach the help to a
task, available to whoever needs it, and you have built a resource.

### Worked example: the charter and the rota in Grade 4

Miss Hameeda ran the charter session on a Thursday in the second week of term. What follows is what
she did and what happened, including what did not work.

**The session, 45 minutes.** She asked the two questions in Sindhi and wrote the answers on the board
exactly as the children said them. "What should never happen here" produced nineteen items in eleven
minutes, three of them unexpected: nobody should be laughed at for a wrong answer; nobody should have
to stand while groups are made; nobody should be told they are stupid. Cutting nineteen to six took
the rest of the session and a vote. The six, in the children's words, translated here:

1. We ask before we help.
2. Nobody is left standing when groups are made.
3. We do not laugh at a wrong answer.
4. Everybody gets asked something.
5. We say it again if someone did not hear.
6. We all take our turn as buddy.

Rules 5 and 6 are about Sahiba and her name is not in them. Miss Hameeda had decided in advance not to
make the charter about one child: a rule about one named child is a label, and a rule for everyone also
covers Sahiba on the days when the problem is somebody else's.

Everybody signed; four children used thumb marks. Miss Hameeda signed first, which several children
found remarkable and talked about at home - which turned out to matter.

**The rota, 15 minutes the following Monday.** Two buddies a week, taken in register order so nobody
could read anything into who was chosen, and fifteen minutes of training for the whole class on the
three things a buddy does, the one thing a buddy never does, and the ask-before-you-help rule
practised twice in pairs, once with the helper being refused.

**What happened in six weeks.** Rule 2 worked almost at once and was the biggest single change. Groups
are now assigned for anything that matters, and when they are not, the rule means the fourth group
forms without anyone being visibly left over. Sahiba has been in a working group in every lesson for
five weeks. Rule 3 took longer, needing the seven-second correction about a dozen times in the first
fortnight, and then largely held.

Rule 4 was the one Miss Hameeda found hardest, because it is about her. She kept a tally for a week,
as Unit 4 taught, and found that nineteen of sixty-two children had not been asked anything by name in
five days. Sahiba was one of the nineteen. That was the most valuable half hour of the term.

Rule 6 produced the moment that mattered. In week four Sahiba was buddy to a boy called Allah Dino who
had been absent for the harvest and had missed a fortnight of arithmetic. She showed him where the
class had got to and how the long division was set out, and he caught up. A different thing is now
true in that room about what Sahiba is for.

**What did not change in six weeks.** She still eats lunch with her cousin. The group of five has not
opened. She is picked tenth now rather than twelfth, which is not a triumph. No child has visited her
house.

Say that plainly, because a trainee who expects a transformation in six weeks will conclude after six
weeks that none of this works and go back to appeals to kindness. Routines change inside a fortnight.
Friendships are slower and partly outside a teacher's reach. What the six weeks bought was a room in
which a friendship had become possible, which it was not in September.

One unforeseen thing. In week three, Nasreen's mother mentioned the charter to Sahiba's grandmother at
a wedding, including the detail about the teacher signing it first. That is the first piece of
information about Miss Hameeda's classroom that ever reached Sahiba's family. Topic 6.2 begins there.

## Activity: Write the charter, then break it

Groups of four, sixty minutes. Best run with a real class in mind.

**Part 1 - build it (25 minutes).** Each group takes one brief:

- Grade 2, 55 pupils, one child who does not speak at school at all though she speaks at home.
- Grade 5, 48 pupils, one boy with a visible physical disability who uses a stick and cannot run.
- Grade 7 girls, 71 pupils, one girl who reads three years below grade and has not read aloud since
  Grade 5.
- Grade 3, 80 pupils, two children with uncorrected low vision who cannot see the board.

Write six rules. Conditions: in the children's words, not yours; in the language that class speaks
among themselves, with an Urdu or English gloss for the group beside you; no rule may name the child;
each rule must name an event rather than a value. Beside each rule write which of the four markers it
moves.

**Part 2 - break it (20 minutes).** Pass your charter to another group, whose job is to defeat it. For
each rule they must either find a way it can be formally kept while the exclusion continues, or find a
reason it will be dead in a month. This is the part that teaches: most first drafts contain at least
two rules that can be kept in letter and broken in substance. "We include everyone" survives a class
in which a child is included by being assigned to a permanent group of one.

**Part 3 - the review clause (15 minutes).** Take your charter back and fix it, then add what no first
draft has: the review. Write exactly when it happens, who asks the questions, how long it takes, and
what happens when the class says a rule is being broken. If your answer is "the teacher reminds them",
say what makes the second reminder work better than the first.

Present to the room in ninety seconds: one rule you rewrote after it was defeated, and your review
clause.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State the four markers of a classroom culture that an observer can count in a single
   lesson.

2. *(Understand)* Explain why four sincere appeals to Grade 4's kindness produced no change, using the
   difference between a feeling and a routine.

3. *(Understand)* Fu and colleagues (2022) found that empathy partially mediates the relationship
   between a classroom's inclusive climate and pupils' attitudes to classmates with disabilities.
   Explain why that finding is practically useful to a teacher, and state two things the study cannot
   be used to claim.

4. *(Apply)* Rewrite each of these as a charter rule in the form a nine-year-old would say it, naming
   an event rather than a value: (a) "We respect diversity"; (b) "We are helpful to those in need";
   (c) "Everyone participates".

5. *(Apply)* A Grade 5 buddy system has one permanently assigned helper for one supported pupil, with
   no end date. Name three things that will have gone wrong within six months: one for each child
   involved and one for the rest of the class.

6. *(Apply)* Design the muffled-instruction activity for a class of 60 Grade 6 pupils where the pupil
   in question has uncorrected low vision rather than a hearing loss. State what you do, what the class
   produces, and the accommodation list you expect to get.

7. *(Analyze)* In the worked example, Sahiba's turn as Allah Dino's buddy is called the moment that
   mattered. Analyze why that single event does more for her standing in the class than six weeks of
   being helped well.

## Summary

A classroom **culture** is what reliably happens in a room, not what is written on its walls or felt
in its teacher's heart. Four markers describe most of it and all four are countable in one lesson:
**who speaks**, **who is chosen**, **who is helped and how**, and **what happens when someone fails**.
Grade 4 scored badly on all four for Sahiba while containing no cruelty at all, which is why four
appeals to kindness changed nothing: Miss Hameeda was addressing a feeling and the problem was a set
of routines.

**Empathy is not a fixed trait**, and it is also not what a teacher works on directly. Fu and
colleagues (2022), studying 392 Chinese pupils aged ten to seventeen, found a neutral average attitude
toward classmates with disabilities, positive relationships among inclusive climate, empathy and that
attitude, and empathy acting as a partial mediator between climate and attitude. The practical reading
is to **test routines you can change**, then check participation and ask pupils about their experience.
The statistical relationship does not establish a direction of cause.
The study is Chinese, correlational and read here at abstract level only, so it is cited for the shape
of the relationship and the neutral baseline, never for a number or for a claim about Pakistani
children.

Three free moves build climate in a class of sixty. **Make perspective-taking a task with an output**:
the muffled instruction gets the class to produce the accommodation list themselves, without the
supported child being mentioned. **Use structured contact with a shared purpose**, above all Unit 3's
jigsaw, where the supported pupil holds a piece the others need. **Name and correct in one sentence**,
in about seven seconds, because a long response makes the subject dramatic while a short one makes it
simply not what happens here. And remember that **the teacher's own behaviour is the strongest signal
in the room**: a class will not value a pupil their teacher visibly expects nothing of, so three
questions a week in an ordinary voice outperforms a term of assemblies.

A **class charter** is the culture written by the people who live in it: six rules at most, in the
children's own words and home language, naming events rather than values, naming no child, signed by
everyone including the teacher, and - the step always skipped - **reviewed for five minutes a month**.

A **buddy system** fails predictably: one kind child is permanently assigned, and six months later the
supported pupil has a carer instead of friends, the helper has lost her own friendships and carries an
adult's duty of care, and the rest of the class has been let off because inclusion now has an owner who
is not them. That is Unit 1's integration, not inclusion. Five rules fix it: **rotate on a written
rota; give a task, not a charge; time-box it to a week; train the class once for fifteen minutes; and
let the supported pupil take her turn as buddy.** The fifth converts the scheme, and it matches the
booklet's "only-as-much-as-needed" principle and its advice that adult helpers be treated as available
support rather than assigned to individual pupils (Meresman, 2014).

Expect routines to change in a fortnight and friendships to take much longer. Six weeks of charter and
rota put Sahiba in a working group in every lesson and gave her one morning as somebody else's buddy.
They did not change where she eats her lunch. What they bought was a room in which a friendship had
become possible.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can name the four markers of a classroom culture and say what an observer would count for each
      one.
- [ ] I can explain why instructing a class to be kind changes nothing, without implying that the
      children are at fault.
- [ ] I can state what Fu and colleagues (2022) found, why the mediation result is useful to a teacher,
      and what the study cannot support.
- [ ] I can run a charter session: the two questions, the children's own words, six rules, the
      signatures and the monthly review.
- [ ] I can write a charter rule that names an event rather than a value, and spot one that can be kept
      in letter while the exclusion continues.
- [ ] I can name the five rules that separate a buddy rota from a minder, and explain why letting the
      supported pupil be a buddy is the one that converts the scheme.
- [ ] I can say what six weeks of this work should and should not be expected to change.

## Try this at your practicum school

Three tasks. The second is the one to do properly.

1. **Read the room cold.** Observe one full lesson in a class that is not yours and score the four
   markers: tally the names asked; watch one group-forming or team-picking event and write the order;
   note who gives help and who receives it; note the first wrong answer and what followed within five
   seconds. Half a page.

2. **Run the muffled instruction.** Ten minutes, in any class you are allowed to teach. Both steps in
   order: the instruction facing away and quiet, then "what should I have done so that you could have
   followed it?" Write down the list the class produces, in their words, and compare it with what you
   would have written yourself. Then write one sentence on what you will do differently when you give an
   instruction, and keep it.

3. **Find the minder.** Find out whether any child in your placement school has a permanently assigned
   helper. Ask the helper, privately and without suggesting an answer, what she does and whether she
   chose it. Ask the class teacher when it started and when it is reviewed. Write what you find and what
   you would propose, remembering that the arrangement was made by somebody who meant well and that
   saying so is part of proposing a change.

## Summative task

**Task.** Produce a **culture plan** of no more than three pages for one real class you have observed or
taught.

Your plan must contain:

1. A cold reading of the class against the four markers, with the counts you actually made rather than
   impressions. Name the lesson, the date and the class size.
2. The one marker you judge to be doing the most damage in that room, with your reason, argued rather
   than asserted.
3. A six-rule charter in the children's own words and home language, with an Urdu or English gloss, each
   rule naming an event, none naming a child, each tagged with the marker it moves.
4. A buddy rota designed against the five rules, including your fifteen-minute training content and the
   week in which the supported pupil takes her turn as buddy.
5. The review clause: when, who asks, how long, and what happens when the class reports a rule being
   broken.
6. An honest paragraph on what you expect to have changed in six weeks and what you expect not to have,
   and the evidence that would tell the difference.

**Mini-rubric** (25 marks)

| Criterion | Marks | What a strong response does |
|---|---|---|
| Observation over impression | 4 | Real counts from a named lesson, including the uncomfortable ones. A plan built on "the class seems friendly" scores nothing here. |
| Diagnosis of the damaging marker *(Analyze)* | 6 | Identifies which marker is doing the most harm in this specific room and argues it from the observed counts, distinguishing a cause from a symptom. Recognises that an absence of cruelty is compatible with complete exclusion. |
| Charter quality *(Apply)* | 5 | Six rules, children's words, home language, events not values, no child named. Each rule survives the "can this be kept while the exclusion continues?" test. |
| Buddy rota against the five rules | 4 | Rotation written, help bounded as a task, time-boxed, training specified, and the supported pupil scheduled as a buddy. A permanently assigned helper scores zero here, however kindly described. |
| The review clause | 3 | Specific and plausible, with an answer to what makes the mechanism work rather than a reliance on the teacher remembering. |
| Honesty about the timescale *(Evaluate)* | 3 | Separates what routines will change in six weeks from what friendship will not, and names evidence that could tell them apart. Neither promises a transformation nor concludes that nothing works. |

The criterion that separates a professional from an enthusiast is the second. Anybody can write six
pleasant rules. The teacher who can say which single thing in her own room is doing the most damage, on
evidence she collected herself, is the one whose charter will still be alive in March.

## Further reading

- Fu, W., Xiao, Y., Yin, C., & Zhou, T. (2022). The relationship of inclusive climate and peers'
  attitude on children with disabilities in China: A mediating role of empathy. *Frontiers in
  Psychology, 13*, 1034232. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1034232 (open access, CC BY 4.0).
  392 pupils aged 10 to 17 in Chinese primary and middle schools; read it for the neutral baseline
  attitude and for empathy as a partial mediator between classroom climate and peer attitudes, the
  result that points a teacher at routines rather than feelings.
- Meresman, S. (2014). *Parents, family and community participation in inclusive education* (Webinar 13,
  companion technical booklet). UNICEF.
  https://www.unicef.org/northmacedonia/media/4161/file/MK_ParentsCommunityIE_Report_ENG.pdf
  Read section II on creating a culture of collaboration, and the "only-as-much-as-needed" principle on
  support, which is the logic that separates a buddy rota from a minder. Topic 6.2 uses this booklet
  much more heavily.
- Ainscow, M. (2020). Inclusion and equity in education: Making sense of global challenges.
  *Prospects, 49*(3-4), 123-134. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11125-020-09506-w (open access, CC BY 4.0).
  The wider argument that inclusion is about presence, participation and achievement, which is the
  standard a classroom culture must be judged against rather than the absence of unkindness.
