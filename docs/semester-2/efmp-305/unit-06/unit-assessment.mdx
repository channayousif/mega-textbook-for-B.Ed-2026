## Unit summary

Unit 5 ended with four things that move a case and the news that three of them are free. This unit
was about the people who have to agree to them.

**Topic 6.1** replaced kindness with routine. A classroom **culture** is what reliably happens in a
room, measurable on four markers an observer can count in one lesson: who speaks, who is chosen, who
is helped and how, and what happens when someone fails. Grade 4 failed all four for Sahiba while
containing no cruelty at all, which is why four appeals to the class's kindness changed nothing.
Empathy is not a fixed trait and it is also not what a teacher works on directly: Fu and colleagues
(2022) found a neutral average attitude to classmates with disabilities and empathy acting as a
partial mediator between classroom climate and that attitude. This is an association, not proof of
cause; classroom routines are a practical place to try changes and check participation. Three free moves build it: perspective-taking as a task with an
output, structured contact with a shared purpose, and a seven-second correction instead of a speech.
The teacher's own micro-behaviour is the strongest signal in the room. A **class charter** works when
it is in the children's words and home language, names events rather than values, names no child, and
is reviewed for five minutes a month. A **buddy rota** works when it rotates, gives a task rather than
a charge, is time-boxed, is trained once, and schedules the supported pupil as a buddy herself - and
fails, producing a carer and a lost helper, when it does none of those.

**Topic 6.2** began with two unanswered notes and a staff room's conclusion that the family took no
interest. Six facts explained the silence and not one was about interest: the mother cannot read, the
notes were in Urdu, the child is raised by her grandmother, the register named a father in Karachi,
the lane's only previous summons concerned a broken window, and the family believed the school
already knew. Khalid and colleagues (2021) report that Punjabi parents of children with disabilities
were *more* inclined towards inclusive education while distrusting whether schools could deliver it,
and the UNICEF booklet names the barriers, including the sharpest school-made one: **parents are
invited only when there is a problem** (Meresman, 2014). The **first-contact protocol** is eight free
steps, and the **four questions** are the meeting itself - the one about how the child signals that
she has not understood changed Sahiba's teaching in a week. **Collaboration is not a handover**:
withdrawal costs the child the subject she misses, leaves twenty-nine days unchanged, and gives
inclusion an owner who is not the teacher. Collaboration is co-planning, co-instruction and
co-assessment, needing joint planning and role equity (Álvarez-Couto et al., 2026), and a monthly
visit should run **watch, plan, then brief work**. The **IEP** is treated here as a shared working
document, not a legal artefact: Pakistani schools are not generally required to produce one, so the
useful version is one page, three columns, held by the class teacher.

**Topic 6.3** took Master Imtiaz's question seriously. Barriers get Unit 5's four levels plus **who
holds it** and **what it costs**, after which "lack of resources" needs to be made specific before a response can be planned. Large classes are real and the teacher cannot fix them, but the misconception that they make
inclusion impossible fails at its first premise: inclusion combines **shared access routines and individual support**, and the one
resource that scales with class size is **the children**. Eight items are available on Monday with no
money and nobody's permission, and the course ends on the first of them.

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

Choose one answer. Each question carries 1 mark.

1. *(Remember)* Which of these is one of the four markers of a classroom culture used in Topic 6.1?
   - A) How many pupils have a disability recorded in the register
   - B) What happens when someone gives a wrong answer
   - C) Whether the classroom has a rules chart on the wall
   - D) How often the teacher mentions inclusion to the class

2. *(Understand)* Fu and colleagues (2022) found that empathy partially mediates the relationship
   between a classroom's inclusive climate and pupils' attitudes to classmates with disabilities. What
   does this suggest a teacher should work on?
   - A) The pupils' feelings, since empathy is what drives the attitude
   - B) Classroom routines, checking their effects rather than assuming the study proves causation
   - C) The attitudes directly, since they are what matters for the supported pupil
   - D) Nothing, since the study was conducted in China and cannot transfer

3. *(Apply)* A Grade 5 teacher has asked her kindest pupil to look after a classmate with a physical
   disability for the rest of the year. Which single change would do most to turn this into a working
   buddy system?
   - A) Praising the helper publicly so that others are encouraged to volunteer
   - B) Asking a second kind pupil to share the duty with the first
   - C) Putting the whole class on a weekly rota that the supported pupil also takes a turn on
   - D) Writing the arrangement into the class charter so the class knows about it

4. *(Remember)* In the first-contact protocol, what is the first thing a teacher should establish
   before arranging to meet a pupil's family?
   - A) Whether the family is literate
   - B) Which language the family speaks at home
   - C) Who actually raises the child day to day
   - D) Whether the head teacher approves of a home visit

5. *(Understand)* Khalid and colleagues (2021) interviewed ten parents from inclusive primary schools
   in Punjab. Which finding did they report about parents of children with disabilities?
   - A) They were less inclined towards inclusive education than other parents
   - B) They were more inclined towards inclusive education, while also reporting dissatisfaction
   - C) They were indifferent to whether their children attended mainstream schools
   - D) They preferred residential special schools in every case

6. *(Understand)* Why does the UNICEF booklet's observation that parents are invited to school only
   when there is a problem matter for a teacher planning a first meeting?
   - A) It shows that schools should send fewer invitations
   - B) It explains why an invitation carries a negative meaning, so the first contact must not be a
     summons about a difficulty
   - C) It proves that parents of children with disabilities are reluctant to cooperate
   - D) It means a written note is always the wrong medium regardless of literacy

7. *(Apply)* A specialist can visit your school for two hours once a month. Which allocation best
   matches the collaboration model taught in Topic 6.2?
   - A) Two hours of individual work with the pupil, which is the specialist's expertise
   - B) One hour with the pupil and one hour writing a report for the file
   - C) Twenty-five minutes observing the ordinary class, thirty minutes planning with the class
     teacher, fifteen minutes checking one question with the pupil, then setting the next date
   - D) Two hours training the whole staff, since that reaches the most children

8. *(Understand)* Topic 6.3 adds two columns to Unit 5's four-level gap analysis. What are they?
   - A) The pupil's name and the date the barrier was noticed
   - B) Who holds the barrier, and what it costs in money and minutes
   - C) The relevant section of the Sindh Act, and the penalty for breaching it
   - D) The number of pupils affected, and the head teacher's opinion

9. *(Understand)* Why does the claim that large classes make inclusion impossible fail, on the
   argument of Topic 6.3?
   - A) Because class sizes in Pakistan are falling and the problem is temporary
   - B) Because a determined teacher can always find extra time for individual pupils
   - C) Because shared access routines can remove some barriers alongside necessary individual support
   - D) Because the Sindh Act requires classes to be reduced to a workable size

10. *(Apply)* Which component requires the teacher to allocate time separately for each pupil receiving
    it, so its time cost doubles if the number needing it doubles?
    - A) Writing the key word of every instruction on the board
    - B) A rotating three-pupil instruction check after each instruction
    - C) Ten minutes of one-to-one reading with the teacher each day for one pupil
    - D) Assigned study pairs across the whole class, rotated monthly

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

Answer within the stated bound. Marks as shown.

1. *(Understand)* In two or three sentences, explain why Miss Hameeda's four appeals to her class's
   kindness produced no lasting change. (4 marks)

2. *(Analyze)* A trainee writes the charter rule "We include everyone." Analyze why this rule is weak,
   and rewrite it as two rules that name events. (4 marks)

3. *(Understand)* List the five rules that separate a buddy rota from a minder, and state in one
   sentence which of the five does most to prevent the supported pupil being labelled. (5 marks)

4. *(Analyze)* A teacher sends two notes home in Urdu and receives no reply. Analyze this outcome,
   giving three distinct explanations that are not about the family's level of interest, and say which
   of the three is the school's own doing. (5 marks)

5. *(Understand)* State the four questions to ask a family at a first meeting, and explain in one
   sentence each what the second and third are for. (5 marks)

6. *(Apply)* Write the three column headings of a one-page shared support plan, and give one example
   line for a pupil who lip-reads Sindhi well and Urdu badly. State who holds the page. (5 marks)

7. *(Understand)* Explain, in no more than four sentences, what an Individualized Education Programme
   is in countries that require one, what Pakistani law requires of a Sindh school instead, and what
   this course therefore recommends a teacher produce. (5 marks)

8. *(Analyze)* "There is a lack of resources at this school." Analyze why this is not a usable
   statement of a barrier, then restate it as one for a school whose classrooms all have a step at the
   door, giving the level, the owner, the free action and the costed request. (5 marks)

9. *(Apply)* Sort the following into "scales to a class of eighty" and "does not scale", with a
   one-line reason for your two hardest cases: separate worksheets for three pupils; assigned study
   pairs; individual written feedback in every exercise book; the key word on the board; a daily
   ten-minute reading session with the teacher; a rotating instruction check. (5 marks)

10. *(Analyze)* Figure 6.4 contains one barrier with no owner inside the school: a family's fear of
    what its own community will say about a child with a disability. Analyze what a classroom teacher
    can and cannot change about it, and name the mechanism by which whatever change is possible
    actually reaches the family. (5 marks)

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

Answer in continuous prose. Marks as shown.

1. *(Evaluate)* Miss Hameeda's Grade 4 contains no cruelty towards Sahiba, and the class would say
   without hesitation that she belongs in the school. Evaluate the claim that this class is therefore
   already inclusive. Use the four markers, Unit 1's distinction between integration and inclusion, and
   the account of what did and did not change in six weeks. (10 marks)

2. *(Create)* A Grade 6 pupil in a rural Sindh school has a learning difficulty. Her mother cannot
   read, speaks Sindhi, has never entered the school and does not travel outside the village; her father
   works in Oman; she is raised mainly by an aunt. Produce the teacher's complete first-contact plan and
   the one-page shared support plan that follows it, and justify three of your choices against the
   evidence in this unit. (10 marks)

3. *(Create)* Design a peer-support scheme for Master Imtiaz's Grade 3 of eighty-one that is different
   from the one in Topic 6.3, using the six design steps. State the failures you are addressing, your
   four components, the fifteen-minute training, the evidence you will collect, and the total cost. Then
   apply the doubling test to your own design and report the result honestly. (10 marks)

4. *(Analyze)* A head teacher in Hyderabad reports to a visiting officer that his school "practises
   inclusive education", citing a monthly specialist visit for three pupils, a kindness award given in
   assembly, and an inclusion chart in every classroom. Analyze each of the three claims against this
   unit, say what the school is in fact doing in each case, and identify the three cheapest changes that
   would make the report true. (10 marks)

5. *(Evaluate)* "Everything worth doing about inclusion in a Pakistani government school requires
   resources the school does not have." Evaluate this claim using all six units of this course. Your
   answer must concede what is true in it, identify precisely where it fails, and finish with the part
   of the work that belongs to the classroom teacher, with its cost. (10 marks)

## Answers and marking guidance

### MCQ answer key

1. B  2. B  3. C  4. C  5. B  6. B  7. C  8. B  9. C  10. C

A note on item 3. Option B is a genuine improvement and a candidate who chooses it has understood
something. C is the intended response because rotation across the whole class plus the supported
pupil's own turn is the change that removes the label, the dependency and the let-off for the rest of
the class at once, where B only halves the burden on one helper.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. Because an appeal addresses a feeling and the exclusion was produced by routines. The things
   actually excluding Sahiba were self-selected friendship groups, captain-picked teams and a pattern
   of not being asked, none of which an instruction to be kind alters. The two or three days of visible
   kindness that followed each appeal were a response to the teacher rather than a change in what
   reliably happens, so the routines reasserted themselves. (2 marks for the feeling-versus-routine
   distinction; 2 marks for naming at least two of the actual routines. A response that blames the
   children gets no more than 1.)

2. It is weak because it names a value rather than an event, so nothing in a classroom is observably
   in breach of it, and it can be formally satisfied by a class in which a child is "included" by
   permanent assignment to a group of one. Acceptable rewrites name events, for example: "Nobody is
   left standing when groups are made" and "Everybody gets asked something." (2 marks for the
   value-versus-event analysis, one of which requires the point that the rule can be kept while the
   exclusion continues; 1 mark per acceptable event-naming rewrite.)

3. Rotate on a written rota; give a task rather than a charge over a person; time-box it, typically to
   a week; train the class once, for about fifteen minutes; and schedule the supported pupil as a buddy
   herself. The fifth does most to prevent labelling, because a pupil who only ever appears in the
   helped column has been given a label with a timetable, while one who also appears in the helping
   column has a role. (1 mark per rule up to 4; 1 mark for the fifth-rule justification.)

4. Three acceptable explanations, from: the mother cannot read, so the note was never read; the notes
   were in Urdu in a Sindhi-speaking household, so even a literate reader may not have engaged with
   them; the child carrying them could not read them either and did not know they mattered; the person
   who actually raises the child was never addressed, because the register names an absent father; and
   the school's only previous summons to that lane concerned a broken window, so an invitation carries
   a negative meaning. All five are the school's doing in the sense that the school chose the medium,
   the language, the addressee and the history; a strong answer identifies the medium-and-language
   choice, or the failure to establish who raises the child, as the clearest instance. (1 mark each for
   three distinct explanations; 2 marks for the school's-own-doing identification, which must be
   argued rather than asserted.)

5. (a) What does she do at home that you are proud of? (b) How do you get her attention when you need
   it, and how does she let you know she has not understood? (c) What has she found hardest about
   school? (d) What would you like her to be able to do by the end of this year? The second is the
   technical question: the family has usually solved a communication problem the school is still
   failing at, and the answer most often changes the teaching within a week. The third asks about the
   school rather than the child, which surfaces things the school is doing and that nobody there has
   thought of. (1 mark for each of the four questions; 1 mark for the two explanations together.)

6. Columns: what we are trying for; who does what; how we will know. An acceptable example line:
   target "to follow the Urdu reading lesson without copying her neighbours"; who does what "teacher
   gives the key vocabulary the day before and writes the key word on the board; family asks for one
   word each evening and sends a voice note on Thursday"; how we will know "teacher's note of what she
   answers when asked directly, once a fortnight". The class teacher holds the page, not the specialist
   and not the file. (1 mark per column heading; 1 mark for a line with a checkable target and a named
   owner; 1 mark for the class teacher holding it.)

7. In countries that require one, an IEP is a formal and often legally mandated document setting out a
   named pupil's goals, the support to be provided and review dates, with parental rights attached.
   Section 9 of the Sindh Empowerment of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2018 requires individualised
   support, appropriate modes of communication, reasonable accommodation and monitoring of
   participation, attainment and completion, but it does not require any such document. This course
   therefore recommends a one-page shared working document, held by the class teacher and short enough
   to be used. (2 marks for the international description; 2 marks for what Sindh law does and does
   not require, which must not overstate the Act; 1 mark for the recommendation.)

8. It is not usable because it names no level, no owner, no action and nothing checkable, so no one can
   act on it and nobody can be shown to have failed. Restated: "Every classroom in this school has a
   step at the door and no pupil using crutches can enter unaided; no estimate for ramps has ever been
   obtained." Level 2 and 3. Owner: Government under section 6(11) for the works, and the head teacher
   for which room is used. Free action: run Unit 4's accessibility walk, teach the affected class in the
   ground-floor room this term, and give the head teacher a written list. Costed request: ramp and
   accessible-toilet works, requested in writing with an estimate attached, through the head teacher.
   (1 mark for the critique; 1 mark for a concrete restatement; 1 mark each for level, owner and the
   free-action-plus-request pair.)

9. Scales: assigned study pairs; the key word on the board; the rotating instruction check. Does not
   scale: separate worksheets for three pupils; individual written feedback in every book; the daily
   ten-minute reading session. The two hardest cases are the separate worksheets and the reading
   session, and both fail for the same reason: their cost is multiplied by the number of pupils served
   while the teacher's available minutes are fixed, so at eighty the time comes out of the other
   seventy-nine and the practice is abandoned within a fortnight. (3 marks for a correct sort, losing
   one per misplacement; 2 marks for the two reasons, which must identify fixed teacher time rather
   than merely saying it is "too much work".)

10. A teacher cannot control community opinion alone or promise an immediate change. She can listen
    to the family, challenge stigma in school and work with willing community partners. Her direct responsibility is to make her classroom a place where stigma does
    not operate: the pupil is asked questions, appears on the buddy rota as a helper, is in a working
    group, and is entered for things. The mechanism that carries this to the family is **the child's own
    account of her day**, because the family trusts her as a witness in a way it does not trust the
    school, and because that account is evidence against the lane's opinion rather than an argument with
    it. A strong answer also notes that the family's own fear is reasonable rather than ignorant.
    (2 marks for the limits of one teacher's control and a realistic partnership response; 2 marks for the classroom
    response stated concretely; 1 mark for identifying the child's own account as the mechanism.)

### ERQ rubrics

Use the analytic criteria below, each scored 0, 1 or 2: 0 means absent or materially incorrect;
1 means partly met, with a missing justification or an impractical detail; 2 means accurate,
specific and justified from the case. Sum the five criterion scores for a total out of 10.
The four bands below illustrate overall response quality; they do not replace criterion scoring.
Award 0 for no relevant response.

| ERQ | Five criteria, 2 marks each |
|---|---|
| 1 | Four-marker evidence; integration versus inclusion; analysis of routine change; limits of six-week outcomes; justified judgement. |
| 2 | Correct carer and access barriers; first-contact choices; four questions; checkable shared plan; justification and consent/privacy. |
| 3 | Specific barrier mechanisms; feasible components with daily access preserved; training; sustainable evidence; honest costing and doubling test. |
| 4 | Specialist-visit analysis; award analysis without assuming an unspecified award is harmful; chart-versus-practice analysis; three matched changes; justification from classroom evidence. |
| 5 | Resource constraints conceded; evidence drawn across six units; analysis of actionable barriers; parallel escalation; costed teacher-level action. |

**ERQ 1 - is a class with no cruelty already inclusive?**

- **Band 4 (9-10).** Answers no, and earns it. The absence of cruelty is granted fully and treated as a
  real finding rather than brushed aside. Each of the four markers is applied to the specific evidence:
  Sahiba is rarely asked, is in the slow group every time, is picked last by a mechanism nobody
  designed, is helped by nobody, and has not attempted anything in front of the class for two years.
  The response identifies that these outcomes require no ill will at any point, which is what makes the
  class's good conscience compatible with complete exclusion. Unit 1's distinction is used precisely:
  Sahiba is present and uncontested, which is integration, and the room has not changed. The six-week
  evidence is read both ways, with the routine changes credited and the unchanged lunch group, the
  closed group of five and the absence of any home visit named without being treated as proof that the
  work failed. Concludes with the right standard: participation and achievement, not goodwill.
- **Band 3 (7-8).** Answers no with at least three markers correctly applied to the evidence and the
  integration-inclusion distinction used correctly, but reads the six-week outcome in only one
  direction, or treats the absence of cruelty as merely insufficient rather than as genuinely
  significant.
- **Band 2 (4-6).** Answers no but argues it mainly from the feeling of the situation, or applies the
  markers without evidence from the class. May slide into blaming the children or the teacher. Or
  answers yes on the strength of the class's acceptance of Sahiba, with some awareness that something
  is missing.
- **Band 1 (1-3).** Retells the situation, or asserts that inclusion means being kind to everyone.

**ERQ 2 - the first-contact and support plan**

- **Band 4 (9-10).** A plan that would actually work in that household. Establishes the aunt as the
  person who raises the child, and says how: by asking the pupil. Chooses a messenger the family
  trusts, a place the mother can be in, and Sindhi throughout, and gives an opening sentence naming
  something specific the child can do. All four questions appear, phrased as they would really be
  spoken rather than in textbook form. Leaves with one change the teacher makes, one free non-literate
  thing the family does, and a channel that does not require reading. The support plan is one page,
  three columns, five lines at most, with named owners and cheap pre-named evidence, held by the class
  teacher, with a next review date. Three choices are justified against the unit's evidence rather than
  against common sense: for example the medium against the booklet's barrier list, the opening on a
  capability against Khalid and colleagues' finding about parents distrusting delivery, or the refusal
  to produce an IEP form against what section 9 actually requires.
- **Band 3 (7-8).** A workable plan with the right language, messenger and questions, and a plausible
  one-page plan, but the justifications are generic or only loosely tied to a named source, or the plan
  contains a line that depends on somebody who may not come without saying so.
- **Band 2 (4-6).** A plan that sends a note, addresses the absent father, holds the meeting in Urdu,
  or opens with the difficulty. Or an IEP-style form produced as though Pakistani law required it.
  Questions present but reduced to asking about the child's problems.
- **Band 1 (1-3).** General statements about the importance of parental involvement, with no protocol.

**ERQ 3 - design a scheme at eighty-one**

- **Band 4 (9-10).** A genuinely different design that still obeys the method. Failures are stated as
  mechanisms rather than as conditions, and at least one of them concerns the teacher's own blindness
  to a pupil losing the thread. The inventory precedes the design and the design uses only what is in
  it. All four components are run by the class rather than by the teacher. The structure is universal
  and the scheduling specific, so no component functions as a label. The fifteen-minute training is
  written out, not gestured at. Evidence is named in advance, cheap, includes asking the pupils
  themselves, and is sustainable to March. The costing is in rupees and minutes. The doubling test is
  applied honestly to the candidate's own design and at least one weakness is reported rather than
  concealed.
- **Band 3 (7-8).** Four class-run components built by the steps, with training and evidence specified
  and the doubling test applied, but one component is really the teacher's attention in disguise, or
  the structure singles out the supported pupils, or the doubling test is applied without finding
  anything.
- **Band 2 (4-6).** Components that would need a class of twenty-five, or that reproduce Topic 6.3's
  scheme with the names changed. Evidence absent or not collectable. Costing missing.
- **Band 1 (1-3).** A list of good intentions, or an argument that nothing can be done at eighty-one.

**ERQ 4 - the head teacher's three claims**

- **Band 4 (9-10).** Each claim is analysed separately and each analysis names what is actually
  happening. The monthly withdrawal visit is identified as a small part of school time, with
  everyday teaching left unchanged, as costing the pupil the subject she misses, and as giving
  inclusion an owner who is not the teacher, which is integration. The kindness award is identified as
  addressing a feeling and assessed for whether it rewards reciprocal help or a permanent minder; the prompt
  does not specify that detail, so credit a reasoned conditional judgement. The chart is identified as display rather than culture, against the four markers. The three
  cheapest changes are genuinely cheap and genuinely effective, drawn from the free list, and each is
  matched to the claim it repairs - for example reordering the specialist visit to watch-plan-work,
  replacing the award with a rotating rota, and running a participation tally so the school can say
  something checkable. Nothing in the answer blames the head teacher for the conditions he did not
  create.
- **Band 3 (7-8).** All three claims analysed correctly with the proportions or the markers used, and
  three plausible cheap changes, but one analysis is thin or one proposed change is not actually free.
- **Band 2 (4-6).** The claims are dismissed as tokenism without the mechanism being explained, or
  only one is analysed properly. Proposed changes require money or a post.
- **Band 1 (1-3).** The school is praised, or condemned, as a whole.

**ERQ 5 - does everything worth doing require resources the school does not have?**

- **Band 4 (9-10).** Concedes what is true, specifically and without grudging: the hearing aid, the
  ramp, the accessible toilet, the sanctioned post, the teacher qualified in sign language or Braille
  and the second teacher for a class of eighty-one are real requirements that a government school
  genuinely cannot meet, and both the Pakistani studies cited in this course record class size and time
  as barriers teachers and parents name themselves. Then locates the failure precisely: the claim
  smuggles in the premise that inclusion is delivered by resources and individual attention, where
  Units 1, 3, 4 and 6 show that shared design can remove barriers alongside individual support. The answer draws on all six units with the
  specifics attached - presence, participation and achievement from Unit 1; identification without
  labelling from Unit 2; differentiation, cooperative structures and flexible assessment from Unit 3;
  seating, arrangement and improvised aids from Unit 4; the section 9 duties and the written escalation
  from Unit 5; culture, the four questions, the shared plan and the class-run scheme from Unit 6 - and
  shows that the great majority of these cost nothing. It distinguishes **removing a barrier** from
  **solving a condition**, and refuses both the cynical and the inflated conclusion. It finishes with
  the teacher's own portion stated concretely and costed in minutes, and it does not end on a
  consoling generality.
- **Band 3 (7-8).** Concedes the resourced items, identifies the design-versus-attention error, draws
  on at least four units with specifics, and finishes on the teacher's portion with some costing. Less
  precise about where exactly the claim fails, or weaker on the barrier-versus-condition distinction.
- **Band 2 (4-6).** Either largely agrees with the claim while listing some free actions, or rejects it
  with an optimistic list and no concession. Units referred to by name without specific content. No
  costing.
- **Band 1 (1-3).** Asserts that teachers must do their best, or that the government must act, without
  analysis.

The criterion that separates a professional from both the cynic and the enthusiast is the last band
descriptor of ERQ 5. The cynic concedes nothing to the free list and does nothing. The enthusiast
concedes nothing to the ramp and the post, and is disbelieved by every teacher in the room. The
professional can say exactly which part is hers, what it costs, and when she will start.

On Monday, ask one pupil what makes an instruction hard to follow. Write the key word on the
board, face the class, and ask the pupil whether that helped. Use the answer to improve the next
instruction. This needs no budget or additional permission.
