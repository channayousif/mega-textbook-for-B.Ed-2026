## A real classroom situation

Three weeks after the charter session, Miss Hameeda decided to meet Sahiba's mother.

She did what she had been taught to do and what every teacher in the school does. She wrote a short,
polite note in Urdu asking the parents of Sahiba to come to the school on Thursday at eleven, and she
gave it to Sahiba to take home.

Nothing happened.

The following week she wrote a second note, a little firmer, and gave it to Sahiba again.

Nothing happened.

In the staff room on the Monday after that, Miss Hameeda said, not unkindly and not for the first
time in the history of that staff room, that it was difficult to help a child when the family took no
interest. Two colleagues agreed. One said that these people send children to school for the midday
meal and the rest is not their concern. It was a short conversation and nobody in it was being
cruel. It was, they all felt, simply a fact about the village.

Here are the facts about the village.

Sahiba's mother, Zainab, cannot read. Neither note was ever read to her, because Sahiba could not
read the Urdu either and did not know they were important.

Zainab has never been inside the school building. She does not know she is allowed to be. The one
time a parent from her lane was called to that school, it was because a boy had broken a window, and
the whole lane heard about it.

Sahiba's father works in Karachi and comes home twice a year. On the register, in the column for
parent, his name is the only one written.

The person who actually raises Sahiba, feeds her, dresses her and knows her better than anyone alive
is her grandmother, Hawwa, who is sixty-one and has never been mentioned in any school record.

And Zainab knows something about Sahiba that would change Miss Hameeda's teaching within a week, and
she has believed for two years that the school already knows it.

Not one of those six facts is about taking no interest. **Every one of them is about something the
school did, or failed to do, and every one of them is cheap to fix.**

## Explanation

### What "parental involvement" means, and the ladder it sits on

"Parental involvement" is used loosely enough to mean anything from signing a report card to sitting
on a school management committee, and the looseness matters because a school can satisfy the weak
version completely while learning nothing about any child.

The UNICEF technical booklet on parents, family and community participation in inclusive education
reproduces a well-known general ladder of participation to make this point, with three broad zones:
**nonparticipation**, where people are managed without a meaningful say; **tokenism**, where they
are informed, consulted or placated but do not share decision-making; and **citizen power**, where they hold partnership, delegated power
or control (Meresman, 2014). The ladder is a general participation scale rather than a
disability-specific instrument, and the booklet uses it as an example rather than a measuring tool,
which is how it is used here.

The school in this case operates in the first two zones. A parents' day, a signed
result card and a summons when something goes wrong are tokenism at best. Nothing in them requires
the school to find out anything.

The same booklet sets out five options for parent engagement, drawn from UNESCO's Open File on
Inclusive Education: families as **activists**, pushing systems toward inclusive policy; families as
**contributors**, supporting learning at home; schools, families and community as **partners**;
families **supporting other families**, which the booklet particularly recommends where families are
poor, isolated or linguistically distinct; and family and community involvement in **school
governance and management** (Meresman, 2014).

Be honest about which of those five a Grade 4 teacher in Goth Allah Dino can reach. Not activism. Not
governance. She can reach the second, the third, and - on a good day, through one parent who turns out
to be willing - the fourth. That is enough. The whole of the capability this topic teaches lives
inside "partners" and "contributors", and it is reachable this month with no budget and nobody's
permission.

#### The misconception: parents of children with disabilities do not want to be involved

This is the assumption made in the opening staff-room conversation. A teacher may reach it
through frustration, but the assumption still needs to be tested. It is also
contradicted by the evidence, including the Pakistani evidence.

Khalid, Malik and Asghar (2021) interviewed ten parents of children with and without disabilities,
drawn from inclusive primary schools in Punjab, using semi-structured interviews and thematic
analysis. Their reported finding is the one that matters here: **parents of children with
disabilities were more inclined towards inclusive education**, not less. What they also found explains
where the appearance of indifference comes from: those parents reported uncertainty and
dissatisfaction, and the sources they named were the reactions of other parents, the quality of
instruction, and institutional support, with large class sizes and time constraints as barriers.

The parents wanted the inclusion. What they distrusted was whether the school could deliver it. A
parent who has concluded that the school cannot help her child, and who has never been given evidence
to the contrary, looks exactly like a parent who does not care.

Virani and Ali (2022), in an exploratory qualitative study with fifteen primary school teachers and five parents of children with special needs in Pakistan,
all purposively selected, name a stronger parent-teacher relationship as one of the things
successful inclusion requires. Unit 5 cited this study for what it says about attitudes and deferred
the parent-teacher point to this topic. This is where it lands: the relationship is named by teachers
themselves as a condition of the thing working, which makes it a professional requirement rather than
a courtesy.

The UNICEF booklet lists the barriers that have traditionally kept families out, and each of them is
visible in Goth Allah Dino: lack of awareness of the educational alternatives for their children;
fear of stigmatisation and hostility from within their own communities; poverty pushing families
toward residential special schools that transfer the cost to the government; and a lack of placement
options near home, particularly for families in rural areas (Meresman, 2014).

Its list of barriers to collaboration is sharper still, because every item is the school's doing.
Policies that exist but are not enforced. People who say collaboration is good but are not prepared
to practise it. Educators who are reluctant to involve parents in decisions about education. And the
one that explains the two unanswered notes: **parents are only invited to come to school when there
are problems in relation to their child, so an invitation acquires a negative meaning and parents
become reluctant to attend** (Meresman, 2014).

Miss Hameeda's notes were not read. But even if they had been read, they were a summons from an
institution whose only previous summons in that lane had been about a broken window.

**And now the honest counterweight, which the same booklet supplies and which a trainee must not be
allowed to skip.** Some parents of children with disabilities do share the negative attitudes toward
disability that prevail in their community, or have genuinely conflicted feelings about their own
child. Families' views on inclusive education can be mixed, particularly where the ordinary school
visibly cannot respond to diversity and segregated provision looks like the safer option
(Meresman, 2014). You will meet a mother who does not want her daughter in the class, and a father
who would rather she stayed at home. The booklet's conclusion about that is the right one: listen,
understand and address the concern, because parents need information and support to become allies.
What you must not do is either romanticise every family or write one off.

### Making the first contact work

Eight steps. None of them costs money. Most of them are about not doing the obvious thing.

1. **Do not rely on a note alone.** Find out whether the household can read the language used and
   whether a note is their preferred channel. Offer a spoken alternative. A written message may
   help a family that wants one; it should not be the only route to a conversation.
2. **Find out who actually raises the child, and ask the child.** The register names a father. The
   person who knows the child may be a grandmother, an aunt, an older sister or an uncle. Two
   minutes with the pupil settles it, and getting this wrong wastes the whole effort.
3. **Offer a contact method the family accepts.** Ask whether they prefer a school meeting, a call
   or a home visit. Arrange visits through the school, with family consent and a suitable colleague.
   A trusted adult may carry an invitation; do not share a child's needs with neighbours or pupils
   to arrange it. Check whether a Lady Health Worker is available rather than assuming she is.
4. **Use the home language.** If the household speaks Sindhi, the meeting is in Sindhi. A mother who
   is spoken to in Urdu will be polite, will agree with everything, and will say nothing you need.
5. **Think about where and with whom.** A woman who does not travel outside the village will not come
   to the school; a veranda with another woman present may be acceptable where an office is not.
   Ask what suits the family rather than offering a time.
6. **Open with what the child can do.** One specific, true, concrete thing. "Sahiba is quick at
   arithmetic and she is the only girl in the class who sets out long division without being told."
   This is not flattery. It is the evidence the family has been waiting two years for, and it decides
   whether anything else in the meeting is believed.
7. **Ask the four questions, and write the answers down.** Below.
8. **Leave with one change each and a channel.** One thing you will change on Monday. One thing the
   family will do that is free and requires no reading. And a way to exchange messages that does not
   require literacy: a voice note on a relative's telephone, a verbal message carried by an older
   cousin at the school, a named day when you will ask the child.

### The four questions

These are the point of the meeting. Everything else is arrangements.

1. **What does she do at home that you are proud of?** It opens the meeting in the right place, and
   it reliably produces a capability the school has never seen.
2. **How do you get her attention when you need it, and how does she let you know she has not
   understood?** This is the technical question, and it is the one that most often changes the
   teaching within a week. The family has solved a problem you are still failing at.
3. **What has she found hardest about school?** Ask about school, not about the child. The answer is
   often something nobody at the school has thought of, and it is sometimes something the school is
   doing.
4. **What would you like her to be able to do by the end of this year?** It establishes that the
   family has expectations, which is the thing most often denied of them, and it gives you a shared
   target rather than a shared concern.

Write only the information needed for support on one sheet and keep it securely with restricted
access. Agree with the family who may receive it, including a successor teacher. Do not put private
family information on the class rota or send it through another pupil. Make the record useful for your
successor. A teacher transfers, and when she does, everything in her head about this child goes with
her unless it is on paper.

### Worked example: the first meeting with Sahiba's mother, held in Sindhi

Miss Hameeda asked Sahiba who looked after her at home. Sahiba said her dadi. That answer alone
redirected the entire effort.

She then asked Hawwa's nephew, who is in Grade 7 and comes past the staff room, to tell his great-aunt
that two teachers would like to come on Thursday afternoon to talk about how well Sahiba is doing in
arithmetic. Three deliberate choices in one sentence: a trusted messenger, a visit rather than a
summons, and a reason that was good news.

**Thursday, about forty minutes, in Sindhi.** Miss Hameeda went with Nasreen's class teacher, a woman
in her fifties who has taught in the village for twenty years. The first ten minutes were about the
weather, the harvest and a cousin's wedding, which is not a delay but the meeting itself beginning.

Then the first question. Hawwa answered it: Sahiba cooks, she looks after a younger cousin, and she
can tell from the gate which of four relatives is approaching by how they walk.

The second question produced the thing. How do you get her attention? *You tap the floor, or you touch
her shoulder, and you wait until she is looking at you before you say anything.* How does she let you
know she has not understood? *She says "haan" and does nothing. When Sahiba agrees without moving, she
has not understood.* Miss Hameeda had been teaching her for seven months and had read that signal as
laziness.

The third question produced the thing that mattered most. What has she found hardest about school?
Zainab said: *she can follow when it is Sindhi. She cannot follow the Urdu.* Sahiba lip-reads, nobody
had ever used that word for it, and she lip-reads the language spoken at home far better than the
language the lessons are in. For two years she had sat through Urdu reading lessons appearing to
follow, by watching the faces of the children around her.

And then the sentence that should be read twice by anyone who has ever concluded that a family takes
no interest. Zainab said she had assumed the school knew all of this, because Sahiba's older brother
had told a teacher in Grade 2.

The fourth question: what would you like her to be able to do by the end of the year? *To read a
page of the Sindhi book aloud without being afraid.*

**What Miss Hameeda gave in return.** Three things, all of them small enough to be kept.

- What she would change on Monday: Sahiba moves to the second row on her better side; she will touch
  her shoulder and wait for her eyes before speaking to her; and the key word of every instruction
  goes on the board in writing, which costs four seconds and helps the other sixty-one as well.
- What she asked the family to do: each evening, ask Sahiba to say one word she learned that day.
  Free, no reading, two minutes, and it gives the family a window into a classroom they have never
  seen.
- The channel: Hawwa's nephew's telephone takes voice notes, and Miss Hameeda would send one on a
  Thursday and could be sent one any day.

**What this was not.** It was not an Individualized Education Programme, and nobody signed anything.
Pakistani schools are not generally required to produce such a document, and pretending otherwise
would have added a form to a situation that needed a conversation. What it was, was forty minutes, a
sheet of paper in the register, and a teaching change on Monday that no training course had been able
to produce in seven months.

### Collaborating with a specialist, and the misconception about what that means

Sahiba's case now needs something Miss Hameeda does not have. She needs to know whether Sahiba's
hearing loss can be helped by a device, what can be done about the Urdu, and whether her apparent
difficulty with one subject is the hearing loss or something else. Those are technical questions.

The realistic Pakistani picture is this. There is no special educator at Government Primary School,
Goth Allah Dino, and there will not be one. There is a special education centre in Dadu town, two
hours away. There is one itinerant teacher, Mr Shahnawaz, attached to the taluka, who is supposed to
visit schools with identified children and who manages, in practice, about one visit a month to any
given school and sometimes less. Many schools in Sindh have less than that and a good many have
nothing at all.

#### The misconception: collaboration means handing the child over to the special educator

This is the most damaging misconception in the unit, because the arrangement it produces feels like
success to everyone involved. The specialist arrives, takes the child out for forty minutes, does
work that is genuinely expert, and leaves. The class teacher is relieved. The head teacher can report
that the child is receiving specialist support. The specialist has done his job.

Three things are wrong with it.

**The child pays for it in the subject she misses.** Withdrawal is never free: she is removed from
whatever the class was doing, which is usually the subject she is weakest in, because that is when
the teacher is most willing to let her go.

**The ordinary school days are unchanged.** A forty-minute monthly session is only a small part of
the pupil's learning time. Its proportion depends on actual days attended and the school timetable.
If the specialist's advice never changes everyday teaching, most learning takes place without it.

**Inclusion acquires an owner who is not the teacher.** This is Topic 6.1's buddy failure at an
institutional scale, and it is Unit 1's integration again: the child is in the school, there is an
arrangement about her, and the teaching has not changed.

The research literature says the opposite of handing over. A qualitative review of twelve co-teaching
studies from 2020 to 2025, following PRISMA, reports that co-teaching encompasses **co-planning,
co-instruction and co-assessment**, and concludes that it works as a strategy for inclusion when it
rests on **joint planning, role equity, and dedicated training and collaboration time** for teachers
(Álvarez-Couto, Hermoso-Larzabal, Galarraga-Arrizabalaga, & Sánchez Cabrero, 2026). The abstract does not establish that the studies include Pakistan and the review is read here at abstract level, so it is cited for what
collaboration consists of rather than for an effect. Two of its three conditions - joint planning and
role equity - cost nothing, and the third names honestly what Pakistan mostly does not provide.
Ainscow (2020) makes the wider version: collaboration is one of the levers by which inclusion moves,
alongside the use of existing resources, rather than something that waits on new money.

And the booklet's principle from Topic 6.1 applies exactly as it did to the buddy rota: support should
follow an "only-as-much-as-needed" rule, and a helper is better treated as available support than as
somebody assigned to an individual child (Meresman, 2014).

### The shared support plan, and how to treat the IEP honestly

You will meet the term **Individualized Education Programme (IEP)** in your reading, in training
workshops and in conversation with special educators, so you need to know what it is. In several
countries it is a formal document, often legally required, setting out a named child's goals,
services and review dates, with specified parental rights attached to it.

This course does not assume that an international IEP procedure applies in every Pakistani school. Unit 5 established what
the law actually requires of a Sindh school: admission without discrimination, accessibility,
reasonable accommodation according to the individual's requirements, individualised support and
appropriate modes of communication, and monitoring of participation, attainment and completion.
Section 9 does not name an IEP document. That observation concerns this section, not every school
rule or inspection arrangement. Check any local requirements rather than assuming an exemption.

So treat the IEP as what is useful about it rather than as a form to be filled: **a shared working
document**, held by the people doing the work, short enough that it is actually used. A one-page
plan that three people read beats a twelve-page plan in a cupboard, and this is not a concession to
Pakistani conditions - it is true everywhere, and the long version's failure mode is well known.

One page, three columns, five lines at most.

- **What we are trying for.** In the child's terms and the family's words where possible. "To read a
  page of the Sindhi book aloud without being afraid" is a better target than "to improve literacy
  skills", because you can tell whether it happened.
- **Who does what.** Specific, named, and small enough to survive a bad week. Figure 6.6 sets out
  what each member holds that nobody else has, and what each must not be asked to do.
- **How we will know.** The evidence, named in advance, and cheap. Two lines a term on participation
  and attainment, as Unit 5's audit required. A tally. The family's weekly word.

Who holds it: **the class teacher.** Not the specialist, who comes once a month, and not the file.
The person who is with the child for six hours a day holds the page and makes the changes, and the
specialist contributes technique to it.

When it is revisited: once a term, twenty minutes, and the date written on the page before the
previous meeting ends. A plan with no next date is a plan that has quietly finished.

### Worked example: the shared support plan with a specialist who comes once a month

Mr Shahnawaz's first visit, in two versions.

**The version that happens.** He arrives at ten, is given tea, is told about Sahiba by the head
teacher, takes her into the empty Grade 5 room for forty minutes, does some excellent work on
listening discrimination, tells Miss Hameeda that Sahiba is a bright girl who needs patience, and
leaves at noon. Everyone is satisfied. Nothing is different on Tuesday.

**The version Miss Hameeda arranged, after writing to him once and asking for it.** Two hours: seventy-five minutes for the steps below, with the remaining forty-five
minutes for a family conversation, agreed records and checking that the teacher can use the advice.

*Twenty-five minutes watching the class teach.* He sits at the back of an ordinary Urdu lesson,
because the question is not what Sahiba can do in a quiet room with an expert; it is what happens to
her in a room with sixty-one other children. What he observes is that she is correctly seated now,
that the key word goes on the board, and that she loses the thread whenever two children answer in
quick succession because she cannot tell who is speaking.

*Thirty minutes with Miss Hameeda and the page.* The part the usual version skips entirely. He brings
three things she could not have known: that the "haan and no movement" signal is a recognised pattern
worth treating as a formal check; that a child who lip-reads one language far better than another
should be given the Urdu lesson's key vocabulary the day before, because lip-reading an unfamiliar
word is nearly impossible; and a possible route to a hearing assessment, whose availability, cost and appointment
arrangements the school and family would need to confirm before travelling. They put two changes on the
page and strike one of Miss Hameeda's existing ones as useless.

*Fifteen minutes with Sahiba, on one specific question.* Not a general session: he checks whether she
can discriminate the sounds the Urdu lesson actually turns on, and tells Miss Hameeda the answer.

*The last five minutes.* The next visit date goes on the page, and the plan for the twenty-nine days
in between is Miss Hameeda's, in her own hand, consisting of four things that cost nothing.

The difference between the two versions is not expertise. Mr Shahnawaz is the same man with the same
knowledge in both. The difference is the **order**: watch, then plan, then work briefly. Everything
he knows reaches the child through the teacher in the second version, and through forty minutes a
month in the first.

### When there is no specialist at all

Most readers of this unit will teach where Mr Shahnawaz does not exist. The question is then what the
team is, and the answer is that the school already has one and does not use it.

**The head teacher** controls the register, the timetable and the room allocation, which is to say he
controls three of the cheapest and most effective accommodations available. He also signs the
escalation.

**Last year's teacher** has two hundred days of knowledge about this child and has never once been
asked for it. Twenty minutes in the staff room at the start of the year is the highest-value, lowest-
cost collaboration in this entire unit, and it almost never happens.

**The Lady Health Worker** attached to the village knows the family, is trusted in the house, and can
carry a message a school cannot. She also knows what free services exist in the district, which is
information schools routinely lack.

**The parent**, who after the four questions is now the most informed member of the team.

**The child**, who should be asked directly what helps, and who at nine is a better authority on
this than anyone in the room.

**The class**, through the charter and the rota of Topic 6.1.

And for the part that genuinely requires the state - a sanctioned post, a teacher qualified in sign
language or Braille - Unit 5 gave you the method: write the request, name the pupil, cite section 9(2),
keep the copy, and teach meanwhile. A device for a named pupil is individualised support, so ground that
request in section 9(1) (reasonable accommodation according to the pupil's requirements) and make the
same written record. The teaching meanwhile is not a holding action while the real solution is awaited.
For most children in most Pakistani schools, it is the solution.

## Activity: The first meeting, with a real obstacle

Pairs, then fours. Fifty minutes. This activity fails if both parties are trying to be agreeable, so
read your brief and hold the line it gives you.

**Part 1 - the role-play (20 minutes, two rounds of ten).** One of you is the teacher making first
contact about a named pupil. One of you is the family member. The family member's brief contains an
obstacle and must not reveal it unless the teacher's approach draws it out.

Family briefs, one per pair:

- You are a grandmother raising an eight-year-old with a physical disability. You cannot read. You
  have never been inside the school. You assume you are being told that the child must leave.
- You are a mother whose eleven-year-old son has a learning difficulty. You speak Sindhi; the teacher
  has begun in Urdu. You will agree with everything said and volunteer nothing.
- You are a father, home for ten days from Dubai, with a nine-year-old daughter with low vision. You
  believe she should stop school after Grade 5 and you have not said so to anyone.
- You are a mother of a deaf seven-year-old. You have been told by a relative that there is a special
  school in the city and that an ordinary school is a waste of her time. You half believe it.

Teachers: you must find out who raises the child, open on something the child can do, ask the four
questions, and leave with one change each and a channel. You have ten minutes, which is realistic.

**Part 2 - what you did not get (15 minutes, in fours).** Each teacher reports one piece of
information she failed to obtain, and the family member says what would have drawn it out. This is
the part that teaches, and most first attempts miss at least two of the four questions entirely.

**Part 3 - the page (15 minutes).** Write the one-page shared support plan for your pupil: three
columns, five lines, named owners, and the evidence you will look for. Then mark on it, honestly,
which lines depend on somebody who may not come.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State the four questions to ask a parent at a first meeting, and say which one most
   often changes the teaching within a week.

2. *(Understand)* Explain why two unanswered notes are weak evidence about a family's interest in
   its child's education, naming three distinct reasons drawn from the opening situation.

3. *(Understand)* Khalid and colleagues (2021) found that parents of children with disabilities were
   more inclined towards inclusive education, while also reporting uncertainty and dissatisfaction.
   Explain how both findings can be true at once, and state the scope limits of the study.

4. *(Apply)* A Grade 6 teacher wants to meet the mother of a pupil with a learning difficulty. The
   mother cannot read, does not travel outside the village, and speaks Sindhi. Set out, in order, the
   first five things the teacher should do, and name the one obvious thing she should not do.

5. *(Apply)* Write the three columns and five lines of a one-page shared support plan for Sahiba as
   the worked example leaves her, using the family's own words for the target.

6. *(Apply)* A specialist visits your school for two hours once a month. Allocate the two hours and
   justify the order you chose in two sentences.

7. *(Analyze)* A head teacher reports that a pupil with a disability "receives specialist support" on
   the strength of a monthly forty-minute withdrawal session. Analyze what is wrong with this as a
   claim about inclusion, using the proportion of school time involved and Unit 1's distinction
   between integration and inclusion.


## Summary

The two unanswered notes were not evidence about a family. They were evidence about a school. Sahiba's
mother cannot read; the notes were in Urdu; nobody had established that the child is raised by her
grandmother; the register named a father who lives in Karachi; and the only previous summons to that
lane had been about a broken window. **Every one of those is cheap to fix, and none of them is a lack
of interest.**

The school in this case operates in the **nonparticipation** and **tokenism** zones of the
general participation ladder the UNICEF booklet reproduces: a parents' day, a signed result card and a
summons when something goes wrong require the school to find out nothing. Of the booklet's five
engagement options, a village-school teacher can realistically reach **contributors** and
**partners**, and that is enough (Meresman, 2014).

**Parents of children with disabilities do want to be involved.** Khalid and colleagues (2021),
interviewing ten parents from inclusive primary schools in Punjab, report that parents of children
with disabilities were *more* inclined towards inclusive education, while also reporting uncertainty
and dissatisfaction traced to the reactions of other parents, the quality of instruction and
institutional support, with class size and time as barriers. A parent who has concluded the school
cannot help her child looks exactly like a parent who does not care. Virani and Ali (2022) record
teachers themselves naming a stronger parent-teacher relationship as a condition of inclusion working.
The UNICEF booklet names the real barriers - unawareness of alternatives, fear of stigmatisation from
within the community, poverty, no provision near home - and the school-made ones, of which the
sharpest is that **parents are only ever invited when there is a problem**. It also supplies the honest
counterweight: some families do share their community's attitudes to disability or hold mixed views,
so the task is to listen and address the concern rather than to romanticise or dismiss.

The **first-contact protocol** is eight steps and costs nothing: do not rely on a note; find out who
actually raises the child by asking the child; go to them or send a trusted messenger; use the home
language; let the family choose the place; open with something specific the child can do; ask the four
questions and write the answers down; leave with one change each and a channel that does not require
literacy.

The **four questions** are the meeting. What does she do at home that you are proud of? How do you get
her attention, and how does she tell you she has not understood? What has she found hardest about
school? What would you like her to be able to do by the end of the year? In Sahiba's case the second
revealed that a nod with no movement means she has not understood, which her teacher had read as
laziness for seven months, and the third revealed that she lip-reads Sindhi well and Urdu badly, in a
school that teaches in Urdu. Write the answers down for your successor, because a teacher transfers.

**Collaboration is not a handover.** The withdrawal model fails three ways: the child pays in the
subject she misses, the other twenty-nine days are unchanged, and inclusion acquires an owner who is
not the teacher, which is Unit 1's integration. A qualitative review of twelve co-teaching studies
describes collaboration as **co-planning, co-instruction and co-assessment**, working where there is
joint planning, role equity and dedicated collaboration time (Álvarez-Couto et al., 2026); two of
those three cost nothing. The right order for a monthly visit is **watch the class, then plan with the
teacher, then work briefly with the child**.

Treat the **IEP** as a shared working document, not a legal artefact. Section 9 of the Sindh Act
requires individualised support but does not name an IEP form. Local school procedures may still
require records. One page, three columns - what we are trying for, who does what, how we will know
- five lines, held by the **class teacher**, with the next review date written on it before the last
meeting ends.

Where there is no specialist, the team is already in the building: the head teacher, who controls the
register, timetable and rooms; **last year's teacher**, who holds two hundred days of knowledge nobody
has asked for; the Lady Health Worker; the parent; the child; and the class. For what genuinely needs
the state, Unit 5's method stands: write the request, name the pupil, cite the clause, keep the copy,
and teach meanwhile - because for most children in most Pakistani schools, the teaching meanwhile is
the solution.

## Try this at your practicum school

Three tasks. The first is compulsory if you do nothing else in this unit.

1. **Ask one child who looks after her.** Choose a pupil with any additional need. Ask her, in
   ordinary conversation, who looks after her at home and who helps her with school things. Then
   check the register. Write down whether they agree. If your placement allows it, do this for three
   children.

2. **Hold the twenty minutes.** Find the teacher who taught your focus pupil last year and ask for
   twenty minutes: what worked, what failed, where she sat, what she was never asked to do, and what
   her family is like to talk to. Write what you learn and, honestly, how long it took you to arrange.

3. **Make one non-literate channel work.** With the class teacher's permission, establish one way of
   exchanging a message with one family that does not depend on reading, and use it twice. Record what
   you sent, what came back, and what failed.

## Summative task

**Task.** Produce a **home-school communication and support plan** of no more than three pages for one
real pupil with an additional need. Use initials rather than a name, and obtain the class teacher's
permission before you begin.

Your plan must contain:

1. Who actually raises this child, how you established it, and what the register says.
2. The barriers to this family's involvement, named specifically, each marked school-made or not, and
   each with what you propose to do about it.
3. Your first-contact plan: messenger, place, language, your opening sentence about what the child can
   do, and your channel. Then the four questions as you would phrase them in the language you would use.
4. A one-page shared support plan: three columns, five lines at most, named owners, named evidence, and
   a next review date.
5. The part that requires somebody above you, with the written request, the clause you would cite, and
   what you will do in the meantime.
6. An honest paragraph on what this family may not want, and how you would respond if the answer to
   the fourth question is that they would rather the child stayed at home.

**Mini-rubric** (25 marks)

| Criterion | Marks | What a strong response does |
|---|---|---|
| Who raises the child | 3 | Established by asking rather than assumed, with the register checked against it and any difference noted. |
| Barrier analysis *(Analyze)* | 6 | Barriers named concretely, correctly sorted into school-made and not, and traced to a cause rather than to the family's character. Recognises that an apparent lack of interest is a conclusion the family has drawn about the school. |
| First contact and the four questions *(Apply)* | 5 | Messenger, place and language chosen for this family. Opens on a specific capability. All four questions present, phrased as they would really be said, in the home language. |
| The one-page plan | 5 | Three columns, five lines, owners named, evidence cheap and named in advance, review date set. Nothing on it depends on a person who may not come, unless that line is marked as such. |
| Escalation and the meanwhile | 3 | Request names the pupil and the clause; the meanwhile is specific and free, and is treated as the main response rather than a holding action. |
| Honesty about reluctance *(Evaluate)* | 3 | Faces the possibility that the family does not want what you want, without romanticising or writing them off, and proposes listening before persuading. |

The criterion that separates a professional from a well-meaning visitor is the second. Anyone can
list barriers. The teacher who can say which of them her own school built, and name the one she will
remove this month, is the one whose second note gets answered.
