## A real classroom situation

Miss Rubina, from Topic 3.1, has spent three weeks trying things. She has tiered a worksheet. She
has run think-pair-share. She has let two pupils answer orally. Each worked, more or less.

Then she sits down on a Sunday to plan Monday's lesson and finds she cannot do all three at once.
She writes a plan with differentiation in it and forgets the assessment. She writes one with a
cooperative structure and finds the structure has no room for Kainat. She produces a page of good
intentions that does not survive first contact with a Monday.

This is the normal experience, and it is not a failure of effort. Three strategies learned
separately do not assemble themselves. What she is missing is an **order of decisions** - knowing
what to decide first, so that each decision constrains the next instead of competing with it.

## Explanation

### Why the order of decisions matters

Most lesson plans start with the activity. A teacher thinks "I will do a group task on
photosynthesis", and then works out what the pupils should learn from it. That order is why
inclusive planning feels impossible: once the activity is fixed, every pupil who does not fit it
becomes a problem to be handled rather than a learner to be planned for.

Inclusive planning reverses it. **Decide the destination, then the obstacles, then the routes.**
The activity is the fourth decision, not the first.

Six decisions, in this order.

#### Decision 1: the objective

One sentence: what must **every** pupil be able to do at the end?

Two tests. Is it specific enough that you could look at a pupil's work and say yes or no? And is
it a single destination, rather than a range of destinations dressed up as one? "Understand
photosynthesis" fails both. "Explain that green leaves use sunlight, water and carbon dioxide to
make the plant's food and release oxygen" passes both.

Write it down before anything else, and write it where you will see it, because every later
decision is checked against it.

#### Decision 2: the barriers

Name the pupils who will not reach the objective if you teach the lesson the obvious way, and say
what is in the way for each. Not categories - pupils, by name.

For Miss Rubina's Grade 6, from the homework pile in Topic 3.1:

| Pupil | What is in the way |
|---|---|
| Sana | Reads about two years below level; the textbook paragraph is the barrier, not the science |
| Kainat | Already knows it; will finish in five minutes and disengage |
| Fourteen pupils | Copy from the textbook because they do not know what a question is asking for |
| Five pupils | Can draw the process but produce no written account of it |
| Noor | Low vision; cannot read the board from row six |
| Zainab | Home language Sindhi; new to Urdu-medium science vocabulary |

This table is the single most useful thing in the plan, and the step teachers skip. You cannot
remove a barrier you have not named.

#### Decision 3: content and process

Now choose the routes in and the ways of working - the differentiation from Topic 3.1. Each choice
answers a barrier from Decision 2, and nothing is included that does not.

- Sana and the fourteen copiers: the key idea as **one sentence** plus a labelled diagram, and a
  tiered worksheet whose first section supplies a word bank.
- Kainat: the extension question she asked herself - why leaves are green - with the chlorophyll
  answer available when she gets there.
- Noor: moved to row one, and everything written on the board also said aloud.
- Zainab: the four key terms given once in Urdu and once in Sindhi.
- The five who draw but do not write: addressed at Decision 5, because theirs is a product
  barrier, not a content or process one.

That last line matters. Resisting the urge to solve every barrier with differentiation is part of
the skill; some barriers belong to the evidence decision.

#### Decision 4: the cooperative structure

Choose **one** structure to carry the main activity. One, not three; a lesson with three new
structures in it teaches the structures rather than the science.

Pick by asking which exclusion in your Decision 2 table is worst. If the problem is that the same
few pupils answer everything, choose **numbered heads together**. If pupils will not speak,
**think-pair-share**. If the material divides into parts, **jigsaw**. If pupils need feedback
within seconds, **pairs-check**.

For this lesson: numbered heads together, because the Decision 2 table shows fourteen pupils who
do not know what the question is asking - and a bench that must get everyone ready to answer is a
bench where somebody explains the question.

Then build in the two conditions explicitly. **Interdependence:** the bench's answer is whichever
number is called, so one pupil's confusion is everybody's problem. **Accountability:** the number
is called at random and the pupil answers alone.

#### Decision 5: the evidence and the criteria

Decide what each pupil will produce and what you will mark it against. Both halves; the criteria
are what keep the standard still while the format moves.

- Most pupils: the tiered worksheet, section two or three.
- Sana, and the five who draw: a labelled four-box sequence plus an oral explanation to Miss
  Rubina while the class finishes. Same criteria.
- Everyone, as a formative check: an exit slip with one question - "name the three things a leaf
  needs to make food" - read in ninety seconds at the end of the day.

Write the criteria once, for everybody: *names all three inputs (3 marks); names the food made
and the gas released (2 marks); says the leaf must be green or mentions sunlight being captured
(1 mark)*. Six marks, whether the pupil wrote it, drew it or said it.

#### Decision 6: the check

Reread Decision 1. For every pupil in the Decision 2 table, ask: **is she still going to the same
place?**

This is where you catch the quiet failure. If Sana's version has become "name two things a leaf
needs", the objective moved and you have written a modification. Go back to Decision 3 and find a
route rather than a shorter destination.

If the objective holds for every named pupil, the plan is finished.

### The plan itself

Here is Monday's lesson, forty minutes, fifty-one pupils, one blackboard.

**Objective, identical for all 51:** explain that green leaves use sunlight, water and carbon
dioxide to make the plant's food and release oxygen.

**Opening (5 min).** Hold up a leaf and a dead brown leaf. "One of these can make food and one
cannot. Thirty seconds: think why." Then: "Tell the person beside you." Takes two answers.
*Differentiation: the question needs no reading. Zainab and Sana are in from the first minute.*

**Input (10 min).** Draw the diagram on the board, saying every word aloud as it is written. Give
the key sentence twice, in Urdu, and give the four key terms once in Sindhi. Pupils copy the
diagram; the word bank for the labels stays on the board.
*Differentiation: content arrives spoken and drawn, not only written. Noor is in row one.*

**Main activity (15 min).** Three questions, numbered heads together. "Why would a plant in a dark
cupboard die even if you water it?" "A plant is given water and light but sealed in a jar with no
air. What is missing?" "Why is the food not made in the roots?" One minute of bench discussion
each, then a number called.
*Cooperative structure: interdependence through the called number; accountability because the
named pupil answers alone. The fourteen copiers have to say what the question is asking.*

**Evidence (8 min).** Tiered worksheet, three sections, pupils choose where to start and go as far
as they can. Sana and the five non-writers do the four-box sequence and explain it to Miss Rubina
at her desk while the others write. Kainat gets the chlorophyll question.
*Flexible assessment: three products, one set of criteria.*

**Close (2 min).** Exit slip: "Name the three things a leaf needs to make food." All 51.

**Preparation:** 20 minutes the first time (writing the worksheet, listing the Decision 2 table),
about 5 minutes thereafter. **Materials:** one worksheet per bench, or three columns on the board.
**Cost:** one photocopy, or nothing.

Notice what the plan is not. It is not three separate adaptations stapled together. Each strategy
occupies the phase of the lesson it suits: differentiation in the input, cooperative learning in
the main activity, flexible assessment in the evidence. That is why it fits in forty minutes.

### Two things that go wrong when teachers first integrate

**Overloading.** The first integrated plan a teacher writes usually contains two cooperative
structures, four differentiated routes and three assessment formats. It collapses, and the teacher
concludes that integration is impossible. The fix is a ceiling: **one** cooperative structure,
**two or three** differentiated routes, **two** product formats. Fewer, done properly, beats more
attempted.

**Losing the objective.** The other failure is subtler. The plan becomes a beautiful set of
activities and nobody can say what every pupil was supposed to learn. The symptom is a plan where
Decision 1 is vague and Decision 6 was never done. The fix is mechanical: write the objective at
the top of the page in a box, and tick each pupil in your barrier table against it before you
teach.

### Planning for yourself, not for an inspection

A closing word about the form of the plan. The template in fig-U3-6 is one page because a plan you
will not write is worth nothing. If your school requires a longer official format, keep that for
the file and keep your own one-pager for teaching.

What makes a plan inclusive is not its layout. It is that somewhere on it there is a list of named
pupils and what stands in their way, and that every adaptation on the page can be traced back to a
name on that list. A plan with elaborate columns and no named pupils is a form. A plan with six
names and six removed barriers is a lesson.

## Activity: Build your integrated plan

This is the unit's main piece of work. Allow 60 minutes. Use the lesson you differentiated in the
Topic 3.1 activity, so you are building on work you have done rather than starting again.

Work through the six decisions **in order** and write each one down before moving to the next. Do
not skip ahead to the activity, however strongly you want to.

1. **Objective (5 min).** One sentence. Apply both tests: specific enough to judge from a pupil's
   work, and a single destination.
2. **Barriers (15 min).** A table of at least five named pupils from that class and what is in the
   way for each. Push past "weak". This is the step worth the most time.
3. **Content and process (10 min).** Two or three differentiated routes, each answering a named
   barrier. Write the actual words, diagrams or sentence starters.
4. **Structure (5 min).** One cooperative structure. State which exclusion from your table it
   addresses, how interdependence is created, and how individual accountability is created.
5. **Evidence (15 min).** What each pupil produces, and **one** set of criteria with marks, used
   for every format.
6. **Check (10 min).** Go down your barrier table pupil by pupil against the objective. For each,
   write "same objective" or "objective moved". For any that moved, go back to step 3 and find a
   route. Then write a costing: preparation minutes, materials, and what is reusable.

When you have finished, show the plan to one colleague and ask them a single question: *can you
tell me what every pupil was supposed to learn?* If they cannot answer from the page, your
Decision 1 is not doing its job.

## Check your understanding

Answer without looking back, then check.

1. List the six planning decisions in order, and say what goes wrong if you make decision 4
   first.
2. Why is the barrier table the step most worth your time? Give the reason in one sentence.
3. A teacher's plan has think-pair-share, jigsaw and pairs-check in one forty-minute lesson.
   Identify the problem and state the ceiling you would apply.
4. Your plan gives one pupil a shorter version of the objective. Which decision should have
   caught this, and what should you do about it?
5. In the photosynthesis plan, which phase carries the cooperative structure and which carries
   the flexible assessment? Explain why that division keeps the lesson inside forty minutes.
6. A pupil's barrier is that she can draw the process but cannot write about it. At which of the
   six decisions is that barrier properly solved, and why not earlier?
7. What single feature distinguishes an inclusive lesson plan from an official planning form?

## Summary

- Three strategies do not assemble themselves. What integrates them is an **order of decisions**.
- The six decisions: **objective**, **barriers**, **content and process**, **structure**,
  **evidence and criteria**, **check**. The activity is the fourth decision, never the first.
- The **objective** must be specific enough to judge from a pupil's work, and must be one
  destination for everybody.
- The **barrier table** - named pupils, named obstacles - is the step that makes the rest
  possible, and the step most often skipped.
- Differentiation, cooperative learning and flexible assessment each belong to a different
  **phase** of the lesson. That is what makes integration fit in forty minutes rather than
  requiring eighty.
- One set of **criteria** serves every product format. The criteria are what hold the standard
  while the format moves.
- Two failure modes: **overloading** (fix with a ceiling of one structure, two or three routes,
  two formats) and **losing the objective** (fix by checking every named pupil against Decision 1
  before teaching).
- The mark of an inclusive plan is that every adaptation on the page traces back to a named pupil
  with a named barrier.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can list the six planning decisions in order and say why the order matters.
- [ ] I can write an objective that passes both the specificity and single-destination tests.
- [ ] I can build a barrier table of named pupils for a class I actually teach.
- [ ] I can choose one cooperative structure by matching it to the worst exclusion in my table.
- [ ] I can write one set of criteria that serves a written, a drawn and an oral product.
- [ ] I can run Decision 6 and recognise when an adaptation has quietly become a modification.
- [ ] I can produce a one-page integrated plan in about an hour, and cost its preparation.
- [ ] I can explain to a colleague what every pupil in my lesson was supposed to learn.

## Try this at your practicum school

Teach the plan you built in the activity. Then do three things.

**Before the lesson**, give your barrier table to your mentor or a colleague and ask them to watch
two of the named pupils during the lesson - not the class, just those two.

**During the lesson**, note the time each phase actually took against what you planned. The
integrated plan almost always overruns somewhere, and knowing where is how the second one fits.

**After the lesson**, mark the evidence against your single set of criteria, then answer in
writing:

1. Did each named pupil in your barrier table reach the objective? Yes, partly, or no.
2. For any who did not, was the barrier you named the real barrier, or a different one?
3. What did your observer see those two pupils doing that you did not notice while teaching?
4. Which phase overran, and what will you cut next time?

Bring the plan, the marked evidence and your four answers to your next tutorial. Bring the
overrun too; the honest timing is more useful to discuss than a plan that went perfectly.

## Summative task

Produce a complete inclusive lesson plan for a class you teach or have observed, and a commentary
justifying it.

**Part A - the plan (one page).** It must show: the objective; the lesson phases with times; what
the teacher does in each phase; the differentiation in each phase; the cooperative structure and
where it sits; the evidence each pupil produces; and the criteria with marks.

**Part B - the commentary (about 600 words).** It must contain:

1. Your barrier table: at least five named pupils and what is in the way for each.
2. For every adaptation in the plan, the barrier it answers and whether it changes content,
   process or product.
3. Your choice of cooperative structure, with the exclusion it addresses, and the operational
   mechanisms for interdependence and accountability.
4. The single set of criteria, and a sentence on how it keeps the standard identical across the
   different products.
5. Your Decision 6 check: each named pupil against the objective, with any modification
   identified and justified.
6. A costing: preparation time, materials, what is reusable, and one sentence on whether you would
   do this weekly or occasionally, with your reason.

**How this is marked.** Four bands, applied to Parts A and B together.

- **Band 4 (excellent).** The objective is precise, single and visibly identical for every pupil.
  The barrier table names real pupils and real obstacles, not categories or deficits. Every
  adaptation traces to a named barrier and is correctly located in content, process or product.
  One cooperative structure is chosen for a stated reason, with interdependence and accountability
  described operationally. One set of criteria genuinely serves all formats. The Decision 6 check
  is done pupil by pupil, and any modification is justified by showing the accommodations
  rejected. The plan is teachable in the stated time in a real Pakistani classroom, and the
  costing is honest, including what overruns.
- **Band 3 (good).** The objective is sound and holds for all pupils. The barrier table is
  specific for most pupils. Most adaptations trace to a barrier and are correctly located. One
  structure, with accountability addressed. Criteria are shared across formats. The check is done,
  if not pupil by pupil. The plan is realistic and costed.
- **Band 2 (developing).** The objective is present but vague, or drifts for one or two pupils.
  The barrier table lists deficits ("weak in maths", "lazy") rather than obstacles. Adaptations
  are generic, or are good but unconnected to any named pupil. The cooperative element is group
  work without interdependence or accountability. Separate criteria are used for different
  formats, so the standard cannot be shown to be constant. The plan is probably not teachable in
  the time given.
- **Band 1 (not yet).** No usable objective, or a plan that gives the pupils who struggle a
  reduced objective. No barrier table, or one that names no pupils. The three strategies appear as
  labels rather than as decisions affecting what happens in the room.

Two notes on common errors. A plan can be rich in activity and still sit in Band 2 if no
adaptation traces to a named pupil: that is the difference between a creative lesson and an
inclusive one. And using separate criteria for the oral and written products is the most frequent
Band 2 cause, because it makes the claim "the standard was the same" unverifiable - which is
precisely the claim Topic 3.2 said you must be able to defend.
