## A real classroom situation

Mr Ashfaq teaches Grade 8 mathematics in a government boys' school in Larkana. Fifty-eight pupils.
He has read that group work helps weaker pupils, so he tries it.

He tells the class to get into groups of six and solve the five problems on the board together.
The benches are bolted to the floor, so "groups" means six boys on one bench turning towards each
other. He walks round for twenty-five minutes. This is what he sees.

On the first bench, one boy solves all five problems while the other five copy. On the second, two
boys argue about problem one and never reach problem two. On the third, nobody starts; they talk
about a cricket match. On the fourth, the boy who usually finishes first does four problems and
tells the others the answers without working. On the fifth, two boys with reading difficulties
have not understood what the task is and nobody has told them.

At the end, he collects the work. Every bench hands in five correct answers. He has no idea who
learned anything. Four boys did the work of twenty-nine.

Mr Ashfaq concludes that group work does not suit his class.

Mr Ashfaq's conclusion is wrong, but his observation is correct. What he ran was **group work**.
Group work is pupils seated together with one task. It reliably produces exactly what he saw: the
confident do the work, the quiet disappear, and the teacher cannot see individual learning.

Cooperative learning is something else, and the difference is structural rather than a matter of
effort or goodwill.

## Explanation

### Cooperative learning: what makes it cooperative

**Cooperative learning** is group work with a structure that makes two things true at once:

- no pupil can succeed unless the others do (**positive interdependence**); and
- every pupil is individually answerable for what they learned (**individual accountability**).

Those two conditions are what Mr Ashfaq's lesson lacked. The bench could hand in five correct
answers with five boys contributing nothing, so there was no interdependence. And no individual
was ever asked to account for anything, so there was no accountability. Remove those two and you
have pupils sitting near each other.

Three further conditions complete the standard account:

- **face-to-face interaction** - pupils actually talk to each other about the material;
- **social skills are taught, not assumed** - how to disagree, how to ask for help, how to check
  someone understood, taught explicitly, because many pupils have never been shown;
- **group processing** - the group spends a minute afterwards on how it worked, not only on the
  answer.

Vernon, Schumaker and Deshler (2020), working in inclusive elementary classrooms, found that it
was *taught strategy instruction* in cooperative working - rather than simply placing pupils in
groups - that produced social and academic effects. That is the finding to carry into your own
classroom: the structure is the intervention. Seating is not.

#### Why cooperative learning matters specifically for inclusion

Think back to Unit 2's pupils. Cooperative structures do particular jobs for particular excluded
learners.

- The pupil who **will not speak in front of fifty** will often speak to one partner. A structure
  that requires talking in pairs before talking to the class gives her a route in.
- The pupil who is **behind on the content** gets it explained in a peer's words, at a peer's
  pace, with the chance to ask again. Serman and Ningrum (2026) report exactly this use in an
  inclusive primary classroom, with pupils described as slow learners improving on fraction
  addition. It is one small study in one subject, so treat it as an illustration rather than a
  general effect size, but the mechanism is one teachers recognise.
- The pupil whose **home language differs** from the language of instruction can use the home
  language in the pair, then bring the answer into the language of the lesson.
- The pupil who is **ahead** consolidates by explaining, which is harder than answering and more
  useful than fifteen more examples.
- The pupil who is **socially isolated** - the one nobody picks - is given a role by the structure
  rather than by the goodwill of his classmates. This is the effect teachers most often
  underestimate.

Note that none of this happens automatically. A badly structured group harms the isolated pupil,
because it displays his isolation. The structure is what protects him.

#### Four structures that work with fixed benches

These are chosen because they need no floor space, no movement of furniture, and no materials.

**1. Think-pair-share.** Pose a question. Pupils think alone (30 seconds, silent). Then each pupil
turns to the pupil beside her and both explain their answer (one minute). Then you take answers
from the class.

Why it is inclusive: nobody has to produce an answer in public that has not been rehearsed in
private. The 30 seconds of silence is the part teachers cut and the part that matters most - it is
what lets the slow processor arrive with something to say.

In Mr Ashfaq's class: "Here is problem one. Thirty seconds, think how you would start. Do not
write." Then: "Tell the boy next to you how you would start, and listen to his way." Then he takes
three answers. Total: two and a half minutes. Fifty-eight boys have each spoken once.

**2. Jigsaw.** Divide the material into as many parts as there are pupils on a bench. Each pupil
becomes responsible for one part, learns it, and teaches it to the bench. The bench is then tested
on **all** the parts.

Why it is inclusive: positive interdependence is built in - a bench cannot do well if one member's
part is missing - and individual accountability follows because each pupil owns a part.

A caution: jigsaw fails if a pupil's part is beyond her. Assign parts deliberately, not randomly.
The pupil who reads at two years below level gets the part with the diagram.

**3. Numbered heads together.** Pupils on each bench number themselves 1 to 6. Pose a question.
The bench discusses until **every** member can answer it. Then call a number: "Number fours,
stand and answer."

Why it is inclusive: this is the single most efficient fix for Mr Ashfaq's problem. Because
anybody may be called, the bench's interest is in making sure its weakest member understands -
which converts the confident pupil from someone who does the work into someone who explains it.
It also removes the dynamic where the same three hands answer every question for a whole year.

**4. Pairs-check.** Pupils work in pairs on a set of problems. Pupil A solves problem one while
pupil B watches and coaches; then they swap for problem two. After every two problems the pair
compares answers with the next pair.

Why it is inclusive: the pupil who needs immediate feedback gets it in seconds rather than when
the homework comes back three days later. Coaching is also where the misconception surfaces -
pupils say the wrong thing out loud, where you can hear it.

#### What usually goes wrong, and the fix

| What goes wrong | Why | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| One pupil does everything | No individual accountability | Numbered heads: anybody may be called |
| Nobody starts | The task is unclear or too large | Shorter task, and model it once yourself first |
| It becomes a social hour | No time pressure and no output | Name the time and the deliverable before they start |
| The quiet pupil stays quiet | Group of six is a public space | Pairs before groups, always |
| The pupil who is behind is carried | Her part is beyond her | Assign roles deliberately by what each pupil can do |
| Pupils are unkind to each other | Social skills were assumed, not taught | Teach and practise one skill, such as "ask, do not tell" |

### Flexible assessment: measuring the learning, not the disability

The second half of this topic is assessment. The principle is one sentence: **assess the
objective, not the pupil's ability to cope with your chosen format.**

Every assessment has a format, and every format has barriers built in that have nothing to do
with the thing being assessed. A forty-minute written science paper assesses science, and also
reading speed, handwriting speed, Urdu fluency, the ability to sit still for forty minutes, and
the ability to work under time pressure. For most pupils those extra demands are invisible.
For some they are the whole difficulty.

#### Accommodation and modification, applied to assessment

Topic 3.1 introduced the distinction. For assessment it becomes sharp enough to argue about, so
be precise.

- An **accommodation** changes *how* the pupil accesses the assessment or responds to it. **The
  standard is identical.** Extra time, an enlarged paper, a reader, an oral response, a separate
  quiet room, a scribe, splitting a long paper into two sittings.
- A **modification** changes *what* is assessed or to *what standard*. Fewer topics, simpler
  questions, a lower pass mark, excusing a section.

The distinction is what lets you answer the staffroom objection: *"If she gets extra time, that is
unfair on the others."*

The answer has two parts. First, as Unit 1 established, giving everyone the same thing is
equality, not fairness; giving each what they need to show the same learning is equity. Second,
and more concretely: extra time does not help a pupil who does not know the science. It helps a
pupil who knows the science and writes slowly. If a pupil's mark goes up with extra time, the
extra time revealed knowledge that the original format concealed. That is the assessment working
better, not the pupil being advantaged.

#### Common accommodations, and what each is for

| Barrier | Accommodation | What it does not do |
|---|---|---|
| Writes slowly | Extra time; or a scribe; or accept bullet points | Does not supply the content |
| Low vision | Enlarged paper; seat at front; read the paper aloud | Does not simplify the question |
| Cannot decode the question but understands the content | Question read aloud, exactly as written | Does not explain or hint |
| Cannot sustain forty minutes | Two sittings with a break | Does not shorten the paper |
| Home language differs | Key technical terms glossed in both languages | Does not translate the whole paper |
| Severe test anxiety | Familiar room, familiar adult, same paper | Does not reduce the demand |

The right-hand column is the professional discipline of the whole table. An accommodation that
starts explaining the question has become teaching, and the mark no longer means what it says.

#### Alternatives to the standard written test

Accommodations adjust a format. Sometimes the better move is a different format. Four that work
in Pakistani schools without equipment:

- **Oral assessment.** Ask the same questions and record the answers against the same criteria.
  The highest-value adaptation available for a pupil whose barrier is writing, and it costs you
  three minutes per pupil for a short task.
- **Practical or demonstration.** For anything procedural - measuring, using apparatus, a
  mathematical construction - watching the pupil do it is better evidence than reading about it.
- **Portfolio over time.** Several pieces collected across weeks rather than one performance on
  one morning. Particularly fair to pupils whose attendance is irregular because of work or
  family duty, which in many government schools is a large group.
- **Teach it to someone.** Having a pupil teach a concept to a classmate, with you listening,
  reveals depth of understanding that a multiple-choice paper cannot reach.

Two rules make any of these defensible. **Use the same criteria** you would have used for the
written version, written down in advance. And **record what you did**, so that a parent, a head
teacher or an inspector can see the standard was held.

#### Formative assessment is where flexibility is cheapest

Everything above concerns summative assessment, where the stakes and the scrutiny are highest.
But most of a teacher's assessment is **formative** - the checking you do during the lesson to
find out whether to move on. Formative assessment is where flexibility costs nothing at all,
because no mark is recorded and nobody can object.

Three formative techniques that read a class of fifty-eight in under a minute:

- **Thumbs on the bench.** Thumb up, sideways or down for "I could do this alone / with help / not
  yet". Low on the bench, not in the air, so pupils are not performing for each other.
- **Mini-whiteboards, or their free equivalent** - one slate, or the back of an exercise book held
  up. Everyone answers every question, and you see fifty-eight answers at once instead of the
  three from the hands that always go up.
- **Exit question.** One question on a slip of paper as pupils leave. Three piles when you read
  them: got it, nearly, not yet. That tells you what to do tomorrow in the time it takes to walk
  to the staffroom.

What these have in common is that they sample **everyone**. The reason a teacher is surprised by
exam results is almost always that the in-lesson checking only ever heard from the pupils who
volunteered.

#### What flexible assessment is not

Say it plainly, because this misconception costs pupils their qualifications: **flexible
assessment is not easier assessment.** A pupil who gets an oral examination on the same content
against the same criteria has been assessed to the same standard. A pupil who is given a shorter
syllabus has been assessed to a lower one. The first prepares her for the Grade 9 board
examination; the second guarantees she will fail it. If you take only one sentence from this
topic, take that one.

## Activity: Run one structure and one accommodation

Two parts. Part A takes one lesson; Part B takes 20 minutes of planning plus one short assessment.

**Part A - numbered heads together (one lesson).**

1. Choose a class and a topic where you normally ask questions and the same pupils answer.
2. Before the lesson, write four questions on the material, pitched so a bench discussing for a
   minute could answer each.
3. In the lesson, have pupils number off on each bench. Pose question one. Say: "Discuss until
   every single person on your bench can answer. I will call a number." Wait a full minute.
4. Call a number. Take the answer from that number on three or four benches.
5. Repeat for the remaining questions.
6. Immediately afterwards, write down: how many different pupils spoke, compared with a normal
   lesson; and what the pupils who never usually speak did during the discussion minute.

**Part B - one accommodation.**

1. Take a short written assessment you are about to give.
2. Identify one pupil for whom the **format** is a barrier, and name the barrier precisely.
3. Choose one accommodation from the table above. Write down, in advance, the criteria you will
   mark against - the same criteria as for everyone else.
4. Give the assessment with that accommodation in place.
5. Afterwards write three sentences: what you changed, what the pupil's work showed that you had
   not seen before, and whether you can honestly say the standard was the same.

If your answer to the third question is "no", you made a modification. That is not a failure, but
label it correctly and ask what accommodation you could have used instead.

## Check your understanding

Answer without looking back, then check.

1. Name the two conditions that distinguish cooperative learning from group work, and explain
   which one Mr Ashfaq's lesson lacked and how you can tell.
2. A teacher wants every pupil in a class of sixty to answer every question rather than the usual
   three volunteers. Name the structure you would recommend and explain the mechanism that makes
   it work.
3. Explain, in a form you could say to a sceptical colleague in two sentences, why giving one
   pupil extra time in an examination is not unfair to the others.
4. A pupil with dysgraphia is given an oral examination covering the same content, marked against
   the same criteria. Accommodation or modification? Say how you decided.
5. A pupil is given a paper covering four of the six topics the class was examined on.
   Accommodation or modification? Say how you decided.
6. Give one reason jigsaw can fail a pupil who is behind on the content, and the fix.
7. Name one formative technique that samples a whole class of fifty-eight in under a minute, and
   say what it tells you that asking for hands does not.

## Summary

- **Cooperative learning** is not group work. It requires **positive interdependence** (nobody
  succeeds alone) and **individual accountability** (everybody answers for their own learning),
  plus face-to-face interaction, explicitly taught social skills, and group processing.
- The **structure is the intervention**. Vernon et al. (2020) found the effects came from taught
  cooperative strategy, not from seating pupils together.
- Four structures that need no space or materials: **think-pair-share**, **jigsaw**, **numbered
  heads together**, **pairs-check**. Each addresses a specific exclusion: the pupil who will not
  speak publicly, the pupil who contributes nothing, the pupil never called on, the pupil who
  needs immediate feedback.
- Assess the **objective**, not the pupil's ability to cope with your format. Every format carries
  demands unrelated to what is being assessed.
- An **accommodation** changes access and holds the standard. A **modification** changes the
  standard. Keep an accommodation from drifting into explanation.
- Alternatives to the written test - oral, practical, portfolio, teaching it to a peer - are
  defensible when you use the **same criteria**, set in advance, and **record** what you did.
- **Formative** assessment is where flexibility is free. Techniques that sample everyone replace
  the three volunteers whose answers mislead you about the class.
- **Flexible assessment is not easier assessment.** Lowering the syllabus guarantees failure at
  the next real examination.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can state the two defining conditions of cooperative learning and spot their absence.
- [ ] I can explain what each of the four structures does for a specific excluded learner.
- [ ] I can run numbered heads together in my own class without rearranging furniture.
- [ ] I can assign jigsaw roles deliberately so no pupil is given a part beyond her.
- [ ] I can tell an accommodation from a modification in an assessment case and justify it.
- [ ] I can answer the "it is unfair to the others" objection with the equity argument and the
      point about what extra time does and does not supply.
- [ ] I can name two alternatives to a written test and the two rules that keep them defensible.
- [ ] I can name a formative technique that samples every pupil in my largest class.

## Try this at your practicum school

Run **think-pair-share** in one lesson, then count something.

Before the lesson, note how many different pupils normally answer a question in that class. Most
teachers who count are surprised: it is often fewer than six out of fifty.

Teach the lesson using think-pair-share for every question you would normally ask by hands. Keep
the 30 seconds of silence; do not shorten it, however uncomfortable it feels.

Afterwards, write half a page covering: how many different pupils spoke; what the pupils who never
normally speak did in the pair stage; what you heard in the pair stage that you would never have
heard from a volunteer; and whether you got through less material than planned, and whether that
mattered.

Bring your two counts - the normal number and the think-pair-share number - to your next
tutorial.

## Summative task

A Grade 9 class of 54 in a government school is three weeks from a school examination in Pakistan
Studies. The teacher has been lecturing and setting past-paper questions. Results in the last two
class tests: 11 pupils scored above 70 per cent, 22 scored between 40 and 60, 21 scored under 35.
Among the lowest 21 are Ayesha, who reads slowly but answers well orally; Imran, who attends
three days a week because he works; and Noor, who has low vision and sits at the back.

Write a plan, for the three weeks, that uses cooperative learning and flexible assessment to
raise the learning of the lowest group **without lowering what any pupil is examined on.**

Your answer must contain:

1. Two cooperative structures you will use, each with the specific lesson content it will carry,
   and for each one a sentence saying which pupils it is designed to reach and by what mechanism.
2. How you will build **individual accountability** into each structure, in operational terms.
3. One formative technique you will use in every lesson, and what you will do differently
   depending on what it tells you.
4. For each of Ayesha, Imran and Noor: one assessment adaptation, labelled accommodation or
   modification, with the criteria you will mark against.
5. One sentence stating what is identical for all 54 pupils at the end of the three weeks.
6. One risk in your plan and how you will know if it is happening.

**How this is marked.** Four bands, applied to the whole response.

- **Band 4 (excellent).** Both structures are correctly specified and matched to content, with the
  inclusion mechanism named for each. Individual accountability is operational, not asserted - the
  reader can see exactly how a pupil is held to account. The formative technique samples the whole
  class and is tied to a stated decision rule. All three adaptations are accommodations, correctly
  labelled, with criteria held constant and stated; where the response considers a modification it
  explains why it was rejected. The "identical for all 54" statement is about the examined
  standard, and the stated risk is a real one with an observable indicator.
- **Band 3 (good).** Both structures are workable and matched to content; mechanisms named for at
  least one. Accountability is addressed. A whole-class formative technique is named. The three
  adaptations are sound and mostly correctly labelled, with criteria mentioned. The standard is
  held for all pupils. A risk is named.
- **Band 2 (developing).** Structures are named but amount to "group work" without
  interdependence or accountability, or are not matched to specific content. The formative
  technique still samples volunteers. Adaptations are plausible but labels are confused, or
  criteria are not stated, so it is unclear whether the standard moved. Imran's irregular
  attendance is treated as his problem rather than as something the plan must accommodate.
- **Band 1 (not yet).** The plan lowers what the lowest 21 pupils are examined on - a shorter
  syllabus, simpler questions or a lower pass mark - which answers a different question from the
  one asked. Or the plan is extra coaching for the weakest pupils with no cooperative structure
  and no assessment adaptation at all.

Two errors to watch for in your own answer. Giving Ayesha an oral examination is correct, but only
if you write the criteria down first; otherwise you cannot show the standard held. And Imran's
three-day attendance is the case where a portfolio assembled over three weeks is genuinely
stronger than one examination morning - a plan that does not notice this has missed the pupil the
task put in front of it.
