## A real classroom situation

Miss Rubina teaches Grade 6 science in a government girls' school in Hyderabad. Fifty-one pupils,
fixed benches in seven rows, one blackboard, one textbook per bench. Today's lesson is
photosynthesis: how green plants make their own food.

She teaches it the way she was taught. She writes the word *photosynthesis* on the board with the
equation beneath it, explains it for twenty minutes, asks the class to copy the diagram from
page 48, and sets the three textbook questions for homework.

When she marks the homework, this is what she finds.

- Nineteen pupils have answered all three questions correctly.
- Fourteen have copied the answer out of the textbook paragraph, word for word, including a
  sentence that does not answer the question asked.
- Nine have written one line each and stopped.
- Five have drawn the diagram beautifully and written nothing.
- Three have handed in nothing. One of them is Sana, who sits at the back and has never handed
  in written work since March.
- One, Kainat, has written an answer that is correct and goes well beyond the textbook, asking at
  the end why leaves are green and not black if black absorbs more light.

Fifty-one pupils received one lesson. Perhaps nineteen of them received a lesson that fitted
them. Miss Rubina did nothing wrong by the standards she was trained in. But thirty-two pupils
spent forty minutes in a room where learning was available to somebody else.

Miss Rubina's homework pile is not a record of fifty-one different abilities. It is a record of
fifty-one different **barriers**, and most of them are removable. That is what this topic is
about.

## Explanation

### What differentiated instruction is and why it matters for inclusion

**Differentiated instruction** means planning one lesson so that pupils who are not in the same
place can all reach the same objective by different routes.

Read that definition again, because two halves of it do the work and teachers usually remember
only one.

- **Different routes.** Pupils get at the material in different ways, work in different ways, or
  show what they know in different ways.
- **The same objective.** The destination does not move. Everyone is going to the same place.

The second half is what separates differentiation from the thing it is most often confused with.
If Sana is given easier work and a lower expectation, and at the end of term she knows less than
her classmates, that is not differentiation. That is a reduced curriculum with a kind face on it.
Differentiation holds the objective still and moves everything else.

Why does inclusion require it? Because of what Units 1 and 2 established. Inclusion says the
school adapts to the child rather than the child to the school (Unit 1). Unit 2 then showed that
any ordinary Pakistani classroom contains sensory, physical and intellectual disability, learning
difficulties, and social differences of language, gender and poverty. Put those two findings
together and you get an obligation: if the classroom contains a range, and the school must adapt
to the range, then the lesson has to be built for a range. A single undifferentiated lesson is a
decision - usually an unexamined one - to teach the pupils in the middle and let the rest manage.

Florian (2014) makes a point worth holding onto here. The question of whether a classroom is
genuinely inclusive is answered by evidence about what learners actually do and achieve, not by
whether the teacher is busy or well-intentioned. Differentiation is not a virtue you display. It
is a set of decisions you can be wrong about, and the test is whether the pupils reached the
objective.

#### What differentiation is not

Four misconceptions cause most of the damage. Name them now and you will save yourself a term of
wasted effort.

1. **"Differentiation means writing a separate lesson for every child."** It does not, and nobody
   could. You write **one** lesson with a small number of planned variations - usually two or
   three. Fifty-one pupils do not need fifty-one plans; they need one plan with three ways in.
2. **"Differentiation means some pupils do easier work."** Easier work lowers the objective.
   Differentiation keeps the objective and changes the route. The distinction is the whole topic.
3. **"Differentiation is extra work at the point of teaching."** It is extra work at the point of
   **planning**, and it reduces work at the point of teaching, because you stop spending the
   lesson putting out fires. The arithmetic is worked through at the end of this topic.
4. **"Differentiation needs resources we do not have."** Some forms do. Most do not. Of the
   examples in this topic, one needs a photocopy and the rest need nothing but planning.

#### A planning vocabulary: the three "multiple means"

The Universal Design for Learning framework (CAST, 2024) gives a useful habit of mind. Rather
than teaching one way and retrofitting adaptations for the pupils it failed, plan from the start
for multiple means of:

- **engagement** - the reasons pupils have for caring about the lesson;
- **representation** - the forms in which the material reaches them;
- **action and expression** - the ways they can show what they know.

UDL is a framework, not a Pakistani requirement, and nothing in the Sindh curriculum obliges you
to use its vocabulary. Its value is the word **start**. Retrofitting is how differentiation turns
into extra labour; designing for the range at planning time is how it turns into less.

Note also what UDL is not. It is not about technology. The guidelines are about the design of
learning, and the three "multiple means" are as available to Miss Rubina with a blackboard and
chalk as to a teacher with a tablet for every pupil.

### Differentiating content, process and product

There are three things in a lesson you can change. Learning which one a particular barrier calls
for is the practical skill of this topic.

#### Content: the route in

**Content** is the material the pupil meets and the form it arrives in. Differentiating content
means changing **how the material reaches the pupil**, not reducing what they are expected to
learn.

In the photosynthesis lesson, Miss Rubina's content was a spoken explanation plus a textbook
paragraph pitched at Grade 6 reading level. Sana reads at about Grade 4 level. The science is not
beyond her; the paragraph is. Differentiating content for Sana could mean:

- the same information in a labelled diagram she can read, with six words instead of sixty;
- the explanation given to her bench orally while the class copies the diagram;
- the textbook paragraph with the four key words underlined, so she knows where to spend effort;
- a one-sentence version of the idea - "green leaves use sunlight, water and air to make the
  plant's food" - which she can hold while the detail is added.

None of those is easier science. Each is the same science arriving by a different door.

For a pupil with low vision, differentiating content might mean a photocopy of the diagram
enlarged on the school machine, or the simple expedient of moving her to the front and reading
aloud everything written on the board. For a pupil whose home language is Sindhi and who is still
building Urdu, it means giving the key term in both languages once, and accepting an answer that
uses the Sindhi word for "food" while the concept is correct.

For Kainat, who already knows the content, differentiating content means giving her **more**, not
more of the same. Her question about why leaves are green is a better starting point than
anything on page 48, and the honest answer - that chlorophyll reflects green light, and that
there are plants with dark red leaves - is where she should spend the lesson.

#### Process: how the pupil works

**Process** is what the pupil actually does during the lesson: the activity, the grouping, the
time, the amount of support.

Miss Rubina's process was uniform: listen for twenty minutes, copy a diagram, answer three
questions alone. That process suits a pupil who can sustain twenty minutes of listening, writes
fluently, and works well alone. It is hard on the pupil who cannot sit still, the pupil who needs
to say a thing out loud before she can write it, and the pupil who writes slowly.

Ways to differentiate process in a room with fixed benches and no extra adult:

- **Tiered activities.** The whole class works on the same objective at three levels of support.
  On photosynthesis: Tier 1 labels a diagram from a word bank; Tier 2 labels it without the word
  bank and writes one sentence; Tier 3 writes the process in their own words and explains what
  would happen in a dark cupboard. Three versions, one objective, one sheet of paper with three
  sections.
- **Scaffolding.** A temporary support that is removed as it stops being needed - a sentence
  starter ("Plants make food by ..."), a word bank, a partly completed diagram, a worked example
  beside the blank one. The word *temporary* matters. A scaffold that is never removed has become
  a ceiling.
- **Varying time and grouping.** Giving the pupils who need it five more minutes; pairing a pupil
  who struggles to start with one who starts easily; letting a pupil say the answer to a partner
  before writing it.
- **Varying the amount of practice.** Kainat does not need fifteen examples to secure the idea.
  Sana may need more than three. Setting "the first five, then as many of the next five as you
  need" differentiates practice without announcing who is in which group.

#### Product: how the pupil shows what they know

**Product** is the evidence the pupil produces. Differentiating product means accepting different
evidence of the **same** learning.

This is the most neglected of the three in Pakistani classrooms, and the one that most often
makes the difference between a pupil who appears to know nothing and a pupil who knows a good
deal but cannot write it down in forty minutes.

Those five pupils who drew the diagram beautifully and wrote nothing are a product problem. So,
very probably, is Sana. If the only acceptable product is a paragraph of written Urdu, then the
lesson measures written Urdu, and any pupil whose barrier is writing will register as a pupil who
has not learned science.

Alternative products for the photosynthesis objective:

- label a diagram and explain it aloud to the teacher or a partner;
- draw the process as a four-box sequence with two words per box;
- answer the teacher's three questions orally while a partner writes the answers down;
- build the process from cut-out paper arrows on the bench;
- teach it to the pupil on the next bench, who then reports what she was taught.

Every one of those can demonstrate that the pupil knows plants use sunlight, water and carbon
dioxide to make food and release oxygen. That is the objective. The paragraph was never the
objective; it was one way of showing it.


### Accommodation and modification: the distinction that protects the standard

Before the topic closes, one pair of terms you will meet throughout Units 3 to 5, and which is
examined in Topic 3.2 for assessment specifically.

- An **accommodation** changes *how* a pupil accesses learning or shows it. The standard is
  unchanged. Enlarging the diagram, allowing an oral answer, giving extra time: the pupil is
  still expected to know the same science to the same depth.
- A **modification** changes *what* the pupil is expected to learn or to what standard. Asking
  Sana to name only two of the three inputs to photosynthesis when the class must name three is
  a modification.

Both exist legitimately, but they are not interchangeable, and the professional error is to reach
for a modification when an accommodation would have done. That error is how a pupil arrives in
Grade 9 with five years of lowered expectations behind her. The rule of thumb: **try every
accommodation you can think of before you modify the objective**, and when you do modify, record
it and know why.

### Does it work, and what does the evidence actually say?

Be careful here, because this is where teacher-education materials routinely overclaim.

Subandiyah and colleagues (2025) studied differentiated instruction in Indonesian language
lessons in Indonesian schools and reported gains in pupil engagement alongside gains in
achievement. Two things to note about how that study is used here. First, it is about a different
subject in a different country, so it is evidence that the mechanism works somewhere, not
evidence about Grade 6 science in Sindh. Second, and more useful, the finding that *engagement*
moved as well as attainment matches what teachers report: differentiation's first visible effect
is usually not higher marks but more pupils doing something.

What this unit does **not** claim is a number. You will see figures quoted for the effect of
differentiation in training materials; treat them with suspicion unless the study is named and
the setting resembles yours. What you can rely on is the argument from Units 1 and 2, which does
not need an effect size: a class contains a range, a single uniform lesson is addressed to part
of that range, and the pupils outside it are not learning. Differentiation is the response to a
fact about your classroom that you can verify yourself by marking one set of homework.

### The time objection, honestly answered

Miss Rubina's real question is not whether differentiation is good. It is whether she can afford
it with fifty-one pupils and five periods a day.

Here is the arithmetic for the photosynthesis lesson, differentiated as described above.

**Extra planning time, first time:** about 25 minutes. Deciding the objective precisely (5 min),
listing who will struggle and why (5 min), writing a three-tier worksheet instead of using the
textbook questions (15 min).

**Extra planning time, next year:** about 3 minutes, because the worksheet exists.

**Extra teaching time:** none. The lesson is the same forty minutes.

**Extra materials:** one photocopy per bench, or none if the three tiers are written on the board
in three columns.

**Time saved:** the fourteen pupils who copied the textbook answer verbatim produced work Miss
Rubina had to mark and which taught her nothing. The three who handed in nothing cost her a
follow-up conversation each.

The honest summary is that differentiation costs planning time once, and the cost falls steeply
on repetition. It is not free. It is much cheaper than the alternative, which is reteaching a
unit that two-thirds of the class did not acquire the first time.

## Activity: Differentiate one lesson three ways

Work on a lesson **you have actually taught**, not a hypothetical one. Allow 40 minutes. You need
your own lesson plan or textbook page, and paper.

**Step 1 - Fix the objective (5 minutes).** Write one sentence beginning "By the end of this
lesson every pupil will be able to ...". Be specific enough that you could tell from a pupil's
work whether she got there. "Understand fractions" fails this test; "add two fractions with the
same denominator" passes it.

**Step 2 - Name three pupils (5 minutes).** Not categories, three real pupils from that class.
For each, write one sentence saying what stands between her and the objective. Push past "she is
weak". *Why* is she weak here? She cannot read the question; she cannot hold two steps in her
head; she will not start; she finishes in four minutes and then disrupts.

**Step 3 - Decide which of the three to change (10 minutes).** For each pupil, decide whether her
barrier is content, process or product, and write the reason. Use fig-U3-1 if you get stuck. A
barrier can involve two; pick the one that is most in the way.

**Step 4 - Write the variation (15 minutes).** For each pupil, write the actual variation - the
sentence starter, the enlarged diagram, the oral answer, the extension question. Write the words
you would really use, not a description of the kind of thing you might do. If your variation
cannot be prepared in ten minutes with the materials in your school, replace it with one that
can.

**Step 5 - Check the destination (5 minutes).** Reread your objective from Step 1. For each of
the three pupils, ask: *is she still going to the same place?* If any variation has quietly
lowered the objective, you have written a modification. Mark it as one, then try to replace it
with an accommodation that keeps the objective whole.

Keep this page. You will use it again in Topic 3.3.

## Check your understanding

Answer without looking back, then check.

1. Write the definition of differentiated instruction in one sentence, making sure your sentence
   contains both halves of it.
2. A teacher gives a pupil with dyslexia a shorter spelling list from the same word family, while
   the class learns twenty words. Is this an accommodation or a modification? Say how you decided.
3. A pupil understands the science but writes four lines in the time others write a page. Which
   of content, process or product is most in the way, and give one variation you would try first.
4. Name the three "multiple means" in the UDL framework, and say which one a word bank serves.
5. A colleague says, "I differentiate - I give the clever ones harder questions and the weak ones
   easier ones." Identify what is wrong with this as a description of differentiation.
6. Give one reason differentiation gets cheaper the second time you teach a lesson.

## Summary

- **Differentiated instruction** means one lesson planned so that pupils in different places
  reach **the same objective** by **different routes**. Both halves matter; dropping the first
  gives you a uniform lesson, dropping the second gives you a reduced curriculum.
- Inclusion requires it because the classroom contains a range (Unit 2) and the school must adapt
  to the range (Unit 1). A single uniform lesson is a decision to teach part of the class.
- You can differentiate three things: **content** (the route in), **process** (how the pupil
  works) and **product** (how she shows what she knows). Diagnose which one the barrier sits in
  before choosing a variation.
- **Product** is the most neglected in Pakistani classrooms. Where written Urdu is the only
  accepted evidence, the lesson measures written Urdu.
- An **accommodation** changes access and keeps the standard; a **modification** changes the
  standard. Exhaust accommodations first, and record any modification you make.
- Useful tools: tiered activities, scaffolding that is removed, varied grouping and time, varied
  practice. Most cost planning time rather than money.
- The evidence base supports the mechanism; this unit claims no effect size, and you should
  distrust any training material that quotes one without naming the study and the setting.

## Try this at your practicum school

Take one class you teach and mark one set of written work with a different question in mind. Do
not mark for correctness. Sort the work into piles by **what got in the way**: could not read the
question; understood but could not write it; finished early and did too little; did not start.

Count the piles. Then answer two questions in writing:

1. Which pile is largest, and is its barrier content, process or product?
2. What single variation, preparable in ten minutes, would move most of that pile?

Try that one variation in your next lesson with that class. Afterwards, write three sentences:
what you changed, what the pupils in that pile did differently, and whether the objective stayed
where it was. Bring the marked pile count and your three sentences to your next tutorial.

## Summative task

A Grade 7 mathematics class of 48 pupils in a government school is beginning "area of a
rectangle". The teacher's objective is that every pupil can calculate the area of a rectangle
given its length and width. The class contains:

- **Hina**, who reads two years below her grade and cannot get started on a worded question;
- **Bilal**, who has the multiplication facts but writes very slowly;
- **Sadia**, who has low vision and sits at the back;
- **Zoya**, who can already do it and finishes in three minutes.

Produce a differentiation plan. Your answer must contain:

1. The objective, written so a marker could tell from a pupil's work whether it was met.
2. For each of the four pupils: which of content, process or product you are changing, the
   specific variation in the words you would use or the materials you would make, and one
   sentence of justification.
3. One statement of what stays identical for all four.
4. For each variation, a label: accommodation or modification. If any is a modification, explain
   why no accommodation would have served.
5. A costing: preparation time in minutes and materials needed, with a note on what is reusable.

**How this is marked.** Four bands, applied to the whole response.

- **Band 4 (excellent).** The objective is precise and testable. All four pupils get a variation
  that is specific enough to use tomorrow and correctly located in content, process or product.
  The objective is visibly identical for all four. Accommodation and modification are used
  correctly, and any modification is justified by showing which accommodations were considered
  and rejected. The costing is realistic for a Pakistani government school and identifies what is
  reusable.
- **Band 3 (good).** The objective is testable. All four pupils get a workable variation,
  correctly located in most cases. The objective holds for all four. The
  accommodation/modification labels are mostly right. Costing is present and broadly plausible.
- **Band 2 (developing).** Variations are offered for all four but some are generic ("give extra
  attention", "simplify the work") rather than specific, or are located in the wrong one of the
  three. The objective may drift for one pupil - typically Hina gets easier mathematics rather
  than a different route into the same mathematics. Labels are applied inconsistently.
- **Band 1 (not yet).** Variations amount to easier work for the pupils who struggle and more of
  the same for Zoya. The objective moves, which means the response has described a reduced
  curriculum rather than differentiation. Costing absent.

The most common Band 2 error is worth naming in advance: handling Hina by giving her rectangles
with smaller numbers. Smaller numbers do not address her barrier, which is getting into a worded
question. They lower the mathematics while leaving the reading problem exactly where it was.
