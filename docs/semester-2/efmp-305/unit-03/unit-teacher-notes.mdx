# Teacher notes: Unit 3

These notes are for the teacher educator delivering this unit to B.Ed trainees. They are not part
of the learner-facing material.

## What this unit is really teaching

The content is three strategies, but the capability is **diagnosis**. A trainee who leaves this
unit able to recite the difference between content, process and product has learned the
vocabulary. A trainee who can look at a pupil's work and say "her barrier is product, so a
tiered worksheet will not help her" has learned the unit.

Weight your teaching accordingly. The definitions take ten minutes. The diagnosis takes the whole
unit, and it is built almost entirely by the activities.

## Sequencing across the three weeks

The guide allocates three weeks (Weeks 5 to 7). A workable division:

- **Week 5 - Topic 3.1.** Open with the homework pile. Do not define differentiation first; put
  the fifty-one pupils' responses on the board and ask the trainees what happened. They will
  arrive at the content/process/product distinction themselves, which is worth far more than
  receiving it. Then name it.
- **Week 6 - Topic 3.2.** Open with Mr Ashfaq's failed group work. Most trainees will have
  experienced exactly this, either as teacher or as pupil, and will defend the conclusion that
  group work does not work in large classes. Let them. Then run **numbered heads together on the
  trainees themselves**, with the material being the five conditions. The experience of being
  unable to hide is the lesson.
- **Week 7 - Topic 3.3.** Mostly workshop. The six decisions take twenty minutes to explain and
  the rest of the week is trainees building their own plan with you circulating. Resist the urge
  to lecture; there is nothing left to tell them that they cannot find by writing a plan badly
  once.

If you have only two weeks because of timetable loss, merge 3.1 and 3.2 input and protect the
Topic 3.3 workshop. The integration is the part they cannot do alone.

## Where trainees reliably go wrong

Six errors, in rough order of frequency. Anticipating them is most of the teaching.

1. **Easier work as differentiation.** By far the most common, and it survives being told. A
   trainee writes a plan in which the pupil who struggles does simpler mathematics, and sincerely
   believes this is differentiation. The intervention that works is not repetition of the
   definition but the Decision 6 check applied to their own plan in front of them: "Read your
   objective. Now read what Hina is doing. Is she going to the same place?" Let them see it
   themselves.
2. **Barriers written as deficits.** The barrier table fills up with "weak", "lazy", "poor
   background", "no interest from parents". None of these is a barrier you can remove. Push every
   time: *weak at what, exactly? What happens when she tries to start?* This is also where Unit
   2's labelling material pays off; remind them of it explicitly.
3. **Group work relabelled.** Trainees will present six pupils on a bench with one worksheet as
   cooperative learning. Ask the two diagnostic questions: can the bench finish this without one
   named pupil? And at the end, how will you know what that pupil learned? If the answers are yes
   and I will not, it is group work.
4. **Overloading the integrated plan.** Almost every first integrated plan has three structures
   and four assessment formats. Do not mark it down before they have taught it; let the lesson
   overrun and then discuss. The ceiling rule lands much harder after the experience than before.
5. **Separate criteria for different products.** A trainee allows an oral answer and then marks it
   impressionistically. This silently destroys the claim that the standard held, and it is the
   error with the most serious long-term consequence, because it is what makes a head teacher
   right to be suspicious. Insist on written criteria **before** the assessment, every time.
6. **Treating UDL as technology.** If your trainees have met UDL elsewhere they may assume it
   requires devices. Say plainly that every example in this unit works with chalk and a
   blackboard.

## Running the activities with large trainee groups

All three activities assume the trainee has a class of their own, or at least one they have
observed. If some do not - common in the first practicum - pair them with someone who does rather
than letting them invent a hypothetical class. Invented pupils have invented barriers, and the
diagnosis skill does not develop.

The Topic 3.3 activity needs a full hour of protected time and produces the unit's best artefact.
If you can only protect one hour in the three weeks, protect that one.

For the "Try this at your practicum school" tasks, the counting is the point. Trainees who report
impressions ("it went well, the pupils were engaged") have not done the task. Ask for the two
numbers: how many pupils normally answer, and how many answered with think-pair-share. The
contrast does the persuading.

## Marking the summative tasks

Three summative tasks across the topics, plus the unit assessment. They are deliberately
cumulative: Topic 3.1's task is one lesson differentiated, Topic 3.2's adds structures and
assessment over three weeks, Topic 3.3's is the full integrated plan. A trainee who did 3.1
properly finds 3.3 much easier, which is worth telling them at the start.

When marking, apply the bands to the **whole** response rather than averaging criteria. The
single most useful discriminator between Band 3 and Band 4 is whether every adaptation traces to
a named pupil with a named barrier. A creative, well-presented plan with no named pupils is a
Band 2, however impressive it looks, and saying so early in the unit saves a great deal of
disappointment later.

For ERQ 3 and the Topic 3.3 task, mark the **Decision 6 check** carefully. A trainee who has
honestly identified one of their own adaptations as a modification and explained why no
accommodation would serve has demonstrated more than a trainee whose plan has no modifications
because they did not look.

## Answers and keys

The learner-facing assessment carries its own bounded answers and marking guidance in its final
section. Nothing additional is held here. If you set the unit assessment under examination
conditions, note that the MCQ key is visible to learners in the published material, so use the
RRQ and ERQ sections for anything summative that matters.
