## Unit summary

This unit, which corresponds to Chapter 11 of the course guide, examined how
mathematical models allow educators and administrators to reason systematically
about uncertainty.

- Topic 11.1 established the distinction between deterministic and probabilistic
  models. A model is a simplified mathematical representation of a real-world
  system. Deterministic models produce a single fixed output from given inputs
  (such as stationery procurement costs). Probabilistic models incorporate
  random variation and output a probability distribution (such as attendance
  patterns or examination results). The topic detailed two foundational
  probabilistic models: the discrete binomial model (counting successes in
  fixed, independent binary trials) and the continuous normal model (a
  symmetric, bell-shaped distribution governed by the empirical rule).
- Topic 11.2 explored real-life educational applications and critical assumptions.
  The binomial model describes blind guessing on multiple-choice tests, showing
  how to calculate guessing probabilities and check test design alongside
  learning goals. A low guessing probability alone does not establish test
  validity. The normal model estimates group counts at chosen thresholds
  (μ ± 1σ, μ ± 2σ) when it fits, without diagnosing individual needs. Finally, the topic emphasized checking underlying
  assumptions: violations of independence (e.g. correlated absences) or
  normality (e.g. skewed classroom test marks) compromise model validity.

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

All school scenarios and data in this assessment are invented. Choose the single
best answer for each question. Each item carries one mark.

1. *(Remember)* What does a mathematical model represent?
   - A) Every recorded detail of a situation
   - B) Selected features and relationships of a situation
   - C) Only the most recent observed outcome
   - D) Only the names of the people involved

2. *(Apply)* A head teacher calculates the total examination fee for a school
   using the formula: Total fees = 150 candidates × Rs 800 per candidate =
   Rs 120,000. Why is this calculation classified as a deterministic model?
   - A) Because it involves a large monetary amount
   - B) Because the inputs determine a single, certain output with no chance
     variation
   - C) Because candidate attendance varies on the day of the exam
   - D) Because the fee was set by a provincial education board

3. *(Understand)* Which statement best describes the difference
   between deterministic and probabilistic models?
   - A) Deterministic models use fractions, whereas probabilistic models use only
     whole numbers
   - B) Deterministic models apply to humans, whereas probabilistic models apply
     only to machines
   - C) Deterministic models predict a single exact outcome, whereas probabilistic
     models output a probability distribution over possible outcomes
   - D) Deterministic models are always theoretical, whereas probabilistic models
     are always empirical

4. *(Remember)* Which of the following is a mandatory condition for applying the
   binomial probability model?
   - A) The variable must be continuous and follow a bell curve
   - B) There must be fixed independent trials, two outcomes and constant
     success probability
   - C) The trials must be dependent on previous outcomes
   - D) The mean and median of the distribution must be equal to zero

5. *(Apply)* A teacher gives an unannounced 10-item true/false quiz. A pupil who
   did not prepare guesses randomly on every item. Under the binomial model, what
   is the pupil's expected score?
   - A) 1 mark
   - B) 2.5 marks
   - C) 5 marks
   - D) 7.5 marks

6. *(Remember)* On a standard multiple-choice question with four choices and
   exactly one right option, what is the probability of selecting the right option
   by pure random guessing?
   - A) 0.10
   - B) 0.25
   - C) 0.50
   - D) 0.75

7. *(Apply)* A monthly assessment consists of 20 multiple-choice questions with
   four options each. If a student guesses randomly on all items, what is the
   expected score, and why?
   - A) 2 marks, because guessing yields roughly 10%
   - B) 5 marks, because expected value is n × p = 20 × 0.25
   - C) 10 marks, because students have a 50% chance of passing
   - D) 8 marks, because the passing mark is 40%

8. *(Apply)* In a large district literacy survey of 5,000 pupils, scores are
   normally distributed with a mean of μ = 55 and a standard deviation of σ = 10.
   Using the empirical rule, what score range contains approximately 95% of all
   pupils?
   - A) 45 to 65
   - B) 35 to 75
   - C) 25 to 85
   - D) 50 to 60

9. *(Understand)* A researcher uses a binomial distribution to model daily pupil
   absences in a rural school. Which scenario represents a serious violation of
   the model's independence assumption?
   - A) Pupils in the school come from various different age groups
   - B) Heavy monsoon rains flood the only access bridge, causing an entire
     hamlet of 15 children to be absent on the same day
   - C) Some pupils are enrolled in Grade 3 while others are enrolled in Grade 5
   - D) The teacher takes attendance at 8:30 AM every morning

10. *(Understand)* A teacher administers an easy 50-mark mastery quiz where 85%
    of the class scores 50 marks and the rest score below 20 marks. Why is it inappropriate to model
    these results using a normal distribution?
    - A) Because the data are negatively skewed with a ceiling effect, violating
      the requirement of symmetry
    - B) Because normal curves cannot be applied to tests out of 50 marks
    - C) Because the sample contains both male and female pupils
    - D) Because the mean score is higher than the standard deviation

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

Answer each question in two to five sentences, or a short labelled calculation.
Use the rounded empirical rule for normal-model estimates.

1. *(Understand)* Define what a mathematical model is in statistics, and state
   two reasons why models are valuable in educational decision-making.

2. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between a deterministic model
   and a probabilistic model, providing one original school-based example of
   each.

3. *(Understand)* Explain in two sentences why a probabilistic model assigns
   probabilities to possible outcomes instead of promising one certain outcome.

4. *(Understand)* State the four conditions required for a random variable to
   follow a binomial distribution.

5. *(Apply)* A teacher designs a 20-item true/false test. Assuming a pupil guesses
   randomly on every item (p = 0.5), calculate the expected score and the
   variance of the score.

6. *(Understand)* State the empirical rule (the 68 - 95 - 99.7 rule) for a normal
   distribution, explaining what percentage of data falls within one, two, and
   three standard deviations of the mean.

7. *(Apply)* A head teacher wants to design a 10-item multiple-choice test with 4
   options per question. Use `P(X ≥ 3) ≈ 47.4%` and `P(X ≥ 5) ≈ 7.8%`
   to explain why a threshold of three gives weak protection against guessing.
   Give one reason why five alone would not prove mastery.

8. *(Analyze)* Explain why an outbreak of viral conjunctivitis (pink eye) in a
   boarding hostel violates the binomial distribution assumption of independent
   trials when modeling student absences.

9. *(Analyze)* A school principal insists on "grading on a curve" for a small
   class of 15 pupils, requiring that exactly 2 pupils receive an A grade, 11
   receive average grades, and 2 receive failing grades. Analyze why forcing a
   small classroom sample into a normal distribution is unsupported by the evidence.

10. *(Analyze)* On a 4-option multiple-choice test, a poorly written question
    contains two distractors that are obviously absurd. Analyze how this flaw
    alters the probability of success for a student who guesses, and explain the
    consequence for the test's validity.

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

Answer each question in a structured paragraph or set of paragraphs. For each
rubric criterion, award 5–6 for the full descriptor, 3–4 for partial evidence,
1–2 for limited evidence and 0 for absent or incorrect evidence. Award the upper
mark when the explanation meets the descriptor fully; otherwise use the lower
mark. Each ERQ has two criteria and a maximum of 12 marks.

1. *(Analyze)* A school management committee in Badin is preparing the annual
   operational plan for a government secondary school with 300 registered pupils.
   The committee must address two tasks:
   - Task A: Ordering annual examination stationery (each pupil receives two
     examination booklets at Rs 40 per booklet).
   - Task B: Budgeting for the school's emergency health clinic, where past
     records show that roughly 4% of pupils experience minor injuries or illness
     during school hours each month, each costing Rs 300 in first-aid supplies.
   
   Classify each task as deterministic or probabilistic, providing full
   justification. Analyze the risks of treating Task B as a rigid deterministic
   calculation, and explain how a probabilistic approach protects school
   operations. (Write 140 to 200 words.)

2. *(Analyze)* A Grade 10 student claims to have guessed on all 20 questions of a
   4-option multiple-choice chemistry test and achieved a score of 15 out of 20
   (75%).
   
   Assume independent, uniform choices. You may use the calculated binomial
   tail probability `P(X ≥ 15) ≈ 0.00000381`.

   Using the binomial probability model:
   - State the expected score for pure guessing on this test.
   - Analyze the statistical likelihood of achieving 15 or more correct answers by
     random guessing alone.
   - Provide an evidence-based conclusion regarding the student's claim,
     identifying alternative explanations (such as partial knowledge or flawed
     distractors), and explain why rarity alone does not prove dishonesty. (Write 140 to 200 words.)

3. *(Evaluate)* The Sindh Education Department administers a standardized
   mathematics screening test to 8,000 Grade 8 students. For this exercise, scores are approximately normally
   distributed with a mean of μ = 60 marks and a standard deviation of σ = 10
   marks.
   - The department plans to award merit scholarships to students scoring above
     μ + 2σ. Determine the cut-off score and calculate the expected number of
     scholarship recipients.
   - The department also plans to assign students scoring below μ - 1σ to
     mandatory after-school tutoring. Determine the cut-off score and estimate
     how many students would fall below that threshold.
   - Evaluate the strengths and limitations of using fixed standard deviation
     thresholds to distribute educational resources across diverse rural and
     urban schools. (Write 150 to 220 words.)

4. *(Evaluate)* An education non-governmental organization (NGO) develops a
   probabilistic risk model to identify primary school pupils at risk of dropping
   out in rural Sindh. The model calculates a "dropout probability score" based
   on four indicators: distance from school, household income, past attendance,
   and older sibling school completion.
   
   A local head teacher rejects the model, arguing: "No formula can tell me what
   a child will do. Last year, a boy with a 90% predicted dropout probability
   completed the year with top marks, so the model is useless."
   
   Evaluate both the head teacher's objection and the NGO's probabilistic model.
   In your response, explain the proper role of probabilistic models in social
   policy, address the misconception regarding individual prediction versus
   group probability, and suggest guidelines for how school leaders should use
   risk models ethically. (Write 160 to 220 words.)

5. *(Evaluate)* At a private high school, the principal requires all teachers to
   adjust their term test marks so that every class has a mean of 60% and a
   symmetric bell curve (normal distribution). In a physics class of 28 students,
   an exceptionally dedicated teacher provided extensive remedial instruction,
   resulting in 22 students scoring above 80% on a rigorous criterion-referenced
   exam. The principal orders the teacher to rescale the marks downwards to
   restore a normal curve.
   
   Evaluate the principal's policy from a measurement and ethical perspective.
   Analyze the difference between norm-referenced and criterion-referenced
   assessment, explain why successful mastery teaching can produce skewed
   distributions, and argue why imposing a normal model in this situation
   undermines both teaching effectiveness and student motivation. (Write 160 to
   220 words.)

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. Model answer: A mathematical model is a simplified formal representation of a
   real-world process or relationship. In education, models are valuable because
   they allow administrators to quantify uncertainty, forecast resource needs
   (such as seating and staffing), and evaluate student performance against
   theoretical expectations.
   **Marks (2):** 1 mark for an accurate definition of a model; 0.5 marks each for two
   valid educational reasons (forecasting, planning, evaluating).

2. Model answer: A deterministic model predicts an exact, unvarying outcome from
   known inputs (e.g. Total notebook cost = 40 pupils × Rs 60 = Rs 2,400). A
   probabilistic model incorporates chance variation and predicts a range of
   outcomes with associated probabilities (e.g. predicting daily attendance where
   a class of 40 has an expected 36 present, but actual numbers fluctuate between
   32 and 40).
   **Marks (3):** 1 mark for the core distinction (exact vs distribution); 1
   mark for a valid deterministic school example; 1 mark for a valid
   probabilistic school example.

3. Model answer: Human behavior and educational settings are subject to numerous
   unobserved, random influences (health, weather, motivation, transport).
   A probabilistic model assigns probabilities to possible individual or group
   outcomes. It represents uncertainty rather than promising one exact result.
   **Marks (2):** 1 mark for identifying unobserved chance factors in human
   settings; 1 mark for explaining that probability distributions represent
   uncertainty about outcomes.

4. Model answer: The four conditions are: (1) a fixed number of trials (n); (2)
   exactly two mutually exclusive outcomes per trial (success/failure); (3) a
   constant probability of success (p) on every trial; and (4) independent
   trials, where one outcome does not affect another.
   **Marks (2):** 0.5 marks for each of the four correctly identified conditions.

5. Model answer: For a 20-item true/false test with random guessing, n = 20 and
   p = 0.5 (q = 0.5).
   Expected value: E(X) = n × p = 20 × 0.5 = 10 marks.
   Variance: Var(X) = n × p × (1 - p) = 20 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 5.
   **Marks (2):** 1 mark for the correct expected value (10); 1 mark for the
   correct variance (5) with formula shown.

6. Model answer: The empirical rule states that for a symmetric, bell-shaped
   normal distribution: approximately 68% of values lie within 1 standard
   deviation of the mean (μ ± 1σ); approximately 95% lie within 2 standard
   deviations (μ ± 2σ); and approximately 99.7% lie within 3 standard
   deviations (μ ± 3σ).
   **Marks (3):** 1 mark for 68% at 1 SD; 1 mark for 95% at 2 SD; 1 mark for
   99.7% at 3 SD.

7. Model answer: On a 10-item 4-option test, random guessing has an expected score
   of 10 × 0.25 = 2.5. A passing score of 3 is so close to 2.5 that about 47.4% of
   pupils who guess blindly would pass by chance. At five, the chance drops to about 7.8%, but a
   passing standard must also reflect learning goals. Five marks alone do not
   establish that the pupil has mastered the intended skills.
   **Marks (2):** 1 mark for explaining why 3 is too close to the expected
   guessing score (2.5); 1 mark for explaining that a higher threshold alone does not
   establish mastery.

8. Model answer: A viral infection is contagious and spreads through direct
   contact among pupils sharing hostel rooms or dining tables. As a result, when
   one pupil falls ill, nearby pupils are significantly more likely to fall ill,
   creating clustered absences. This violates the assumption of independence,
   which requires that one pupil's absence has zero impact on another's.
   **Marks (2):** 1 mark for identifying contagion as a cause of clustering; 1
   mark for connecting clustering to the violation of the independence assumption.

9. Model answer: Forcing a small group of 15 pupils into a fixed bell
   curve is flawed because: (1) a small sample gives limited evidence about a population
   distribution, and (2) there is no evidence here that achievement follows a
   normal curve. A normal population model can generate small samples, but it
   does not require class grades to follow fixed quotas. Imposing failures can
   penalize pupils who meet learning goals.
   **Marks (2):** 1 mark for citing small sample instability; 1 mark for
   explaining the injustice of penalizing mastery under artificial norming.

10. Model answer: When two absurd options are eliminated, the student guesses
    between only 2 plausible options. The probability of guessing correctly rises
    from p = 0.25 to p = 0.50. On a 10-item test, the expected guessing score
    jumps from 2.5 to 5.0 marks, increasing the chance of a high score through option elimination. This
    weakens the model assumption that every choice has p = 0.25.
    **Marks (2):** 1 mark for calculating the new guessing probability (0.50); 1
    mark for explaining the threat to test validity.
