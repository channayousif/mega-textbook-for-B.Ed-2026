## A real classroom situation

In a fictional Nawabshah government high school, a pupil scores five on a
20-item four-option science test and says every choice was a guess. The teacher
explains that independent, equally likely choices give an expected score of
`20 × 0.25 = 5`. The pupil asks whether a guesser could reach the pass mark of
ten. The teacher investigates that possibility with a binomial model. She also
asks whether the choices really were independent and equally likely.

## Explanation

### Real-life application 1: guessing on multiple-choice tests

A guessing model describes a narrow process, not a pupil's learning. Assume
one right option per item, uniform random choice among four options, independent
choices and one mark per right response. Then p = 0.25 on every item. These
conditions connect the test to the binomial distribution (Lane, 2023, Chapter 5;
Illowsky & Dean, 2023, Section 4.3).

**Worked example:** For ten items, `E(X) = 10 × 0.25 = 2.5`. The most likely
whole-number score is two. To find the chance of passing at five, add every
passing score's probability:

`P(X ≥ 5) = P(X = 5) + P(X = 6) + ... + P(X = 10)`.

The first term is `10C5 × 0.25^5 × 0.75^5 ≈ 0.0584`. Adding the remaining
terms gives about 0.0781, or 7.8%. This means roughly eight passes per 100
comparable tests across many repetitions, not exactly eight in every batch.
The bar for five alone is about 5.8%; it is not the probability of five or more.

| Pass mark on ten items | Probability of reaching it by blind guessing |
|---|---|
| Three | About 47.4% |
| Five | About 7.8% |
| Six | About 2.0% |

The threshold of three gives weak protection against guessing. However, a higher
threshold alone does not establish mastery. Set standards from learning goals,
then use these probabilities as one check on test design. At a fixed passing
percentage above 25%, a longer independent four-option test usually reduces
blind-guessing risk. For twenty items and a threshold of ten, the probability
is about 1.39%. These are calculated probabilities, not observed pass rates.

A rare result does not prove dishonesty. If a pupil claims to have guessed
15 of 20 correctly, `P(X ≥ 15) ≈ 0.00000381` under the stated model. That
result is extremely rare but possible. Partial knowledge, clues in options or
other departures from uniform guessing are also possibilities. Check the
assumptions and seek further evidence before making a judgement about a pupil.

### Real-life application 2: diagnostic assessment and the empirical rule

A threshold is a score used to select a group for a stated purpose. A planner
might estimate how many pupils fall below one or two standard deviations from
the mean before budgeting for a follow-up reading check. The empirical rule
provides approximate normal-curve areas for this calculation (Illowsky & Dean,
2023, Section 6.1).

**Worked planning example:** In an invented provincial exercise, suppose
10,000 Grade 5 literacy scores are approximately normal with μ = 50 and σ = 12.
The middle 68% is roughly between `50 - 12 = 38` and `50 + 12 = 62`.
A proposed reading-kit rule selects scores below two standard deviations from
the mean. Its cut-off is `50 - 2 × 12 = 26`.

The rounded empirical rule puts about 2.5% below this cut-off:
`10,000 × 0.025 = 250` kits. The more precise normal lower-tail area is about
2.28%, so this rounded rule is unsuitable for a precise order. Actual counts
can differ because of model error and variation. Check real eligibility counts
where available and plan a reserve before ordering.

A low score relative to peers does not diagnose a learning difficulty. A high
score alone does not identify giftedness. Support decisions need evidence of
what a pupil can do, language access, teaching opportunities and follow-up
assessment. These are invented planning thresholds, not Sindh policy or clinical
criteria. Even a well-fitting curve cannot decide which learning goals matter.

### When models fail: checking underlying assumptions

An assumption is a condition the model treats as true. Before applying a model,
name what is being predicted, check its conditions and compare predictions with
observations. Use the binomial conditions from Topic 11.1 rather than choosing
that model just because the outcome is a count.

**Worked attendance check:** A Badin school has 100 pupils, each with an estimated
attendance probability of 0.9. There is a fixed number and two outcomes per pupil.
But 30 pupils share a bus. A breakdown affects them together, making independence
doubtful. The expected attendance of 90 may still help, while binomial spread
can understate the risk. Compare normal-service days with disruption days before
using the model to order meals.

In five invented days, attendance counts are 86, 94, 89, 91 and 90. Their mean
is 90, matching the forecast. But matching an average does not establish a good
model. The first day's error is `86 - 90 = -4`: four fewer than predicted.
Look for repeated errors on wet days or transport-disruption days. Check a model
on fresh days as well as the records used to estimate p.

For testing, item difficulty can produce different success probabilities when
pupils answer from knowledge. Under blind uniform guessing, difficulty alone
does not change p = 0.25. But if a pupil eliminates two implausible options,
guessing between the remaining two gives p = 0.5. That pupil no longer follows
the four-option guessing model. Distinct questions also do not guarantee
independent choices.

For a normal model, inspect a plot. A mastery test may have scores piled near
the maximum, with a tail toward lower scores. This is not the symmetric bell
shape the model assumes (Lane, 2023, Chapter 7). Similar mean, median and mode
alone are insufficient evidence. A small class gives limited evidence about
population shape, but a normal population can generate small samples. A large
sample does not guarantee a normal distribution either.

**Misconception: an imperfect model is useless.** Ask whether the errors matter
for the decision. Revise a model or plan separately for rainy days when errors
cluster then. Forcing marks into a bell curve changes evidence instead of
checking model fit.

A dropout-risk probability can guide support without labelling a child's future
as fixed. Check whether observed rates match predicted risks for comparable
groups and whether the model works similarly across schools. Protect individual
data, allow record correction and consider circumstances the model misses.
An exceptional pupil outcome alone neither validates nor refutes a model.

## Activity: Simulating test guessing

Work in pairs for about 20 minutes. Recall the four binomial conditions before
opening your notes. Use four equally likely numbered slips; number one represents
a right response.

1. Draw a slip, record success or failure, replace it and mix thoroughly.
2. Repeat ten times to simulate one test. Count its successes.
3. Swap roles: one partner draws while the other records and checks replacement.
4. Produce five simulated tests and calculate their average score.
5. Pool results with four other pairs. Find the proportion scoring at least five.

Compare the pooled mean with 2.5 and the passing proportion with about 7.8%.
Discuss why 25 simulated tests need not match either value exactly. Explain how
omitting replacement changes the process and may break independence or constant
probability. Each pair reports one model condition and one observed difference.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Apply)* Find the expected score from independent blind guesses on a
   40-item four-option test.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why an illness spreading among pupils can violate the
   independence assumption in an attendance model.
3. *(Apply)* For approximately normal scores with μ = 60 and σ = 8, find the
   cut-off with about 2.5% below it using the empirical rule.
4. *(Apply)* Most pupils score full marks on a mastery test. Explain why the
   normal empirical rule is a poor way to estimate its low-score group.

## Summary

The guessing model estimates chance outcomes under specific assumptions; it does
not establish test quality or pupil honesty. The empirical rule estimates group
counts when a normal approximation fits. Check predictions against data and use
learning evidence before turning a planning threshold into a pupil decision.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher to discuss an anonymous score histogram, or use the
invented five-day attendance data above. Do not copy pupil records or collect
names. In one paragraph, identify a proposed model, one checked condition, one
unchecked condition and one planning decision. Ask what extra evidence would
change your choice.

## Summative task

A district committee proposes a ten-item four-option numeracy screen for
2,500 pupils. Policy 1 calls a score of three "competent". Policy 2 assumes a
normal distribution and assigns the bottom 16% to summer classes.

Evaluate both policies in 150–250 words. Use the guessing probabilities to
analyze Policy 1. For Policy 2, explain what shape evidence is needed, what
happens if the test has a ceiling effect, and why support requires evidence of
learning needs. Propose a better decision process.
