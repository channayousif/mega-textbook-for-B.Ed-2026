# Unit 11 teacher notes

These notes are for the teacher educator delivering GQUR-301 Unit 11, which
corresponds to Chapter 11 of the course guide (Week 11). They support the
delivery of probabilistic models, connecting statistical theory to practical
school administration and classroom assessment.

## Sequencing suggestion

| Session | Topic | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Session 1 | 11.1 Introduction to probabilistic models | Concept of a model; deterministic vs probabilistic thinking; conditions for binomial and normal models. |
| Session 2 | 11.2 Applications in real life | Multiple-choice guessing simulations; diagnostic screening via empirical rule; checking model assumptions. |

## Teaching strategies

### Topic 11.1 Introduction to probabilistic models

**Opening inquiry.** Write two questions on the board: (1) "How much will it
cost to buy 50 geometry boxes at Rs 120 each?" and (2) "How many students
will pass the matriculation exam this year?" Ask trainees to contrast them:
the first is deterministic, the second is a probability distribution.

**The Binomial conditions.** Rather than reciting the four binomial conditions,
present borderline scenarios and have trainees test each one. For instance,
during a monsoon flood, ask whether one child's attendance is independent of
their neighbor's. This shows that models are approximations whose assumptions
must be checked.

### Topic 11.2 Applications in real life

**Physical simulation.** Run the guessing activity with numbered slips, replacing
and mixing after each draw. A ten-item four-option test gives about a 7.8% chance
of at least five correct; a small simulation can differ substantially from this
expectation.

**The bell curve debate.** Lead a discussion on "grading on a curve". Ask: "If
all 30 pupils master the curriculum and score above 85%, should you force 3 of
them to fail to preserve a bell curve?" Use this to highlight the distinction
between norm-referenced ranking and criterion-referenced mastery.

## Common misconceptions and how to address them

If a trainee says an imperfect prediction is useless, ask them to compare
ordering meals from records with guessing without records. If someone
treats probability as destiny, ask whether a 70% chance excludes failure.
Use a mastery-score histogram to challenge the assumption that scores must
form a bell curve. Never alter marks merely to make the model fit.

## Practical work

1. **Test-Guessing Simulation (Topic 11.2).** Student-teachers pool simulated tests across groups using four equally likely numbered slips
   with replacement,
   plot the empirical frequency distribution of scores, and compare it with the
   theoretical binomial distribution.
2. **School Data Modeling (Topics 11.1-11.2).** In small groups, student-teachers
   select one administrative function from their practicum school (e.g. textbook
   ordering, remedial class scheduling, or exam hall invigilation) and draft a
   proposal explaining how probabilistic modeling improves decision-making.

## Assessment guidance

The unit assessment carries 10 MCQs, 10 RRQs and 5 ERQs. Use each topic's "Check
your understanding" items for formative feedback during sessions, and require
student-teachers to justify answers with reasoning rather than intuition.

The sequencing and simulations here are proposed ways to use the guide's listed
methods (lectures, discussion, group work, assignments), not additional
requirements. Treat invented thresholds as planning examples only. A normal
curve does not diagnose learning needs, and a small probability is not proof of
a false claim.
