## A real classroom situation

In a fictional government high school in Sukkur, the vice principal budgets
Rs 6,000 for 120 answer booklets at Rs 50 each. These inputs give one fixed
cost. Attendance records suggest that about 5% of pupils may be absent on exam
day. A junior teacher treats that as exactly six absences and plans fewer seats.
The vice principal asks what happens if only three pupils are absent. An expected
count can help planning, but it does not promise a particular day's count.

## Explanation

### What is a model? Deterministic versus probabilistic

A **model** is a simplified representation of a situation. A mathematical model
uses numbers and relationships to describe selected features. It leaves out
other details, so we must ask whether those omissions matter for our decision.

A **deterministic model** has no random part. The same inputs produce the same
output. For 120 booklets at Rs 50 each, `cost = 120 × 50 = Rs 6,000`.
This is exact under the stated inputs, even though a real supplier might later
change the price. Changing an input changes the calculation; it does not make
the rule probabilistic.

A **probabilistic model** assigns probabilities to possible outcomes. It
represents uncertainty. Instead of promising six absences, an attendance model
assigns probabilities to different absence counts. Lane's binomial distribution
is one example of this approach (Lane, 2023, Chapter 5).

**Worked comparison:** A Larkana teacher needs two worksheets per pupil for
40 registered pupils. Printing for every pupil costs exactly `40 × 2 × Rs 3 =
Rs 240` at Rs 3 per sheet. Predicting how many pupils will attend is different.
If attendance probability is estimated at 0.9 for each pupil, the expected
attendance is 36. That average can guide a forecast, but printing for exactly
36 pupils risks leaving someone without work. The decision needs a safety
margin as well as an average.

### Two foundational probabilistic models: binomial and normal

The **binomial model** describes a count of successes in repeated trials.
A trial is one attempt or observation; success is simply the outcome being
counted, not a judgement about a child. For example, success might mean a pupil
is present. Lane (2023, Chapter 5) and Illowsky and Dean (2023, Section 4.3)
require these conditions:

1. A fixed number of trials, n.
2. Two possible outcomes per trial: success or failure.
3. The same success probability, p, on every trial.
4. Independent trials: knowing one outcome does not change the probability
   of another outcome.

For X successes, the probability of exactly k is:

`P(X = k) = nCk × p^k × (1 - p)^(n - k)`.

Here `nCk = n! / (k! × (n - k)!)` counts the ways to choose which trials
succeed. The factorial sign means multiply whole numbers down to one:
`3! = 3 × 2 × 1 = 6`; by definition, `0! = 1`.
The expected count is `E(X) = n × p`. Variance, a measure of spread in squared
units, is `Var(X) = n × p × (1 - p)`.

**Worked binomial example:** A pupil chooses randomly and independently on
five true/false questions. There are five trials, two outcomes per trial,
constant p = 0.5 and independent choices. Separate questions alone do not
establish independence; it is an assumption about how the choices are made.

The probability of all five being right is
`P(X = 5) = 1 × 0.5^5 × 0.5^0 = 0.03125`, about 3.1%.
The expected count is `5 × 0.5 = 2.5`, while variance is `5 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 1.25`.
A pupil cannot get 2.5 whole questions right. This average describes what happens
across many repetitions, not a required score on one test.

For exactly two right, `5C2 = 5! / (2! × 3!) = 10`.
Therefore `P(X = 2) = 10 × 0.5^2 × 0.5^3 = 0.3125`.
The factor ten matters: the two successes can occur in different positions.

The **normal model** uses a continuous, symmetric bell-shaped probability
curve. Its centre is the mean, μ, and its spread is the standard deviation, σ.
These two numbers are its parameters: they determine which normal curve is
being used. Mean, median and mode coincide in the theoretical model, and total
area under the curve is one (Lane, 2023, Chapter 7).

The empirical rule gives approximate areas: 68% within μ ± σ, 95% within
μ ± 2σ, and 99.7% within μ ± 3σ (Illowsky & Dean, 2023, Section 6.1).
A percentage here describes a model area or expected proportion, not a quota
that an observed class must satisfy.

**Worked normal example:** In an invented Hyderabad district example, suppose
2,000 mathematics scores are approximately normal with μ = 65 and σ = 10.
The interval 55–75 is one standard deviation either side of the mean, so it
contains about `0.68 × 2,000 = 1,360` pupils. The interval 45–85 contains
about 95%, or 1,900 pupils. The remaining 5% is split equally between the tails,
so about 2.5%, or 50 pupils, score above 85 using the rounded rule.

Scores are discrete and bounded, while a normal curve is continuous and has no
bounds. It is only an approximation to marks. Check that the observed shape
is roughly symmetric and bell-shaped, with negligible predicted probability
beyond the score limits. More pupils alone do not make scores normal.

### Common misconceptions about statistical models

**"An imperfect model is useless."** A useful approximation can improve planning.
Check its errors against real data and the cost of a poor decision. An attendance
average may help order meals while still requiring a reserve for busy days.

**"Probability predicts each individual's outcome."** A probability can describe
uncertainty about one pupil or a group, but it does not promise a result. A
70% estimated pass probability allows both passing and failing. Across repeated
comparable groups, about 70 passes per 100 are expected if the model fits.

**"Every quantity follows a bell curve."** Counts of absences are discrete.
An easy mastery test may have many high scores and a long tail toward lower
scores. Neither must be forced into a normal curve.

A **criterion-referenced assessment** compares work with stated learning goals;
a **norm-referenced assessment** compares it with a reference group. Checking
whether each Sukkur pupil can multiply two-digit numbers is different from
ranking those pupils. Neither purpose requires changing marks to fit a bell.

## Activity: Deterministic or probabilistic?

Work in pairs for about 20 minutes. Without looking back, explain the difference
between the two model families to each other. Then classify these situations:

- Buying 45 textbooks at a fixed Rs 400 each.
- Predicting how many pupils will miss school during a chickenpox outbreak.
- Calculating a percentage from 72 marks out of 100.
- Predicting marks on a new district test.
- Counting heads in 50 independent tosses of a fair coin.

One partner identifies inputs and possible outputs; the other challenges the
choice. For uncertain situations, consider binomial, normal or neither. State
which assumptions you would need to check. Swap roles halfway through, then
compare with another pair. A plausible model choice needs evidence, not just a
familiar formula.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Define a deterministic model and a probabilistic model in one
   sentence each.
2. *(Understand)* State the four binomial conditions and explain why an expected
   count can be fractional.
3. *(Apply)* A school buys 250 exercise books at Rs 60 each. Calculate the cost
   and justify which model family describes this calculation.
4. *(Apply)* Assume 60 independent pupils each have a pass probability of 0.85.
   Find the expected number passing and explain why it is not a guaranteed count.

## Summary

A deterministic rule gives one output from fixed inputs; a probabilistic model
represents possible outcomes and uncertainty. The binomial model counts successes
under four conditions, while the normal model describes a continuous bell-shaped
distribution. A model is useful when its assumptions and errors suit the decision.

## Summative task

A Larkana school has 200 pupils. Sports day needs three ribbons for each of
60 events, at Rs 20 per ribbon. Each attending pupil also needs a Rs 50 juice
box. Past events suggest attendance probability 0.9, but rain and shared transport
can affect several pupils together. Unused juice cannot be returned.

Write 150–250 words classifying the two requirements, calculating the fixed
ribbon cost and expected attendance, and justifying a juice-order plan. Analyze
both shortage and waste, and explain whether binomial independence is plausible.
