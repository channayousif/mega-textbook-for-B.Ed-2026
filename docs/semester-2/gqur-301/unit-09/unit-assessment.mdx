# Unit 9 assessment

## Unit summary

A probability statement needs a clear experiment, event and denominator. An outcome is one result; a sample space lists every possible result; an event selects the results that meet a condition. Dividing counts gives probability only when the detailed outcomes are equally likely. Observed proportions describe records and require care when used to estimate future chances.

Complement, addition and multiplication rules answer different questions. An inclusive 'or' includes overlap, so addition subtracts the overlap once. A 'both' calculation uses a conditional probability unless independence is justified. Mutually exclusive events cannot occur together. Independent events leave each other's chances unchanged. Checking both counts and assumptions is part of quantitative reasoning.

## Summative assessment

Try the questions before consulting the final section. Use exact fractions where convenient and show the event and denominator in calculations. All school data below are invented. Unless a question states otherwise, selecting a pupil means choosing uniformly at random from the stated class. The first five MCQs and RRQs assess Topic 9.1; the remaining five assess Topic 9.2. ERQs 1–2 assess Topic 9.1, ERQs 3–4 assess Topic 9.2, and ERQ 5 integrates both.

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. A teacher draws one numbered slip from a bag. What is an outcome? *(Remember)*
   - A) The complete procedure for mixing slips.
   - B) The single numbered slip obtained.
   - C) The list of all possible slips.
   - D) The chance assigned to an event.

2. A fair draw uses slips numbered 1 to 10. Which set represents the event 'number greater than 7'? *(Understand)*
   - A) 7, 8, 9, 10.
   - B) 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7.
   - C) 8, 9, 10.
   - D) 1, 2, 3.

3. A bag holds nine identical slips, four marked for a story task and five for a vocabulary task. What is P(story) under a fair draw? *(Apply)*
   - A) 4/9.
   - B) 4/5.
   - C) 1/2.
   - D) 5/9.

4. A fictional class meets an attendance threshold on 21 of 28 days. Which statement reports the evidence most carefully? *(Apply)*
   - A) The next four days will include exactly three threshold days.
   - B) Each pupil has a proven attendance probability of 0.75.
   - C) The threshold has probability 0.5 because it is met or not met.
   - D) The observed threshold frequency is 0.75 for these 28 days.

5. A fair coin has produced four heads on independent tosses. What is the probability of tails on its next toss? *(Apply)*
   - A) 1/5.
   - B) 1/2.
   - C) 4/5.
   - D) 1.

6. In a class, P(reading club) = 0.65. What is P(not reading club)? *(Apply)*
   - A) 0.65.
   - B) 0.50.
   - C) 0.35.
   - D) 1.65.

7. Of 40 pupils, 22 join art, 18 join drama and 10 join both. What is the probability that a selected pupil joins at least one? *(Apply)*
   - A) 30/40.
   - B) 40/40.
   - C) 10/40.
   - D) 12/40.

8. One fair six-sided die is rolled. Which pair of events is mutually exclusive? *(Understand)*
   - A) An even number; a number greater than 3.
   - B) A number below 4; an odd number.
   - C) A number above 2; a number below 6.
   - D) An even number; an odd number.

9. Two fair draws from a bag containing three red and three white counters use replacement and remixing. What is the chance of red on both draws? *(Apply)*
   - A) 1/2.
   - B) 1/4.
   - C) 1/5.
   - D) 1/3.

10. The same bag is used without replacement. What is the chance of red on both draws? *(Apply)*
   - A) 1/4.
   - B) 1/2.
   - C) 1/5.
   - D) 2/3.

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

1. In three short sentences distinguish an experiment, its sample space and an event. Use a single draw from slips 1 to 4 throughout. *(Understand)*
2. A random draw uses ten identical slips numbered 1 to 10. Calculate the probability of a multiple of 3 and state the outcomes you counted. Answer in two sentences. *(Apply)*
3. In two sentences explain why two labels, 'arrives early' and 'does not arrive early', do not imply probabilities of 1/2. State one kind of evidence you would request. *(Understand)*
4. A fictional school records rain on 6 of 30 school days. Calculate the observed proportion and write one cautious sentence about using it to plan next month's sports day. *(Apply)*
5. In three sentences analyze a claim that a probability of 1.2 means 'very certain'. Give one possible counting error and one check you would make. *(Analyze)*
6. Of 50 pupils, 28 join reading, 20 join science and 12 join both. Calculate the probabilities of either activity and neither, showing the overlap correction. *(Apply)*
7. Using the same class, calculate P(science given reading) and explain its denominator in one sentence. *(Apply)*
8. A bag has four blue and two white counters. Two are drawn fairly without replacement. Calculate the probability of two blue counters and explain why the second factor differs from the first. *(Apply)*
9. In three sentences analyze why two mutually exclusive events with positive probabilities cannot be independent. Use a single fair-die example. *(Analyze)*
10. In a fictional class of 20, 10 pupils join reading, 8 join art and 4 join both. Test whether these membership events are independent for a randomly selected pupil. Give the two probabilities you compare and your conclusion. *(Analyze)*

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. A college wants a fair draw to choose one of eight trainee groups. A trainee offers two outcomes, 'our group' and 'another group', and assigns 1/2 to each. In 180–250 words, evaluate this model, design a fair procedure, list an appropriate sample space and calculate the probability of your group being chosen. Explain how you would check the procedure before using it. *(Evaluate)*
2. A fictional attendance record meets a specified threshold on 24 of 30 days. A report claims, 'This proves the next five days will include four high-attendance days, and each pupil has probability 0.8 of attending.' In 180–250 words, analyze both inferences, calculate the observed fraction and write a defensible replacement report. Specify exactly what the observations describe. *(Analyze)*
3. A fictional class has 60 pupils: 32 join reading, 26 join science and 14 join both. In a worked report, find the four non-overlapping region counts. Calculate the probabilities of at least one activity, neither, and not reading. Explain why 'not reading' differs from 'science', and audit the totals. *(Analyze)*
4. A bag contains two red and four white identical counters. Design two experiments, one with replacement and one without. Calculate the chance of two red results for each. In 180–250 words, defend whether the red-result events are independent and whether they are mutually exclusive in each experiment. Use the conditional second-draw chance in your explanation. *(Evaluate)*
5. A fictional school trial records 40 days. On 12 it rains, on 10 transport is late, and on 6 both occur. A trainee says, 'Rain and delay are two possibilities, so each has chance 1/2. Both happen with chance (12/40) × (10/40). Either happens on 22 of 40 days. These values are exact predictions for next month.' In 250–350 words, evaluate every claim, calculate defensible observed probabilities and explain the limits of future use. Define an experiment that makes the recorded proportions exact probabilities for the recorded days. *(Evaluate)*

## Answers and marking guidance

These are intentionally public self-study explanations, separate from any graded LMS bank. For calculations, accept equivalent fractions, decimals or percentages. Award method marks for a suitable event definition and rule even when a later arithmetic slip occurs. Do not award interpretation marks for a numerical answer whose event or denominator is wrong.

### MCQ answer key

1. B. An outcome is the one result obtained, rather than the procedure or all possible results.
2. C. 'Greater than 7' excludes 7 and includes 8, 9 and 10.
3. A. Four of the nine equally likely slips meet the event.
4. D. This describes the observed class-level record without promising a future pattern or an individual pupil's chance.
5. B. Previous results leave the chance of the next independent fair toss unchanged.
6. C. The complement has probability 1 - 0.65 = 0.35.
7. A. The union contains 22 + 18 - 10 = 30 pupils.
8. D. A single result cannot be both even and odd.
9. B. Replacement restores the bag, so (3/6) × (3/6) = 1/4.
10. C. Without replacement, (3/6) × (2/5) = 1/5.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

Each RRQ is worth 4 marks. The stated components specify how to divide the marks; prose need not match these words.

1. The experiment is drawing one slip without looking. The sample space contains 1, 2, 3 and 4. An event could be an even number, containing 2 and 4. **Marks:** experiment (1), complete sample space (1), suitable event with its outcomes (2).
2. The event contains 3, 6 and 9. Its probability is 3/10 = 0.3 under the fair-draw procedure. **Marks:** identifies all three outcomes and no extras (2), denominator 10 (1), probability 3/10 (1).
3. Two labels can collect unequal shares of the outcomes, so category names alone do not establish equal chance. Request arrival records over a defined period or a justified chance model. **Marks:** distinguishes category count from likelihood (2), relevant evidence (1), clear event or observation period (1).
4. The observed fraction is 6/30 = 0.2. This describes those school days; using it for next month assumes conditions are sufficiently comparable and does not guarantee rain on a particular day. **Marks:** fraction (1), decimal or percentage (1), identifies observed period (1), suitable limit on prediction (1).
5. A probability cannot exceed 1. A count might duplicate days or include days outside the denominator period. Check that each favourable observation belongs to the stated total and is counted once. **Marks:** rejects the probability (1), gives a specific possible error (1), explains the event-total relationship (1), proposes a relevant check (1).
6. Either has probability (28 + 20 - 12)/50 = 36/50 = 0.72. Neither has probability 14/50 = 0.28. **Marks:** overlap correction (1), union calculation (1), correct complement method (1), neither result (1).
7. P(science given reading) = 12/28 = 3/7. We restrict attention to the 28 reading members and count the 12 who also join science. **Marks:** overlap numerator (1), conditional denominator (1), fraction (1), interpretation (1).
8. P(two blue) = (4/6) × (3/5) = 2/5. After a blue first draw, three blue counters remain among five total, so the second factor changes. **Marks:** first factor (1), second factor (1), product (1), explanation of changed contents (1).
9. On one fair die, even and odd each have probability 1/2 but cannot occur together. Knowing even occurred makes the chance of odd zero. This differs from 1/2, so independence fails. **Marks:** valid example (1), positive marginal chances (1), zero conditional or joint chance (1), justified conclusion (1).
10. The actual overlap is 4/20 = 0.2. The product is (10/20) × (8/20) = 0.2, so these events are independent in the stated finite selection model. **Marks:** actual overlap probability (1), marginal product (1), comparison (1), conclusion tied to the selected pupil and this class (1). Overlap is compatible with independence.

### ERQ rubrics

Each ERQ is worth 10 marks. For each criterion below, give zero for absent or contradictory work, about half of the available marks for a relevant but incomplete argument, and full marks for correct work supported by the stated counts or procedure. For an odd-numbered allocation, use whole marks to distinguish partial from nearly complete reasoning. Alternative valid methods receive equal credit.

1. **Fair-draw design:** specifies identical slips, hidden selection and thorough mixing (3); lists eight distinct group outcomes and obtains 1/8 (2); analyzes why 'our group' versus 'another group' collects one versus seven equally likely outcomes (3, Analyze); proposes a practical fairness audit and acknowledges its assumptions (2, Evaluate). A strong response connects the physical procedure to equal chances instead of merely calling the draw random.
2. **Attendance inference:** calculates 24/30 = 0.8 and names the threshold-day event (2); analyzes why a proportion does not prescribe a five-day sequence (3, Analyze); distinguishes a class-level day threshold from an individual pupil's attendance (3, Analyze); writes a bounded replacement report identifying the period and uncertainty about future conditions (2). Do not require a different numerical forecast, since no evidence supplies one.
3. **Club regions:** obtains reading only 18, both 14, science only 12 and neither 16 (4, one each); calculates union 44/60, neither 16/60 and not reading 28/60 (3, one each); explains that not reading includes science only and neither, unlike science which includes the overlap (2, Analyze); checks that all regions sum to 60 (1). A labelled sketch is acceptable but cannot replace explanations of the selected events.
4. **Replacement comparison:** specifies both random procedures and their second-draw contents (2); calculates (2/6) × (2/6) = 1/9 with replacement and (2/6) × (1/5) = 1/15 without replacement (3); justifies independence in the first and dependence in the second using unchanged versus changed conditional chances (3, Analyze); explains that both red events can occur in either experiment, so neither pair is mutually exclusive (2, Evaluate). Full reasoning must refer to two stages, not just two colour labels.
5. **Integrated school claim:** defines uniform selection of one of the 40 recorded days and names rain and delay events (2); rejects equal-category reasoning and reports P(rain) = 12/40 = 0.3 and P(delay) = 10/40 = 0.25 (2); compares actual joint 6/40 = 0.15 with independent product 0.075, using P(delay given rain) = 6/12 = 0.5 to explain the failure of independence (2, Analyze); corrects union to (12 + 10 - 6)/40 = 16/40 = 0.4 and explains double-counting (2, Analyze); evaluates the future claim by distinguishing a recorded-day selection from a forecast under changing conditions (2, Evaluate). Dependence in this record does not by itself establish that rain caused the delay.
