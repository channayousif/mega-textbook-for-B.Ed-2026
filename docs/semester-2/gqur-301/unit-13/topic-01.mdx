## A real classroom situation

A teacher education college in Hyderabad asks its B.Ed trainees to investigate a
practical question: "Do pupils who study longer each week tend to score higher on
the monthly mathematics test?" Each trainee collects two numbers from 10 pupils at
their practicum school: weekly study hours and the last test score. The scatter
plot of the 10 pairs shows a cloud that rises from left to right, loosely, as you
would expect. But the supervising lecturer asks, "How strong is that upward
pattern? Can we put a number on it so we can compare it with another class?" The
trainee hesitates. She knows the plot looks positive, but she has no single
number to report.

That single number is the **correlation coefficient**, and learning to compute and
interpret it is the work of this topic.

## Explanation

### Types of correlation

In Unit 12 you described the direction of a scatter plot as positive, negative or
none. **Correlation** is the formal name for that direction together with the
strength of the linear pattern. We distinguish three types:

- **Positive correlation**: the cloud rises from left to right. As one variable
  increases, the other tends to increase. Study hours and test score usually show a
  positive correlation.
- **Negative correlation**: the cloud falls from left to right. As one variable
  increases, the other tends to decrease. The number of absences and test score
  usually show a negative correlation.
- **Zero (or no) correlation**: the cloud shows no discernible linear slope. The two
  variables do not move together in a straight-line pattern. A pupil's shoe size and
  their test score, for example, show no linear correlation.

These three types are about the *direction and approximate strength* of the linear
pattern. The exact strength is captured by the correlation coefficient.

### The correlation coefficient

The <Glossary term="Correlation coefficient" /> (denoted **r**) is a single number
that measures both the direction and the strength of the *linear* relationship
between two variables. It always lies between -1 and +1:

- **r = +1**: perfect positive linear correlation; all points lie exactly on a line
  sloping upward.
- **r = -1**: perfect negative linear correlation; all points lie exactly on a line
  sloping downward.
- **r = 0**: no linear correlation.
- **0 < r < +1**: positive correlation; the closer to +1, the stronger the linear
  pattern.
- **-1 < r < 0**: negative correlation; the closer to -1, the stronger the linear
  pattern.

A common rule of thumb (Lane, 2023, Chapter 4) for interpreting the magnitude of r
in classroom data:

| Range of \|r\| | Descriptor |
|---|---|
| 0.00 - 0.30 | Weak or none |
| 0.30 - 0.70 | Moderate |
| 0.70 - 1.00 | Strong |

Note that this scale describes *linear* correlation. Two variables can have a clear
non-linear relationship (for example, a U-shaped curve) and still have r close to 0.
The correlation coefficient only measures straight-line association.

### Computing r for a small data set

The formula for the Pearson correlation coefficient between two variables x and y,
for n paired cases, is:

$$r = \frac{n\sum xy - \sum x \sum y}{\sqrt{[n\sum x^2 - (\sum x)^2][n\sum y^2 - (\sum y)^2]}}$$

You do not need to memorise this formula, but you should be able to compute r step
by step for a small data set using a table. The steps are:

1. List the n pairs (x, y).
2. Compute x², y² and xy for each row.
3. Sum each column: Σx, Σy, Σx², Σy², Σxy.
4. Substitute into the formula.

**Worked example.** The data below give weekly study hours (x) and test score (y)
for 5 pupils (a smaller set than the trainee's 10, for easier arithmetic).

| Pupil | x (hours) | y (score/100) | x² | y² | xy |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 2 | 40 | 4 | 1600 | 80 |
| B | 4 | 55 | 16 | 3025 | 220 |
| C | 6 | 60 | 36 | 3600 | 360 |
| D | 8 | 75 | 64 | 5625 | 600 |
| E | 10 | 85 | 100 | 7225 | 850 |
| **Sum** | **30** | **315** | **220** | **21075** | **2110** |

Substituting (n = 5):

- Numerator: 5(2110) - (30)(315) = 10550 - 9450 = 1100
- Denominator part 1: 5(220) - (30)² = 1100 - 900 = 200
- Denominator part 2: 5(21075) - (315)² = 105375 - 99225 = 6150
- Denominator: √(200 × 6150) = √1230000 ≈ 1109.1
- r ≈ 1100 / 1109.1 ≈ 0.99

This r of approximately 0.99 indicates a very strong positive linear correlation
between study hours and test score for these 5 pupils, which matches the near-straight
cloud you would see on the scatter plot.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees believe that a correlation of 0 means the
two variables have no relationship at all. This is not so. r = 0 means no *linear*
relationship; the variables could still follow a curved pattern. Always say "no
linear correlation" rather than "no relationship" when you interpret r = 0.

Another common error is to treat the correlation coefficient as a measure of
causation. It is not. A high positive r between study hours and test score tells you
the two variables move together in a straight-line pattern; it does not tell you that
studying longer *causes* higher scores. That distinction matters every time you
report a correlation.

### What the sign and size of r tell a teacher

In practicum work you will often face a question like, "Is there a link between
pupil attendance and test performance?" Computing r gives you a defensible answer:

- If r is close to -1 or +1, the linear pattern is strong; you can be confident the
  link exists (though not that one causes the other).
- If r is close to 0, there is no straight-line link; attendance and performance may
  still relate in a non-linear way, but a straight-line summary would be misleading.
- The sign tells you the direction: negative r means higher attendance goes with
  lower scores (unusual, worth checking your data), positive r means they move
  together.

Report r with its sign and a brief interpretation, never as a bare number.

## Activity: Compute and interpret r in pairs

Work in pairs. About 25 minutes.

1. Each pair receives (or constructs) a small bivariate data set of 6 to 8 paired
   values from a plausible school situation: for example, number of library books
   borrowed in a term and a reading test score.
2. Compute r step by step using the table method shown above. One partner works
   the first half of the arithmetic (columns x², y², xy), the other works the
   second half (sums and the formula). Then swap and check each other's work.
3. Write one sentence interpreting the sign and magnitude of r, and one sentence
   stating what r does *not* tell you about the relationship.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* In your own words, state the difference between positive and negative correlation.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why a correlation coefficient of 0 does not necessarily mean the two variables have no relationship at all.
3. *(Apply)* A trainee computes r = -0.45 between the number of late arrivals and a pupil's end-of-term grade for 8 pupils. State the direction and strength of the correlation using the rule-of-thumb scale, and write one sentence describing what this means in plain language.

## Summary

Correlation describes the direction and strength of a linear relationship between
two variables. The correlation coefficient r puts a number on that relationship:
it ranges from -1 (perfect negative) through 0 (no linear correlation) to +1
(perfect positive). The sign gives the direction; the absolute value gives the
strength. r measures association, not causation, and it only captures straight-line
patterns; a non-linear relationship can exist even when r is near 0.

## Try this at your practicum school

At your practicum school, collect two numerical variables from 8 to 10 pupils (for
example, number of hours spent on homework in a week and the score on the most
recent mathematics test). Plot the scatter plot, then compute r. Write a short
paragraph (no more than 80 words) reporting r with its sign and strength, and end
with one sentence stating what r does not tell you.
