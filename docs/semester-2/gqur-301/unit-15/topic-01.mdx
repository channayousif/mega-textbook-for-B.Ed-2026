## A real classroom situation

A head teacher in Matiari has introduced a new reading method for Class 4. The
district average on the Grade 4 Urdu reading test over the past five years is
72 marks out of 100, with a standard deviation of 10 marks. After one term, the
head teacher tests a class of 35 pupils who used the new method. Their mean
score is 75. She asks: "Is my class truly doing better than the district
average, or could a rise of 3 marks just be the usual ups and downs of test
scores?" A hypothesis test answers exactly this question. It does not prove the
new method works. It measures how surprising the sample result would be if, in
reality, the method made no difference.

## Explanation

### What a hypothesis test does

A **hypothesis test** is a procedure that uses sample data to decide between
two competing claims about a population (Illowsky & Dean, 2023, Chapter 9). One
claim is called the **null hypothesis**, written *H*<sub>0</sub>. It represents
"no effect" or "no difference" and is the claim we test against. The other is
the **alternative hypothesis**, written *H*<sub>1</sub> (or *H*<sub>a</sub>).
It represents the effect or difference the researcher suspects.

The test does not prove *H*<sub>1</sub> is true. It asks: "If *H*<sub>0</sub>
were true, how likely is it that we would see a sample result at least this
extreme?" If that probability is very small, the data are hard to reconcile
with *H*<sub>0</sub>, and we reject *H*<sub>0</sub> in favour of
*H*<sub>1</sub>.

### Step 1: State the hypotheses

The null hypothesis always contains equality: *H*<sub>0</sub>: *μ* = *μ*<sub>0</sub>.
The alternative may be two-tailed (*μ* ≠ *μ*<sub>0</sub>), left-tailed
(*μ* &lt; *μ*<sub>0</sub>), or right-tailed (*μ* &gt; *μ*<sub>0</sub>),
depending on the question.

**Worked example.** A district mean reading score is 72. A head teacher wants to
know whether the new method produces a *different* score (not specifically a
higher one).

- *H*<sub>0</sub>: *μ* = 72 (the new method gives the same mean as the district).
- *H*<sub>1</sub>: *μ* ≠ 72 (the new method gives a different mean).

If instead the head teacher wanted to test whether the new method gives a
*higher* mean, the alternative would be *H*<sub>1</sub>: *μ* &gt; 72 (a
one-tailed, right-tailed test). The choice depends on the research question, not
on the data.

### Step 2: Choose the significance level

The **significance level**, written *α*, is the threshold for "too surprising".
It is the probability of rejecting *H*<sub>0</sub> when *H*<sub>0</sub> is
actually true (a "false alarm"). The most common choice is *α* = 0.05, meaning
we accept a 5% risk of a false alarm. *α* = 0.01 is stricter; *α* = 0.10 is
more lenient (Lane & Ziemer, n.d., "Hypothesis Testing").

### Step 3: Choose the test and compute the test statistic

The **test statistic** is a single number that measures how far the sample
result is from what *H*<sub>0</sub> predicts, in units of standard error. For a
mean with a known population standard deviation *σ*, the test statistic is a
*z*-score:

*z* = (<span style={{whiteSpace: 'nowrap'}}>x̄ − μ<sub>0</sub></span>) / (σ / √n)

where x̄ is the sample mean, *μ*<sub>0</sub> is the value under *H*<sub>0</sub>,
and *n* is the sample size.

**Worked example.** For the Matiari reading class: x̄ = 75, μ<sub>0</sub> = 72,
σ = 10, n = 35.

Standard error = σ / √n = 10 / √35 = 10 / 5.92 = 1.69.

*z* = (75 − 72) / 1.69 = 3 / 1.69 = **1.78**.

A *z*-score of 1.78 means the sample mean is 1.78 standard errors above the
district mean. Topic 15.2 works through the full calculation.

### Step 4: Find the p-value

The **p-value** is the probability of getting a test statistic at least as
extreme as the one observed, *if H*<sub>0</sub> is true* (Illowsky & Dean,
2023, Section 9.2, NIST/SEMATECH, Section 1.3.5.3). A small p-value means the
observed result is unlikely under *H*<sub>0</sub>.

<Figure id="fig-U15-2" src="/img/figures/gqur-301/unit-15/fig-U15-2.svg" alt="A normal distribution curve centred at the null hypothesis mean of 72. Both tails beyond the critical values of about 68.69 and 75.31 are shaded, showing the rejection regions for a two-tailed test at the 5% significance level. The observed sample mean of 75 lies just inside the upper critical value, so it is not in a rejection region." />

For a two-tailed test with *z* = 1.78, the p-value is 2 × P(*Z* &gt; 1.78) =
2 × 0.0375 = **0.075**. For a one-tailed (right) test it would be P(*Z* &gt;
1.78) = 0.0375. Topic 15.2 explains how these come from the standard normal
table.

### Step 5: Make the decision

Compare the p-value with *α*:

- If p-value ≤ *α*: **reject *H*<sub>0</sub>**. The result is statistically
  significant. The data provide enough evidence for *H*<sub>1</sub>.
- If p-value &gt; *α*: **do not reject *H*<sub>0</sub>**. The result is not
  statistically significant. The data do not provide enough evidence for
  *H*<sub>1</sub>.

**Worked example.** With *α* = 0.05 and a two-tailed p-value of 0.075:

0.075 &gt; 0.05, so we **do not reject *H*<sub>0</sub>**. The 3-mark rise could
plausibly happen by chance even if the new method made no real difference. The
data do not provide sufficient evidence that the new method changes the mean
score.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think "the null hypothesis is what the
 researcher believes". It is the opposite: the null hypothesis is usually the
 claim of "no effect" that the researcher is trying to challenge. The
 researcher believes the *alternative* and hopes the data will reject the null.
A second common error is thinking "a small p-value proves the alternative
 hypothesis". A small p-value only says the data are surprising under the null.
 It does not measure the size of the effect or prove the alternative is true.
Third, "failing to reject the null" is **not** the same as proving the null is
 true. It means the data are not surprising enough to rule it out. The
 distinction matters when you report test results to a school.

## Activity: State the hypotheses in pairs

Work in pairs. About 15 minutes.

1. Each pair writes five short claims a teacher or head teacher might make about
   their school, for example "Our Class 6 math score is higher than the
   district average of 65" or "The attendance rate on Fridays is different
   from the rate on other days".
2. For each claim, state *H*<sub>0</sub> and *H*<sub>1</sub> and say whether
   the test is two-tailed, left-tailed or right-tailed. Give a reason.
3. Swap your list with the next pair. Check their hypotheses. For any claim
   where you disagree, discuss and agree. Claims about "higher" versus
   "different" are the ones most likely to cause disagreement.
4. As a class, collect the claims where pairs disagreed and discuss why the
   wording of the claim decides the alternative.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State in one sentence what a hypothesis test decides.
2. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between the null hypothesis and the
   alternative hypothesis, with one school example of each.
3. *(Apply)* A coach claims that the average time a pupil takes to run 400
   metres is less than 75 seconds. State *H*<sub>0</sub> and *H*<sub>1</sub>
   and say whether the test is one-tailed or two-tailed.
4. *(Apply)* A two-tailed test gives a p-value of 0.03 with *α* = 0.05. State
   the decision and explain what it means in one sentence.

## Summary

A hypothesis test uses sample data to decide between a null hypothesis (no
effect) and an alternative hypothesis (the suspected effect). You choose a
significance level *α*, compute a test statistic, find the p-value (how
surprising the result is under the null), and reject the null only when the
p-value is at most *α*. A small p-value does not prove the alternative, and
failing to reject the null does not prove the null is true.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can state the null and alternative hypotheses for a school claim and choose the correct tail.
- [ ] I can explain what a p-value measures and what it does not measure.
- [ ] I can apply the decision rule: reject the null when the p-value is at most the significance level.
- [ ] I can explain why "do not reject the null" is not the same as "the null is true".

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher for one claim they hear about teaching or pupil
performance, for example "Group work improves test scores" or "Morning
attendance is the same as afternoon attendance". Write *H*<sub>0</sub> and
*H*<sub>1</sub> for that claim, say whether the test should be one-tailed or
two-tailed, and explain what a p-value would mean to the teacher in plain
language.

## Summative task

A district education officer in Shaheed Benazizabad says the average Class 7
science score in the district is 60 out of 100. A teacher who uses group
investigations thinks her class does *better* than this.

a) State *H*<sub>0</sub> and *H*<sub>1</sub> and say whether the test is
   one-tailed or two-tailed.

b) The teacher tests 30 pupils and finds a mean of 64. Explain, without
   calculating the test statistic, what a p-value would measure in this
   context.

c) In 80-120 words, analyze what it would mean if the p-value came out as
   0.02 with *α* = 0.05. Explain what the teacher could and could not
   conclude, and why the result would not "prove" that group investigation
   raises scores.
