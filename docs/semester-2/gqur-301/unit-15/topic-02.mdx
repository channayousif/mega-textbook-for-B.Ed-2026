## A real classroom situation

A mathematics teacher in Sukkur has been teaching Class 8 for several years.
Over that time, the district-wide mean on the end-of-year Class 8 math test has
settled at 68 marks out of 100, with a standard deviation of 12 marks. This year
her class of 40 pupils scores a mean of 71.5. She wonders: "Is this class truly
above the district average, or is a rise of 3.5 marks just normal year-to-year
variation?" Because the district standard deviation is known from many years of
data, a **z-test** answers the question.

## Explanation

### When to use a z-test

A **z-test for a mean** applies when three conditions hold (Illowsky & Dean,
2023, Section 9.3, NIST/SEMATECH, Section 7.1.3):

1. The data are a random sample from the population of interest.
2. The population standard deviation *σ* is **known**.
3. Either the population is normally distributed or the sample is large enough
   (*n* ≥ 30) for the Central Limit Theorem to make the sample mean
   approximately normal.

In the Sukkur math example, *σ* = 12 is known from district data and *n* = 40 is
large, so a z-test is appropriate. If *σ* were unknown or the sample were
small, Topic 15.3's t-test would be the right choice.

### The test statistic

The **z-test statistic** measures how many standard errors the sample mean is
from the null-hypothesis mean:

> *z* = (x̄ − μ<sub>0</sub>) / (σ / √n)

where x̄ is the sample mean, μ<sub>0</sub> is the value under *H*<sub>0</sub>,
*σ* is the known population standard deviation, and *n* is the sample size.

### Step-by-step: the Sukkur math class

**Step 1 - State the hypotheses.** The teacher suspects her class does *better*
than the district average, so:

- *H*<sub>0</sub>: *μ* = 68.
- *H*<sub>1</sub>: *μ* &gt; 68 (right-tailed test).

**Step 2 - Choose the significance level.** The teacher chooses *α* = 0.05.

**Step 3 - Compute the test statistic.**

Standard error = σ / √n = 12 / √40 = 12 / 6.32 = 1.90.

*z* = (71.5 − 68) / 1.90 = 3.5 / 1.90 = **1.84**.

**Step 4 - Find the p-value.** For a right-tailed test, the p-value is the area
to the right of *z* = 1.84 under the standard normal curve. From the standard
normal table, P(*Z* &gt; 1.84) = 1 − 0.9671 = **0.0329**.

**Step 5 - Decide.** Since 0.0329 ≤ 0.05, we **reject *H*<sub>0</sub>**. The
data provide sufficient evidence at the 5% level to conclude that this class's
mean is above the district average.

### One-tailed and two-tailed p-values

How you find the p-value depends on the alternative hypothesis (Illowsky & Dean,
2023, Section 9.2):

- **Right-tailed** (*H*<sub>1</sub>: *μ* &gt; μ<sub>0</sub>): p-value = P(*Z*
  &gt; *z*).
- **Left-tailed** (*H*<sub>1</sub>: *μ* &lt; μ<sub>0</sub>): p-value =
  P(*Z* &lt; *z*).
- **Two-tailed** (*H*<sub>1</sub>: *μ* ≠ μ<sub>0</sub>): p-value = 2 × P(*Z*
  &gt; |*z*|).

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think "a small p-value proves the
 alternative hypothesis". A small p-value means the observed sample mean would
 be surprising *if the null were true*. It does not measure the size of the
 difference (3.5 marks may be statistically significant but practically small),
 and it does not rule out other explanations: the class may have had a
 particularly strong year group, extra tutoring, or a teacher who also gave
 extra practice. The test judges the number, not the cause.

### What "reject" really means

Rejecting *H*<sub>0</sub> means the data are hard to reconcile with the claim
that the true mean equals μ<sub>0</sub>. It does **not** mean the effect is
large, important, or caused by the factor the teacher suspects. A result can
be statistically significant but trivial in practice. When you report a test
result to a head teacher, always state the direction and the actual difference
(x̄ − μ<sub>0</sub>), not just "significant" or "not significant".

## Activity: Carry out a z-test in small groups

Work in groups of three. About 20 minutes.

1. Each group invents a teaching-data scenario: a known district mean and
   standard deviation, a class size, and a sample mean. The numbers should be
   realistic (a district mean between 50 and 80, a standard deviation between 8
   and 15, a class size between 20 and 50).
2. State *H*<sub>0</sub> and *H*<sub>1</sub> and choose *α* = 0.05.
3. Compute the standard error, the test statistic, and the p-value (use a
   standard normal table or a calculator). State the decision.
4. Swap your scenario with the next group and check their work. Discuss any
   disagreement. Groups that chose a two-tailed test while the next group
   thinks it should be one-tailed are the most productive to argue about.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State the three conditions for a z-test for a mean.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why the standard error gets smaller as the sample
   size gets larger, and what that means for the test.
3. *(Apply)* A district mean is 70 with σ = 15. A class of 49 pupils has a mean
   of 74. Compute the test statistic for *H*<sub>1</sub>: *μ* &gt; 70 and find
   the p-value.
4. *(Apply)* For the same scenario with a two-tailed alternative, what would
   the p-value be and what decision would you make at *α* = 0.05?

## Summary

A z-test for a mean applies when the population standard deviation is known and
the sample is large enough. Compute the test statistic *z* = (x̄ − μ<sub>0</sub>)
/ (σ / √n), find the p-value from the standard normal curve (one-tailed or
two-tailed depending on the alternative), and reject *H*<sub>0</sub> when the
p-value is at most *α*. A statistically significant result means the data are
surprising under the null, not that the effect is large or important.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can state when a z-test is appropriate and when it is not.
- [ ] I can compute the test statistic from the sample mean, the null mean, the known σ and the sample size.
- [ ] I can find the p-value for a one-tailed and a two-tailed z-test from the standard normal table.
- [ ] I can explain what "reject the null hypothesis" does and does not mean in plain language.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher for a recent class test average and the school or
subject average over the past few years (if one exists). Note the class size.
Treat the school average as μ<sub>0</sub> and the past-year standard deviation
(if available) as *σ*. State hypotheses, carry out a z-test, and write two
sentences about what the result means for the teacher, including what it does
not prove.

## Summative task

A school in Ghotki reports that the district mean on the Grade 6 English test
is 60 marks with a standard deviation of 14 marks. A teacher's class of 44
pairs pupils has a mean of 63.5 marks. The teacher claims the class is truly
above the district average.

a) State *H*<sub>0</sub> and *H*<sub>1</sub>, choose *α*, and compute the
   test statistic and p-value.

b) State the decision and explain what it means in one sentence.

c) In 80-120 words, analyze what the head teacher can and cannot conclude
   from this result. Address whether the test shows the teacher's method is
   better, and what other explanations remain possible.
