## A real classroom situation

A newly trained teacher in Jacobabad has been using group work in her Class 5
science class. She wants to know whether the class mean differs from the
district mean of 58 marks. She tests her class of 15 pupils and finds a mean of
62 and a standard deviation of 7.5 marks from her own sample. She does not know
the district's population standard deviation, and the class is small. A **t-test**
handles exactly this situation: a small sample and an unknown population spread.

## Explanation

### When to use a t-test

A **t-test for a mean** applies when (Illowsky & Dean, 2023, Section 9.4):

1. The data are a random sample.
2. The population standard deviation *σ* is **unknown**.
3. Either the population is approximately normal or the sample, though small,
   shows no strong skew or outliers.

The t-test replaces the unknown *σ* with the sample standard deviation *s*. The
cost is a heavier-tailed distribution: the **t-distribution**. It has one
parameter, the **degrees of freedom**, df = *n* − 1. As df grows, the
t-distribution approaches the normal. With df ≥ 30 the two are close enough
that a z-test gives nearly the same answer. Below df = 30 the difference
matters.

In the Jacobabad science example, *σ* is unknown, *n* = 15 gives df = 14, and
the sample is small, so a t-test is the right choice.

### The test statistic

The **t-test statistic** has the same structure as the *z*, but uses *s* instead
of *σ*:

> *t* = (x̄ − μ<sub>0</sub>) / (s / √n)

The denominator *s* / √n is the **standard error of the mean** estimated from
the sample.

### Step-by-step: the Jacobabad science class

**Step 1 - State the hypotheses.** The teacher wants to know whether the mean
*differs* (not specifically whether it is higher):

- *H*<sub>0</sub>: *μ* = 58.
- *H*<sub>1</sub>: *μ* ≠ 58 (two-tailed test).

**Step 2 - Choose the significance level.** *α* = 0.05.

**Step 3 - Compute the test statistic.**

Standard error = *s* / √n = 7.5 / √15 = 7.5 / 3.87 = 1.94.

*t* = (62 − 58) / 1.94 = 4 / 1.94 = **2.06**.

Degrees of freedom = *n* − 1 = 15 − 1 = **14**.

**Step 4 - Find the p-value.** For a two-tailed test with df = 14, the p-value
is 2 × P(*T* &gt; 2.06). From a t-table, the critical value for df = 14 at
*α* = 0.05 (two-tailed) is 2.145. Since 2.06 &lt; 2.145, the p-value is
slightly **greater than 0.05**. (Using software, the exact two-tailed p-value
is about 0.058.)

**Step 5 - Decide.** Since the p-value &gt; 0.05, we **do not reject
*H*<sub>0</sub>**. The data do not provide sufficient evidence at the 5% level
to conclude that the class mean differs from the district mean.

### Why the thicker tails matter

The t-distribution has thicker tails than the normal because using *s* instead
of *σ* adds uncertainty. For a given sample result, the p-value from a t-test
is usually larger than the p-value from a z-test on the same data. This is
conservative: it makes it harder to reject the null when the population spread
is unknown. **Never use a z-test with *s* in place of *σ*** for a small sample.
That understates the p-value and can produce a false sense of significance.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think "a small p-value proves the
 alternative hypothesis". As with the z-test, a small p-value only means the
 observed sample mean is surprising if the null is true. It does not measure
 the size of the difference or prove the alternative. A second error is using
 the z-test when *σ* is unknown and *n* is small. The z-test assumes *σ* is
 known; replacing it with *s* without switching to the t-distribution gives a
 p-value that is too small. A third error is treating the significance level
 as a bright line: a p-value of 0.058 and a p-value of 0.045 are not
 meaningfully different, even though one is "not significant" and the other is
 "significant" at *α* = 0.05. Report the actual p-value and let the reader judge.

### Reporting the result

A complete statement of a t-test result includes: the test statistic, the
degrees of freedom, the p-value, and the decision, followed by a plain-language
conclusion.

> "A one-sample t-test gave *t*(14) = 2.06, *p* = 0.058. At the 5% level the
> null hypothesis is not rejected. The data do not provide enough evidence to
> conclude that the class mean differs from the district mean of 58. A larger
> sample might reach a different conclusion."

## Activity: z or t? decision practice

Work in pairs. About 15 minutes.

1. Each pair writes four short scenarios describing a teacher testing a claim
   about a class mean. For two of them, the population standard deviation is
   known (use a z-test). For the other two, it is unknown and the sample is
   small (use a t-test).
2. Swap scenarios with the next pair. For each scenario, decide whether a
   z-test or a t-test is appropriate and give a reason in one sentence.
3. For one of the t-test scenarios, compute the test statistic and degrees of
   freedom from invented numbers.
4. As a class, discuss any scenario where pairs disagreed. Scenarios where the
   sample is large but *σ* is unknown are the most productive to debate (a
   t-test still works, but with a large df it gives nearly the same answer as a
   z-test).

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State two conditions that make a t-test the right choice
   instead of a z-test.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why the t-distribution has thicker tails than the
   standard normal, and what that does to the p-value for the same test
   statistic.
3. *(Apply)* A sample of 10 pupils has a mean of 45 and a standard deviation
   of 8. The null hypothesis is *μ* = 40. Compute *t* and the degrees of
   freedom for a two-tailed test.
4. *(Apply)* A t-table gives the critical value for df = 9 at *α* = 0.05
   (two-tailed) as 2.262. For the *t* you computed in item 3, state the
   decision and explain what it means in one sentence.

## Summary

A t-test applies when the population standard deviation is unknown, especially
with a small sample. It replaces *σ* with the sample standard deviation *s* and
uses the t-distribution with df = *n* − 1 instead of the normal. The thicker
tails of the t-distribution make it harder to reject the null than a z-test on
the same data, which is the correct penalty for the extra uncertainty. Report
the test statistic, degrees of freedom, p-value, and decision, and interpret the
result in plain language.

- [ ] I can compute the t-test statistic and the degrees of freedom from a sample.

- [ ] I can explain why using the z-test with *s* instead of *σ* for a small sample is wrong.

## Try this at your practicum school

With your cooperating teacher's permission, collect a small set of recent test
marks (10-20 pupils) from one class, with no names. Compute the mean and
standard deviation. Choose a comparison value (a school average, a district
figure, or a pass mark). Carry out a t-test, report *t*, df and the p-value,
and write two sentences about what the result means and what it does not prove.

## Summative task

A teacher in Dadu district claims her Class 6 math mean differs from the
district mean of 55 marks. She tests a random sample of 12 pupils: the sample
mean is 59 and the sample standard deviation is 6.5 marks.

a) State *H*<sub>0</sub> and *H*<sub>1</sub>, explain why a t-test is the
   right choice, and compute *t* and the degrees of freedom.

b) The t-table gives the critical value for df = 11 at *α* = 0.05 (two-tailed)
   as 2.201. State the decision and explain what it means.

c) In 80-120 words, analyze what the teacher can and cannot conclude. Address
   whether the result shows her teaching is better, what the p-value means in
   plain language, and what other explanations remain possible.
