## A real classroom situation

At a teacher-training college in Jamshoro, two trainees are planning a
mathematics lesson. They have written four problems for their Class 9 pupils:

1. How many three-digit codes can be set on the school's stationery cupboard
   lock, where digits may repeat?
2. In how many ways can 5 pupils stand in a line for a class photograph if
   two best friends insist on standing together?
3. How many ways can a cleanliness committee of 2 boys and 2 girls be chosen
   from 5 boys and 4 girls?
4. How many committees of 3 can be chosen from those same 9 pupils if the
   committee must include at least one girl?

The first trainee says, "These are all just combinations." The second says,
"No, they are all permutations." Both are wrong, and neither can explain why.
What both are missing is a method for deciding.

## Explanation

### Choosing the method: order and repetition

Permutations and combinations are not interchangeable. For the same items
they give different answers, so the first job in any counting problem is to
decide which situation you are in. Two questions settle most school problems
(the flowchart above):

1. **Does order matter?** Use the swap test from Topic 8.2. If swapping two
   chosen items gives a different outcome, order matters.
2. **Can an item be used again?** In a lock code a digit can appear twice;
   in a line of pupils a pupil cannot stand in two places.

That gives three methods:

| Order matters? | Repetition? | Method | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yes | Yes | multiplicative rule, n × n × ... = nʳ | 3-digit lock code: 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000 |
| Yes | No | P(n, r), or n! if all n are arranged | 3-digit code with no repeated digit: P(10, 3) = 720 |
| No | No | C(n, r) | 3 digits chosen as a set: C(10, 3) = 120 |

**Common misconception.** "Permutations and combinations are interchangeable;
just use whichever formula you remember." The table shows three different
answers, 1,000, 720 and 120, for three different questions about the same ten
digits. The numbers are linked (720 = 120 × 3!, because each set of 3 digits
can be ordered in 3! ways), but they count different things. Choosing the
wrong method does not give a slightly wrong answer; it answers a different
question.

**Worked example 1 (the lock).** A lock code is an ordered string, and the
problem says digits may repeat: 10³ = **1,000** codes. If the school wanted a
safer rule of "no repeated digit", the count would fall to P(10, 3) = 720.

### Combining methods: stages, restrictions and cases

Real problems often need more than one step. Unit 7's two rules still apply:
**multiply** across stages done one after another, **add** across separate
cases that cannot both happen (Abramson, 2021).

**Worked example 2 (the committee of 2 boys and 2 girls).** This is done in
two stages. Choose the boys: C(5, 2) = 10. Choose the girls: C(4, 2) = 6. Every
pair of boys can go with every pair of girls, so multiply: 10 × 6 = **60**
committees.

**Worked example 3 (the photograph with two friends together).** Treat the two
friends as a single block. Then there are 4 "units" to arrange: 4! = 24
orders. Inside the block the friends can stand in 2! = 2 orders. Multiply:
24 × 2 = **48** lines. Compare this with 5! = 120 for no restriction: the
restriction reduces the count, as it should.

**Worked example 4 (at least one girl).** "At least one" problems have many
cases (1 girl, 2 girls, 3 girls). It is quicker to count everything and take
away what is not allowed:

- All committees of 3 from 9 pupils: C(9, 3) = 84.
- Committees with no girl (all boys): C(5, 3) = 10.
- Committees with at least one girl: 84 - 10 = **74**.

You can check this by adding the cases: 1 girl, C(4, 1) × C(5, 2) = 40; 2 girls,
C(4, 2) × C(5, 1) = 30; 3 girls, C(4, 3) = 4. And 40 + 30 + 4 = 74.

### Checking that an answer makes sense

A counting answer is easy to get wrong and hard to "see", so build in checks:

- **List a small case.** Shrink the problem (3 pupils instead of 9) and list
  the outcomes. If the formula and the list disagree, the method is wrong.
- **Compare with the unrestricted count.** A restriction ("must stand
  together", "no repeats") can only lower the count.
- **Compare combinations with permutations.** For the same n and r,
  C(n, r) is never larger than P(n, r).
- **Ask whether the size is believable.** If a class of 40 could be split
  into groups in "3 ways", or a committee of 2 from 4 people in "1,000 ways",
  something has gone wrong.

These checks are the "evidence-based" part of quantitative reasoning: you do
not just produce a number, you give reasons to trust it.

## Activity: Sort, solve and swap

Work in groups of three. About 20 minutes.

1. Your tutor gives each group six problem cards drawn from school life
   (timetables, teams, seating, codes, prizes, committees).
2. Sort the cards into three piles using the flowchart: multiplicative rule,
   permutation, combination. Some cards need two methods; put them on the
   pile of their main step and note the second.
3. Solve each card. One member solves, one checks with a small listed case,
   and one writes the reason for the choice of method. Rotate roles for each
   card.
4. Swap your cards with another group and mark each other's work. Discuss any
   card where the two groups chose different methods.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State the two questions you ask to decide between the
   multiplicative rule, a permutation and a combination.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why "at least one" problems are often easier to
   solve by subtracting from the total.
3. *(Apply)* A school has 6 male and 5 female teachers. How many ways can a
   4-member examination committee with exactly 2 men and 2 women be chosen?
   Show each stage.

## Summary

Before counting, decide what kind of problem it is. If order matters and items
can repeat, use the multiplicative rule, nʳ. If order matters without repeats,
use P(n, r), or n! when everything is arranged. If order does not matter, use
C(n, r). Multiply across stages, add across separate cases, treat "must be
together" items as a block, and count "at least one" by taking away the cases
that are not allowed. Always check the answer against a small listed case and
against common sense.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can use the order and repetition questions to choose a counting method, and give the reason.
- [ ] I can solve a problem that needs two stages, a restriction or an "at least one" condition.
- [ ] I can check a counting answer with a small case, a comparison and a size check.

## Try this at your practicum school

Look for a real decision in your practicum school that involves counting:
setting the order of exam papers across a week, forming house teams, picking
a quiz team with a captain, or choosing duty rosters. Write it as a counting
problem, decide the method with the flowchart, and solve it. Then write two or
three sentences on what the number tells the school, for example whether
"trying every option" is realistic or whether a rule is needed.

## Summative task

The Head of a primary school in Larkana must form a 3-member welfare committee
from 4 senior and 6 junior teachers. The committee will have a chair, a
secretary and a treasurer, and must include at least one senior teacher.

a) A colleague says, "It is a committee, so the answer is C(10, 3) = 120."
   Analyze what is wrong with this answer. Name the two features of the
   problem the colleague has ignored.
b) Solve the problem correctly. (Hint: first count the 3-person groups that
   include at least one senior teacher, then count the ways to give out the
   three posts.)
c) In 80-120 words, evaluate whether the method you used is the simplest one,
   and describe one check you would use to trust the final number.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Diagnosis (Analyze)* | 0-3: identifies both the distinct posts (order matters) and the "at least one senior" condition, with reasons / one feature with reason / features named without reason / none. |
| *Solution (Apply)* | 0-3: C(10, 3) - C(6, 3) = 100 groups, × 3! = 600 assignments, correct and clearly staged / minor error / major error / none. |
| *Evaluation and check (Evaluate)* | 0-3: compares with another valid route (for example P(10, 3) - P(6, 3) = 720 - 120 = 600) and gives a sensible check / one of these / vague / none. |
