## A real classroom situation

The head teacher of a girls' secondary school in Nawabshah must send 3 of her
8 teachers to a two-day training workshop in Karachi. A trainee teacher in the
staff room wants to know how many different groups could go. Using Topic 8.1,
she writes 8 × 7 × 6 = 336. A senior teacher shakes her head: "It does not
matter who is chosen first. Ms Ayesha, Ms Farah and Ms Saima are the same group
whichever order you name them in." The trainee's 336 counts every group more
than once. How many times, and what is the real answer?

This topic shows you how to count selections like this, where only the group

## Explanation

### Selections: when order does not matter

A <Glossary term="Combination" /> is a selection of items in which order does
not matter. Only *which* items are chosen counts, not the order in which they
are named. A workshop group, a cricket team picked from a squad, or the three
questions a pupil attempts in an exam are all combinations. Lane (2023,
Chapter 5) makes the contrast with permutations directly: in combinations,
order does not count.

**Common misconception.** Trainees often treat any committee as a
permutation, because the members are chosen "one after another". The order of
choosing does not decide the matter; the *result* does. If the three teachers
in the workshop group all do the same job, then the group of Ayesha, Farah and Saima is
one result however it was reached, so it is a combination. If instead the group had
a leader, a note-taker and a presenter, swapping two people would change the
result, and it would be a permutation. Ask: "If I swap two chosen people, is it
a different outcome?" Yes means permutation; no means combination.

### From permutations to combinations

**Worked example 1.** Count the workshop groups in two steps.

1. Count ordered choices as if order mattered: P(8, 3) = 8 × 7 × 6 = 336.
2. Each group of 3 teachers appears in that count once for every order of its
   members, and 3 people can be ordered in 3! = 6 ways (the first figure above).
   So divide: 336 ÷ 6 = **56** different groups.

The same reasoning gives the general rule. The number of combinations of n
distinct items taken r at a time is written **C(n, r)** (also ⁿCᵣ or "n choose
r"):

**C(n, r) = P(n, r) ÷ r! = n! ÷ (r! × (n - r)!)**

The extra r! in the bottom removes the r! orders of each chosen group
(Abramson, 2021). Check: C(8, 3) = 40,320 ÷ (6 × 120) = 40,320 ÷ 720 = 56.

**Worked example 2.** Three pupils, Ali, Sana and Bilal, volunteer and the
teacher needs two of them to carry the class register to the office. The
possible pairs are Ali and Sana, Ali and Bilal, and Sana and Bilal:
C(3, 2) = 3.
If instead one carries the register and the other carries the attendance
sheet, the jobs differ and P(3, 2) = 6. Listing a small case like this is the
best check that you have picked the right formula.

<Glossary term="Selection" /> problems from school life are everywhere:

- Choosing 11 players for a cricket team from a squad of 14:
  C(14, 11) = 364.
- A board examination paper that says "attempt any 5 questions out of 8":
  C(8, 5) = 56 different sets of questions.
- Ten teachers at a staff meeting each shake hands once with every other
  teacher: C(10, 2) = 45 handshakes.

### Choosing r is the same as leaving out n - r

Every time you choose 11 players from 14, you also decide which 3 are left
out. So C(14, 11) = C(14, 3) = (14 × 13 × 12) ÷ (3 × 2 × 1) = 364, and the
second form is far quicker to work out. In general **C(n, r) = C(n, n - r)**.
In the examination example, choosing which 5 questions to attempt is the same
as choosing which 3 to skip: C(8, 5) = C(8, 3) = 56. Two special cases follow:
C(n, n) = 1 (take everything) and C(n, 0) = 1 (take nothing), which again
relies on 0! = 1.

A useful check follows from the formula. For the same n and r, a combination
count is never larger than the permutation count, because each group is
counted once instead of r! times. If your combination answer is larger, a
step has gone wrong.

## Activity: Committee or line-up?

Work in pairs. About 15 minutes.

1. Each partner writes four short school selection situations on separate slips
   of paper. Make two of them situations where order matters (posts with
   different jobs, positions in a race) and two where it does not (a group, a
   team, a set of books).
2. Swap slips. For each slip, your partner decides "permutation" or
   "combination", gives the swap test reason, and computes the count.
3. Discuss any slip where you disagreed. Rewrite its wording so that the
   answer is no longer in doubt.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Write the formula for C(n, r) and state what n and r stand for.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why C(5, 2) is smaller than P(5, 2), and by what
   factor.
3. *(Apply)* A library committee of 4 teachers is to be chosen from 9 teachers,
   with no special posts. How many different committees are possible? Show your
   working, and use C(n, r) = C(n, n - r) if it helps.

## Summary

A combination is a selection in which order does not matter: only the group
counts. Because each group of r items can be ordered in r! ways,
C(n, r) = P(n, r) ÷ r! = n! ÷ (r! × (n - r)!). Choosing r items is the same as
leaving out n - r, so C(n, r) = C(n, n - r). The swap test decides between the
two methods: if swapping two chosen items changes the outcome, count
permutations; if not, count combinations.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can use the swap test to decide whether order matters in a selection.
- [ ] I can compute C(n, r) and explain why it divides the permutation count by r!.
- [ ] I can use C(n, r) = C(n, n - r) to shorten a calculation.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher how pupils are chosen for a school activity, such
as a quiz team, a cleanliness committee or a group for a science fair. Count
how many different groups are possible with C(n, r). Then check whether the
roles inside the group make order matter, and if they do, recount with
P(n, r). Share both numbers with the teacher and explain the difference.

## Summative task

A school in Thatta will send 4 pupils from a Class 8 debating club of 10 to an
inter-school contest.

a) Compute the number of ways to choose the 4 pupils if they form one team with
   no special roles.
b) Compute the number of ways if the 4 pupils will speak first, second, third
   and fourth.
c) In 80-120 words, analyze the link between your two answers and explain to a
   parent who thinks the second answer "must be a mistake" why it is so much
   larger.
