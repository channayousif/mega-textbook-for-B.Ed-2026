## A real classroom situation

A Class 5 teacher in Hyderabad is preparing the morning assembly. Three
pupils, Ali, Sana and Bilal, will stand in a line at the front: the first
recites the Qur'an verse, the second reads the thought for the day and the
third leads the national anthem. Sana asks, "Can we take turns, so that every
day we stand in a different order?" The teacher wonders how many school days
that would last. With three pupils she can list the orders on the board. Then
the head teacher adds two more pupils to the line, and listing suddenly looks
much harder.

This topic shows you how to count such arrangements without listing them all.

## Explanation

### Arrangements: when order matters

A <Glossary term="Permutation" /> is an arrangement of items in a definite
order. "Ali, Sana, Bilal" and "Sana, Ali, Bilal" use the same three pupils, but
they are different permutations, because a different pupil recites the verse.
Lane (2023, Chapter 5) puts it simply: in permutations, order counts.

**Worked example 1.** How many ways can the three pupils stand in the line?
Use the multiplicative rule from Unit 7, one place at a time:

- First place: any of the 3 pupils can stand there.
- Second place: 2 pupils are left.
- Third place: only 1 pupil is left.

So there are 3 × 2 × 1 = **6** arrangements. The first figure above lists all
six, so you can check that none is missing and none is repeated. Sana's plan
lasts six school days.

With five pupils the same reasoning gives 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = **120**
arrangements, which is about six months of school days. Listing 120 lines by
hand is slow and easy to get wrong; multiplying is quick and certain.

### Factorial notation

Products like 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 appear so often that they have a short name.
The <Glossary term="Factorial" /> of a whole number n, written **n!** and read
"n factorial", is the product of all whole numbers from n down to 1:

- 3! = 3 × 2 × 1 = 6
- 4! = 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24
- 5! = 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 120
- 6! = 720, and 10! = 3,628,800

So **n distinct items can be arranged in a line in n! ways**. By agreement,
**0! = 1**. This is not a trick: there is exactly one way to arrange nothing
(do nothing), and the agreement makes the formula below work in every case
(Abramson, 2021).

**Common misconception.** Many trainees read 5! as 5 × 5 or as 5⁵. It is
neither. A factorial multiplies a number by every smaller whole number down to
1, so 5! = 120, while 5 × 5 = 25 and 5⁵ = 3,125. Ask what each factor stands
for: in 5! the factors 5, 4, 3, 2, 1 are the shrinking number of pupils still
available for each place in the line. Nobody can stand in two places at once,
so the choices go down by one each time.

### Arranging some of the items: P(n, r)

Often we arrange only some of the items. Suppose 8 prefects are candidates, and
the school will choose a head prefect and a deputy head prefect. The posts are
different, so order matters: Hina as head with Kamran as deputy is not the same
result as Kamran as head with Hina as deputy.

**Worked example 2.** Head prefect: 8 choices. Deputy: 7 choices left. Total:
8 × 7 = **56** ways.

The number of permutations of n distinct items taken r at a time is written
**P(n, r)** (many textbooks, including Chaudhry and Kamal (2008), write it as
ⁿPᵣ). It is the product of r factors, starting at n and going down by one:

**P(n, r) = n × (n - 1) × ... × (n - r + 1) = n! ÷ (n - r)!**

Check it with the prefects: P(8, 2) = 8! ÷ 6! = 40,320 ÷ 720 = 56. The division
simply cancels the 6 × 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 for the pupils who are not chosen.

**Worked example 3.** A district speech competition in Sukkur has 10
finalists. How many ways can the first, second and third prizes be awarded?
P(10, 3) = 10 × 9 × 8 = **720**. When r = n, all items are arranged and
P(n, n) = n! ÷ 0! = n!, which is why 0! must equal 1.

### When some items are alike

The formula above assumes every item is different. If some items are
identical, some arrangements look the same and must not be counted twice. A
teacher arranging the letters of KARACHI on a display board has 7 letters, but
the two A's are identical, so swapping them gives no new word. The count is
7! ÷ 2! = 5,040 ÷ 2 = **2,520**. In general, divide n! by the factorial of the
size of each group of identical items (Abramson, 2021).

## Activity: How many ways to line up?

Work in groups of four. About 15 minutes.

1. Choose three members of your group. Physically stand in a line in every
   possible order while a fourth member records each order. Did you find six?
   How did you make sure you missed none?
2. Now add the fourth member. Before you try, predict the number of orders
   using factorials. Test your prediction by listing only the orders that start
   with one particular person, then multiply by 4.
3. Together, write one school situation in which order clearly matters (posts,
   prizes, turns, a timetable) and compute its count with P(n, r).

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Write the meaning of 6! as a product and give its value. State
   what 0! equals.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why choosing a class monitor and an assistant monitor
   from 30 pupils is a permutation, using the word "order".
3. *(Apply)* A teacher has 7 different charts and space on the wall for 4 of
   them in a row. How many different wall displays are possible? Show the
   formula and the working.

## Summary

A permutation is an arrangement in which order matters. n distinct items can be
arranged in n! ways, where n! multiplies n by every smaller whole number down
to 1, and 0! = 1 by agreement. Arranging r of n distinct items gives
P(n, r) = n! ÷ (n - r)!, which is just the multiplicative rule with fewer
choices at each step. When some items are identical, divide by the factorials
of the repeated groups.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can work out a factorial and explain what each of its factors stands for.
- [ ] I can explain why two arrangements of the same items are different permutations.
- [ ] I can use P(n, r) to count arrangements of some of the items, and check the result by listing a small case.

## Try this at your practicum school

With your cooperating teacher's permission, find one real arrangement problem
in the school: the order of speakers in an assembly, the order in which groups
present, or the duties on a weekly classroom rota. Count the possible
arrangements with a factorial or with P(n, r). Then suggest how the teacher
could use the count, for example to make sure every pupil gets a fair turn at
each duty over the term.

## Summative task

A school in Mirpurkhas has 6 teachers available for 3 different duties at the
annual sports day: starter, timekeeper and announcer. Each teacher can do at
most one duty.

a) Compute the number of ways the duties can be assigned, using P(n, r), and
   show the same answer with the multiplicative rule.
b) A trainee lists the possibilities and finds only 20. Explain what mistake
   the trainee has most likely made.
c) In 60-100 words, analyze how the answer would change if two teachers were
   identical twins whom nobody can tell apart, and whether that change matters
   for the school.
