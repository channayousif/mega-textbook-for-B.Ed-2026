## A real classroom situation

The same teacher from Topic 12.1 has now plotted her scatter plot and sees the
cloud of points rising from left to right. She says, "So more study hours cause
higher scores. I will tell the parents." You hesitate. The plot shows a pattern, but
does it prove that one thing causes the other? What exactly can you say, and what
must you hold back from saying?

## Explanation

### Three descriptors of a relationship

When you look at a scatter plot, describe what you see along three dimensions:

**Direction** is the overall slope of the cloud. A <Glossary term="Positive correlation" /> (or positive relationship) means the cloud rises from left to
right: as one variable increases, the other tends to increase. The teacher's plot
shows a positive direction. A <Glossary term="Negative correlation" /> means the
cloud falls from left to right: as one variable increases, the other tends to
decrease. A plot of absences against test scores would show a negative direction.
A plot with no discernible slope shows no directional relationship.

**Strength** is how closely the points follow a pattern. Strong linear
relationships show points clustered tightly around an invisible straight line;
weak linear relationships show more scatter around that line. Points tightly
following a curve can show a strong non-linear relationship even when no straight
line fits well. Do not confuse strength with direction: a plot can
be strongly negative or weakly positive.

**Form** is the shape of the pattern. For this course the main distinction is
between a roughly straight-line pattern (linear) and a curved one (non-linear).
The teacher's cloud looks roughly linear. If the cloud curved upward at one end,
that would be a non-linear form, which requires more advanced tools than this
course covers.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees believe that any visible pattern in a
scatter plot proves one variable causes the other. This is not so. A scatter plot
shows association, not causation. Ice cream sales and drowning incidents both rise
in summer; the scatter plot of one against the other shows a strong positive
relationship, but ice cream does not cause drowning. A third variable (temperature)
drives both. Always say "is associated with" rather than "causes" unless you have
evidence beyond the plot.

### Outliers in a scatter plot

An <Glossary term="Outlier" /> in a bivariate plot is a point that falls far from
the general pattern of the rest. Outliers matter because they can distort the
direction and strength you would report, and because they often point to a data
error or to a case that follows a different rule.

To spot an outlier, look for a point that is far from the main cloud in either
direction. Ask whether the value is plausible. In the teacher's data, a pupil who
studies 10 hours but scores 30 would be an outlier; it might be a recording error,
or it might be a pupil with a genuine difficulty worth investigating. Either way,
describe the relationship both with and without the outlier, and note its presence.

### What a scatter plot cannot tell you

A scatter plot can show that two variables are associated and describe the
direction, strength and form of that association. It cannot tell you:

1. That one variable causes the other (causation requires more evidence).
2. Whether a third, unseen variable is driving both.
3. What happens outside the range of the data you have plotted.

Hold these limits in mind every time you interpret a plot. They protect you from
the most common error in statistical reasoning: reading more into the picture than
it actually shows.

## Activity: Describe three plots

Work in groups of three. About 15 minutes.

Your instructor will give you three prepared scatter plots (or sketch them on the
board): one showing a strong positive relationship, one showing a weak negative
relationship, and one with no clear pattern. For each plot:

1. State the direction (positive, negative, or none).
2. State the strength (strong, moderate, or weak).
3. State the form (linear, non-linear, or no discernible form).
4. Identify any outlier, if present.

Each group writes their four answers for each plot on a slip of paper, then the
class compares answers. Where groups disagree, discuss the reasoning until you
reach agreement.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the three descriptors used to describe the relationship shown in a scatter plot.
2. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between a strong positive relationship and a weak positive relationship.
3. *(Apply)* A scatter plot of weekly exercise time (hours) and resting heart rate (beats per minute) shows a cloud falling from left to right with points spread fairly widely. Describe the direction and strength of the relationship.

## Summary

Describe a scatter plot's pattern along three dimensions: direction (positive,
negative, or none), strength (tight or spread-out cloud), and form (linear or
non-linear). Note outliers and describe their effect. Most importantly, a scatter
plot shows association, not causation: say "is associated with" rather than
"causes" unless you have evidence beyond the plot.
