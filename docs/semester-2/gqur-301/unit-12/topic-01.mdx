## A real classroom situation

A mathematics teacher at a government high school in Sukkur keeps two records for each
of her 10 pupils at the end of a term: the number of hours each pupil reported
studying mathematics at home in a typical week, and the score each pupil obtained on
the term mathematics test (out of 100). She suspects the two are related but cannot
see the pattern from two separate lists. She asks you, "Can I show this on one
diagram?"

## Explanation

### What makes data bivariate

All the data you have worked with so far in this course have been **univariate**: one
record per case. A list of test marks is univariate, one mark per pupil. A scatter
plot needs **bivariate data**: two records, of the same kind, linked case by case.
Each row gives a pair of values for one case. In the teacher's record, one row
gives, for pupil A, 6 study hours and a score of 58. The pair belongs together;
separating the two numbers destroys the information.

The two variables in a bivariate pair are not equal in role. One is treated as the
<Glossary term="Explanatory variable" /> (the variable you believe may explain or
predict the other); the other is the <Glossary term="Response variable" /> (the
outcome you want to understand). In the teacher's record, study hours is the
explanatory variable and test score is the response variable, because the question is
"does study time help explain the score?" The choice matters for how you plot and
interpret the data, but it does not prove causation (a point Topic 12.2 returns to).

### Plotting the points

A <Glossary term="Scatter plot" /> puts one variable on the horizontal axis and the
other on the vertical axis, then plots one point for each case at the coordinates
given by its pair of values. The convention is to place the explanatory variable on
the horizontal axis and the response variable on the vertical axis.

For the teacher's data:

1. Label the horizontal axis "Study hours per week" and mark a scale from 0 to the
   maximum value (say, 0 to 12).
2. Label the vertical axis "Test score (out of 100)" and mark a scale from 0 to 100.
3. For each pupil, find the horizontal position from the study-hours value, then
   move vertically to the test-score value, and place a point.
4. Do not join the points. A scatter plot shows the pattern, not a path.

**Worked example.** Pupil A studied 6 hours and scored 58. From the origin, move 6
units right on the horizontal axis, then 58 units up on the vertical axis, and place
a dot. Repeat for all 10 pupils. The result is a cloud of points; its shape,
direction and spread are what you will learn to read in Topic 12.2.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think that the two axes can be swapped
without consequence. In fact, swapping them changes which variable is treated as
explanatory, which changes how you interpret the plot. Keep the explanatory variable
on the horizontal axis and the response variable on the vertical axis unless you have
a specific reason to do otherwise.

### A small data set to practise with

The table below gives the 10 pupils' data. Use it to construct your own scatter
plot on graph paper.

| Pupil | Study hours/week | Test score/100 |
|---|---|---|
| A | 6 | 58 |
| B | 3 | 42 |
| C | 8 | 71 |
| D | 5 | 55 |
| E | 9 | 78 |
| F | 2 | 38 |
| G | 7 | 65 |
| H | 4 | 48 |
| I | 10 | 82 |
| J | 1 | 30 |

Plotting these points should produce a cloud that rises from left to right, which is
the visual signature of a positive relationship. Topic 12.2 teaches you to describe
that signature in words.

## Activity: Plot and compare

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

1. Using the data in the table above, each partner plots the scatter plot
   independently on graph paper, labelling both axes and giving the plot a title.
2. Compare your plots. Do the points fall in the same positions? If not, find the
   discrepancy and correct it.
3. Now swap the axes: plot study hours on the vertical axis and test score on the
   horizontal axis. Discuss: does the shape of the cloud change? Does the
   interpretation change? Write one sentence each.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* In your own words, state the difference between univariate and bivariate data in one sentence.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why, in a scatter plot, we place the explanatory variable on the horizontal axis and the response variable on the vertical axis.
3. *(Apply)* A nurse records, for each of eight patients, the number of hours of sleep the previous night and a wellness score the next morning. Identify the explanatory and response variables, and describe how you would set up the axes of the scatter plot.

## Summary

Bivariate data give two linked values per case. A scatter plot displays them as
points on two axes, with the explanatory variable on the horizontal axis and the
response variable on the vertical axis. Plot the points; do not join them. The
cloud's shape is the raw material for the interpretation in Topic 12.2.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can distinguish bivariate from univariate data.
- [ ] I can identify the explanatory and response variables in a bivariate question.
- [ ] I can construct a small scatter plot from a table of paired values.

## Try this at your practicum school

At your practicum school, collect a small bivariate data set of 8 to 12 paired values
that a teacher or administrator might plausibly record (for example, number of
absences and test score, or height in cm and weight in kg). Plot the scatter plot on
paper, label the axes, and bring it to the next session.

## Summative task

Using the data in the table above (study hours and test score), construct a scatter
plot on graph paper. Label both axes, give the plot a title, and then state the
explanatory and response variables. In two or three sentences, describe what the
overall shape of the cloud suggests about the relationship between study hours and
test score.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Plot construction* | 0-3: all 10 points correctly plotted and axes labelled / most points correct with minor errors / several errors. |
| *Variable roles* | 0-2: correctly identifies and explains explanatory and response variables / identifies without clear explanation / neither. |
| *Description* | 0-3: clear description of the cloud's direction and overall pattern / partial description / none. |
