## Unit summary

This unit, which follows Chapter 12 of the course guide, introduced bivariate
analysis: the study of two variables measured together on the same cases.

- Topic 12.1 gave the tool: the scatter plot. Bivariate data are pairs of values
  linked case by case. We place the explanatory variable on the horizontal axis
  and the response variable on the vertical axis, and plot one point per case. We
  do not join the points; the cloud is the object of study.
- Topic 12.2 taught you to read the cloud. Describe a relationship along three
  dimensions: direction (positive, negative, or none), strength (tight or
  spread-out), and form (linear or non-linear). Note outliers and describe their
  effect. Most importantly, a scatter plot shows association, not causation: it can
  tell you that two variables move together, but it cannot tell you that one causes
  the other.

With these skills you can look at a scatter plot and give a precise, honest
account of what it shows and what it does not, which is the foundation for the
correlation and regression ideas in Unit 13.

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

Choose the single best answer for each question.

1. *(Remember)* Bivariate data are data in which:
   - A) All values are numbers
   - B) Two variables are measured together on each case
   - C) Two separate groups are compared
   - D) The same variable is measured twice

2. *(Remember)* In a scatter plot, the explanatory variable is placed on which axis?
   - A) The vertical axis
   - B) The horizontal axis
   - C) Either axis, with no fixed rule
   - D) The left axis of a back-to-back plot

3. *(Apply)* A researcher records, for each of 12 pupils, the number of hours of
   television watched per week and the score on a reading test. Which variable is
   most naturally the explanatory variable?
   - A) Reading score, because it is the outcome
   - B) Hours of television watched, because it may help explain the score
   - C) Neither; both are response variables
   - D) The variable with the larger range

4. *(Apply)* You plot a scatter diagram and the points form a cloud that rises
   from left to right. What does this suggest?
   - A) No relationship between the variables
   - B) A negative relationship between the variables
   - C) A positive relationship between the variables
   - D) A non-linear relationship only

5. *(Understand)* A scatter plot shows a cloud that falls from left to right with
   points spread widely. How would you describe the relationship?
   - A) Strongly positive
   - B) Strongly negative
   - C) Weakly negative
   - D) No relationship

6. *(Understand)* An outlier in a scatter plot is a point that:
   - A) Lies exactly on the trend line
   - B) Is the first point you plotted
   - C) Falls far from the general pattern of the rest
   - D) Has the highest value on the horizontal axis

7. *(Apply)* A scatter plot of the number of absences and the test score for 20
   pupils shows a cloud falling from left to right. A trainee concludes "absences
   cause lower scores". What is the most careful statement?
   - A) The plot proves the trainee is right
   - B) The plot shows absences and lower scores are associated, but does not prove causation
   - C) The plot is worthless because it does not prove causation
   - D) The plot would prove causation if the points formed a line

8. *(Remember)* Which three descriptors are used to describe a relationship in a
   scatter plot?
   - A) Mean, median and mode
   - B) Range, variance and standard deviation
   - C) Direction, strength and form
   - D) Explanatory, response and outlier

9. *(Understand)* A scatter plot of height in cm and weight in kg for 15 adults
   shows a cloud that rises from left to right and is tightly clustered around an
   invisible line. How would you describe it?
   - A) Weakly positive and roughly linear
   - B) Strongly positive and roughly linear
   - C) Strongly negative and non-linear
   - D) No directional relationship

10. *(Apply)* A teacher wants to explore whether the distance a pupil travels to
    school relates to their attendance rate. How should she set up the scatter
    plot?
    - A) Distance on the horizontal axis, attendance on the vertical axis
    - B) Attendance on the horizontal axis, distance on the vertical axis
    - C) Either arrangement gives the same interpretation
    - D) A scatter plot cannot show this relationship

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

Answer each question in a few sentences or a short paragraph.

1. *(Understand)* Explain in your own words the difference between univariate and bivariate data, and give one example of each from a school.

2. *(Understand)* Explain why we do not join the points in a scatter plot, and what we look at instead.

3. *(Apply)* A health worker records, for each of 10 patients, the number of
   cigarettes smoked per day and a lung-function score. Identify the explanatory
   and response variables, describe how you would set up the axes, and state what
   direction you would expect the cloud to show.

4. *(Apply)* The table below gives paired data for 8 pupils (number of books read
   in a term, and a vocabulary test score out of 50). Without plotting, describe
   what you expect the scatter plot to look like and why.

   | Pupil | Books read | Vocabulary score |
   |---|---|---|
   | 1 | 2 | 22 |
   | 2 | 5 | 35 |
   | 3 | 1 | 20 |
   | 4 | 8 | 44 |
   | 5 | 3 | 28 |
   | 6 | 6 | 38 |
   | 7 | 4 | 32 |
   | 8 | 7 | 41 |

5. *(Analyze)* A scatter plot of weekly rainfall (mm) and umbrella sales in a
   shop shows a strong positive relationship. A shopkeeper says, "Rain causes
   people to buy umbrellas." Evaluate this claim: is it supported by the plot,
   and what else would you need to check before accepting it? (Answer in 4-6
   sentences.)

6. *(Analyze)* A scatter plot of study hours and test score for 20 pupils shows a
   positive relationship, but one point is far below the main cloud (a pupil who
   studied 9 hours but scored 25). Describe how this outlier affects the
   strength and direction you would report, and what you should do about it.
   (Answer in 4-6 sentences.)

7. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between a scatter plot that shows a
   strong positive relationship and one that shows a weak positive relationship.
   Use the idea of "how closely the points follow a single stream".

8. *(Apply)* A scatter plot of class size and average test score for 15 schools
   shows a cloud that falls from left to right, moderately spread out. Describe
   the relationship in words, using all three descriptors (direction, strength,
   form).

9. *(Analyze)* Two trainees look at the same scatter plot. Trainee A says, "The
   points are scattered everywhere, so there is no relationship." Trainee B says,
   "There is a weak positive relationship, because the cloud rises slightly on
   average." Explain which trainee is more careful and why. (Answer in 3-5
   sentences.)

10. *(Evaluate)* A school report claims: "Pupils who eat breakfast every day score
    15 per cent higher on tests. We plotted the data and the scatter plot shows a
    positive relationship." Evaluate this claim by describing two questions you
    would ask about the data behind it (for example, about the sample, the
    variables, or a third variable) and why each question matters. (Answer in 5-7
    sentences.)

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

Answer each question in a structured paragraph or set of paragraphs.

1. *(Analyze)* The data table below gives, for 10 government schools in a taluka,
   the average class size and the average score on a standardised mathematics
   test (out of 100).

   | School | Avg class size | Avg math score |
   |---|---|---|
   | A | 55 | 48 |
   | B | 30 | 62 |
   | C | 45 | 52 |
   | D | 20 | 71 |
   | E | 60 | 44 |
   | F | 35 | 58 |
   | G | 25 | 66 |
   | H | 50 | 50 |
   | I | 40 | 55 |
   | J | 15 | 75 |

   (a) State the explanatory and response variables and describe how you would
   set up the scatter plot.
   (b) Without plotting, describe the expected direction, strength and form of
   the relationship, and justify your answer from the data.
   (c) Identify any outlier or unusual case, if present.
   (Write 150-200 words for parts (b) and (c) together.)

2. *(Analyze)* A scatter plot of two variables shows a cloud that rises from left
   to right, is moderately spread out, and includes one point far above the main
   cloud. Describe the relationship using all three descriptors. Then explain how
   the outlier could affect the strength and direction you would report, and
   describe the relationship with the outlier removed. (Write 120-180 words.)

3. *(Evaluate)* A researcher claims that "watching more television causes lower
   reading scores in primary school pupils", and presents a scatter plot showing
   a negative relationship between weekly television hours and a reading test
   score. Evaluate this claim by discussing: (a) what the scatter plot actually
   shows; (b) at least one third variable that could explain the pattern; and (c)
   what additional evidence, beyond the scatter plot, would be needed to support
   a causal claim. (Write 150-200 words.)

4. *(Evaluate)* A head teacher wants to know whether the number of pupils in a
   class relates to the average attendance rate in that class. She has data for
   all 20 classes in her school. Describe how you would explore this question
   using a scatter plot (including variable roles, axes, and what to look for).
   Then state two limitations of what the scatter plot can tell her about this
   question. (Write 120-180 words.)

5. *(Evaluate)* Two scatter plots are drawn from the same bivariate data set. Plot
   A uses a scale from 0 to 100 on both axes; Plot B uses a scale from the
   minimum to the maximum value on each axis, making the cloud fill the plot.
   Describe how the choice of scale affects the visual impression of strength.
   Then explain why a scatter plot, however well drawn, cannot by itself prove
   that one variable causes the other, and give one concrete school-based
   example of two variables that are associated but not causally related.
   (Write 150-200 words.)

## Answers and marking guidance

### MCQ answer key

| Item | Answer | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | B | Bivariate data are two variables measured together on each case. |
| 2 | B | Convention places the explanatory variable on the horizontal axis. |
| 3 | B | Hours of television watched is the variable that may explain the score, so it is explanatory. |
| 4 | C | A cloud rising left to right suggests a positive relationship. |
| 5 | C | Falling left to right is negative; widely spread is weak. |
| 6 | C | An outlier falls far from the general pattern of the rest. |
| 7 | B | A scatter plot shows association, not proven causation. |
| 8 | C | The three descriptors are direction, strength and form. |
| 9 | B | Rising left to right is positive; tightly clustered is strong; roughly a line is linear. |
| 10 | A | Distance is the explanatory variable and goes on the horizontal axis; attendance (outcome) on the vertical. |

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. Univariate data give one value per case (for example, a list of test scores); bivariate data give two linked values per case (for example, study hours and test score for each pupil). (2 marks: 1 for the distinction, 1 for a valid example of each.)

2. Joining the points would imply a sequence or path from case to case, which is not the purpose. We look at the cloud as a whole: its direction, strength and form. (2 marks: 1 for why we do not join, 1 for what we look at.)

3. Explanatory: cigarettes smoked per day; response: lung-function score. Place cigarettes on the horizontal axis and lung function on the vertical axis. Expect a negative direction (more cigarettes, lower lung function). (3 marks: 1 for variable roles, 1 for axes, 1 for expected direction.)

4. Expect a positive direction (more books read, higher vocabulary score), roughly linear, moderately strong, because the pairs increase together in the table. (2 marks: 1 for the expected direction and form, 1 for justification from the data.)

5. The plot supports an association, not proven causation. Rain and umbrella sales may both rise because of a third factor (the monsoon season). To accept causation, you would need to rule out other explanations and check the timing and mechanism. (3 marks: 1 for distinguishing association from causation, 1 for a plausible third variable, 1 for additional evidence needed.)

6. The outlier (9 hours, score 25) pulls the cloud downward on the right, making the relationship appear weaker and possibly slightly less positive than it is for the rest. Report the direction and strength both with and without the outlier, and check whether the pupil's score is a recording error or a genuine case. (3 marks: 1 for the effect on strength, 1 for the effect on direction, 1 for the recommended action.)

7. A strong positive relationship has points clustered tightly around an invisible line rising left to right; a weak positive relationship has a wider, more spread-out cloud that still rises on average. The difference is how closely the points follow a single stream. (2 marks: 1 for each description.)

8. Direction: negative (as class size increases, average score tends to decrease). Strength: moderate (the cloud is spread out but the trend is visible). Form: roughly linear. (2 marks: 2 for all three correct; 1 for two correct.)

9. Trainee B is more careful. A rising average trend, even with scatter, indicates a weak positive relationship; saying "no relationship" ignores the overall slope. The scatter determines the strength, not the presence or absence of a relationship. (2 marks: 1 for choosing B, 1 for the reasoning.)

10. Any two relevant questions: Was the sample representative of all pupils, or only a group? How was "eating breakfast" measured (self-report, observation)? Could a third variable (for example, household income or parental education) explain both breakfast and test scores? Why it matters: a biased sample, a loose measure, or a third variable would weaken the causal claim. (3 marks: 1 for each relevant question, 1 for the explanation of why one matters.)
