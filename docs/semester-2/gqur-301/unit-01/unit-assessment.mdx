## Unit summary

This unit, which follows Chapter 1 of the course guide, showed that statistics
begins with the data itself, before any calculation.

- Topic 1.1 gave the data-type classification: qualitative data record
  categories; quantitative data are numbers that support arithmetic, and they
  are either discrete (counts with separate values) or continuous
  (measurements that can take any value in a range). The type decides which
  summaries and charts make sense.
- Topic 1.2 distinguished primary sources (data you collect yourself, for your
  own purpose) from secondary sources (data someone else collected for another
  purpose), and gave three practical checks for choosing between them: whether
  suitable data already exist, whether they match your group and question, and
  what time and resources you have.

With these two ideas you can look at any figure in a school register, a
government table or a research report and ask the right first questions: what
kind of data is this, and where did it come from?

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

Choose the single best answer for each question.

1. *(Remember)* Which of the following is an example of qualitative data?
   - A) The number of pupils in a class
   - B) The height of each pupil
   - C) The subject each pupil studies
   - D) The marks each pupil scored on a test

2. *(Remember)* Discrete quantitative data are distinguished from continuous
   quantitative data by the fact that discrete data:
   - A) Are always larger numbers
   - B) Are counts that take separate, distinct values
   - C) Cannot be shown in a table
   - D) Are always less accurate

3. *(Apply)* A researcher records the temperature of a classroom every hour for
   one week. What type of data is this?
   - A) Qualitative
   - B) Discrete quantitative
   - C) Continuous quantitative
   - D) Ordered qualitative

4. *(Apply)* A head teacher records each pupil's roll number. Why is the roll
   number qualitative data even though it is written as a number?
   - A) Because it is a whole number
   - B) Because adding or averaging roll numbers has no meaning
   - C) Because it was collected by the school
   - D) Because it changes every year

5. *(Understand)* Which pair shows one discrete and one continuous variable, in
   that order?
   - A) Number of siblings; time taken to walk to school
   - B) Weight of a school bag; height of a pupil
   - C) Favourite subject; number of absences
   - D) Time to finish a test; number of correct answers

6. *(Remember)* A primary source of data is best described as data that:
   - A) Come from a government office
   - B) The user collects directly, for their own purpose
   - C) Are printed in a textbook
   - D) Are about primary school pupils

7. *(Understand)* A teacher wants the average household size in the village
   where her school is located. She finds a Pakistan Bureau of Statistics report
   with this figure. What kind of source is this?
   - A) Primary, because it is about her village
   - B) Secondary, because it was collected by someone else for another purpose
   - C) Primary, because it is a government document
   - D) Secondary, because it is printed on paper

8. *(Apply)* A teacher collects data by asking each pupil how many siblings they
   have. This is a primary source because:
   - A) The data are about her own pupils
   - B) She collected the data herself for her own purpose
   - C) The data are numerical
   - D) She wrote the answers in her own notebook

9. *(Apply)* A teacher wants to know how long her own pupils spend on homework
   each evening. No school or government record holds this. What should she do?
   - A) Use the national census tables
   - B) Use last year's school register
   - C) Collect primary data from her pupils
   - D) Use the district's annual examination report

10. *(Understand)* Which is the main weakness of a secondary source compared
    with a primary source?
    - A) It always takes longer to obtain
    - B) It was collected for another purpose and may not match your question
    - C) It is always less accurate
    - D) It can only contain qualitative data

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

Answer each question in a few sentences or a short paragraph.

1. *(Understand)* Explain in your own words the difference between qualitative
   and quantitative data, and give one example of each from a school.

2. *(Understand)* Explain why "the number of correct answers on a 20-mark test"
   is discrete data while "the time taken to finish the test" is continuous data.

3. *(Apply)* Classify each of these as qualitative, discrete quantitative or
   continuous quantitative: (a) a pupil's home district; (b) the number of books
   in a classroom library; (c) a pupil's weight in kg; (d) the grade (A to E)
   on a report card.

4. *(Analyze)* A trainee says "class 7 is continuous data because 7 is a
   number". Identify the error in this reasoning and correct it in two or three
   sentences.

5. *(Analyze)* A height recorded as 145 cm has no decimal point. Explain why
   height is still continuous data.

6. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between a primary and a secondary
   source of data, and give one example of each that a teacher might use.

7. *(Apply)* A researcher wants the average number of pupils per classroom in
   government schools across Sindh. Recommend a primary or secondary source,
   name a specific one, and give your reason.

8. *(Apply)* A teacher wants to know why pupils in her class are often late on
   Mondays. Should she use a primary or a secondary source? Give two reasons.

9. *(Analyze)* A newspaper claims that "70 per cent of government schools in
   Sindh lack a science laboratory". Identify one question you would ask about
   the source of this figure before accepting it, and explain why it matters.

10. *(Analyze)* A study uses the school enrolment register (secondary) to list
    villages and then interviews pupils (primary) about walking distance.
    Explain what each source contributes that the other could not.

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

Answer each question in a structured paragraph or set of paragraphs.

1. *(Analyze)* A teacher records the following for each pupil: gender, number of
   absences last month, height in cm, favourite subject, and score on a 50-mark
   test. Classify each variable by data type in a table. Then explain, for each
   variable, whether an average can be meaningfully calculated and why. (Write
   no more than 150 words after the table.)

2. *(Analyze)* A school survey form asks: name, age in years, class, number of
   people at home, distance from school in km, and "How do you feel about
   mathematics? (dislike / neutral / like)". Classify each item, and explain
   which items a trainee is most likely to misclassify and why. (Write 120-180
   words.)

3. *(Evaluate)* A School Management Committee is deciding whether to build a new
   classroom. One member says, "Use last year's enrolment register." Another
   says, "Count the pupils ourselves this week." Evaluate both suggestions:
   identify the source type each represents, give one strength and one
   limitation of each for this decision, and make a clear recommendation.
   (Write 120-180 words.)

4. *(Evaluate)* You are planning a study of how many pupils in your district
   travel more than 3 km to school. Evaluate whether you would use a primary
   source, a secondary source or both, naming specific sources and justifying
   your choice in terms of relevance, cost and timeliness. (Write 150-200 words.)

5. *(Evaluate)* A school improvement plan claims: "Pupils who study more than
   two hours at home each day score 20 per cent higher on tests." Describe three
   questions you would ask about the data behind this claim, covering the type
   of each variable, the source of the data, and how the 20 per cent was
   calculated. For each question, explain why it matters for judging the claim.
   (Write 150-200 words.)

## Answers and marking guidance

### MCQ answer key

| Item | Answer | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | C | The subject studied is a category, not a number. |
| 2 | B | Discrete data are counts that take separate, distinct values. |
| 3 | C | Temperature is a measurement that can take any value in a range. |
| 4 | B | Roll numbers are labels; arithmetic on them is meaningless, so they are qualitative. |
| 5 | A | Siblings are counted (discrete); walking time is measured (continuous). |
| 6 | B | A primary source is defined by who collects it and for what purpose. |
| 7 | B | The PBS collected the data for its own purpose, so for the teacher it is secondary. |
| 8 | B | She collected the data herself for her own question. |
| 9 | C | No existing source covers her pupils and question, so she must collect primary data. |
| 10 | B | Secondary data were collected for someone else's question and may not fit yours. |

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. Qualitative data describe categories or qualities; quantitative data are numbers on which arithmetic is meaningful. Examples: subject taught (qualitative); test marks (quantitative). (2 marks: 1 for the distinction, 1 for a valid example of each.)

2. Correct answers are counted: 12 or 13 is possible but not 12.5, so the data are discrete. Time is measured: between any two times another time is possible, so it is continuous. (2 marks: 1 for each explanation.)

3. (a) qualitative; (b) discrete quantitative; (c) continuous quantitative; (d) qualitative (ordered categories). (2 marks: 1 for three correct, 2 for all four.)

4. The error is judging type by appearance. "Class 7" is a label for a group; adding or averaging class labels means nothing, so it is qualitative (an ordered category), not continuous. Continuous data are measurements that can take any value in a range. (2 marks: 1 for identifying the error, 1 for the correct classification with reason.)

5. The type depends on the underlying variable, not on how precisely it was recorded. Height can take any value in a range (145.2, 145.27 cm...); recording to the nearest cm rounds a continuous measurement. (2 marks: 1 for "the variable, not the recording", 1 for the rounding point.)

6. A primary source is collected by the user for their own purpose; a secondary source already exists, collected by someone else for another purpose. Examples: a teacher's own questionnaire (primary); a Pakistan Bureau of Statistics report or the school register (secondary). (2 marks: 1 for the distinction, 1 for valid examples.)

7. Secondary. Name the Sindh school census or annual school statistics from the provincial education department, or Pakistan Bureau of Statistics or district education office data. Collecting primary data from every school would be impractical for one researcher. (3 marks: 1 for secondary, 1 for a named source, 1 for the reason.)

8. Primary: no existing record holds reasons for lateness in her class, and she needs answers about her own pupils and this specific question. A short questionnaire or conversation is cheap at class scale. (2 marks: 1 for primary, 1 for two valid reasons.)

9. Any one: who collected the data and how; whether all government schools or only some were included; how "lack a science laboratory" was defined; when the data were collected. Explanation: a partial sample, a loose definition or old data would weaken the claim. (2 marks: 1 for a relevant question, 1 for the explanation.)

10. The register gives a complete, ready-made list of villages and pupils quickly and cheaply, which interviews could not easily provide. The interviews give walking distance, which the register does not record. Each fills the other's gap. (3 marks: 1 for each contribution, 1 for the complementarity.)
