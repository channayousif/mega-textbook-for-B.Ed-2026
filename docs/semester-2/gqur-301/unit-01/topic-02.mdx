## A real classroom situation

A teacher in a rural school near Tando Allahyar wants to find out how many
children in her grade 5 class walk more than 2 kilometres to school. She has two
options. She could ask each pupil directly and record the distance - this would
take a lesson period but she would get exactly the information she needs. Or she
could look in the school's enrolment register, which records each pupil's
village but not the walking distance. The register is quick to check but does
not answer her question directly. She is not sure which route to take, or
whether she could use both.

Her dilemma is one every researcher faces. The information you need may already
exist somewhere, or you may have to collect it yourself. Understanding the two
source types helps you decide which to use, and when.

## Explanation

### Primary sources: you collect it yourself

A <Glossary term="Primary source" /> is data you collect yourself, directly,
for your own purpose. You design the questions, you choose the method, you
record the answers. Examples in a school setting include:

- A questionnaire you give to pupils about their study habits.
- A count you make of pupils present on a given day.
- A measurement you take of each pupil's height.
- An interview you conduct with a parent about home support for learning.

The strength of a primary source is **control and relevance**: you collect
exactly what you need, in the format you need it, for the group you care about.
The weakness is **cost in time and effort**: designing a good questionnaire,
getting it to every pupil, checking the responses - all of this takes time you
may not have.

### Secondary sources: someone else collected it

A <Glossary term="Secondary source" /> is data that already exists, collected by
someone else, for some other purpose. You find it and use it for your own
question. Examples in a school setting include:

- The school enrolment register (collected by the administration for record-keeping).
- The Pakistan Bureau of Statistics census tables (collected by the government
  for national planning).
- A published research article on education in Sindh (collected by a
  university researcher).
- The District Education Office's annual report on school results.

The strength of a secondary source is **speed and low cost**: the data already
exist, and you can often access them in an afternoon. The weakness is **limited
relevance**: the data were collected for someone else's question, so they may
not cover your exact group, your exact variable, or your exact time period.

### Choosing between them

The choice depends on three practical questions:

1. **Does the data I need already exist?** If yes, check a secondary source
   first. If no, you must collect primary data.
2. **Does the existing data cover my group and my question?** A national census
   will not tell you about your class. A school register will not tell you
   about walking distance. If the secondary source does not match your need,
   go primary.
3. **Do I have the time and resources to collect my own data?** If the answer
   is no, a secondary source - even an imperfect one - may be your only
   practical option.

In practice, many studies use both. A teacher might check the enrolment
register (secondary) to get the list of villages, then ask pupils directly
(primary) about walking distance. The two sources complement each other.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees assume that "primary data is always
better than secondary data." This is not true. A well-kept government data set
collected from thousands of schools is far more reliable for a national picture
than a questionnaire you manage to give to 30 pupils in one afternoon. The right
source depends on the question, not on a fixed hierarchy.

## Activity: Match the source to the question

Work in pairs. About 15 minutes.

For each of the three questions below, decide whether a primary or secondary
source is more appropriate. For each, name a specific source and explain your
choice in one or two sentences.

1. What is the average class size in government primary schools in district
   Matiari this year?
2. How many of the pupils in your grade 6 class eat breakfast before coming to
   school?
3. What percentage of children in Sindh complete primary school?

Compare your answers with another pair. Where you disagree, discuss whether
both sources could work and what the trade-off would be.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* In your own words, state the difference between a primary and a secondary source in one sentence.
2. *(Understand)* A teacher wants to know the most common reason for pupil absence in her class. Explain why a primary source (asking pupils directly) might give different results from a secondary source (the official absence register).
3. *(Apply)* A head teacher is asked to report on the reading ability of all grade 4 pupils in the school. She has only one afternoon to prepare the report. Recommend a primary or secondary source, name a specific one, and explain your reasoning.

## Summary

Data come from two kinds of sources. Primary sources are collected by you, for
your purpose, giving you control at the cost of time. Secondary sources already
exist, collected by someone else, giving you speed at the cost of relevance.
The right choice depends on whether the data exist, whether they match your
question, and what resources you have.

## Try this at your practicum school

At your practicum school, find one example of a primary source and one example
of a secondary source that a teacher or administrator uses. For each, write
down: who collected it, for what purpose, and whether it could be used for a
different purpose. Bring your examples to the next session.

## Summative task

A district education officer wants to know whether providing free textbooks
improved test scores in grade 5 across all government schools in the district.
Describe one primary source and one secondary source she could use. For each,
state one advantage and one limitation for this specific question. Then
recommend which source she should rely on most and give one reason for your
recommendation.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Source identification* | 0-2: one plausible primary and one plausible secondary source named / one named / none. |
| *Advantage and limitation* | 0-3: clear, specific advantage and limitation for each source / partial / none. |
| *Recommendation* | 0-2: recommendation with a reason tied to the specific question / recommendation without reason / none. |
