## A real classroom situation

A teacher at a government secondary school in Hyderabad keeps a notebook at the
start of the term. In it she records: the number of pupils present each day,
the subject taught each period, the marks each pupil scored on the first test,
whether each pupil is male or female, and the time each pupil took to finish a
timed exercise. When her head teacher asks her to "analyze the data", she stares
at the notebook. Some entries are numbers, some are words, some are counts, some
are measurements. She is not sure where to begin because she has never been
taught that these are different *types* of data, each needing a different kind of
summary.

Her uncertainty is common, and it matters. If you do not know what type of data
you have, you cannot choose the right summary or the right graph. A mean makes
sense for test marks but not for subjects taught. A bar chart works for both, but
a histogram does not work for subjects. This topic gives you the classification
that makes those choices clear.

## Explanation

### Two broad families: qualitative and quantitative

<Glossary term="Data" /> are pieces of information collected for a purpose. The
first and most important distinction is between two families.

**Qualitative data** (also called categorical data) describe qualities or
categories. They answer questions like "what kind?" or "which one?" The entries
are words, labels or names, even if they look like numbers. The subjects taught
in a school - Urdu, Mathematics, Science - are qualitative. So is the gender of
a pupil (male / female). So is the colour of a textbook cover. You cannot add
"Urdu" and "Mathematics" and get a meaningful answer, which is the tell-tale
sign: qualitative data do not support arithmetic.

**Quantitative data** are numerical measurements or counts. They answer
questions like "how many?" or "how much?" and the entries are numbers you can
meaningfully add, subtract, average or compare. The number of pupils present,
the marks on a test, the time to finish an exercise - all are quantitative.

### Two kinds of quantitative data: discrete and continuous

Quantitative data themselves come in two kinds, and the difference matters for
how you group them in tables and graphs.

**Discrete quantitative data** are counts. They take separate, distinct values,
usually whole numbers. The number of pupils in a class is discrete: you can have
28 or 29 pupils, but not 28.5. The number of books on a shelf, the number of
absences in a week, the number of correct answers on a test - all are discrete.
If you can list every possible value, the data are discrete.

**Continuous quantitative data** are measurements. They can take any value
within a range, limited only by the precision of your measuring instrument. The
height of a pupil could be 145.2 cm or 145.23 cm, depending on your scale. The
time to finish an exercise could be 12.4 minutes or 12.43 minutes. The weight of
a school bag, the temperature of a classroom, the distance from home to school -
all are continuous. Between any two values, another value is always possible.

### A simple test for classifying any data item

When you are unsure, ask two questions in order:

1. **Is the entry a number that supports arithmetic?** If no, the data are
   qualitative. If yes, go to question 2.
2. **Is the number a count (discrete) or a measurement (continuous)?** If you
   can count the possible values, it is discrete. If any intermediate value is
   possible, it is continuous.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees assume that "qualitative data is less
rigorous or less important than quantitative data." This is not true. Knowing
that most absentees are in the qualitative category "Monday" is just as useful
as knowing the quantitative average attendance. The two families answer
different questions; neither is superior.

## Activity: Sort the notebook

Work in pairs. About 15 minutes.

Take the teacher's notebook from the opening situation. For each of the five
entries below, classify it as qualitative, discrete quantitative, or continuous
quantitative. For each, write one sentence explaining your choice.

1. Number of pupils present each day
2. Subject taught each period
3. Marks each pupil scored on the first test (out of 50)
4. Whether each pupil is male or female
5. Time each pupil took to finish the timed exercise (in minutes)

Compare your classifications with another pair. Where you disagree, explain your
reasoning and try to reach agreement.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* In your own words, state the difference between qualitative and quantitative data in one sentence.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why "the number of pupils in each class" is discrete, while "the weight of each pupil's school bag" is continuous.
3. *(Apply)* A head teacher records the following for each teacher: years of experience, subject specialization, and whether the teacher has a B.Ed degree. Classify each item as qualitative, discrete quantitative, or continuous quantitative.

## Summary

Data come in two broad families: qualitative (categories, described in words)
and quantitative (numbers, supporting arithmetic). Quantitative data split
further into discrete (counts, distinct values) and continuous (measurements,
any value in a range). Classifying your data correctly is the first step toward
summarizing and displaying it well.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can distinguish qualitative from quantitative data with a clear reason.
- [ ] I can distinguish discrete from continuous quantitative data.
- [ ] I can classify a mixed list of classroom data items by type.

## Try this at your practicum school

At your practicum school, collect five pieces of data that a teacher or
administrator records regularly (for example, attendance figures, test marks,
grades, subjects, gender). For each, write down the data type and a one-sentence
explanation. Bring your list to the next session and compare with a colleague.

## Summative task

A teacher records the following for each of her 30 pupils: class (6, 7, or 8),
number of siblings, height in cm, favourite subject, and score on a 20-mark
quiz. For each variable, state its data type (qualitative, discrete
quantitative, or continuous quantitative). Then explain, in one sentence each,
why a mean can be meaningfully calculated for some of these variables but not
for others.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Classification* | 0-3: all five variables correctly classified / three or four correct / two or fewer. |
| *Reasoning* | 0-3: clear explanation of why the mean applies to some variables and not others, referencing the data type / partial explanation / none. |
| *Accuracy* | 0-2: no errors in the reasoning about arithmetic applicability / one error / two or more. |
