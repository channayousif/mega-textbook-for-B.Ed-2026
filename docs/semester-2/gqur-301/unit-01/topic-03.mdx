## A real classroom situation

A teacher has just marked a 30-mark quiz for her grade 7 class of 25 pupils.
The marks, in no particular order, are: 18, 22, 15, 20, 23, 12, 19, 21, 17,
24, 14, 16, 20, 22, 13, 18, 25, 11, 19, 17, 21, 16, 23, 15, 20. She wants to
know how the class did overall, but the raw list tells her little. She could
sort the numbers, but with 25 entries that only helps a little. She needs a way
to see the shape of the marks at a glance - where they cluster, where they
spread, whether there are very low or very high scores.

Her need is a common one. A <Glossary term="Frequency distribution" /> is a
table that shows how often each value or each interval of values occurs. It
turns a raw list into a picture of the data's shape. This topic shows you how
to build one, step by step.

## Explanation

### What a frequency distribution shows

A frequency distribution table has two essential columns: the **class
intervals** (the ranges of values) and the **frequency** (the count of data
points that fall in each interval). Two optional but useful columns are
**relative frequency** (the proportion of the total in each interval) and
**cumulative frequency** (the running total).

For the quiz marks above, grouping into intervals of width 5 gives:

| Class interval | Frequency |
|---|---|
| 10-14 | 5 |
| 15-19 | 9 |
| 20-24 | 10 |
| 25-29 | 1 |

From this table you can see at a glance that most marks cluster in the 15-24
range, with the 20-24 interval having the highest count. The raw list did not
show this so clearly.

### Step-by-step: building a frequency table from raw data

Follow these steps for any small to medium data set.

1. **Sort the data** from smallest to largest. This helps you see the range
   and spot any values that look unusual.
2. **Decide the number of intervals.** A common rule of thumb is 5 to 15
   intervals. With 25 data points, 4 to 6 intervals usually works well. Too few
   intervals hide the shape; too many leave most intervals empty.
3. **Set the interval width.** Divide the range (largest value minus smallest)
   by the number of intervals and round to a convenient number. For the quiz
   marks, the range is 25 - 11 = 14; with 5 intervals the width is about 3,
   but a width of 5 gives cleaner intervals.
4. **Define the intervals** so that every data point falls in exactly one
   interval. Use non-overlapping boundaries. Write them as "10-14", "15-19",
   and so on.
5. **Tally** each data point into its interval. A tally column (using vertical
   strokes) makes counting easier.
6. **Count** the tally strokes to get the frequency for each interval.

### Cumulative frequency: a running total

A <Glossary term="Cumulative frequency" /> adds a third column to the table:
for each interval, it gives the total frequency up to and including that
interval. It answers the question "how many data points are at or below this
value?"

For the quiz marks table:

| Class interval | Frequency | Cumulative frequency |
|---|---|---|
| 10-14 | 5 | 5 |
| 15-19 | 9 | 14 |
| 20-24 | 10 | 24 |
| 25-29 | 1 | 25 |

The cumulative frequency column tells you that 14 pupils scored 19 or below,
and 24 scored 24 or below. This is useful when you want to know what proportion
of the class scored at or below a certain level.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees assume that "frequency tables are only
for small data sets and are not used in real statistical work." In fact,
frequency distributions are the foundation of histograms and of the probability
distributions you will meet in Unit 4. The skill of grouping data into
intervals and counting frequencies is used throughout statistics, not only in
classroom exercises.

## Activity: Build a table together

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Use the following data set: the number of books read in the last month by 20
pupils in a grade 6 class: 3, 5, 2, 7, 4, 6, 3, 8, 1, 5, 4, 6, 2, 7, 3, 5, 4,
6, 5, 3.

1. Sort the data and find the range.
2. Decide on a reasonable number of intervals and interval width.
3. Build the frequency table with class intervals, tally, frequency, and
   cumulative frequency.
4. Write one sentence describing what the table tells you about reading
   habits in this class.

Compare your table with another pair. If your intervals differ, discuss which
grouping reveals the pattern more clearly.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the essential columns of a frequency distribution table and the two optional columns mentioned in this topic.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why a data set of 100 values might need more intervals than a data set of 20 values.
3. *(Apply)* The following marks (out of 10) were recorded for 16 pupils: 4, 6, 7, 5, 8, 3, 6, 7, 5, 4, 9, 6, 5, 7, 6, 5. Construct a frequency table with intervals of width 2, starting from 2-3. Include a cumulative frequency column.

## Summary

A frequency distribution table groups raw data into intervals and counts how
many values fall in each. The steps are: sort, choose the number of intervals,
set the width, tally, and count. Cumulative frequency adds a running total
that tells you how many values lie at or below each interval. The table reveals
the shape of the data - where it clusters and how it spreads - far more clearly
than a raw list.

## Try this at your practicum school

At your practicum school, collect a small data set of your choice (for example,
the marks of one class on a recent quiz, or the number of pupils absent each
day for one week). Build a frequency distribution table and a cumulative
frequency table. Write two sentences describing what the tables reveal. Bring
your work to the next session.

## Summative task

The following are the heights (in cm) of 20 pupils in a grade 8 class: 142,
148, 151, 145, 155, 139, 147, 152, 144, 150, 146, 153, 141, 149, 143, 154,
140, 156, 138, 151.

Construct a frequency distribution table using intervals of width 5 starting
from 135-139. Include a frequency column and a cumulative frequency column.
Then answer: how many pupils are shorter than 150 cm? Write the answer using
your cumulative frequency column.

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Table construction* | 0-3: intervals correct, frequencies correct, cumulative column correct / two of three correct / one or none. |
