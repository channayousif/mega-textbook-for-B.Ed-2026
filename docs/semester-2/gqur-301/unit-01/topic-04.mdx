## A real classroom situation

The head teacher of a government girls' school in Sukkur has been asked to
present the school's examination results to the School Management Committee.
She has the numbers: 45 pupils passed with distinction, 78 passed in the first
division, 32 in the second division, 12 in the third, and 5 failed. She could
read the numbers aloud, but she knows that people remember pictures better than
lists. She is not sure whether to use a bar chart, a histogram, or a pie chart,
or whether it matters which one she picks.

Her question is a good one, because the choice of chart shapes the message. A
bar chart compares categories. A histogram shows the shape of a distribution.
A pie chart shows parts of a whole. Using the wrong one can mislead the reader
- or simply fail to show what you want to show. This topic gives you the
principles for choosing well.

## Explanation

### Bar charts: comparing categories

A <Glossary term="Bar chart" /> displays categorical data. Each category gets a
bar, and the height of the bar shows the frequency or value for that category.
The bars are separated by gaps, which signals that the categories are distinct
and not continuous.

Bar charts are the right choice when:
- Your data are qualitative (subjects, gender, districts) or discrete counts
  grouped into a small number of categories.
- You want to compare the size of one category against another.
- The order of categories does not matter, or you want to arrange them by size
  for emphasis.

For the examination results, a bar chart works well because the divisions
(distinction, first, second, third, fail) are categories, and the head teacher
wants to compare how many pupils are in each.

### Histograms: showing the shape of a distribution

A <Glossary term="Histogram" /> looks similar to a bar chart but serves a
different purpose. It displays quantitative data that have been grouped into
intervals (as in a frequency distribution). The bars touch each other - there
are no gaps - which signals that the variable is continuous and the intervals
are adjacent.

Histograms are the right choice when:
- Your data are quantitative and continuous (or discrete with many values).
- You want to see the shape of the distribution: where it peaks, whether it is
  symmetric, whether it has a long tail.
- You have already built a frequency distribution table.

For the examination results, a histogram would be wrong because the divisions
are categories, not intervals on a continuous scale. If the head teacher had
the raw marks (0 to 100), a histogram of those marks would show the shape of
the class's performance - whether most marks clustered in the middle, or
spread evenly, or skewed toward the top or bottom.

### Pie charts: showing parts of a whole

A <Glossary term="Pie chart" /> divides a circle into slices, one for each
category. The size of each slice shows the proportion of the whole that the
category represents.

Pie charts are the right choice when:
- Your data are categorical and you want to emphasize proportions.
- You have a small number of categories (typically no more than five or six;
   more than that makes the chart hard to read).
- The total of the categories makes a meaningful whole (for example, 100% of
   the pupils).

For the examination results, a pie chart would show what fraction of all 172
pupils falls into each division. It answers the question "what proportion?"
rather than "how many?"

### Choosing between them: a simple guide

| Use a bar chart when... | Use a histogram when... | Use a pie chart when... |
|---|---|---|
| You have categories | You have grouped quantitative data | You want to show proportions of a whole |
| You want to compare sizes | You want to see the shape of a distribution | You have few categories (5 or fewer) |
| Order does not matter | Order matters (intervals run low to high) | The categories sum to a meaningful total |

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think "a histogram and a bar chart are
the same thing, just drawn differently." They are not. The gaps between bars
in a bar chart tell the reader that the categories are separate. The touching
bars in a histogram tell the reader that the data are continuous and the
intervals cover the whole range without gaps. Using a bar chart for continuous
data (or a histogram for categorical data) sends the wrong signal to the
reader.

## Activity: Draw the same data three ways

Work in groups of three. About 25 minutes.

Use the examination results data from the opening situation: distinction 45,
first division 78, second division 32, third division 12, failed 5.

1. One person draws a bar chart.
2. One person draws a pie chart.
3. One person explains, in writing, why a histogram would be inappropriate
   for this data.

Compare your charts. For each, write one sentence answering: what does this
chart make easiest to see? Then discuss: if you were the head teacher, which
chart would you show the Management Committee, and why?

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the three chart types covered in this topic and the key visual feature that distinguishes a histogram from a bar chart.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why a histogram is the appropriate chart for the marks of 100 pupils on a test, while a bar chart is the appropriate chart for the number of pupils in each subject.
3. *(Apply)* A teacher records the favourite subject of each pupil in her class: Urdu 12, Mathematics 18, Science 15, English 10, Social Studies 8. Which chart type would you use to show the proportion of the class that prefers each subject? Draw the chart (or describe it in writing) and explain your choice.

## Summary

Bar charts compare categorical data with separated bars. Histograms show the
shape of a continuous distribution with touching bars. Pie charts show
proportions of a whole with slices of a circle. The right choice depends on
your data type and the question you want to answer. Using the wrong chart can
confuse the reader or send the wrong message.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can choose the right chart type for a given data set and explain why.
- [ ] I can explain the difference between a bar chart and a histogram in terms of what they communicate.
- [ ] I can produce a bar chart, histogram, or pie chart from a small data set.

## Try this at your practicum school

At your practicum school, find one chart or graph displayed in the office, a
classroom, or a report. Identify the chart type, describe the data it shows,
and judge whether the chart type is appropriate for the data. If you think a
different chart would work better, explain why. Bring your observation to the
next session.

## Summative task

The following table shows the number of pupils (out of 200) who scored in each
mark range on a school test:

| Mark range | Number of pupils |
|---|---|
| 0-19 | 15 |
| 20-39 | 35 |
| 40-59 | 60 |
| 60-79 | 55 |
| 80-100 | 35 |

1. Produce a histogram for this data. (Describe the axes and the height of
   each bar; you may draw it on paper.)
2. Explain in one sentence why a histogram is appropriate here rather than a
   bar chart.
3. Write one sentence describing what the histogram reveals about the class's
   performance.

| Criterion | Levels |
