## Unit summary

A school investigation begins with a question that data can answer. A model gives a simplified rule with conditions, not a guarantee. Compare its predictions with observations, look for a pattern in disagreements, and reconsider the data or assumptions when necessary. A central prediction and a practical allocation can differ because the consequences of shortage and surplus differ.

The problem-solving cycle connects the question, plan, checked data, analysis and conclusion. Definitions and denominators must remain visible. A pupil-day is an attendance occasion; a count of pupil-days is not a count of distinct pupils. Missing observations are not zeros. A finding about one class does not automatically describe a district, and an association does not establish a cause.

The course methods work together. Data types guide tables and graphs. Sampling connects the observed group to any wider claim. Centre needs spread; counting needs a decision about order; probability needs defined outcomes and conditions. Scatter plots help check relationships before a line is used. Estimation and testing address uncertainty under assumptions. Choose a method because it answers the question, explain its result in ordinary language, and identify what it cannot settle.

## Summative assessment

Attempt the questions before reading the final section. All numerical school cases here are synthetic unless a task explicitly asks you to consult a real published public table. Use a calculator if needed and show the reasoning for short and extended responses. Topic coverage is: MCQs 1–3 and RRQs 1–3 for modelling; MCQs 4–6 and RRQs 4–6 for the investigation cycle; MCQs 7–10 and RRQs 7–10 for integration. ERQs 1–3 address individual topics; ERQs 4–5 connect them.

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. What best describes a statistical model? *(Remember)*
   - A) A complete record of each original observation
   - B) A simplified pattern allowing for variation
   - C) A guarantee that a future count will equal the mean
   - D) A rule removing the need to check measurements

2. A supplied reading rule is predicted score = 12 + 2x. At x = 4 an observed score is 18. What is observed minus predicted? *(Apply)*
   - A) -2
   - B) 2
   - C) 6
   - D) 20

3. A prediction rule was checked for x from 1 to 5. Which proposed use needs the strongest warning about extending beyond observed data? *(Apply)*
   - A) Predicting at x = 2
   - B) Predicting at x = 4
   - C) Predicting at x = 3
   - D) Predicting at x = 20

4. What should be fixed first when two observers use different definitions of late arrival? *(Understand)*
   - A) The number of decimal places in the result
   - B) The colour scheme of the final bar chart
   - C) The collection plan and operational definition
   - D) The choice of software for calculating totals

5. A class has 30 enrolled pupils and 24 present, of whom 6 are late. What is the late proportion among those present? *(Apply)*
   - A) 6 / 30 = 20%
   - B) 6 / 24 = 25%
   - C) 24 / 30 = 80%
   - D) 6 / 6 = 100%

6. A daily late count is blank because the register was not completed. How should the trainee initially treat it? *(Apply)*
   - A) As missing, requiring clarification
   - B) As zero, preserving a complete table
   - C) As the preceding day's observed count
   - D) As the count that keeps the average stable

7. Two classes have the same mean score. Which additional summary directly helps compare their variation? *(Understand)*
   - A) The name of the subject tested
   - B) The date of their timetable meeting
   - C) The median alone without other information
   - D) The standard deviation on the same score scale

8. Eight trainees choose three committee members without assigning roles. Which expression counts the possible committees? *(Apply)*
   - A) 8 × 3
   - B) 8! / 5!
   - C) 8! / (3! × 5!)
   - D) 8 + 7 + 6

9. Which tool should first show the pattern between travel time and arrival delay measured for each pupil? *(Apply)*
   - A) A bar chart of travel categories only
   - B) A scatter plot of the paired measurements
   - C) A pie chart of all delays combined
   - D) A single mean of both variables pooled

10. What does a confidence interval for a population mean aim to estimate? *(Understand)*
   - A) The population mean through an interval procedure with stated coverage
   - B) The proportion of individual pupils inside the observed sample range
   - C) The chance that a convenience sample was randomly selected
   - D) The effect of every unmeasured influence on pupil learning

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

1. In three sentences, distinguish a prediction, an assumption and a model check using attendance as the example. *(Understand)*
2. A supplied model predicts y = 30 + 4x. At x = 3, y is observed as 39. Calculate the prediction and the observed-minus-predicted difference. Explain the sign in one sentence. *(Apply)*
3. A line fits five old observations closely but predicts poorly in the following week. In three sentences, explain two things to investigate before adopting it for routine decisions. *(Analyze)*
4. Rewrite "Does the timetable work?" as one answerable statistical question about reading access. In two further sentences, name the population and observation unit. *(Apply)*
5. Across five days, 80 present pupil-days include 12 late arrivals. There are 100 enrolled pupil-days. Calculate the late proportion among present pupil-days and among enrolled pupil-days. In one sentence explain the difference. *(Apply)*
6. A report says "20% of our pupils are habitually late" because 18 of 90 present pupil-days involved late arrival. In three sentences identify the unsupported step and propose one improvement to the records. *(Analyze)*
7. Synthetic scores are 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12. Calculate the mean, median and range. In two sentences explain why a mean alone is insufficient for planning support. *(Apply)*
8. Four trainees have distinct roles of chair and recorder to fill. Calculate the number of ordered choices and compare it with the number of unordered two-person groups. State why they differ in one sentence. *(Apply)*
9. A volunteer group reports a small p-value after comparing its scores with a school target. In three sentences explain why this does not establish a district-wide finding or show that the effect is educationally important. *(Analyze)*
10. A school report includes a positive travel-time and lateness association. In three sentences distinguish what the paired observations show, what a fitted line might predict, and what a causal claim would need. *(Analyze)*

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. A class's synthetic attendance totals over five comparable days are 21, 23, 22, 24 and 20, with 28 enrolled pupils. Write 300–400 words comparing a recent-mean prediction with preparing for full enrolment. Calculate centre and range, state assumptions, defend an allocation, and propose an observable model check. *(Evaluate)*
2. Using the five-day table in Topic 16.2, write a 300–400 word investigation report. Follow the problem-solving cycle, reproduce two useful summaries, inspect the Friday pattern, and justify a limited school action with a follow-up. Explain why the record cannot establish which individual pupils are habitually late. *(Analyze)*
3. Two synthetic groups have scores 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 and 2, 5, 8, 11, 14 respectively on the same test. A trainee reports equal means as evidence of equal learning needs, then proposes a t-test before describing how pupils were selected. Write a 300–400 word critique connecting centre, spread, sampling and inference. Include at least one spread calculation and defend the next step. *(Evaluate)*
4. Consult a real, public Pakistan Bureau of Statistics table with education counts, such as the [2023 census detailed results](https://www.pbs.gov.pk/result-excel/). Record the table title, source URL, year, population and units. In 350–450 words, define one question the table can answer, reproduce two relevant published counts with exact row or column locators, calculate one comparison, and defend a limited decision. Explain one question the table cannot answer. Use no personal records and do not invent figures if access fails; record the access problem and submit a collection plan instead. *(Evaluate)*
5. A practicum school asks whether moving reading time would improve access and scores. Write a 350–450 word plan joining all three topics. Define the question, population, units and collection safeguards. Choose descriptive summaries and a candidate model, name two assumptions, specify a check, and distinguish estimation from testing if a wider inference is proposed. Defend a decision rule and explain what stronger evidence a causal claim would require. *(Evaluate)*

## Answers and marking guidance

This is public self-study guidance. Credit alternative reasoning when it answers the question, uses the data accurately and respects the limitations. A persuasive conclusion must follow from evidence; it need not recommend the same allocation as another learner.

### MCQ answer key

1. B. A model simplifies a pattern while allowing observations to vary.
2. A. The prediction is 20, so 18 - 20 = -2.
3. D. Twenty is far outside the observed range of 1 to 5.
4. C. A shared definition is needed before counts can be compared.
5. B. Those present are the required denominator: 6 / 24 = 0.25.
6. A. An unrecorded count is unknown, not evidence of zero arrivals.
7. D. Standard deviation describes spread in the score's original units.
8. C. The combination formula counts groups without assigning order.
9. B. A scatter plot preserves the pairing and shows the relationship.
10. A. The interval targets a population quantity, under its procedure's assumptions.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. A prediction is an expected count, such as 25 present pupils tomorrow. An assumption is that the next day is comparable to the five days used. A check compares the prediction with later attendance. **3 marks:** one for each distinct, suitable statement. Do not award the assumption mark for a second prediction.
2. Predicted y = 30 + 4(3) = 42. Observed minus predicted = 39 - 42 = -3. The model overpredicts by three units. **3 marks:** one for the prediction, one for the difference, one for the interpretation. A consistent interpretation after an arithmetic slip can earn its own mark.
3. Check whether measurement procedures or school conditions changed, and whether the apparent fit depended on a few old records. Reserve comparable later observations for checking predictions. **4 marks:** one for each of two relevant issues and one for explaining how each would affect use. Merely demanding a more complex equation does not show the required reasoning.
4. Example: "Among this class's present pupil-days during next week's five ordinary school days, what proportion arrived after reading began?" The population of interest is those attendance occasions in the named class and period. The observation unit is one pupil on one day. **4 marks:** one for a measurable outcome, one for group and time, one for population, one for unit. Other precise questions can earn full credit.
5. Among present pupil-days: 12 / 80 = 15%. Among enrolled pupil-days: 12 / 100 = 12%. The first conditions on attendance; the second includes absence occasions in its denominator. **4 marks:** one for each numerical proportion and one for explaining each denominator. The numerical difference does not indicate a change in the late count.
6. Repeated late records might belong to the same pupils, so 18 late pupil-days do not mean 18 distinct late pupils. "Habitually" also needs an agreed definition and longer observation. Internally coded repeated records could distinguish pupils if collected with school approval and safeguards, without publishing identifiers. **4 marks:** one for the unit error, one for habitual status, one for a relevant improvement, one for its purpose or safeguard.
7. Mean = 40 / 5 = 8; median = 8; range = 12 - 4 = 8. The mean does not reveal which pupils are far from it or the spread of needs. Inspect the distribution before planning support. **5 marks:** one for each calculation, one for explaining omitted variation, one for an appropriate implication. Naming another average without explaining its purpose earns no implication mark.
8. Ordered roles: 4 × 3 = 12. Unordered pairs: 4! / (2! × 2!) = 6. Swapping chair and recorder changes the ordered outcome, while swapping the names on a two-person group list does not. **3 marks:** one for each count and one for the distinction about order. Accept an exhaustive list if it is complete and interpreted.
9. A volunteer group may differ from the district's pupils, and a small p-value cannot repair its selection. A p-value is not the size of a learning difference. Report the magnitude, scale and classroom consequences before claiming practical importance. **4 marks:** one for selection, one for scope, one for the p-value distinction, one for suitable additional evidence. Do not require a numerical effect size when the question provides no scores.
10. The paired observations show an association within the recorded group. A checked line may predict delay within the observed travel-time range under comparable conditions. A causal claim needs a design that addresses alternative explanations, such as transport route or starting time, rather than the line alone. **3 marks:** one each for observation, prediction and causal limit. Credit a clear comparison design with relevant caveats.

### ERQ rubrics

Each task is worth 12 marks. Use the task-specific criteria below. For every criterion, **3** means the response is accurate, complete and justified with the relevant evidence; **2** means mostly sound with one material omission; **1** means a relevant idea is stated but the reasoning or evidence is weak; **0** means absent, unrelated or contradicted by the record. Apply these levels separately to each of the four criteria. Do not turn word count into a substitute for judging reasoning.

1. **Centre and spread:** identifies a mean of 22 and range of 4 pupils, with units. **Models and conditions:** distinguishes predicting 22 from planning for up to 28 and states comparability assumptions. **Judgement (Evaluate):** weighs shortage against spare materials and defends an allocation rather than claiming certainty. **Check and revision:** proposes comparing later attendance with predictions and a relevant trigger for revisiting the rule. Full credit permits any defensible allocation that remains feasible for the class.
2. **Question and plan:** defines late arrival, period and pupil-day observation unit. **Numerical evidence:** correctly uses two summaries, such as mean present 18, late share 18/90 = 20%, or Friday's late share 6/18 = 33.3%. **Analysis (Analyze):** distinguishes the Friday pattern from an explanation and identifies repeated-record and generalisation limits. **Action and follow-up:** proposes a feasible, reversible action with a comparable follow-up measure. A report that labels 18 late pupil-days as 18 distinct pupils cannot earn full analysis credit.
3. **Centre:** establishes that both means and medians are 8 and explains the limitation of that result. **Spread:** correctly compares ranges 4 and 12, or sample standard deviations about 1.58 and 4.74, stating the version and units. **Method critique (Evaluate):** explains why a t-test requires a clear question, selection design and suitable assumptions; a test result cannot establish equal needs by itself. **Next evidence:** proposes examining score distributions and selection conditions before deciding on support or inference. Credit reasoned alternatives rather than requiring a particular significance result.
4. **Provenance:** supplies a real table title, date, URL and exact locators for the copied counts. **Computation:** reproduces the two counts accurately and shows a comparison with its denominator and units. **Interpretation (Evaluate):** makes a bounded claim that respects the table's categories, period and population. **Decision and limits:** links a plausible decision to the result and explains an unanswered question, such as an individual cause or a later trend. When access fails, assess the alternative collection plan: award provenance credit for an honest access record and identifiable target table; computation credit only for a correct proposed operation with clearly specified inputs, not invented values. It remains a plan, not a completed real-data analysis.
5. **Question and design:** states a measurable access or score question, observation units, population and safeguards. **Connected methods:** selects suitable summaries and a usable model, distinguishing any proposed estimate from a test. **Model checking (Analyze):** states two relevant conditions and a check with new comparable observations, explaining what disagreement would mean. **Defended decision (Evaluate):** gives a decision rule, addresses uncertainty and alternative explanations, and describes stronger evidence for causation. Naming every course formula without connecting it to the investigation does not earn connected-methods credit.
