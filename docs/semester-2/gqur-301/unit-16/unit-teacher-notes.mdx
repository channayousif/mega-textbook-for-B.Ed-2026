## Teaching strategies

The course guide specifies discussion, question-answer work, brainstorming, group assignments and presentations. Use these approaches to make reasoning visible. Begin with a short discussion: "If the prediction is 25, why might we prepare 30 sheets?" A likely response is that the model must be wrong whenever attendance differs. Ask learners to separate a typical prediction from a decision under uncertainty. Keep the question, rule, evidence and limitation visible on the board throughout the discussion.

Sequence the topics as a connected investigation. First teach the model card and work through one observed-minus-predicted calculation. Next carry the class question through planning, checking records and reporting. Finally ask learners to select methods across the earlier units. If a learner can name a formula but cannot state its purpose, return to the question before requesting a calculation. Do not reward the number of methods used in a report.

Groups need distinct roles and a shared product. Rotate proposing, checking and representing those affected by a decision. During presentations, require one speaker to state the numerical finding and another to state its limit. Invite listeners to reproduce a calculation before commenting on the recommendation. Support learners who need more processing time with printed tables and time to rehearse explanations in pairs. Accept an oral explanation accompanied by a written calculation where appropriate.

## Likely misconceptions

A common confusion is treating repeated observations as different people. Ask learners to mark one fictional pupil's five attendance occasions on paper. Compare the count of pupils with the count of pupil-days. Another confusion is treating missing counts as zeros. Leave one table cell blank and ask which total is known before any software is opened. Discuss why a plausible replacement still needs a stated justification.

A learner may claim that statistical significance proves classroom importance. Bring the discussion back to the scale of the difference and the decision it could change. Another may read a confidence interval as covering most pupils. Ask what quantity the interval aims to estimate. Use the earlier estimation and testing units for further teaching; this unit integrates those ideas rather than replacing their full explanations.

## Practical work

Use the supplied synthetic tables for group assignments, individual report drafting and short presentations. Provide calculators or squared paper as alternatives to spreadsheets. For spreadsheet work, keep a raw-data sheet, label derived columns and independently check one total and fraction by hand. Have groups exchange reports and trace each conclusion to the relevant table entry. Do not require a device or internet connection to complete the core school investigation.

For the public-data assessment, provide a printed official table with its title, URL, year and row or column labels when access is unreliable. Learners should still record provenance and explain the calculation. Do not replace a missing published count with an invented one. Real school data require the school's agreement and appropriate safeguards; the synthetic alternative permits the learning task without requesting personal pupil records.

Close with a brief reflection on what additional evidence would change the recommendation. Keep drafting feedback separate from independent G3 review. The assessment's final self-study guidance is intentionally public and is not a protected LMS quiz bank.
