## A real classroom situation

A teacher in a government high school in Larkana is doing a small research
project on pupil absenteeism. She wants to interview 30 pupils about why they
miss school. Her colleague says, "Just pick the ones you know." Her head
teacher says, "Pick the ones with the worst attendance records - they are the
ones you want to study." A third colleague says, "Put all the names in a hat
and draw 30." Each suggestion would produce a very different group of 30
pupils, and the conclusions she reaches would depend on which method she picks.

## Explanation

### Random sampling: every member has a known chance

In <Glossary term="Random sampling" />, every member of the population has a
known, non-zero chance of being selected. This does not mean "haphazard" or
"informal" - it means the selection follows a defined probabilistic procedure.
Random sampling is the gold standard because it allows you to quantify how well
the sample represents the population.

**Simple random sampling.** Every member of the population has an equal chance
of selection, and every possible sample of the desired size has the same
chance of being the one selected. The "names in a hat" method is a simple
random sample. In practice you would use a random number table or a computer
to select the sample.

**Systematic sampling.** You select every *k*th member from a list. For
example, from a list of 500 pupils, you might select every 10th name to get a
sample of 50. You randomise the starting point (pick a random number between 1
and 10 to decide where to begin). Systematic sampling is easy to administer
but can be biased if the list has a hidden pattern.

**Stratified sampling.** You divide the population into distinct subgroups
(strata) that you believe matter for the question, then draw a random sample
from each stratum. For example, if you are studying pupil performance and you
believe grade level matters, you might stratify by grade (grade 6, 7, 8, 9, 10)
and sample proportionally from each. Stratified sampling ensures that each
subgroup is represented and often gives more precise estimates than simple
random sampling.

### Non-random sampling: selection by judgement or convenience

In <Glossary term="Non-random sampling" />, the selection is based on
judgement, convenience or accessibility rather than a probabilistic procedure.
These methods are easier and cheaper but do not allow you to quantify
representativeness.

**Convenience sampling.** You select whoever is easiest to reach. A teacher
who interviews the pupils who happen to be in the courtyard at break time is
using convenience sampling. This is the weakest method for drawing conclusions
about a larger group, but it is common in exploratory work.

**Purposive sampling.** You select individuals who meet specific criteria
relevant to the research question. A teacher who deliberately selects pupils
with the worst attendance records is using purposive sampling. This is the
right choice when the research question is about a specific subgroup, but the
results cannot be generalised to the whole population.

**Quota sampling.** You decide in advance how many individuals from each
category you need, then fill those quotas by any means. For example, you might
decide to interview 15 boys and 15 girls, then approach pupils until each
quota is filled. Quota sampling is like stratified sampling but without the
random selection within each stratum.

### Choosing a method: three questions

1. **Does a sampling frame exist?** A sampling frame is a complete list of the
   population. Random sampling requires one. If you have no list of all
   government schools in the district, you cannot draw a simple random sample.
2. **Is random selection practical?** If the population is scattered across
   many villages and you have limited travel budget, random sampling may be
   impractical.
3. **Does the population have distinct subgroups that matter?** If yes,
   stratified sampling may give you a better picture than simple random.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees assume that "random sampling is always
the best method". It is the best method *for generalising to the population*,
but if your research question is about a specific subgroup (for example, "what
are the reasons for chronic absenteeism among the worst-absent pupils?"),
purposive sampling is the right choice. The method must fit the question.

## Activity: Match the method to the question

Work in pairs. About 15 minutes.

For each research question below, recommend a sampling method (simple random,
systematic, stratified, convenience, purposive or quota). For each, write one
sentence explaining your choice.

1. What is the average number of textbooks per pupil across all government
   primary schools in a district with 200 schools?
2. What are the reasons for dropout among girls who left school before
   completing grade 8?
3. What do teachers in this school think about the new examination system?

Compare your choices with another pair. Where you disagree, discuss whether
both methods could work and what the trade-off would be.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the three random sampling methods and the three non-random sampling methods described in this topic.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why systematic sampling is not the same as simple random sampling, even though it starts from a random point.
3. *(Apply)* A researcher wants to study the reading habits of pupils in a school of 1,200 pupils across grades 6 to 10. She believes reading habits differ by grade. Recommend a sampling method and explain why.

## Summary

Random sampling methods (simple, systematic, stratified) give every member of
the population a known chance of selection and allow you to quantify
representativeness. Non-random methods (convenience, purposive, quota) are
easier but do not support generalisation. The right choice depends on whether a
sampling frame exists, whether random selection is practical, and whether the
research question requires generalisation or focuses on a specific subgroup.

## Summative task

A head teacher wants to estimate the average number of years of teaching
experience among the 80 teachers in her school district. She has a complete
list of all 80 teachers, organised by school.

a) Recommend a sampling method and explain your choice in one or two sentences.
b) A colleague suggests using convenience sampling (just asking the teachers
   she sees at the next staff meeting). Explain one strength and one weakness
   of this suggestion compared to your recommendation.
