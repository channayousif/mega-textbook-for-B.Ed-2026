## A real classroom situation

Back at the Hyderabad girls' school from Topic 6.1, the mathematics teacher has
now computed the standard deviation of both Class 6 sections. Both have a mean
of 12 out of 20. Section A has a standard deviation of about 1.1 marks; Section
B has a standard deviation of about 6.0 marks. At the staff meeting the head
teacher asks, "So which section is better?" The teacher's answer is that
"better" is the wrong question. The two numbers tell her **how** to teach each
section, not which one to praise. This topic shows you how to read a standard
deviation and turn it into a decision.

## Explanation

### Reading the standard deviation in a school context

Topic 6.2 showed you how to compute the standard deviation (SD). Interpreting
it means putting it **beside the mean** and asking what it says about the
people behind the numbers.

**Worked example.** Compare the two Class 6 sections.

| | Section A | Section B |
|---|---|---|
| Marks (out of 20) | 10, 11, 12, 12, 12, 12, 13, 14 | 4, 5, 7, 10, 14, 17, 19, 20 |
| Mean | 12 | 12 |
| Range | 4 | 16 |
| Squared deviations add to | 10 | 284 |
| Variance (÷ 8) | 1.25 | 35.5 |
| Standard deviation | about 1.1 | about 6.0 |

1. **Same centre.** Both means are 12, so on average the sections did equally
   well. The mean alone would end the discussion here.
2. **Different spread.** Section A's SD (about 1.1 marks) says a typical pupil
   is only about one mark from 12. Section B's SD (about 6.0 marks) says a
   typical pupil is about six marks away from 12, either above or below.
3. **What it means for teaching.** Section A is a **consistent** group: one
   pace and one set of exercises will probably suit most pupils. Section B is a
   **varied** group: some pupils need the basics retaught while others are ready
   for harder work, so the teacher should plan groups or different levels of
   task.

A useful rough guide for single-peaked data is that many of the values lie
within one SD of the mean, and a value more than two SDs from the mean is
unusually far from it (Illowsky & Dean, 2023, Section 2.7). In Section A, the
band from 12 - 1.1 to 12 + 1.1 (about 10.9 to 13.1) holds six of the eight
pupils. In Section B the band is much wider, about 6.0 to 18.0, and it holds
only four. The first figure above shows these two bands. Treat this as a rule
of thumb, not a law: with eight pupils, and with data that are skewed or have
two peaks, the proportions can be quite different.

**Locating one pupil.** The SD also tells you how unusual a single mark is.
Divide the pupil's distance from the mean by the SD:

> **Distance in SDs = (mark - mean) ÷ SD**

A mark of 14 in Section A is (14 - 12) ÷ 1.1, about **1.8 SDs** above the
mean: one of the strongest results in that section. The same mark of 14 in
Section B is (14 - 12) ÷ 6.0, only about **0.3 SDs** above the mean: an
ordinary result in that section. The same raw mark means different things in
different groups, and only the spread can show it.

Finally, remember what Topic 6.2 showed: the SD is pulled by extreme values. If
one or two marks are far from the rest, look at them separately before deciding
that the whole class is "varied" (NIST/SEMATECH, Section 1.3.5.6).

### Is more spread good or bad?

**Common misconception.** Many trainees believe that "a larger standard deviation
is always bad". The standard deviation is a description, not a grade. Whether a
large or small spread is welcome depends on the purpose of the data:

- **When large spread is useful.** A reading test given at the start of the
  year to form reading groups *should* spread pupils out. If everyone got
  nearly the same score, the teacher would learn nothing about who needs which
  group.
- **When small spread is a warning.** If every pupil in a class scores 18, 19
  or 20 out of 20, the SD is tiny. That may mean excellent teaching, but it may
  also mean the test was too easy to show what the stronger pupils can do.
- **When small spread is the goal.** In daily attendance, a small SD around a
  high mean is good news: almost every pupil comes almost every day. A large
  SD would mean some pupils are missing much more school than others.
- **When large spread is simply a fact.** A multi-grade class in a one-room
  rural school in Tharparkar will naturally have widely spread ages and
  reading levels. A large SD there is not a failure; it is information the
  teacher must plan for.

So the right question is never "is the SD large?" but "what does this spread
mean for this purpose, and what should I do about it?" The second figure above
summarises these readings.

## Activity: Two sections, one staff meeting

Work in groups of three. About 20 minutes.

Two Class 8 English sections in a Karachi school took the same test out of 50.

- Section Iqbal: mean 31, SD 3
- Section Jinnah: mean 31, SD 11

1. One member plays the head teacher, one the Section Iqbal teacher and one the
   Section Jinnah teacher.
2. Each teacher explains to the "head teacher", in two or three sentences, what
   the SD says about their section and what they plan to do next term.
3. The head teacher asks each teacher one question: "Where does a pupil with 42
   marks stand in your section?" Each teacher answers using distance in SDs.
4. Swap roles once, then together write one sentence the head teacher could put
   in the school report that uses both the mean and the SD fairly.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Understand)* Two classes have the same mean mark. Class P has an SD of 2
   and Class Q an SD of 9. Describe in two sentences how the two classes
   differ.
2. *(Apply)* In a class with mean 40 and SD 5, how many SDs above or below the
   mean are the marks 50 and 35?
3. *(Apply)* Give one school situation in which a large SD is useful, and one in
   which a small SD is a warning sign. Explain each in one sentence.

## Summary

The standard deviation must be read beside the mean. A small SD means a
consistent group, a large SD a varied one, and that difference often matters
more for teaching than the mean itself. Distance in SDs shows how unusual one
value is within its own group, so the same raw mark can be strong in one class
and ordinary in another. A larger SD is not automatically bad: whether spread
is welcome depends on the purpose of the data.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can describe what a small and a large standard deviation say about a class.
- [ ] I can compare two groups with the same mean by using their standard deviations.
- [ ] I can work out how many standard deviations a mark is from the mean.
- [ ] I can judge whether a given spread is helpful or a warning, for a stated purpose.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher whether two sections, or two tests of the same
class, could be compared (without pupils' names). Find the mean and SD of each.
Write a short note to the teacher: what the spreads suggest, and one practical
teaching idea that follows, such as grouping, extra practice for some pupils, or
a harder extension task for others.

## Summative task

A Class 9 science teacher in Sukkur gives the same 20-mark test to two sections.

- Section Rose: mean 13, SD 1.5
- Section Lily: mean 13, SD 5.5

A pupil named Ayesha scored 17 in Section Rose, and a pupil named Bilal scored 17
in Section Lily.

a) Find how many SDs above the mean each of the two pupils is.
b) In 120-160 words, write a note to the head teacher that compares the two
   sections using the mean and the SD, explains why Ayesha's and Bilal's equal
   marks do not mean equal standing, and recommends one teaching decision for
   each section.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Computation (Apply)* | 0-2: Ayesha about 2.7 SDs and Bilal about 0.7 SDs above the mean / one correct / none. |
| *Interpretation of spread (Analyze)* | 0-3: explains that the sections share a centre but differ in consistency, and uses the SDs to explain the different standing of equal marks / partial / none. |
| *Justified teaching decision (Evaluate)* | 0-3: recommends a decision for each section that follows from its spread (for example one pace with extension for Rose, grouped or differentiated work for Lily) and avoids calling either section "better" / partial / none. |
