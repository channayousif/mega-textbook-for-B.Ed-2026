## A real classroom situation

A head teacher in a primary school near Thatta is worried about attendance. She
looks at the number of days each of eight Class 4 pupils was absent last month:
2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9. The mean is 5 days. "Are most pupils close to five
days," she asks, "or are some much worse than others?" The range (9 - 2 = 7)
only tells her about the two extreme pupils. She wants one number that takes
**every** pupil into account and says how far, typically, a pupil is from the
mean. That number is the standard deviation, and this topic shows you how to
compute it.

## Explanation

### Variance: the average squared distance from the mean

The idea is simple: measure how far each value is from the mean, then find a
kind of average of those distances.

The distance of one value from the mean is called its
<Glossary term="Deviation">deviation</Glossary>:

> **Deviation = value - mean**

A value above the mean has a positive deviation; a value below the mean has a
negative one. For the attendance data (mean 5), the pupil absent 9 days has a
deviation of 9 - 5 = +4, and the pupil absent 2 days has a deviation of
2 - 5 = -3.

We cannot simply average the deviations. They always add to **zero**, because
the mean is the balance point of the data (Unit 4): the values above it exactly
balance the values below it. So we **square** each deviation first. Squaring
makes every deviation positive, and it gives extra weight to values far from
the mean (Illowsky & Dean, 2023, Section 2.7).

The <Glossary term="Variance">variance</Glossary> is the mean of the squared deviations:

> **Variance = (sum of squared deviations) ÷ N**

where N is the number of values. Lane (2023, Chapter 3) describes the variance
in exactly these words: the average squared difference of the values from
their mean.

**Worked example.** The figures above set out the full computation for the
attendance data. Follow it line by line.

| Days absent (x) | Deviation (x - 5) | Squared deviation |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | -3 | 9 |
| 4 | -1 | 1 |
| 4 | -1 | 1 |
| 4 | -1 | 1 |
| 5 | 0 | 0 |
| 5 | 0 | 0 |
| 7 | 2 | 4 |
| 9 | 4 | 16 |
| **Total: 40** | **0** | **32** |

1. **Mean:** 40 ÷ 8 = 5 days.
2. **Deviations:** subtract 5 from each value (second column). Check: they add
   to 0.
3. **Squares:** square each deviation (third column).
4. **Variance:** add the squares and divide by N: 32 ÷ 8 = **4**.

There is one problem with the variance. Because we squared the deviations, its
unit is squared too. The variance here is 4 "days squared", which has no
everyday meaning. That is why we take one more step.

### Standard deviation: back to the original units

The <Glossary term="Standard deviation">standard deviation</Glossary> is the square root of the variance:

> **Standard deviation = √variance**

5. **Standard deviation:** √4 = **2 days**.

Taking the square root undoes the squaring, so the standard deviation is back in
the same unit as the data: days, marks or rupees (Illowsky & Dean, 2023,
Section 2.7). You can read it as "the typical distance of a value from the
mean". For the Thatta class, a typical pupil's absence is about 2 days away
from the mean of 5. The head teacher now has her answer: most pupils are
fairly close to five days, though the pupil absent 9 days is twice the typical
distance away and deserves a conversation.

**Dividing by N or by n - 1.** In the worked example the eight pupils are the
whole group the head teacher cares about, so we divide by N = 8. This is the
**population** variance. When the values are a **sample** used to estimate the
spread of a larger group (for example, eight pupils chosen to represent a whole
district), statisticians divide by n - 1 instead, because dividing by n tends
to underestimate the spread of the larger group (Lane, 2023, Chapter 3). For
the same data that gives 32 ÷ 7 = 4.57 and a standard deviation of about 2.14
days. Your calculator may show both: the population standard deviation is often
labelled σ (the Greek letter sigma) and the sample one *s*. In this course,
divide by N unless a question says the data are a sample.

**Two more facts** worth knowing:

- The standard deviation is **never negative**, and it is 0 only when every
  value is the same (no spread at all).
- Like the mean, it is **pulled by extreme values**. Because deviations are
  squared, one value far from the mean adds a great deal. In the attendance
  data, the single pupil absent 9 days contributes 16 of the total of 32
  (NIST/SEMATECH, Section 1.3.5.6).

**Common misconception.** Many trainees say that "variance and standard
deviation are the same thing". They are closely related, but they are not the
same number and not in the same unit. The standard deviation is the square root
of the variance: a variance of 4 days squared is a standard deviation of 2 days,
and a variance of 25 marks squared is a standard deviation of 5 marks. When you
describe spread in words to a teacher or a parent, use the standard deviation,
because it is in the units they understand. The variance is mainly a step on
the way, and it is used in more advanced methods later in the course.

## Activity: Pass the table

Work in groups of four. About 20 minutes.

Use the marks out of 10 of six pupils in a Mirpurkhas classroom: 3, 5, 6, 6, 7, 9.

1. Pupil 1 finds the mean and writes it at the top of a blank computation table.
2. Pupil 2 fills in the deviation column and checks that it adds to 0.
3. Pupil 3 fills in the squared-deviation column and adds it up.
4. Pupil 4 computes the variance (divide by N) and the standard deviation.
5. As a group, check every step, then write one sentence that a parent could
   understand, using the standard deviation in marks.
6. Finally, change the 9 to 10 and predict, before computing, whether the
   standard deviation will go up or down. Then check.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Define a deviation, the variance and the standard deviation,
   each in one sentence.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why we square the deviations before averaging them,
   and why we then take a square root.
3. *(Apply)* Find the variance and the standard deviation of the marks 4, 6, 8,
   10, 12, treating them as the whole group. Show the computation table.
4. *(Apply)* A data set has a variance of 9 marks squared. State its standard
   deviation, with its unit.

## Summary

A deviation is the distance of one value from the mean. Deviations always add
to zero, so we square them before averaging. The variance is the mean of the
squared deviations; it uses every value, but its unit is squared. The standard
deviation is the square root of the variance, so it is back in the units of the
data and can be read as the typical distance of a value from the mean. Divide
by N for a whole group and by n - 1 for a sample used to estimate a larger
group. Because the deviations are squared, a single extreme value can raise the
standard deviation sharply.

## Summative task

The number of homework tasks handed in by seven Class 7 pupils in a Nawabshah
school over one month was: 6, 8, 9, 10, 10, 11, 16.

a) Compute the mean, the deviations, the variance and the standard deviation,
   treating the seven pupils as the whole group. Show the computation table.
b) Remove the 16 and recompute the standard deviation of the remaining six.
c) In 80-120 words, explain what part (b) shows about how the standard
   deviation responds to one extreme value, and why that matters when a
   teacher reports the spread of a class.
