## A real classroom situation

A government girls' school in Hyderabad has two sections of Class 6. Both sat
the same mathematics test, marked out of 20, and eight pupils from each section
were checked. The mean in both sections is exactly 12. The head teacher says,
"The two sections are performing the same." The mathematics teacher is not so
sure. In Section A every pupil scored between 10 and 14. In Section B one pupil
scored 4 and another scored 20. Same mean, but the teacher knows these are not
the same class to teach.

The teacher needs a number that describes how spread out the marks are. This
topic gives you the simplest one.

## Explanation

### Range: the simplest measure of spread

A measure of central tendency answers "where is the middle?" A measure of
<Glossary term="Dispersion">dispersion</Glossary> answers a second question: "how spread out are
the values around that middle?" Small dispersion means the values are close
together. Large dispersion means they are scattered.

The simplest measure of dispersion is the <Glossary term="Range">range</Glossary>:

> **Range = largest value - smallest value**

**Worked example.** Take Section A's marks from the classroom situation.

1. **Sort** the values: 10, 11, 12, 12, 12, 12, 13, 14.
2. **Find** the largest value (14) and the smallest value (10).
3. **Subtract:** 14 - 10 = **4**.

Now Section B: 4, 5, 7, 10, 14, 17, 19, 20. The largest is 20 and the smallest
is 4, so the range is 20 - 4 = **16**. The second figure above sets out both
computations side by side.

Both sections have a mean of 12, but Section B's range is four times Section A's.
The range has already told the teacher something the mean could not: Section A
is a fairly even group, while Section B has pupils far below and far above the
middle. Lane (2023, Chapter 3) and the NIST/SEMATECH *e-Handbook* (Section
1.3.5.6) both define the range this way.

Notice three facts about the range:

- It is in the **same unit** as the data. A range of 16 marks is 16 marks.
- It is **never negative**, and it is 0 only when every value is the same.
- It is **quick**. A teacher can find it in seconds from a marks register.

### What the range hides

The range uses only **two** values: the largest and the smallest. Every value in
between is ignored. That is its strength (it is fast) and its weakness.

Look at a third section, Section C, which also sat the test: 4, 11, 12, 12, 12,
12, 13, 20. Its mean is again 12, and its range is 20 - 4 = 16, exactly the
same as Section B. Yet the two sections look very different:

- In Section B the marks are scattered all the way from 4 to 20.
- In Section C six of the eight pupils scored 11, 12 or 13. Only two pupils,
  one at each end, are far from the middle.

The range calls B and C equally spread. A teacher looking at the actual marks
would not agree. Section C is mostly an even group with two pupils who need
attention; Section B needs a different plan for almost every pupil.

The range is also very sensitive to a single unusual value (an
**outlier**). Suppose a ninth pupil, who had missed most of the term, joined
Section A for the test and scored 2. The range would jump from 4 to 12, even
though the other eight pupils had not changed at all. The NIST/SEMATECH *e-Handbook* (Section 1.3.5.6)
warns for the same reason that the range depends on only two points and should
be used with care.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees believe that "the range tells you
everything about spread". It does not. The range tells you the distance between
the two most extreme values and nothing about how the other values are arranged
between them. Sections B and C above have the same range but very different
spreads. To describe how *all* the values sit around the mean, we need a
measure that uses every value: the variance and standard deviation of Topic 6.2.

## Activity: Same range, different story

Work in pairs. About 15 minutes.

1. Partner 1 writes down eight whole-number marks out of 20 with a range of
   exactly 10 in which most marks are bunched together.
2. Partner 2 writes down eight marks out of 20, also with a range of exactly 10,
   in which the marks are spread evenly across the whole range.
3. Swap lists. Each partner checks the other's range and draws both lists as
   dots on a number line, as in the first figure above.
4. Together, write two sentences: one on what the range told you about the two
   lists, and one on what it did not tell you.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Define dispersion in one sentence, and write the formula for the
   range.
2. *(Apply)* The number of days each of seven pupils was absent last term was
   3, 0, 5, 2, 8, 1, 2. Find the range, showing the two values you used.
3. *(Understand)* Explain why two data sets with the same mean and the same
   range can still be spread differently. Use a short example of your own.

## Summary

Dispersion describes how spread out the values in a data set are, which the
mean, median and mode cannot show. The range is the simplest measure of
dispersion: the largest value minus the smallest. It is quick and easy to
explain, but it uses only the two most extreme values, so it ignores how the
rest of the data are arranged and it can be changed sharply by one unusual
value. Two data sets with the same range can have very different spreads.

## Try this at your practicum school

With your cooperating teacher's permission, look at the marks of one class in
one recent test (without recording pupils' names). Find the mean and the range.
Then look at the marks themselves: are most pupils bunched together with one or
two far away, or are they spread right across the range? Write one sentence
describing the class that uses both the range and what you saw in the marks.

## Summative task

Two Class 5 sections in a school in Larkana took the same Urdu dictation, marked
out of 10.

- Section Red: 2, 6, 6, 7, 7, 7, 8, 10
- Section Blue: 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 9, 10

a) Find the mean and the range of each section.
b) Draw both sections as dots on two number lines.
c) In 80-120 words, explain to the head teacher whether the range is enough to
   compare the spread of the two sections, using what your number lines show.
