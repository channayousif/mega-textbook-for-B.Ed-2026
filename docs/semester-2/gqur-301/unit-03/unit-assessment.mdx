## Unit summary

This unit, which follows Chapter 3 of the course guide, gave you the language
for reasoning from part of a group to the whole.

- Topic 3.1 defined the population (the whole group a question is about), the
  sample (the part actually measured), the parameter (a number describing the
  population, usually unknown) and the statistic (the matching number computed
  from the sample). Inference is the step from statistic to parameter, and
  sampling is needed because measuring a whole population is usually too costly,
  too slow or impossible.
- Topic 3.2 compared random sampling (simple random, systematic, stratified),
  where every member has a known chance of selection, with non-random sampling
  (convenience, purposive, quota), which is easier but cannot be generalised
  with confidence. Three questions guide the choice: is there a sampling frame,
  is random selection practical, and do distinct subgroups matter?

How a sample is chosen matters more than how large a fraction of the population
it is.

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

Choose the single best answer for each question.

1. *(Remember)* Which of the following is a parameter?
   - A) The mean of a sample of 30 pupils
   - B) The true average height of all pupils in the school
   - C) The median of a sample's test marks
   - D) The most common answer in a survey of 50 parents

2. *(Apply)* A quality inspector tests 20 light bulbs from a batch of 2,000 to
   estimate the percentage that fail. The 20 bulbs are the:
   - A) Population
   - B) Parameter
   - C) Sample
   - D) Statistic

3. *(Understand)* Why do most studies of all government schools in Sindh use a
   sample rather than the whole population?
   - A) Because a sample always gives the exact parameter
   - B) Because measuring every school costs too much time and money
   - C) Because populations cannot be defined
   - D) Because samples need no planning

4. *(Apply)* A district officer computes the average class size of 30 schools she
   visited. This average is a:
   - A) Parameter
   - B) Population
   - C) Statistic
   - D) Sampling frame

5. *(Understand)* Inference in statistics means:
   - A) Measuring every member of the population
   - B) Using a sample statistic to draw a conclusion about a population parameter
   - C) Choosing the sample by convenience
   - D) Drawing a chart of the sample

6. *(Remember)* In which sampling method does every member of the population have
   an equal chance of selection?
   - A) Convenience sampling
   - B) Purposive sampling
   - C) Simple random sampling
   - D) Quota sampling

7. *(Apply)* A researcher believes family size differs between urban and rural
   areas of a district. Which method is most appropriate for estimating the
   district's average family size?
   - A) Convenience sampling
   - B) Simple random sampling
   - C) Stratified sampling with urban and rural strata
   - D) Purposive sampling

8. *(Apply)* A teacher selects every 5th pupil from an alphabetical list of 100
   pupils, starting from a randomly chosen pupil among the first five. This is:
   - A) Simple random sampling
   - B) Systematic sampling
   - C) Stratified sampling
   - D) Convenience sampling

9. *(Understand)* What is a sampling frame?
   - A) The size of the sample
   - B) A complete list of the members of the population
   - C) The chart used to show sample results
   - D) The time period of the study

10. *(Apply)* A trainee interviews the first 20 parents who arrive at a school
    meeting. This sample is best described as:
    - A) Simple random
    - B) Stratified
    - C) Convenience
    - D) Systematic

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

Answer each question in a few sentences or a short paragraph.

1. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between a population and a sample, with
   one example of each from a school context.

2. *(Understand)* Define parameter and statistic, and explain how they are
   related.

3. *(Apply)* A head teacher surveys 40 of the 600 pupils in her school about how
   they travel to school and finds that 55 per cent walk. Identify the
   population, the sample, the statistic and the parameter.

4. *(Analyze)* A trainee says, "If my sample is big enough, it does not matter
   how I choose it." Explain why this is wrong, using an example.

5. *(Analyze)* Two samples of 30 schools from the same district give average
   class sizes of 41 and 44. Explain why two honest samples can give different
   statistics, and what this means for inference.

6. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between stratified sampling and quota
   sampling, and state which one is random and why.

7. *(Apply)* A researcher wants to study the study habits of university students
   in Sindh but has no complete list of students. Explain which sampling methods
   she can and cannot use, and why.

8. *(Apply)* Describe how you would draw a simple random sample of 10 pupils from
   a class register of 45 pupils, step by step.

9. *(Analyze)* A study of reasons for chronic absenteeism interviews only the 15
   pupils with the most absences. Analyze whether purposive sampling is
   appropriate here, and what the study can and cannot conclude.

10. *(Analyze)* A college surveys trainees by placing a questionnaire on its
    website and using whoever responds. Identify the likely bias in this sample
    and explain how it could affect the results.

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

Answer each question in a structured paragraph or set of paragraphs.

1. *(Analyze)* A researcher surveys 100 households in a village of 500 to
   estimate the average number of children per household, interviewing
   households near the main road. (a) Identify the population, sample, parameter
   and statistic. (b) Analyze one strength and one limitation of this method.
   (c) Recommend a better method and explain why it would be better. (Write
   120-180 words.)

2. *(Evaluate)* A head teacher claims that "our school's average test score is 75
   out of 100, the highest in the district". You find the average was computed
   from only the top 20 of the school's 200 pupils. Evaluate the claim: identify
   the sampling problem, explain how it distorts the result, and state what an
   honest figure would require. (Write 120-180 words.)

3. *(Evaluate)* A report states: "We sampled 30 schools, which is 10 per cent of
   the 300 schools in the district, so our results are representative."
   Evaluate this claim, explaining whether the fraction sampled determines
   representativeness, what matters more, and what further information you
   would need. (Write 120-180 words.)

4. *(Evaluate)* You plan a study at your practicum school of how many hours pupils
   spend on homework each week. Describe the population and the parameter of
   interest, recommend a sampling method and justify it against at least one
   alternative, and explain how you would collect the data. (Write 150-200
   words.)

5. *(Analyze)* The provincial education department wants to estimate the
   proportion of primary schools in Sindh with working toilets for girls.
   Analyze how you would design the sample: the sampling frame, whether and how
   to stratify, and one practical difficulty with its effect on the results.
   (Write 150-200 words.)

## Answers and marking guidance

### MCQ answer key

| Item | Answer | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | B | The true average of all pupils is a population parameter. |
| 2 | C | The 20 bulbs tested are the subset measured, the sample. |
| 3 | B | Measuring every school is usually too costly and slow. |
| 4 | C | A number computed from a sample is a statistic. |
| 5 | B | Inference is reasoning from a sample statistic to a population parameter. |
| 6 | C | Simple random sampling gives every member an equal chance. |
| 7 | C | Stratifying by area captures the known difference between subgroups. |
| 8 | B | Every kth member from a list, after a random start, is systematic sampling. |
| 9 | B | A sampling frame is a complete list of the population. |
| 10 | C | Taking whoever arrives first is convenience sampling. |

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. The population is the whole group the question is about; the sample is the part actually measured. Example: all pupils in a school (population); 30 pupils chosen for a survey (sample). (2 marks: 1 for the distinction, 1 for examples.)

2. A parameter is a number describing a population, usually unknown; a statistic is the matching number computed from a sample and used to estimate the parameter. (2 marks: 1 for each definition with the link.)

3. Population: all 600 pupils. Sample: the 40 surveyed. Statistic: 55 per cent of the sample walk. Parameter: the true percentage of all 600 who walk. (2 marks: 1 for three correct, 2 for all four.)

4. A large but biased sample stays biased. Example: surveying 2,000 pupils only in city schools cannot represent rural pupils however many are asked. How the sample is chosen matters more than its size. (2 marks: 1 for the error, 1 for a valid example.)

5. Each random sample contains different schools, so its statistic varies by chance (sampling variation). A statistic is an estimate, not the exact parameter, so conclusions must allow for this uncertainty. (3 marks: 1 for sampling variation, 1 for "estimate", 1 for the implication.)

6. Stratified sampling divides the population into groups and draws a random sample from each; quota sampling fills a set number from each group by any convenient means. Stratified is random because selection within each stratum is by chance. (2 marks: 1 for the distinction, 1 for which is random and why.)

7. Without a sampling frame she cannot use simple random, systematic or stratified sampling. She can use convenience or purposive sampling (or build a frame, for example from university lists), but must be cautious about generalising. (3 marks: 1 for what she cannot use, 1 for a feasible method, 1 for the reason.)

8. Number the register 1-45; use a random number source (a table, a phone generator or numbered slips drawn from a box) to pick 10 different numbers; select those pupils, ignoring repeats. (2 marks: 1 for numbering the frame, 1 for a genuine chance mechanism.)

9. Appropriate, because the question is about this specific group and its reasons; a random sample would include few chronic absentees. The study can describe reasons among these pupils but cannot estimate how common those reasons are in the whole school. (3 marks: 1 for appropriateness, 1 for what it can conclude, 1 for what it cannot.)

10. Self-selection (voluntary response) bias: those who respond are likely to have internet access and strong opinions. Results may over-represent these trainees and misstate the views of the whole college. (2 marks: 1 for the bias, 1 for its effect.)
