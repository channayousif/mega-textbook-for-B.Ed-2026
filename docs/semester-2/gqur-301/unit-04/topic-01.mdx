## A real classroom situation

A grade 6 teacher at a government school in Mirpurkhas has marked a short quiz
out of 20. The ten marks are 12, 14, 15, 16, 17, 17, 18, 18, 19 and 3. The head
teacher asks her for "the class average" to put in the monthly report. She adds
the marks, gets 149, divides by 10 and writes 14.9. Then she looks at the list
again. Nine of the ten pupils scored 12 or more, and most scored 15 or more, yet
the "average" is below 15. One pupil, who was ill on the day, scored 3.

Her arithmetic is right. What she needs is a clear idea of what the number 14.9
means, and when it is a fair summary. This topic gives you both.

## Explanation

### The mean: sharing the total equally

A measure of central tendency is a single number that stands for the centre of
a data set. The most familiar one is the <Glossary term="Mean" />, often called
the arithmetic average: the sum of all the values divided by the number of
values.

**mean = Σx ÷ n**

Here Σx (read "sigma x") means "add up every value" and n is the number of
values. For the quiz marks:

mean = (12 + 14 + 15 + 16 + 17 + 17 + 18 + 18 + 19 + 3) ÷ 10 = 149 ÷ 10 = **14.9**

There are two useful ways to picture the result. The first is **fair sharing**:
if the 149 marks were pooled and shared out equally, each pupil would get 14.9.
The second is a **balance point** (see the second figure above): put the marks as weights on a beam
and the beam balances at 14.9. The distances of the marks below 14.9 add to
15.7, and the distances of the marks above it also add to 15.7, so they cancel
exactly. This is true of the mean of any data set (Lane, 2023, Chapter 3).

### The mean from a frequency table

When values repeat, writing every one out is slow. A frequency table (Unit 2)
lists each value once with its frequency f, and the mean becomes

**mean = Σfx ÷ Σf**

Multiply each value by its frequency, add the products, and divide by the total
frequency. Suppose a 10-mark spelling test gave these results:

| Mark x | Pupils f | f × x |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 2 | 10 |
| 6 | 3 | 18 |
| 7 | 4 | 28 |
| 8 | 1 | 8 |
| **Total** | **10** | **64** |

The mean is 64 ÷ 10 = 6.4. This is exactly what you would get by writing out
5, 5, 6, 6, 6, 7, 7, 7, 7, 8 and dividing their sum by 10: the table only saves
writing. A common error is to divide 64 by the number of rows (4) instead of by
the number of pupils (10), which gives an impossible 16 on a 10-mark test.

### Worked example: average class size

A head teacher in Hyderabad wants to know the average number of pupils per
section across her six grade 5 sections, to support a request for another
teacher. The section sizes are 32, 38, 41, 35, 44 and 40.

**Step 1: add.** 32 + 38 + 41 + 35 + 44 + 40 = 230.

**Step 2: count.** There are 6 sections, so n = 6.

**Step 3: divide.** 230 ÷ 6 = 38.33, which she reports as about 38 pupils per
section.

Notice that no section has exactly 38.33 pupils. The mean need not be one of
the values, and it need not be a whole number even when every value is.

### When one value distorts the mean

Because every value enters the sum, every value moves the mean, and a value far
from the rest moves it a long way. Without the mark of 3, the other nine quiz
marks have a mean of 146 ÷ 9 = 16.2. Adding one low mark pulls the mean down
by more than a mark. On the balance beam, a far-away weight has a long lever arm.

A value that lies far from the rest of the data is called an outlier. Before
reporting a mean, look at the data for outliers and ask whether the mean still
describes a typical member of the group.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees believe that "the mean is always the
best measure of central tendency". The mean is the most widely used measure
and it uses all the information, but that same property makes it sensitive to
outliers and to skewed data. When the mean and the bulk of the data disagree,
as in the quiz, another measure may describe the centre more honestly. Topic
4.2 introduces that measure, the median.

## Activity: Two routes to one mean

Work in pairs. About 15 minutes.

Ask the members of your group how many brothers and sisters each has, and
collect answers from at least 12 people (use other groups' answers if needed).

1. Write the raw list and compute the mean by adding and dividing.
2. Build a frequency table (value, frequency, f × x) and compute the mean
   with Σfx ÷ Σf.
3. Check that both routes give the same answer. If they do not, find the error.
4. Is there any value far from the rest? Compute the mean with and without it,
   and write one sentence on how much it moved.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Write the formula for the mean of a list of values, and say in
   words what each symbol stands for.
2. *(Understand)* Explain, using the idea of a balance point, why adding one very
   high value to a data set raises the mean.
3. *(Apply)* In a survey of 20 households, 4 households have 2 children, 9 have
   3, 5 have 4 and 2 have 6. Compute the mean number of children per household
   from the frequency table.

## Summary

The mean is the sum of the values divided by their number. It can be read as
the equal share each member would get, or as the point where the data balance.
For data in a frequency table, multiply each value by its frequency, add, and
divide by the total frequency. Because every value counts, a single outlier can
pull the mean away from where most of the data lie, so always look at the data
before you report its mean.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher for the attendance figures of one class for the
last ten school days. Compute the mean daily attendance. Then look for any day
that was very different (a holiday eve, a rainy day) and compute the mean
without it. Write two sentences for the teacher on which figure better describes
a normal day, and why.

## Summative task

A school records the number of days each of 12 pupils was absent in a term:
0, 1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 29.

a) Compute the mean number of days absent.
b) Build a frequency table for the same data and confirm the mean from it.
c) Compute the mean without the pupil who was absent for 29 days.
d) In 80-120 words, explain to the head teacher what the comparison of (a) and
   (c) shows, and whether the mean in (a) describes a typical pupil.
