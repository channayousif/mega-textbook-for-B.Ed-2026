## A real classroom situation

A small garment workshop in Karachi employs 12 workers. The owner tells a
visiting inspector that "the average weekly wage here is over Rs 31,000". The workers
say most of them earn about Rs 25,000. Both can be telling the truth. The weekly
wages (thousands of rupees) are 18, 20, 22, 24, 25, 25, 25, 28, 30, 35, 40, 85,
and the last figure is the supervisor's. The owner quoted the mean; the workers
are describing the median and the mode.

This topic puts the three measures side by side so that you can see why they
disagree, and what their disagreement tells you.

## Explanation

### Comparing mean, median and mode

Each measure answers a slightly different question, and each has a property the
others lack (the table above summarises them):

- The **mean** uses every value. That makes it informative, and also means one
  extreme value can drag it a long way.
- The **median** uses only the order of the values. It ignores how far away the
  extremes are, so outliers barely move it. It also works for **ranked
  categories** such as grades A to E, where there is an order but no arithmetic:
  if 11 pupils got grades A, A, B, B, B, C, C, D, D, E, E, the median grade is
  the 6th, which is **C**.
- The **mode** uses only the counts. It is the only measure for unordered
  categories, and it is the only one that can be missing or have more than one
  value.

For the workshop wages:

- **Mean:** sum = 377, n = 12, mean = 377 ÷ 12 = **31.4** (thousand rupees).
- **Median:** the 6th and 7th sorted values are 25 and 25, so median = **25**.
- **Mode:** 25 occurs three times, more than any other wage, so mode = **25**.

The mean is about Rs 6,400 above the median because the supervisor's Rs 85,000
pulls it up. Without that one wage, the mean of the other 11 is
292 ÷ 11 = 26.5, close to the median.

### What the comparison suggests about shape

For many data sets with **one peak**, the three measures fall in a typical
pattern (the first figure above):

- **Roughly symmetric:** mean, median and mode are close together.
- **Skewed right** (a long tail of high values, like wages or incomes): the
  mean is pulled furthest towards the tail, so usually mode < median < mean.
- **Skewed left** (a long tail of low values, like marks on an easy test with a
  few very low scores): usually mean < median < mode.

So if a report gives mean 45, median 38 and mode 32, a right skew is the most
likely explanation, and it is worth looking at the data before trusting the
mean. Illowsky and Dean (2023, Section 2.6) present the same pattern.

### The limits of the rule

This pattern is a **rule of thumb, not a law**, and a careful teacher states it
as one:

- **Symmetry does not guarantee one central mode.** The data 0, 0, 2, 2 are
  perfectly symmetric, with mean and median both 1, yet they have two modes,
  0 and 2, and neither is at the centre.
- **Two-peaked data break the pattern.** If a class contains two groups with
  different results, the mode may sit at either peak, far from the mean and
  median.
- **Small data sets are unreliable.** With only a few values, one tie can
  decide the mode, and the order of the three measures can come out either way
  even when the data look skewed.

Use the comparison as a prompt to look at the data, ideally on a dot plot or
histogram (Unit 2), not as a substitute for looking.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees memorise "right skew means mean >
median > mode" and apply it to every data set. The order holds for most
single-peaked distributions, but it can fail for bimodal data, for small data
sets, and for discrete data with ties. Always check the shape directly before
drawing a conclusion from the order.

## Activity: Same mean, different stories

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Here are the marks (out of 10) of two groups of 9 pupils:

- Group A: 3, 4, 5, 5, 5, 6, 6, 7, 4
- Group B: 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 8, 9, 9, 9

1. Compute the mean, median and mode of each group.
2. Draw a dot plot of each group on the same scale.
3. Which group's three measures agree? Which group's measures disagree, and how
   does the dot plot explain it?
4. Write two sentences you would say to the class teacher about Group B that the
   mean alone would hide.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the measure of central tendency that uses every value, the
   one that uses only the order, and the one that uses only the counts.
2. *(Understand)* A data set has mean 25, median 28 and mode 30. Which skew is
   most likely, and why should you still check the data directly?
3. *(Apply)* Find the median grade of these 9 pupils: B, A, C, B, D, A, B, C, E.
   Explain why the mean grade cannot be computed.

## Summary

The mean uses every value, the median uses only the order and the mode uses only
the counts. Outliers and long tails pull the mean away from the other two, so
comparing the three suggests the shape of the data: close together for roughly
symmetric data, mean towards the tail for skewed data. This pattern is common
for single-peaked data but is not guaranteed; bimodal and small data sets can
break it, so the comparison is a reason to look at the data, not a replacement
for looking.

## Summative task

The ages (years) of 11 teachers at a school are 28, 29, 31, 33, 33, 35, 38, 40,
42, 45, 58.

a) Compute the mean, median and mode.
b) In 100-150 words, describe what the comparison of the three measures
   suggests about the shape of this distribution. State one reason why you
   should check that suggestion against a dot plot rather than rely on the
   order of the measures alone.
