## A real classroom situation

A teacher in Sukkur records the favourite subject of each of the 30 pupils in
her class: Urdu 8, Mathematics 12, Science 6, English 3, Social Studies 1. She
has already worked out the mean (38.2) and the median (37.5) of their last test
marks. Then the head teacher asks: "Which subject do most of your pupils like
best?" Neither the mean nor the median can answer that. You cannot add "Urdu"
and "Science", and there is no middle subject. She needs a third measure.

The measure she needs is the mode, and this topic shows you how to find it for
every kind of data.

## Explanation

### The mode: the most frequent value

The <Glossary term="Mode" /> is the value that occurs most often in a data set.
To find it:

1. **Count** how often each value occurs (a tally or a frequency table from
   Unit 2 does this).
2. **Pick** the value with the highest count.

In the favourite-subject data, Mathematics occurs 12 times, more than any other
subject, so the mode is **Mathematics**. On a bar chart the mode is simply the
tallest bar (the first figure above).

The mode is the only measure of central tendency that works for **unordered
categories** such as subjects, home languages or districts. It also works for
numbers: the shoe sizes 7, 8, 8, 9, 9, 9, 10 have mode **9**, because 9 occurs
three times. Notice that the mode is always an actual value from the data,
unlike the mean or, sometimes, the median.

### One mode, two modes or none

Unlike the mean and the median, the mode need not be a single value:

- **Unimodal:** one value has the highest count (the shoe sizes above).
- **Bimodal:** two values tie for the highest count. The marks 4, 5, 5, 5, 6, 7,
  8, 8, 8, 9 have modes **5 and 8**, each occurring three times.
- **Multimodal:** three or more values tie.
- **No mode:** every value occurs equally often, as in 2, 4, 6, 8. Saying "the
  mode is 2" here would be wrong.

A bimodal data set is worth a second look. Two peaks in a class's marks often
mean two groups inside the class, for example pupils who had a tuition class
and pupils who did not, and a single average would hide that.

### The mode of grouped data: the modal class

When data are grouped into intervals, as in a frequency table of marks, the
individual values are no longer visible. We then name the **modal class**, the
interval with the highest frequency. If 3 pupils scored 0-9, 8 scored 10-19 and
5 scored 20-29, the modal class is **10-19**. Textbooks such as Chaudhry and
Kamal (2008) also give a formula to estimate a single modal value inside that
class; for this course, naming the modal class is enough.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think "the mode only matters for
qualitative data". The mode is the *only* option for unordered categories, but
it is useful for numbers too, whenever the question is about what is most
common: the shoe size a shop should stock most of, the class size that occurs
most often in a district, or the mark most pupils obtained. The question, not
the data type alone, decides whether the mode is the right measure (Lane, 2023,
Chapter 3).

## Activity: Survey and tally

Work in groups of four. About 15 minutes.

1. Ask every member of your class two questions: "Which language do you speak
   at home?" and "How many people live in your home?"
2. Tally both sets of answers in frequency tables.
3. Find the mode of each. Is either data set bimodal, or without a mode?
4. For the household-size data, also find the median. Write one sentence on
   which of the two measures answers "what is the most common household size?"

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Define the mode, and name the kind of data for which it is the
   only measure of central tendency.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why the data set 3, 5, 7, 9 has no mode, and why the
   data set 2, 2, 6, 9, 9 has two.
3. *(Apply)* A frequency table of pupils' travel time to school shows 0-9
   minutes: 12 pupils, 10-19 minutes: 17, 20-29 minutes: 9, 30 or more: 4. State
   the modal class and explain what it tells the head teacher.

## Summary

The mode is the most frequent value. It is found by counting, it is always an
actual value from the data, and it is the only measure of central tendency for
unordered categories. A data set can have one mode, two or more, or none at
all, and two modes can signal two different groups. For grouped data we name
the modal class. Use the mode whenever the question is "what is most common?"

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher whether you may record one simple categorical fact
about a class, such as the home language or the way pupils travel to school.
Tally it and find the mode. Then suggest one decision the school could make
using that mode, for example the language of a notice sent home.

## Summative task

A school canteen recorded the snack chosen by each of 40 pupils on one day:
samosa 14, pakora 9, fruit 5, biscuits 12. It also recorded how many snacks each
of 10 pupils bought in a week: 2, 3, 3, 5, 5, 1, 3, 5, 0, 4.

a) Find the mode of each data set.
b) For the second data set, also find the mean and the median.
c) In 80-120 words, advise the canteen manager which single figure to use when
   deciding how much of each snack to prepare, and which to use when planning
   weekly stock per pupil. Justify each choice.
