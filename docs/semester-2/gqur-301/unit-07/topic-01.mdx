## A real classroom situation

A government girls' school in Hyderabad is changing its summer uniform. The
committee agrees that a shirt may be white or sky blue, and trousers may be
grey or navy. The head teacher asks a trainee teacher to write down every
possible uniform so that parents can see them all. The trainee writes the
first combinations that come to mind: white with grey, sky blue with navy,
white with navy, and then white with grey again. Her list has four lines, so
she says there are four uniforms. One combination is missing and one is
written twice. The count happens to be right, but the list is wrong, and a
list that is right by luck cannot be trusted.

This topic shows you how to count possibilities without missing any or
counting any twice.

## Explanation

### Outcomes and systematic listing

An <Glossary term="Outcome" /> is one possible result of a choice or an
event. Each complete uniform (a shirt together with trousers) is one outcome
of the uniform decision. Counting means finding how many different outcomes
there are.

The simplest way to count is to list every outcome. A list is only useful if
it is **systematic**: it follows a fixed order so that no outcome is missed
and none is written twice. A reliable method is:

1. **Fix** the first choice at its first option.
2. **Run through** every option of the second choice.
3. **Move** the first choice to its next option and repeat.

**Worked example.** For the Hyderabad uniform, write W for white, B for sky
blue, G for grey and N for navy.

- Fix the shirt at W: WG, WN.
- Move the shirt to B: BG, BN.

The systematic list is WG, WN, BG, BN: four outcomes, each exactly once. The
trainee's haphazard list missed BG and repeated WG (the first figure above).

### The addition rule of counting

Sometimes a choice is made from **separate groups**, and only one option is
taken in total. At a school Annual Day in Larkana, each pupil enters exactly
one event: one of 3 sports (cricket, kabaddi, sack race) **or** one of 2
stage events (speech, tableau). No event belongs to both groups. The number
of possible entries is 3 + 2 = 5.

This is the <Glossary term="Addition rule of counting" />: if one choice can
be made in *m* ways and a second, separate choice in *n* ways, and the two
groups share no option, then choosing **one** option from either group can be
done in *m* + *n* ways (Abramson, 2021, Section 9.5). The rule extends to
three or more groups: just add each group's count. The word that signals it
is **"or"**: one option from this group *or* from that group.

The addition rule and the multiplicative rule of Topic 7.2 are together
called the basic <Glossary term="Counting principle">counting principles</Glossary>. For further practice
problems, the course guide names the Pakistani textbook by Chaudhry and Kamal
(2008), a print text cited here at bibliographic level only.

### When the groups overlap

The addition rule needs groups that share no outcome. If they do overlap,
adding counts the shared outcomes twice.

**Worked example.** In a Class 7 section of 40 pupils, 12 are in the cricket
club and 8 are in the science club. Three pupils are in both clubs. A teacher
who adds 12 + 8 = 20 has counted those 3 pupils twice, once in each club. The
number of pupils in at least one club is:

12 + 8 - 3 = 17

So 40 - 17 = 23 pupils are in neither club. The rule of thumb: add the
groups, then subtract whatever was counted twice. A quick check is to list
the shared members and ask, "Did I count this pupil once or twice?"

### Common misconception: "you can always count by listing"

Listing is the right first tool. It makes the outcomes visible and it is how
we check a rule. But many trainees believe any counting problem can be solved
by writing out every case. Consider the three-digit combination lock on a
school's examination cupboard. Each digit can be 0 to 9, so the codes run
from 000 to 999: that is 1,000 outcomes. Writing them all would take hours,
and the chance of missing or repeating one is high. Listing works for small
problems and for checking. For larger ones we need a rule that counts
without listing, which is the job of Topic 7.2 (Lane, 2023, Chapter 5).

## Activity: List it, then add it

Work in pairs. About 15 minutes.

1. Partner A writes a haphazard list of every possible "snack and drink"
   choice at a school tuck shop that sells samosa or pakora, and water,
   lassi or tea. Partner B writes a systematic list by fixing the snack first.
2. Compare the two lists. Did either partner miss or repeat an outcome? How
   many outcomes are there?
3. Now the tuck shop runs a lunch offer: a pupil takes **one** item, either
   one of 4 kinds of sandwich or one of 3 kinds of fruit. Count the choices
   with the addition rule.
4. Together, write one sentence explaining why the snack-and-drink question
   and the lunch-offer question are counted differently.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Define an outcome, and state the addition rule of counting,
   including the condition it needs.
2. *(Understand)* In the club example, explain in your own words why 12 + 8
   gives the wrong number of pupils in at least one club.
3. *(Apply)* A school library lets each pupil borrow one book this week,
   chosen from 7 story books or 4 science books. How many different choices
   are there? Which rule did you use?
4. *(Apply)* List systematically every way to choose one class monitor from
   Ali or Sana and one assistant from Zara, Bilal or Hina. How many outcomes
   are there?

## Summary

An outcome is one possible result of a choice. Listing outcomes in a fixed
order (fix the first choice, run through the second, then move on) makes
sure none is missed or repeated. When one option is chosen from separate
groups that share nothing, the addition rule gives the total: add the
groups. When the groups overlap, subtract what was counted twice. Listing is
the foundation and the check, but it becomes impractical for large problems.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can list the outcomes of a two-part school choice systematically.
- [ ] I can use the addition rule and say when it applies.
- [ ] I can correct a count where two groups overlap.
- [ ] I can explain why listing alone is not enough for large problems.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your cooperating teacher how pupils are grouped for one school activity,
such as houses, clubs or co-curricular teams. Find out how many pupils are in
two groups at once. Use the addition rule with the overlap removed to work
out how many pupils take part in at least one group, and compare your answer
with the school's own record.

## Summative task

A secondary school in Mirpurkhas runs two after-school clubs. The computer
club has 25 members, the first-aid club has 18 members, and 6 pupils belong
to both. In a report to the district office, the principal writes: "43 of our
pupils take part in after-school clubs."

a) Explain where the figure 43 came from and what is wrong with it.
b) Work out the correct number of pupils in at least one club.
c) Next term the school adds a third club, gardening, with 10 members, none
   of whom are in the other two clubs. Use the addition rule to update your
   answer, and say why the rule can be applied directly this time.
d) In 60-100 words, advise the principal how to check such counts in
   future.
