## A real classroom situation

At a middle school in Larkana, the head teacher is drafting Monday's
timetable for Class 6. Period 1 must be a language: Urdu, English or Sindhi.
Period 2 must be Mathematics or Science. She asks a trainee teacher, "How
many different ways can I fill the first two periods?" The trainee answers
quickly: "Three languages plus two subjects, so five." The head teacher
writes them out on the staffroom board: Urdu then Maths, Urdu then Science,
English then Maths, English then Science, Sindhi then Maths, Sindhi then
Science. There are six, not five. Adding gave the wrong answer because the
two choices are not alternatives. Both are made, one after the other.

## Explanation

### The multiplicative rule

Many school decisions are made in **stages**: first one choice, then
another, and every outcome needs a choice at each stage. The timetable has
two stages. Stage 1 can be filled in 3 ways. For **each** of those 3, stage
2 can be filled in 2 ways. So there are 3 groups of 2, which is 3 x 2 = 6
timetables.

This is the <Glossary term="Multiplicative rule" /> (also called the
fundamental counting principle): if a first choice can be made in *m* ways
and, for each of them, a second choice can be made in *n* ways, then the two
choices together can be made in *m* x *n* ways (Abramson, 2021, Section 9.5).
For more stages, keep multiplying. Lane (2023, Chapter 5) uses the same idea
for a restaurant menu: 3 soups, 6 main dishes and 4 desserts give
3 x 6 x 4 = 72 possible meals. The words that signal the rule are **"and
then"**: this choice *and then* that one.

**Worked example 1: uniforms.** The Hyderabad uniform from Topic 7.1 now
adds a winter option: no sweater, a grey sweater or a maroon sweater.

| Stage | Choice | Ways |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Shirt (white, sky blue) | 2 |
| 2 | Trousers (grey, navy) | 2 |
| 3 | Sweater (none, grey, maroon) | 3 |

Total uniforms = 2 x 2 x 3 = 12. Notice that "no sweater" counts as one of
the options at stage 3. Leaving it out would give 2 x 2 x 2 = 8 and miss
four uniforms.

**Worked example 2: ID codes.** A school gives each pupil a code made of a
section letter (A, B, C or D) followed by two digits (0 to 9 each), such as
B07. There are 3 stages: 4 ways, then 10, then 10. The school can issue
4 x 10 x 10 = 400 different codes in all. Because the letter is the pupil's
own section, each section has only 10 x 10 = 100 codes, so the format works
only if no section has more than 100 pupils, whatever the school total. If
the school has 450 pupils, four sections of 100 hold at most 400, so at
least one section must run out. A third digit gives 10 x 10 x 10 = 1,000
codes per section (4,000 in all).

**Worked example 3: when choices shrink.** Three pupils, Ali, Sana and Zara,
are to sit in the three front seats. The first seat can take any of 3
pupils. Once it is filled, only 2 pupils are left for the second seat, and
then 1 for the third. The rule still works with the shrinking counts:
3 x 2 x 1 = 6 seating orders. Unit 8 builds this idea into permutations.

### Tree diagrams: seeing the rule

A <Glossary term="Tree diagram" /> shows a staged choice as branches. Each
stage adds a set of branches to every branch before it. Each path from the
start to an end point is one outcome (Illowsky & Dean, 2023, Section 3.5).

**Worked example 4: a school lunch.** A school canteen offers rice or roti,
then daal, chicken or sabzi, then water or lassi. The tree starts with 2
branches. Each of them splits into 3, giving 6. Each of those splits into 2,
giving 12 end points (the first figure above). Counting the end points and
multiplying 2 x 3 x 2 give the same answer, 12, which is exactly why the
rule works.

**Common misconception: "a tree diagram is only a drawing."** A tree is a
counting method, not decoration. It lists every outcome in a systematic
order (the method of Topic 7.1, drawn as branches), so it lets you check a
multiplication, and it still works when the rule does not. Suppose each
pupil chooses one sport, cricket or hockey, and then a position. Cricket
offers 2 positions (batter, bowler) and hockey offers 3 (forward, midfield,
defence). The number of positions depends on the sport, so there is no
single number to multiply by at the second stage. The tree shows 2 end points under cricket and 3
under hockey: 2 + 3 = 5 outcomes. Multiply along a path, add across
separate branches.

### Add or multiply? Deciding in a real problem

The most common error in this unit is the trainee's in Larkana: **"choices
made one after another should be added"**. They should be multiplied,
because each option at the first stage combines with every option at the
next. Before you calculate, ask two questions:

1. **Does each outcome need one choice from every group?** If yes (a
   timetable needs a Period 1 subject *and* a Period 2 subject), the choices
   are stages: **multiply**.
2. **Does each outcome use only one option in total?** If yes (an Annual Day
   entry is one sport *or* one stage event), the groups are alternatives:
   **add**, after checking that they do not overlap.

**Worked example 5: both rules in one problem.** A pupil travels to a
Sukkur school either by van (2 van routes) or by rickshaw and then on foot
(3 rickshaw stands, then 2 walking paths from the stand). The rickshaw
journey has stages: 3 x 2 = 6 ways. The van and rickshaw journeys are
alternatives: 2 + 6 = 8 ways in all.

## Activity: Build a timetable tree

Work in groups of three. About 20 minutes.

1. Your group plans the first three periods for one class. Period 1 is one
   of 3 languages, Period 2 is Mathematics or Science, and Period 3 is one of
   Art, Physical Education or Islamiat/Ethics (count Islamiat/Ethics as one
   option, because pupils attend one or the other in the same period).
2. One member works out the number of timetables with the multiplicative
   rule. Another draws the tree on chart paper. The third lists the outcomes
   systematically.
3. Compare your three answers. If they disagree, find the mistake together.
4. Now change one condition: on Fridays only Mathematics is allowed in
   Period 2. Recount, and write one sentence on how the change shows up in
   the tree.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State the multiplicative rule in your own words, and name
   the words that usually signal it.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why 3 + 2 is the wrong count for the Larkana
   timetable, using the idea of stages.
3. *(Apply)* An examination paper is printed in 5 subjects, each in 3
   versions (A, B and C). How many different papers must be printed?
4. *(Apply)* A pupil chooses one of 4 houses and then one of 2 T-shirt
   sizes for sports day. Draw a tree diagram and use it to count the kits.

## Summary

When a decision is made in stages and every outcome needs a choice at each
stage, multiply the number of ways at each stage. A tree diagram shows why:
each option branches into every option of the next stage, and every path is
one outcome. Choices can stay the same at each stage or shrink as they are
used up, and the rule handles both. When choices are alternatives, add
instead. Many real problems need both: multiply along a path, add across
separate branches.

- [ ] I can explain to a pupil why adding staged choices gives the wrong answer.

## Try this at your practicum school

Look at one real system at your practicum school that gives each pupil or
item a code, such as admission numbers, locker labels or library book codes.
Work out how many different codes the format allows, using the
multiplicative rule, and compare it with the number of pupils or books.
Note whether the format will run out as the school grows.

## Summative task

For the annual sports day, a school in Nawabshah gives each pupil a kit
made of a T-shirt in one of 4 house colours, shorts or a tracksuit
(2 choices), and a cap or no cap (2 choices). A teacher tells the store
keeper, "We need 4 + 2 + 2 = 8 kit types."

Separately, each pupil enters **one** event: one of 3 running races or one
of 2 jumping events.

a) Work out the correct number of kit types, and draw a tree diagram for
   at least the first two stages.
b) Work out the number of possible event entries.
c) In 80-120 words, explain to the teacher why the kit types are
   multiplied and the event entries are added. Use the two deciding
   questions from this topic.
