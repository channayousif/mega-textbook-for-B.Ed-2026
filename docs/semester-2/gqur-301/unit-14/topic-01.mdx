## A real classroom situation

A district education officer in Sanghar wants to know the average number of
pupils per classroom across all 120 government primary schools in the district.
Visiting every school is too costly and time-consuming, so she visits a random
sample of 15 schools and records the number of pupils in one classroom at each.
The sample mean comes out to 34.2 pupils. She writes the report and types "the
average classroom size in the district is 34.2 pupils", then pauses. She knows
the number is based on only 15 schools. She wonders how much trust to put in a
single number, and whether the true district average might be meaningfully
different from 34.2.

<Figure id="fig-U14-1" kind="diagram" src="/img/figures/gqur-301/unit-14/fig-U14-1.svg" alt="A number line from 28 to 42 with an unknown population mean shown as a question mark and the point estimate of 34.2 marked as a solid dot labelled 'our best single guess'." width={780} height={420} />

Her hesitation is well placed. A single number from a sample is a useful start,
but it is only an estimate. This topic teaches you how to compute that single
best guess and how honest to be about what it does not tell you.

## Explanation

### From population parameter to sample statistic

In Unit 3 you learned the difference between a <Glossary term="Parameter" />
(a number that describes a population) and a <Glossary term="Statistic" /> (a
number computed from a sample). The district's true average classroom size is a
parameter: it exists, but the officer does not know it because she has not
measured every school. The sample mean of 34.2 is a statistic: it is computed
from the data she has.

When we use a statistic to estimate a parameter, we call the statistic an
<Glossary term="Estimator" />. The sample mean is the most common estimator of
the population mean. The value it takes in a particular sample is called a
<Glossary term="Point estimate" />. In the officer's case, 34.2 is the point
estimate of the district's mean classroom size.

### The logic of a point estimate

Why the sample mean? Two reasons:

1. **On average it is right.** If you repeated the sampling many times, the
   sample means would centre on the true population mean. An estimator with this
   property is called unbiased: it does not consistently over- or under-estimate.
   The sample mean is an unbiased estimator of the population mean (Lane, 2023,
   Chapter 9).

2. **It uses all the data.** Unlike the median, the mean uses every observed
   value, so it responds to the full shape of the sample.

### Worked example: estimating a mean

The officer's sample of 15 schools gives these classroom sizes:

`31, 28, 35, 40, 33, 29, 36, 34, 32, 38, 30, 37, 34, 31, 35`

Step 1: Sum the values.

31 + 28 + 35 + 40 + 33 + 29 + 36 + 34 + 32 + 38 + 30 + 37 + 34 + 31 + 35 = 513

Step 2: Divide by the sample size (n = 513 / 15 = 34.2).

The point estimate of the population mean is 34.2 pupils per classroom.

<Figure id="fig-U14-2" kind="table" src="/img/figures/gqur-301/unit-14/fig-U14-2.svg" alt="A table comparing two point estimates: a mean estimate of 34.2 pupils from classroom sizes and a proportion estimate of 0.60 from a school library survey, with formulas shown." width={780} height={420} />

### Point estimates for proportions

The same logic applies to proportions. If 9 of the 15 sampled schools have a
functioning library, the point estimate of the district-wide proportion is:

p = 9 / 15 = 0.60

The officer would estimate that 60 per cent of schools in the district have a
functioning library. Here the sample proportion is the estimator of the
population proportion.

### A point estimate is not the truth

The most important thing to understand about a point estimate is what it is
not. It is not the population value. The officer's 34.2 is her best guess, but
the true district average might be 32 or 37. The sample gives her one plausible
value, and the only reason to prefer it to any other single number is the logic
above. Without more information, she has no way to say how close 34.2 is.

**Common misconception.** Trainees often treat a point estimate as if it were
the exact population value, especially when it comes from a large or official
sample. A point estimate is always an inference, not a measurement. The larger
and more carefully chosen the sample, the more trust you can place in it, but it
remains an estimate until you see the whole population. The next topic shows how
to express that uncertainty honestly.

## Activity: Estimate from your own data

Work in groups of three. About 20 minutes.

Each group collects a small data set from around the school: for example, the
number of books on five colleagues' shelves, the number of minutes it takes five
pupils to solve a given problem, or the number of pupils in five randomly chosen
classrooms.

1. Compute the mean of your sample. This is your point estimate of the
   population mean for your chosen question.
2. One member of the group now adds an extreme value to the data (for example,
   a colleague with 200 books). Recompute the mean.
3. Discuss: how much did one value move the estimate? What does that tell you
   about the trust you can place in a single number?

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* In your own words, state the difference between a parameter and a point estimate in one sentence.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why the sample mean is called an unbiased estimator of the population mean.
3. *(Apply)* A random sample of 10 classrooms gives the following numbers of pupils: 28, 32, 35, 30, 34, 31, 29, 33, 36, 32. Compute the point estimate of the mean classroom size.

## Summary

A point estimate is a single number computed from a sample and used to estimate
an unknown population parameter. The sample mean estimates the population mean;
the sample proportion estimates the population proportion. A point estimate is a
best guess, but it is not the truth. The next topic shows how to attach a range
to that guess so that the uncertainty is visible.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can distinguish a parameter from a point estimate.
- [ ] I can compute a point estimate of a mean and a proportion from sample data.
- [ ] I can explain why a point estimate is not the same as the population value.

## Try this at your practicum school

At your practicum school, ask the head teacher for the number of pupils in five
classrooms (or choose five classrooms yourself). Compute the mean. Write one
sentence stating your point estimate of the mean classroom size for the school,
and one sentence explaining what this number does and does not tell you.

## Summative task

A teacher surveys a random sample of 12 households near her school to find the
number of school-age children in each. The data are: 2, 3, 1, 4, 2, 3, 5, 2, 1,
3, 2, 4.

1. Compute the point estimate of the mean number of school-age children per household.
2. A colleague says, "The true mean is exactly 2.67." Is this claim justified?
   Explain your reasoning in two or three sentences.
3. State one reason why the estimate might be too high and one reason why it might be too low.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Computation* | 0-2: correct mean (2.67) with working / right answer with little working / incorrect. |
| *Evaluating the claim (Analyze)* | 0-3: identifies that the sample mean is an estimate, not the true value, and gives a reason (sampling variability, the sample is small, not all households were surveyed) / partial reasoning / none. |
| *Sources of error* | 0-2: one plausible reason each for over- and under-estimation / one reason only / none. |
