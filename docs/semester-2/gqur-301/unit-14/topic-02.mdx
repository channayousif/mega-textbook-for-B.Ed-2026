## A real classroom situation

The same district education officer from Topic 14.1 has her point estimate:
34.2 pupils per classroom, from 15 schools. She now faces the harder question.
The officer reviewing her report asks, "You are telling me the average is 34.2,
but how sure are you? Could it be 30? Could it be 38?" The officer realizes that
a single number does not answer that question honestly. She needs to report a
range that captures the uncertainty, together with a statement of how confident
she is that the true value lies inside it.

## Explanation

### Why a range is more honest than a single number

A <Glossary term="Point estimate" /> says: "Our best guess is 34.2." A <Glossary
term="Confidence interval" /> says: "Our best guess is 34.2, and a plausible
range for the true value, given the data, is 30.5 to 37.9." The range
communicates what the single number hides: that the sample might easily have
come out a little higher or lower, and the true value could lie anywhere nearby.

An interval estimate is built around the point estimate by adding and
subtracting a <Glossary term="Margin of error" />:

```
Confidence interval = point estimate ± margin of error
```

The margin of error depends on three things: the variability in the data, the
sample size, and how confident you want to be.

### The pieces of a confidence interval for a mean

When the population standard deviation is unknown (which is almost always the
case in practice), we use the sample standard deviation (s) to estimate it, and
the <Glossary term="Normal model" /> (or, for small samples, the t-model) to
find the critical value.

**Step 1: Compute the sample mean (x).** This is the point estimate from
Topic 14.1.

**Step 2: Compute the standard error.** The standard error measures how much
sample means vary from sample to sample. It is:

```
Standard error = s / sqrt(n)
```

where s is the sample standard deviation and n is the sample size. A larger
sample gives a smaller standard error: bigger samples produce more stable
means.

**Step 3: Choose the confidence level and find the critical value.** The
confidence level is the percentage of intervals that would capture the true
population value if you repeated the sampling many times. The most common
choice is 95 per cent. For a 95 per cent confidence interval using the normal
model, the critical value (z*) is 1.96 (Illowsky & Dean, 2023, Chapter 8).

The <Glossary term="Confidence level" /> answers a subtle but important
question. A 95 per cent confidence level does *not* mean there is a 95 per
cent chance that the true value lies inside the particular interval you
computed. It means: if you repeated this sampling and interval-building process
many times, about 95 per cent of the intervals you built would contain the true
value (Lane, 2023, Chapter 10). The interval you have in front of you either
contains the true value or it does not; the confidence level describes the
method's long-run success rate, not the probability for this one interval.

**Step 4: Compute the margin of error.**

```
Margin of error = z* × (s / sqrt(n))
```

**Step 5: Build the interval.**

```
Confidence interval = x ± margin of error
  = (x - margin of error,  x + margin of error)
```

### Worked example

The officer's sample of 15 schools: x = 34.2, s = 3.8, n = 15.

Step 1: x = 34.2

Step 2: Standard error = 3.8 / sqrt(15) = 3.8 / 3.87 = 0.98

Step 3: For a 95 per cent confidence level, z* = 1.96.

Step 4: Margin of error = 1.96 × 0.98 = 1.92

Step 5: Confidence interval = 34.2 ± 1.92 = (32.28, 36.12)

The officer can report: "We estimate the mean classroom size in the district to
be between 32.3 and 36.1 pupils, with 95 per cent confidence." This is a much
more honest and useful statement than "34.2" alone.

### Common misconception

The most common error is to say: "There is a 95 per cent probability that the
true mean lies between 32.3 and 36.1." Once the interval is computed, the true
mean is either inside it or not; there is no probability left to assign. The 95
per cent refers to the reliability of the *process* that produced the interval,
not to the particular interval you hold in your hands. Think of it like a
net: a net that catches the fish 95 per cent of the time is a reliable net,
but once you have cast it and pulled it in, you either caught the fish or
you did not.

### What makes an interval wider or narrower?

Two things that the officer can control:

1. **Sample size.** A larger sample gives a smaller standard error, which gives
   a smaller margin of error, which gives a narrower interval. Doubling the
   sample size does not halve the interval: because of the square root, you need
   four times the sample size to halve the margin of error.

2. **Confidence level.** A higher confidence level (99 per cent instead of 95
   per cent) requires a larger critical value (2.58 instead of 1.96), which gives
   a wider interval. More certainty costs more width.

This is the fundamental trade-off: you cannot be more certain and more precise
at the same time without collecting more data.

## Activity: Build and compare intervals

Work in pairs. About 25 minutes.

Use the following sample (n = 12) of travel times (in minutes) from a survey of
pupils at a government school in Hyderabad:

`15, 20, 25, 18, 22, 30, 12, 28, 17, 23, 21, 19`

1. Compute the sample mean and sample standard deviation (use the sample
   formula, dividing by n-1 for s).
2. Compute the standard error.
3. Compute a 95 per cent confidence interval for the mean travel time.
4. Now compute a 99 per cent confidence interval (use z* = 2.58).
5. Compare the two intervals: which is wider, and why?
6. If the sample had been 48 pupils with the same mean and standard deviation,
   by roughly what factor would the margin of error shrink?

## Summary

A confidence interval is a point estimate plus or minus a margin of error,
giving a plausible range for the population value. The margin of error depends
on the data's variability, the sample size, and the confidence level. A 95 per
cent confidence level means that in repeated sampling, 95 per cent of such
intervals would capture the true value. A larger sample gives a narrower
interval; a higher confidence level gives a wider one.

## Summative task

A researcher surveys a random sample of 25 government primary schools in a
district and records the number of textbooks per pupil. The sample mean is 2.4
with a standard deviation of 0.7.

1. Compute a 95 per cent confidence interval for the mean number of textbooks per pupil.
2. A policy document claims: "We are 95 per cent certain that the true mean is
   between 2.1 and 2.7." Evaluate whether this statement correctly describes the
   confidence interval. If not, rewrite it correctly.
3. Explain what would happen to the width of the interval if the sample had 100
   schools instead of 25 (assuming the same mean and standard deviation).

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Computation* | 0-2: correct standard error, margin, and interval (2.12 to 2.68) / one error / two or more errors. |
| *Interpretation (Evaluate)* | 0-3: identifies that "95 per cent certain the true value is in this interval" is incorrect, explains the correct frequentist meaning, and rewrites the sentence / identifies the error but explanation is incomplete / none. |
| *Sample-size reasoning (Analyze)* | 0-2: states the interval would be roughly half as wide because SE shrinks with the square root of n (from 25 to 100 is a factor of 4, so SE halves) / partial reasoning / none. |
