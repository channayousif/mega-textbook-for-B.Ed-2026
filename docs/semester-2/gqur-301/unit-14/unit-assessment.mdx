## Unit summary

This unit, which follows Chapter 14 of the course guide, taught you how to use
sample data to estimate an unknown population value and how to express the
uncertainty in that estimate honestly.

- Topic 14.1 introduced the point estimate: a single statistic (usually the
  sample mean or sample proportion) used as the best guess of an unknown
  population parameter. The sample mean is unbiased and uses all the data, but
  a point estimate is not the truth: it is one plausible value that would change
  if you drew a different sample.
- Topic 14.2 built the confidence interval around the point estimate by adding
  and subtracting a margin of error. The margin depends on the data's
  variability, the sample size, and the chosen confidence level. A 95 per cent
  confidence level means that in repeated sampling, 95 per cent of such
  intervals would capture the true value. Larger samples give narrower
  intervals; higher confidence levels give wider ones.

With these two tools you can move from "here is a number" to "here is a number,
here is a plausible range, and here is how much trust you can place in it."

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

Choose the single best answer for each question.

1. *(Remember)* A point estimate is best described as:
   - A) The exact value of a population parameter
   - B) A single value computed from a sample and used to estimate a parameter
   - C) The range within which a parameter is guaranteed to lie
   - D) The largest value a parameter could take

2. *(Remember)* The sample mean is an estimator of:
   - A) The sample standard deviation
   - B) The population proportion
   - C) The population mean
   - D) The margin of error

3. *(Apply)* A random sample of 16 schools gives a mean class size of 38 with a
   sample standard deviation of 6. What is the point estimate of the population
   mean class size?
   - A) 6
   - B) 16
   - C) 38
   - D) Cannot be determined without the population standard deviation

4. *(Apply)* A survey of 40 households finds that 12 have a computer. What is the
   point estimate of the population proportion of households with a computer?
   - A) 0.25
   - B) 0.30
   - C) 0.33
   - D) 0.40

5. *(Understand)* The standard error of the mean measures:
   - A) The variability of individual observations in the sample
   - B) The variability of the sample mean from sample to sample
   - C) The difference between the sample mean and the population mean
   - D) The width of the confidence interval

6. *(Understand)* A 95 per cent confidence interval for a mean is (45, 55). This
   means that:
   - A) 95 per cent of the data values lie between 45 and 55
   - B) There is a 95 per cent chance the population mean is between 45 and 55
   - C) If we repeated the sampling many times, about 95 per cent of such
     intervals would contain the population mean
   - D) The sample mean is exactly 50

7. *(Apply)* A sample of 25 observations has a standard deviation of 10. What is
   the standard error of the mean?
   - A) 0.4
   - B) 2.0
   - C) 5.0
   - D) 10.0

8. *(Apply)* For a 95 per cent confidence interval with a sample mean of 60, a
   standard error of 3, and a critical value of 1.96, the margin of error is:
   - A) 1.96
   - B) 3.00
   - C) 5.88
   - D) 60.00

9. *(Understand)* Which of the following will make a confidence interval narrower?
   - A) Increasing the confidence level from 95 per cent to 99 per cent
   - B) Decreasing the sample size
   - C) Increasing the sample size
   - D) Increasing the standard deviation

10. *(Apply)* A sample of 30 pupils gives a mean test score of 72 with a standard
     deviation of 8. Using z* = 1.96, which interval is the correct 95 per cent
     confidence interval?
     - A) (69.0, 75.0)
     - B) (68.2, 75.8)
     - C) (70.5, 73.5)
     - D) (64.0, 80.0)

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

Answer each question in a few sentences or a short paragraph.

1. *(Understand)* In your own words, explain the difference between a parameter and a point estimate. Give one example of each.

2. *(Understand)* Explain why the sample mean is described as an unbiased estimator of the population mean.

3. *(Apply)* A random sample of 9 classrooms has a mean of 33 pupils with a sample standard deviation of 5. Compute the standard error of the mean and explain what it tells you.

4. *(Apply)* A sample proportion of 0.45 is computed from a sample of 100 observations. Explain what this number estimates and why it is not necessarily the true population proportion.

5. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between the standard deviation of a sample and the standard error of the mean, in your own words.

6. *(Analyze)* A researcher reports a point estimate of 50 with a 95 per cent confidence interval of (46, 54). Another researcher reports the same point estimate of 50 but with a 95 per cent confidence interval of (40, 60). What can you say about the two samples? Give two possible reasons for the difference.

7. *(Apply)* A sample of 20 schools gives a mean enrolment of 250 with a standard deviation of 40. Compute a 95 per cent confidence interval for the mean enrolment (use z* = 1.96).

8. *(Analyze)* A newspaper headline reads: "95 per cent of teachers agree that class size matters." Explain why this headline, without a confidence interval or a sample size, is insufficient for evaluating the claim.

9. *(Apply)* A sample of 16 observations has a mean of 120 and a standard deviation of 15. Compute the margin of error for a 95 per cent confidence interval. Show your working.

10. *(Analyze)* A trainee says, "A wider confidence interval is always worse than
     a narrower one." Evaluate this statement. When might a wider interval be
     acceptable or even desirable?

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

Answer each question in a structured paragraph or set of paragraphs.

1. *(Analyze)* A teacher computes a point estimate of the mean homework time of her pupils as 40 minutes, based on a sample of 15 pupils. She tells the head teacher, "The mean homework time is 40 minutes." Critique this statement. Explain what is missing and rewrite it as an honest report using a confidence interval, showing the computation (assume a sample standard deviation of 12 minutes and z* = 1.96). (Write 100-150 words after the computation.)

2. *(Evaluate)* A district report claims: "We are 95 per cent certain that the average number of pupils per classroom in the district is between 31 and 37." Evaluate the correctness of this statement from a frequentist perspective. Identify any error, explain why it matters, and rewrite the claim correctly. (Write 100-150 words.)

3. *(Analyze)* Two researchers each take a random sample from the same population and each computes a 95 per cent confidence interval for the mean. Researcher A's interval is (55, 65). Researcher B's interval is (58, 62). Without knowing the population mean, what can you infer about the two samples? Explain at least two things you can conclude. (Write 80-120 words.)

4. *(Evaluate)* A policy maker wants to estimate the average travel time to school for pupils in a rural district. She can afford a sample of either 25 or 100 schools. Evaluate the trade-off between the two sample sizes in terms of cost and precision, and recommend which she should choose, justifying your recommendation with a calculation of the margin of error for both options (assume a standard deviation of 20 minutes and z* = 1.96). (Write 150-200 words.)

5. *(Evaluate)* A school survey reports: "The proportion of pupils who read every day is 0.60, with a 95 per cent confidence interval of 0.54 to 0.66." Describe what this result means in plain language a head teacher can understand, explain one common misinterpretation to avoid, and state one practical decision the head teacher could make based on this result. (Write 120-180 words.)

## Answers and marking guidance

### MCQ answer key

| Item | Answer | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | B | A point estimate is a single value from a sample used to estimate a parameter. |
| 2 | C | The sample mean is the standard estimator of the population mean. |
| 3 | C | The point estimate of the mean is the sample mean, 38. |
| 4 | B | 12/40 = 0.30. |
| 5 | B | The standard error measures how much sample means vary from sample to sample. |
| 6 | C | A 95 per cent confidence level describes the long-run capture rate of the method. |
| 7 | B | SE = 10 / sqrt(25) = 10 / 5 = 2.0. |
| 8 | C | Margin of error = 1.96 × 3 = 5.88. |
| 9 | C | A larger sample size reduces the standard error and narrows the interval. |
| 10 | A | SE = 8 / sqrt(30) = 1.46; margin = 1.96 × 1.46 = 2.86; interval = (69.14, 74.86), which rounds to (69.0, 75.0). |

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. A parameter describes the population (for example, the true mean classroom size in a district); a point estimate is a single value from a sample used to estimate it (for example, the sample mean class size of 34.2). (2 marks: 1 for the distinction, 1 for a valid example of each.)

2. The sample mean is unbiased because its sampling distribution centres on the population mean: if you repeated the sampling, the average of all the sample means would equal the true population mean. (2 marks: 1 for "unbiased", 1 for the reasoning about repeated sampling.)

3. SE = 5 / sqrt(9) = 5 / 3 = 1.67. The standard error tells you how much sample means would vary from sample to sample: a sample mean typically differs from the population mean by about 1.67 pupils due to sampling alone. (2 marks: 1 for correct SE, 1 for a correct interpretation.)

4. The point estimate 0.45 estimates the true population proportion. It is not necessarily the true value because a different sample would give a different proportion; the estimate is subject to sampling variability. (2 marks: 1 for what it estimates, 1 for why it is not the truth.)

5. The standard deviation of a sample measures how spread out individual observations are around the sample mean. The standard error of the mean measures how much the sample mean itself would vary from sample to sample. (2 marks: 1 for each correct description.)

6. Both samples give the same point estimate (50) but different interval widths. Possible reasons: Researcher B has a larger sample size (smaller SE), or Researcher B's data have less variability (smaller s), or a combination of both. (3 marks: 1 for identifying same centre and different width, 1 each for two valid reasons.)

7. SE = 40 / sqrt(20) = 40 / 4.47 = 8.94. Margin = 1.96 × 8.94 = 17.52. Interval = (250 - 17.52, 250 + 17.52) = (232.48, 267.52). We are 95 per cent confident that the mean enrolment is between 232 and 268 pupils. (3 marks: 1 for correct SE, 1 for correct margin and interval, 1 for the interpretation.)

8. The headline gives a point estimate (0.95) but no sample size, no confidence interval, and no information about how the teachers were selected. Without these, you cannot judge the precision of the estimate, the risk of bias, or whether the result would generalise to the wider population. (2 marks: 1 for identifying what is missing, 1 for why it matters.)

9. SE = 15 / sqrt(16) = 15 / 4 = 3.75. Margin = 1.96 × 3.75 = 7.35. (2 marks: 1 for correct SE, 1 for correct margin.)

10. The statement is too simple. A wider interval is less precise, but it may result from a higher confidence level (more certainty) or from greater variability in the data, neither of which is inherently bad. A wider interval that still excludes a meaningful threshold may be sufficient for a decision. (3 marks: 1 for rejecting "always worse", 1 for the confidence-level reason, 1 for a valid scenario where width is acceptable.)
