## A real classroom situation

A B.Ed student sits down to write an essay on "The role of homework in primary learning." She stares
at the blank page for twenty minutes. She knows the topic, she has opinions about it, but she does not
know how to start. She writes a first sentence, crosses it out, writes another, crosses that out too.
Eventually she gives up and tells herself she is "not a writer."

<Figure id="fig-U2-1" kind="concept-map" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-02/fig-U2-1.svg" alt="A concept map placing 'Pre-writing techniques' at the centre with five branches - brainstorming produces a list of raw ideas, listing produces organized points, free-writing produces connected thoughts, mind-mapping produces a visual web, outlining produces a structured plan." width={780} height={470} />

Her problem is not that she cannot write. It is that she is trying to write without first preparing.
This topic gives you techniques to generate ideas and shape them before you draft.

## Explanation

### Why pre-write?

<Glossary term="Pre-writing">Pre-writing</Glossary> is everything you do before you write the first
draft. It has two jobs: generate ideas (get thoughts onto paper) and shape them (organize those
thoughts into a plan). Writers who skip pre-writing often struggle with blank-page anxiety, wander
off-topic or produce drafts that need heavy revision. The time you spend pre-writing saves time later.

### Brainstorming and listing

**Brainstorming** means writing down every idea that comes to mind about your topic - quickly,
without judging or editing. Set a timer for five minutes and write whatever occurs to you. Do not
worry about grammar, order or whether an idea is "good enough." The goal is quantity, not quality.

**Listing** is a tidier version of brainstorming. Instead of a free-for-all, you make a numbered or
bulleted list of points you want to make. Listing works well when you already have some idea of your
main points and want to capture them in order.

### Free-writing and mind-mapping

**Free-writing** means writing continuously for a set time (say, ten minutes) without stopping. If you
get stuck, write "I am stuck" until the next thought comes. Free-writing helps you discover what you
actually think about a topic - often you know more than you realize.

**Mind-mapping** is a visual technique. Write your topic in the centre of a page, then draw branches
for main ideas, and sub-branches for details. A mind-map shows connections between ideas that a list
might hide. Axelerod and Cooper (2016) recommend mind-mapping for topics with several related parts
because the visual layout helps you see how ideas group together.

### Questioning and outlining

**Questioning** means asking who, what, why, how and so what about your topic. For the homework
essay, you might ask: Who gives homework? What does research say about its effects? Why do some
teachers use it heavily? How do pupils feel about it? Answering these questions generates content.

**Outlining** is the final pre-writing step: you arrange your ideas into the order they will appear
in your draft. A simple outline lists your main points (I, II, III) and under each, the supporting
points (A, B, C). An outline is your roadmap - it does not have to be detailed, but it should tell you
what you will write about and in what order.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think pre-writing is a waste of time - "I already know what I
want to write." But knowing what you want to write and having a plan for writing it are different
things. Pre-writing surfaces ideas you did not know you had and prevents the blank-page struggle the
opening vignette describes. Even five minutes of brainstorming before you draft makes a difference.

## Activity: Pre-write a topic

Work in pairs. About 25 minutes.

Take the topic: **"The benefits of reading for pleasure in primary school."**

1. Spend five minutes brainstorming - write every idea that comes to mind without stopping.
2. Now spend five minutes making an outline from your brainstorm - pick the three strongest points and
   arrange them in a logical order.
3. Compare your outline with your partner's. Where do your points overlap? Where do they differ?
   Discuss which outline would produce the stronger essay and why.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name four pre-writing techniques and state what each one produces.
2. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between brainstorming and outlining in one sentence each, using the idea of purpose.
3. *(Apply)* A pupil says, "I want to write about pollution but I do not know where to start." Which two pre-writing techniques would you recommend and why?

## Summary

Pre-writing has two jobs: generate ideas and shape them. Brainstorming and listing capture raw ideas.
Free-writing and mind-mapping explore connections. Questioning probes the topic. Outlining arranges
ideas into a plan. Using even one or two of these techniques before you draft makes writing easier
and more focused.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can use at least three pre-writing techniques to generate ideas on a new topic.
- [ ] I can create a simple outline from a brainstormed list of ideas.
- [ ] I understand the purpose of each pre-writing technique and when to use it.

## Try this at your practicum school

Take a topic your cooperating teacher is about to teach. Spend ten minutes pre-writing using two
techniques from this topic (for example, brainstorming then outlining). Show your outline to your
teacher and ask whether it covers what the lesson needs to address.

## Summative task

Choose one of these topics: "Why pupils should ask questions in class" or "The role of games in
learning." Use two pre-writing techniques to generate and organize ideas (show your work: the raw
brainstorm or free-write AND the outline). Then write two sentences explaining why you chose those two
techniques for this topic.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Techniques used* | 0-2: uses two techniques and shows the output for each / uses one technique / no pre-writing shown. |
| *Outline quality* | 0-3: outline has a clear logical order and covers the topic / partial order or gaps / no discernible plan. (**Analyze**: the outline shows a deliberate organizational choice - e.g. general to specific, problem to solution.) |
| *Justification* | 0-2: explains why the chosen techniques suit the topic / vague or missing. |
