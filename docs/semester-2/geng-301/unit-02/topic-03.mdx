## A real classroom situation

A B.Ed student finishes her essay and hands it in feeling satisfied. She checked every sentence for
spelling and grammar - no errors. But her tutor's feedback reads: "Your introduction does not set up
your argument, your third paragraph belongs in the middle, and your conclusion introduces a new idea.
You have edited but not revised."

<Figure id="fig-U2-3" kind="diagram" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-02/fig-U2-3.svg" alt="A diagram comparing revising (content, organization, argument) and editing (grammar, word choice, sentences) as two separate processes." width={780} height={470} />

The student confused two separate stages. She edited carefully but never revised. This topic makes the
distinction clear and gives you a process for each.

## Explanation

### Revising: what is said and how it is arranged

<Glossary term="Revising">Revising</Glossary> means looking at the big picture. Ask these questions:

- Does my introduction set up my main argument?
- Does each paragraph connect to my thesis?
- Are my paragraphs in a logical order?
- Have I included enough evidence or examples?
- Does my conclusion round off the essay, or does it wander into a new topic?

Revision may involve adding a paragraph, cutting a section that goes off-topic, or rearranging the
order of paragraphs. Johnson-Sheehan and Paine (2016) describe revision as "re-seeing" your draft -
reading it as if you were the intended reader and noticing where you lose the thread.

### Editing: how it is written

<Glossary term="Editing">Editing</Glossary> means looking at the sentence level. Ask these questions:

- Are there grammatical errors (subject-verb agreement, tense consistency, fragments)?
- Are my word choices specific and appropriate for the audience?
- Are any sentences too long or unclear?
- Is the tone consistent throughout?

Editing is where you fix the errors that distract a reader from your ideas. But editing too early is
wasted effort - there is no point polishing a sentence you may delete during revision.

<Figure id="fig-U2-7" kind="table" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-02/fig-U2-7.svg" alt="Table showing common editing errors with before-and-after examples: subject-verb agreement, vague word choice and run-on sentences." width={780} height={470} />

**Common misconception.** Many trainees use "revising" and "editing" as synonyms. They are not. Revising
changes what you say and how you organize it. Editing fixes how you say it at the sentence level. The
order matters: revise first (fix the big problems), then edit (clean up the sentences). If you edit
first, you waste time polishing sentences you may later cut.

## Activity: Revise then edit

Work in pairs. About 25 minutes.

Read this draft paragraph written by a B.Ed student. First revise it (content and organization),
then edit it (sentences).

Original paragraph:

> "Homework is given by teachers to pupils. It helps them to learn. Some pupils do not like it. They
> say it is too much. Teachers give homework because they think it is good. But some parents say it
> causes stress. It is a good thing but also a bad thing. It depends on the teacher."

1. **Revision (12 min):** With your partner, identify two structural problems (missing topic sentence,
   no clear main idea, paragraphs that wander). Rewrite the paragraph with a clear topic sentence and
   one main idea developed with at least two supporting points.
2. **Editing (13 min):** Now edit your revised version for sentence-level issues - vague words ("thing",
   "good"), grammar and word choice. Improve at least three sentences.
3. Compare your final version with another pair. What did each pair change at the revision stage and
   what at the editing stage?

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State two questions you would ask when revising and two you would ask when editing.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why you should revise before you edit, not the other way around.
3. *(Apply)* A pupil says, "I checked all my grammar so my essay is finished." Using the distinction
   from this topic, explain what step they may have missed and why it matters.

## Summary

Revising focuses on content and organization - what is said and in what order. Editing focuses on
sentences - grammar, word choice and clarity. Revise first, then edit. Editing before revising wastes
effort on sentences you may cut. Both stages are necessary for a polished final draft.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can revise a draft by checking its content and organization, not just its sentences.
- [ ] I can edit a draft for grammar, word choice and sentence clarity.
- [ ] I understand why revising comes before editing in the writing process.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find a short notice or circular in your school (a timetable change, a meeting announcement, a holiday
notice). Evaluate it for both revision-level issues (is the information complete? is it logically
ordered?) and editing-level issues (any grammar errors? unclear wording?). Write one revision-level
and one editing-level improvement.

## Summative task

Take the structured draft paragraph you wrote in Topic 2.2. First, revise it: check that it has a
clear topic sentence, that all sentences connect to one main idea, and that it is adequately
developed. Then edit it: fix at least two sentence-level problems (grammar, vague words, sentence
length). Submit the original and final versions with a note listing what you changed at each stage.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Revision* | 0-3: addresses content and organization with clear improvements / minor changes / no visible revision. (**Analyze**: revision changes affect what is said or how it is organized, not just word-level.) |
| *Editing* | 0-3: fixes at least two sentence-level problems with clear improvements / one fix / no visible editing. (**Analyze**: editing targets grammar, word choice or sentence clarity specifically.) |
| *Process distinction* | 0-2: note correctly separates revision changes from editing changes / confused or missing. |
