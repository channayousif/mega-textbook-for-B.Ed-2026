## Unit summary

This unit walked you through the writing process from first idea to reviewed draft.

- Topic 2.1 covered pre-writing techniques: brainstorming, listing, free-writing, mind-mapping,
  questioning and outlining.
- Topic 2.2 gave the three-stage drafting process: rough, structured and refined.
- Topic 2.3 distinguished revising (content and organization) from editing (grammar and sentences).
- Topic 2.4 covered proof reading (spelling, punctuation, formatting) and peer review with a checklist.

Together these give you a complete process: prepare before you write, draft in stages, revise then
edit, and finally proof read and get feedback.

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

Choose the single best answer for each question.

1. *(Remember)* Which stage of the writing process comes immediately after pre-writing?
   - A) Revising
   - B) Drafting
   - C) Proof reading
   - D) Peer review

2. *(Remember)* How many stages does the drafting process in this unit have?
   - A) Two
   - B) Three
   - C) Four
   - D) Five

3. *(Understand)* A pupil says, "I fixed all the grammar in my essay, so it is finished." What did
   they likely skip?
   - A) Proof reading
   - B) Revising
   - C) Drafting
   - D) Outlining

4. *(Understand)* Which of the following is an example of revising (not editing)?
   - A) Correcting a spelling mistake
   - B) Reordering two paragraphs so the argument flows better
   - C) Replacing a comma with a full stop
   - D) Fixing a subject-verb agreement error

5. *(Apply)* A pupil has a blank page and no ideas. Which pre-writing technique would you recommend
   first?
   - A) Proof reading
   - B) Editing
   - C) Brainstorming
   - D) Peer review

6. *(Apply)* A pupil writes a rough draft and stops every sentence to correct spelling. What advice
   should you give?
   - A) "Keep checking spelling - it helps the draft stay clean"
   - B) "Leave spelling checks for later - the rough draft should focus on getting ideas down"
   - C) "Start again with a better outline"
   - D) "Hand it in for peer review now"

7. *(Apply)* Which of these is the best evidence that a writer revised (not just edited)?
   - A) There are no spelling errors
   - B) A paragraph was moved to a better position
   - C) All commas are in the right place
   - D) The handwriting is neat

8. *(Apply)* A peer reviewer writes only "Nice work!" on your draft. What is the main problem with
   this feedback?
   - A) It is too positive
   - B) It is too vague to help you improve
   - C) It uses informal language
   - D) It should have been written in Urdu

9. *(Apply)* You have finished editing your essay. What should you do next?
   - A) Start pre-writing again
   - B) Hand it in immediately
   - C) Proof read for spelling, punctuation and formatting
   - D) Write a new rough draft

10. *(Apply)* A writer says, "I used mind-mapping to explore how my ideas connect, then I made an
    outline." Which two stages of the process did they use?
    - A) Revising and editing
    - B) Pre-writing and pre-writing
    - C) Drafting and proof reading
    - D) Editing and peer review

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

Answer each question in the space indicated.

1. *(Understand)* In two sentences, explain the difference between revising and editing, using the idea of focus (what each one looks at).

2. *(Remember)* Name the three stages of drafting in order and state the main job of each in one phrase.

3. *(Apply)* A pupil says, "I do not need pre-writing - I already know what I want to write." Give
   two reasons why pre-writing would still help them.

4. *(Apply)* Read this sentence from a rough draft: "The thing that the teacher did was a very good
   thing and the pupils were happy about it." Identify the clarity problem and rewrite the sentence
   clearly in no more than one sentence.

5. *(Apply)* A pupil has finished editing their draft. List three things they should check during
   proof reading.

6. *(Understand)* Explain why revising should happen before editing, not after.

7. *(Apply)* You are doing a peer review of a classmate's draft. Write two specific questions from the
   four-question checklist in Topic 2.4 that you would ask.

8. *(Analyze)* A student writes an essay without planning. The first paragraph introduces one topic,
   the second jumps to an unrelated topic, and the third returns to the first. Identify which stage of
   the writing process the student most likely skipped and explain your reasoning.

9. *(Apply)* A pupil mind-maps their topic and produces a good web of ideas, but their draft is still
   disorganized. What step between mind-mapping and drafting might they have missed?

10. *(Analyze)* A student revises their draft by cutting a paragraph, adding two examples and moving
    the conclusion to the end. Then they edit by fixing three spelling errors and breaking up a long
    sentence. Classify each change as either revision or editing.

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

Answer each question in a short paragraph (80-140 words unless stated otherwise).

1. *(Analyze)* This unit described drafting as a three-stage process (rough, structured, refined) and
   said you should not polish during the rough draft. Compare this view with the common belief that
   "a good writer gets it right the first time." Explain, with an example, why the three-stage view
   is more useful for student-teachers.

2. *(Apply)* A B.Ed pupil has written a draft essay but has not revised or edited it. Their essay has
   good ideas but the paragraphs are in the wrong order and there are several vague sentences. Describe
   the steps you would advise them to take, in order, and explain what each step would fix.

3. *(Analyze)* A pupil says, "Peer review is just asking someone if your essay is good." Using what
   you learned in Topic 2.4, explain what is wrong with this view and describe what a useful peer
   review actually involves. Give an example of useful feedback on a draft about "homework in primary
   school."

4. *(Evaluate)* A student writes a rough draft, immediately edits it for grammar, then revises by
   cutting two paragraphs. Evaluate this student's process: what did they do in the wrong order, and
   what problems might that cause?

5. *(Create)* You are teaching a Grade 6 class about the writing process. Write a short explanation
   (50-70 words) that describes, in language a Grade 6 pupil could understand, the difference between
   "writing your first ideas down" (rough draft) and "making your writing better" (revising/editing).
   Then describe one classroom activity you would use to help pupils practise one of these stages.

## Answers and marking guidance

This section is for self-study. Try every question above before you look back at the keys below.

### MCQ answer key

1. **B** - Drafting comes after pre-writing. Revising, proof reading and peer review come later.
2. **B** - Three: rough draft, structured draft, refined draft.
3. **B** - Revising. They edited (grammar) but may never have revised (content and organization).
4. **B** - Reordering paragraphs is revising because it changes organization. A, C and D are editing (surface-level).
5. **C** - Brainstorming generates ideas from nothing. The other stages come later.
6. **B** - Rough drafts should focus on ideas, not surface correctness. Spelling checks come at proof reading.
7. **B** - Moving a paragraph shows revision (organizational change). A, C and D are editing/formatting.
8. **B** - Vague praise does not help the writer improve. Effective feedback is specific.
9. **C** - Proof reading comes after editing. The process is pre-write → draft → revise → edit → proof read.
10. **B** - Mind-mapping and outlining are both pre-writing techniques.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. **Model:** Revising focuses on content and organization - what is said and how it is arranged. Editing focuses on sentences - grammar, word choice and clarity. **Marks (2):** 1 - names content/organization for revising; 1 - names sentences for editing.

2. **Model:** Rough draft (get ideas down, do not edit), structured draft (fix organization, add missing points), refined draft (improve sentences). **Marks (3):** 1 per correct stage with its main job.

3. **Model:** Pre-writing surfaces ideas you did not know you had and prevents blank-page struggle. Even if you know your topic, organizing ideas into an outline saves time later. **Marks (2):** 1 per valid reason.

4. **Model:** The words "thing" and "good" are vague. Rewrite: "The teacher used think-pair-share for ten minutes, and the pupils said it helped them understand the passage." **Marks (2):** 1 - identifies vagueness; 1 - provides a specific rewrite.

5. **Model:** Spelling errors, punctuation errors, formatting inconsistencies (headings, spacing). **Marks (2):** 1 for each correct category named (need all three for full marks, partial for two).

6 **Model:** Revising changes content and organization. If you edit first, you may polish sentences that get cut during revision - wasted effort. **Marks (2):** 1 - explains the order; 1 - names the waste of effort.

7. **Model:** Any two from: Can I identify the main idea? Does each paragraph have a clear topic sentence? Are there sentences I had to read twice? Are there grammar or spelling errors? **Marks (2):** 1 per valid question from the checklist.

8. **Model:** The student likely skipped pre-writing (outlining). Without an outline, there is no plan for what each paragraph should contain, so the writer jumps between topics. **Marks (2):** 1 - names the skipped stage; 1 - explains the consequence.

9. **Model:** They missed outlining. Mind-mapping explores connections but does not arrange them in order. An outline would organize the ideas into a draft-ready sequence. **Marks (2):** 1 - names outlining; 1 - explains why it bridges mind-mapping and drafting.

10. **Model:** Cutting a paragraph = revision (content). Adding two examples = revision (content). Moving the conclusion = revision (organization). Fixing spelling = editing. Breaking a long sentence = editing. **Marks (2):** 1 - correctly classifies all changes; 1 - explains the distinction.
