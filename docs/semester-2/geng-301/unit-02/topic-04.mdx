## A real classroom situation

Two B.Ed students finish their essays on the same night. The next morning, Student A hands in her
essay without reading it again. Student B reads her essay once more slowly, finds three spelling
errors and a sentence that does not make sense, and fixes them before submitting. Student B gets a
higher mark - not because her ideas are better, but because her final version has no small errors
distracting the reader.

## Explanation

### Proof reading: the final check

<Glossary term="Proof reading">Proof reading</Glossary> is the last thing you do before submitting a
text. Unlike revising and editing, which deal with content and sentences, proof reading looks for small
surface errors:

- **Spelling errors**: words misspelled, especially ones spell-check misses (e.g. "their" for "there").
- **Punctuation errors**: missing full stops, commas in the wrong place, apostrophes misused.
- **Formatting errors**: inconsistent headings, uneven spacing, font changes, missing page numbers.

Proof reading works best when you read slowly and aloud - your ear catches errors your eye skips.
Writing teachers commonly advise reading a draft at least once with fresh eyes, ideally after leaving it
for a few hours. If you proof read immediately after editing, your brain sees what it *intended* to
write, not what is actually on the page.

### Peer review: fresh eyes on your work

<Glossary term="Peer review">Peer review</Glossary> means exchanging drafts with a classmate and giving
each other structured feedback. A second reader catches problems you have become blind to - unclear
explanations, missing information, jumps in logic.

Effective peer review is not just saying "it is good" or "I did not like it." Use a simple checklist:

1. Can I identify the main idea (thesis) in the first paragraph?
2. Does each paragraph have a clear topic sentence?
3. Are there any sentences I had to read twice to understand?
4. Are there any errors in grammar or spelling I noticed?

Give your partner two strengths and two suggestions. Be specific: "Paragraph 2 was clear because you
gave a concrete example" is more useful than "Paragraph 2 was good."

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think asking someone to read their draft is a sign of weakness.
The opposite is true. Professional writers, researchers and textbook authors all use peer review. Your
classmate catches what you cannot because they read your text with fresh eyes. The goal is not praise -
it is a better final draft.

## Activity: Proof read and peer review

Work in pairs. About 25 minutes.

1. **Proof reading (10 min):** Take the refined draft paragraph you produced in Topic 2.3. Proof read
   it slowly, reading aloud. Check for spelling, punctuation and any sentence that does not read
   smoothly. Mark any errors you find.
2. **Peer review (15 min):** Exchange your proof read draft with your partner. Using the four-question
   checklist above, read your partner's draft and write two strengths and two suggestions. Return the
   draft with your feedback.
3. Read the feedback you received. Decide which suggestion you will act on and why.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name three things proof reading checks for that revising and editing do not focus on.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why proof reading works best after leaving the draft for a few hours.
3. *(Apply)* A peer reviewer writes only "Good job!" on your draft. Using this topic, explain what
   is wrong with that feedback and what two things a useful peer review should include.

## Summary

Proof reading is the final check for spelling, punctuation and formatting errors. Peer review gives
your draft a second pair of eyes through a structured checklist. Both happen after revising and
editing. Together they catch the small problems that distract a reader and lower the quality of an
otherwise strong piece of writing.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can proof read a draft for spelling, punctuation and formatting errors.
- [ ] I can give structured peer feedback using a checklist rather than vague praise.
- [ ] I understand why proof reading and peer review come last in the writing process.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask two colleagues or fellow student-teachers to swap a short piece of writing (a lesson plan note, a
reflective journal entry) and give each other feedback using the four-question checklist. Notice what
a second reader catches that you missed in your own writing.

## Summative task

Take the paragraph you revised and edited in Topic 2.3. First, proof read it: check for spelling,
punctuation and formatting, documenting what you checked and correcting any errors found. Second,
exchange drafts with a peer: complete the four-question peer review checklist for your partner's draft,
providing two specific strengths and two actionable suggestions. Third, write a brief response (two
sentences) explaining how you will address the feedback you received on your own draft.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Proof reading* | 0-2: documents an accurate check across at least two categories, correctly fixing errors or confirming an error-free text / checks only one category / check superficial or uncorrected errors. |
| *Peer review and response* | 0-3: completes the four-question checklist for a peer with specific, actionable suggestions and includes a reasoned response to received feedback / partial checklist or feedback without response / generic feedback only. (**Analyze**: evaluates specific text features and justifies how received feedback will improve the draft.) |
