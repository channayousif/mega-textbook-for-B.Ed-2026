## A real classroom situation

A B.Ed student has brainstormed and outlined her essay on homework. She feels ready. She opens a fresh
document and tries to write the "perfect" first draft - every sentence polished, every word chosen
carefully. After an hour she has three beautifully written sentences and a throbbing headache. She is
exhausted and the essay is nowhere near done.

<Figure id="fig-U2-2" kind="flowchart" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-02/fig-U2-2.svg" alt="A flowchart showing the three-stage drafting process: rough draft (get ideas down, do not edit), structured draft (organize paragraphs, add missing points), refined draft (improve sentences, check word choice)." width={780} height={470} />

Her mistake is trying to do everything at once. Drafting works better in stages, with a different job at
each stage. This topic explains that process.

## Explanation

### The three stages of drafting

<Glossary term="Drafting">Drafting</Glossary> is not one task - it is three.

1. **Rough draft.** Get your ideas down from your outline. Do not stop to fix spelling, grammar or word
   choice. If you cannot think of the right word, write a placeholder and keep going. The goal of the
   rough draft is to have something on the page - a complete first version you can work with. Williams
   and Bizup (2017) remind us that "you cannot edit a blank page."

2. **Structured draft.** Now read what you have written and fix the organization. Do your paragraphs
   follow your outline? Are there gaps where you need more evidence? Are any paragraphs in the wrong
   order? Add, cut and rearrange. At this stage you are still not polishing sentences - you are making
   sure the structure holds together.

3. **Refined draft.** Now improve the sentences themselves. Replace vague words with specific ones.
   Break up overly long sentences. Check that each paragraph has a clear topic sentence. This is where
   your draft starts to read like finished writing.

### Writing for purpose and audience from the first draft

Even in your rough draft, keep two questions in mind: *Why am I writing this?* and *Who will read
it?* Your purpose (to explain, to analyze, to inform) and your audience (your tutor, a head teacher,
a parent) shape your tone, your vocabulary and how much background you provide. A paragraph written
for an academic tutor uses more formal language than one written for a parent newsletter. Neither is
wrong - but mixing them up confuses the reader.

<Figure id="fig-U2-6" kind="table" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-02/fig-U2-6.svg" alt="Table comparing the three drafting stages by main job, focus and what to avoid at each stage." width={780} height={470} />

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think a "draft" means a nearly finished version. In this
course, "rough draft" means exactly that - rough. It is supposed to have problems. You fix those
problems in the structured and refined drafts. If you try to make your rough draft perfect, you will
write very slowly and lose the flow of your ideas.

## Activity: Three-stage drafting

Work individually. About 30 minutes.

Take the outline you made in Topic 2.1 on "The benefits of reading for pleasure in primary school" (or
make a quick one now).

1. **Rough draft (10 min):** Write a first draft from your outline. Do not stop to fix errors. If you
   get stuck, write "[fix later]" and move on.
2. **Structured draft (10 min):** Read your rough draft. Cut anything off-topic, add anything missing,
   rearrange if needed. Mark any paragraphs that seem out of order.
3. **Refined draft (10 min):** Improve three sentences in your draft - replace vague words, break up a
   long sentence or sharpen a topic sentence.

Keep all three versions. They show your drafting process.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the three stages of drafting and state the main job of each.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why a rough draft should NOT be polished. Give one reason tied to the purpose of the rough draft.
3. *(Apply)* A pupil writes a rough draft and stops every sentence to check spelling. What advice would you give and why?

## Summary

Drafting works in three stages: rough (get ideas down), structured (fix organization) and refined
(improve sentences). Trying to polish in the rough draft slows you down. Keeping your purpose and
audience in mind from the start helps you choose the right tone and vocabulary.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can produce a rough draft without stopping to edit.
- [ ] I can distinguish structural problems (organization) from sentence-level problems (word choice).
- [ ] I can improve a draft by working through the three stages rather than all at once.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask a cooperating teacher whether they draft in stages or try to get it right the first time. Ask them
to show you an early draft of something they wrote (a lesson plan, a report note). Notice what problems
an early draft naturally has and how those get fixed.

## Summative task

Using the outline you prepared in Topic 2.1, produce a structured draft of one body paragraph (80-120
words). Then refine it by improving at least two sentences. Submit both the structured and refined
versions, with a brief note (two sentences) explaining what you changed between the two.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Structured draft* | 0-2: follows the outline and is organized / partly organized / disorganized. |
| *Refined draft* | 0-3: shows clear sentence-level improvements with at least two specific changes / minor changes / no visible improvement. (**Analyze**: the improvements address specific problems in the structured draft - vagueness, length, unclear topic sentence.) |
| *Explanation* | 0-2: explains what changed and why / vague or missing. |
