## A real classroom situation

A B.Ed student submits an essay with strong body paragraphs but no real conclusion. The final paragraph
simply stops mid-thought. The tutor writes: "Your essay walks the reader through three strong points,
then abandons them at the door. What should the reader take away?"

<Figure id="fig-U3-4" kind="concept-map" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-03/fig-U3-4.svg" alt="A concept map showing essay structure - introduction, three body paragraphs and conclusion - with arrows showing the flow and cohesion devices between them." width={780} height={470} />

The student built the middle of the essay well but did not finish the job. This topic covers the final
piece: how to end well and how to hold the whole essay together.

## Explanation

### Four types of conclusions

A <Glossary term="Conclusion">conclusion</Glossary> is more than "the last paragraph." It is the
writer's final chance to leave an impact. Four types work for expository essays:

1. **Summary conclusion** - restates the thesis and main points in different words. Safe and clear, but
   can feel repetitive if done poorly.
2. **Call to action** - tells the reader what to do with the information: "Every primary school in
   Sindh should set aside twenty minutes daily for free reading."
3. **Full circle** - returns to the hook from the introduction. If you opened with a question, answer
   it. If you opened with an anecdote, complete it.
4. **Forward-looking** - points to what comes next: what the reader might investigate, what policy
   might change, what questions remain.

Whatever type you choose, the conclusion must do two things: signal that the essay is ending (use
words like *in conclusion, overall, to summarize*) and leave the reader with a clear final thought.
It must NOT introduce a brand-new topic.

### Cohesion and coherence at essay level

Unit 1 introduced coherence for single paragraphs. At essay level, it means the whole text reads as
one connected argument. Two tools hold an essay together:

- **Transitional devices between paragraphs** (Topic 3.3) - they signal how each paragraph relates to
  the previous one.
- **Thesis-to-paragraph connections** - every body paragraph's topic sentence connects back to the
  thesis, so the reader always knows where they are in the argument.

Williams and Bizup (2017) put it simply: a reader should never have to ask, "Why is the writer telling
me this right now?" If they do, cohesion has broken.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think the conclusion should be as long as a body paragraph.
It need not be. A strong conclusion can be three to four sentences. Its job is to round off the essay,
not to develop a new point. A long conclusion often signals that the writer does not know how to stop.

## Activity: Write two conclusions for the same essay

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Take the essay you have been building across Topics 3.1-3.3 (thesis: "A well-managed classroom gives
pupils a sense of safety, encourages participation and supports learning").

1. Write a **summary conclusion** (3-4 sentences): restate the thesis in different words and name the three
   points without simply copying them.
2. Write a **call to action conclusion** (3-4 sentences): tell a head teacher or fellow teacher what they
   should do, based on your argument.
3. Compare the two. Which is more effective for this topic and audience? Why?

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name four types of conclusions and state when each is most useful.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why a conclusion should not introduce a brand-new topic.
3. *(Apply)* A pupil writes a conclusion that begins: "Another important thing is school uniforms..."
   Using what you learned, explain what is wrong and what the writer should do instead.

## Summary

A conclusion rounds off the essay with a final thought - it does not introduce new topics. Four types
work: summary, call to action, full circle and forward-looking. Cohesion at essay level comes from
thesis-to-paragraph connections and transitional devices. A strong conclusion is brief and leaves the
reader with a clear takeaway.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can write a conclusion that rounds off an essay without introducing new topics.
- [ ] I can choose a conclusion type that fits the topic and audience.
- [ ] I can check an essay for cohesion by reading each topic sentence and confirming it connects to the thesis.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find two essays in a Grade 7 or 8 English textbook (or past B.Ed assignments if available). Identify the
conclusion type in each and evaluate: does it round off the essay well? Note one strong and one weak
conclusion and what makes each so.

## Summative task

Write a conclusion (40-60 words) for the classroom management essay (thesis from Topic 3.3). Choose
either the full circle or forward-looking type and state which you chose. Then write two sentences
explaining: (a) why you chose that type for this topic, and (b) what your conclusion adds beyond
simply repeating the thesis.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Conclusion quality* | 0-3: rounds off the essay clearly with a final thought / repeats thesis without adding / introduces new topic. (**Analyze**: the conclusion adds value - it does not just restate.) |
| *Explanation* | 0-2: gives a specific reason for the type chosen and explains what it adds / vague. |
