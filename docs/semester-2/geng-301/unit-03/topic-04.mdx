## A real classroom situation

A B.Ed student submits an essay with strong body paragraphs but no real conclusion. The final paragraph
simply stops mid-thought. The tutor writes: "Your essay walks the reader through three strong points,
then abandons them at the door. What should the reader take away?"

The student built the middle of the essay well but did not finish the job. This topic covers the final
piece: how to end well and how to hold the whole essay together.

## Explanation

### Four types of conclusions

A <Glossary term="Conclusion">conclusion</Glossary> is more than "the last paragraph." It is the
writer's final chance to leave an impact. Four types work for expository essays:

1. **Summary conclusion** - restates the thesis and main points in different words. Safe and clear, but
   can feel repetitive if done poorly.
2. **Call to action** - tells the reader what to do with the information: "Every primary school in
   Sindh should set aside twenty minutes daily for free reading."
3. **Full circle** - returns to the hook from the introduction. If you opened with a question, answer
   it. If you opened with an anecdote, complete it.
4. **Forward-looking** - points to what comes next: what the reader might investigate, what policy
   might change, what questions remain.

Whatever type you choose, the conclusion must do two things: signal that the essay is ending (use
words like *in conclusion, overall, to summarize*) and leave the reader with a clear final thought.
It must NOT introduce a brand-new topic. *Writing for Success* (2015, section 9.4) advises writers to
"restate your thesis at the beginning of your conclusion" and warns that "Introducing new material in
your conclusion has an unsettling effect on your reader."

A conclusion can also combine types. "In conclusion, reading builds vocabulary and confidence. Every
school should therefore give pupils twenty minutes of free reading each day" is a summary followed by a
call to action. When you classify a conclusion, name every type you can see and point to the words
that show each one.

### Cohesion and coherence at essay level

Unit 1 introduced coherence for single paragraphs. At essay level, two related ideas matter, and it
helps to keep them apart:

- **Cohesion** is about the *language links* between sentences and
  paragraphs: transition words, repeated key words, and pronouns such as *this* or *these* that point
  back to an earlier idea.
- **<Glossary term="Coherence">Coherence</Glossary>** is about the *logic* of the whole essay: the points come in a sensible order, each one
  belongs to the thesis, and the reader can follow the line of thought.

An essay can have cohesion without coherence. Every paragraph may start with "Furthermore", yet the
points can still jump about or wander away from the thesis. Two tools hold an essay together:

- **Transitional devices between paragraphs** (Topic 3.3) - they signal how each paragraph relates to
  the previous one.
- **Thesis-to-paragraph connections** - every body paragraph's topic sentence connects back to the
  thesis, so the reader always knows where they are in the argument.

A simple reader test: a reader should never have to ask, "Why is the writer telling me this right now?"
If they do, check coherence first (is the point in the right place, and does it belong to the thesis?),
then cohesion (is the link to the previous paragraph signalled?).

### Worked example: reorganizing a jumbled essay

Here is the plan of a pupil's short essay, in the order the pupil wrote it:

1. "In conclusion, a well-managed classroom helps everyone."
2. "Because pupils feel safe, they are more willing to answer questions and join group work."
3. "Clear routines, such as a fixed way to enter the room and hand in work, help pupils feel safe."
4. "Think back to your own primary school. Which classroom did you enjoy most, and why?"
5. "A well-managed classroom gives pupils a sense of safety, encourages participation and supports learning."
6. "When pupils take part, the teacher can see what they understand and help them learn more."

**Step 1 - Find the thesis.** Sentence 5 states the controlling idea and its three points (safety,
participation, learning). It belongs at the end of the introduction.

**Step 2 - Find the introduction.** Sentence 4 is a question hook. It comes first, so the introduction
is 4 then 5.

**Step 3 - Order the body points to match the thesis.** The thesis names safety, then participation,
then learning, so the body order is 3, 2, 6. This order is also logical: each point causes the next.

**Step 4 - Place the conclusion last.** Sentence 1 ends the essay. It is weak because it only says the
classroom "helps everyone", so the writer should restate the three points in new words.

**Step 5 - Check the links.** Sentence 2 already links back with "Because pupils feel safe". Sentence 6
links with "When pupils take part". These are cohesive links that show the logical chain.

Final order: 4, 5, 3, 2, 6, 1.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think the conclusion should be as long as a body paragraph.
It need not be. A strong conclusion can be three to four sentences. Its job is to round off the essay,
not to develop a new point. A long conclusion often signals that the writer does not know how to stop.

## Activity: Write two conclusions for the same essay

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Use the classroom-management essay from the worked example above. Its thesis is: "A well-managed
classroom gives pupils a sense of safety, encourages participation and supports learning." Its three
body paragraphs make these points:

- Clear routines help pupils feel safe.
- Pupils who feel safe are more willing to answer questions and join group work.
- When pupils take part, the teacher can see what they understand and help them learn more.

If you wrote the safety paragraph in the Topic 3.3 summative task, you may use it as well.

1. Write a **summary conclusion** (3-4 sentences): restate the thesis in different words and name the three
   points without simply copying them.
2. Write a **call to action conclusion** (3-4 sentences): tell a head teacher or fellow teacher what they
   should do, based on your argument.
3. Compare the two. Which is more effective for this topic and audience? Why?

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name four types of conclusions and state when each is most useful.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why a conclusion should not introduce a brand-new topic.
3. *(Apply)* A pupil writes a conclusion that begins: "Another important thing is school uniforms..."
   Using what you learned, explain what is wrong and what the writer should do instead.

## Summary

A conclusion rounds off the essay with a final thought - it does not introduce new topics. Four types
work: summary, call to action, full circle and forward-looking. Cohesion at essay level comes from
thesis-to-paragraph connections and transitional devices; coherence comes from a logical order of
points that all serve the thesis. To repair a jumbled essay, find the thesis, order the body points to
match it, and check the links. A strong conclusion is brief and leaves the
reader with a clear takeaway.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can write a conclusion that rounds off an essay without introducing new topics.
- [ ] I can choose a conclusion type that fits the topic and audience.
- [ ] I can check an essay for cohesion by reading each topic sentence and confirming it connects to the thesis.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find two essays in a Grade 7 or 8 English textbook (or past B.Ed assignments if available). Identify the
conclusion type in each and evaluate: does it round off the essay well? Note one strong and one weak
conclusion and what makes each so.

## Summative task

Write a conclusion (40-60 words) for the classroom management essay (thesis and points from the worked
example in this topic). Choose
either the full circle or forward-looking type and state which you chose. Then write two sentences
explaining: (a) why you chose that type for this topic, and (b) what your conclusion adds beyond
simply repeating the thesis.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Conclusion quality* | 0-3: rounds off the essay clearly with a final thought / repeats thesis without adding / introduces new topic. (**Analyze**: the conclusion adds value - it does not just restate.) |
| *Explanation* | 0-2: gives a specific reason for the type chosen and explains what it adds / vague. |
