## A real classroom situation

A pupil writes an essay with a clear thesis: "Daily reading at home improves vocabulary, comprehension
and confidence." But each body paragraph simply repeats the thesis with a different example tacked on.
The tutor writes: "Your paragraphs say the same thing three times. Each one needs to develop a
different point from your thesis."

<Figure id="fig-U3-3" kind="flowchart" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-03/fig-U3-3.svg" alt="A flowchart showing body paragraph construction: topic sentence connected to thesis, then point, evidence, explanation and transition." width={780} height={470} />

The pupil had a thesis but did not know how to build body paragraphs that developed it point by point.
This topic gives you that skill.

## Explanation

### The body paragraph's job

Each body paragraph develops one point from the thesis. If your thesis has three points
(vocabulary, comprehension, confidence), you need three body paragraphs, each focused on one point.

A body paragraph has the same structure as the single paragraph in Unit 1 - but with one addition: it
must connect to the thesis.

### Topic sentences that connect to the thesis

A topic sentence in a body paragraph does two things: it states the paragraph's main point AND it
connects back to the thesis. Compare:

- **Weak topic sentence**: "Reading is good." (vague, no connection to thesis)
- **Strong topic sentence**: "Daily reading at home builds vocabulary far more effectively than
  vocabulary drills alone." (specific, connects to the "vocabulary" point in the thesis)

### Supporting evidence

Evidence makes your point believable. Four types work in expository essays:

1. **Examples** - a concrete case that illustrates the point.
2. **Reasons** - why the point is true.
3. **Facts or statistics** - data that supports the claim.
4. **Short quotations** - from a researcher, teacher or credible source.

After giving evidence, always explain *how* it supports your point. Evidence alone does not speak for
itself - you must connect it to your argument.

<Figure id="fig-U3-8" kind="table" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-03/fig-U3-8.svg" alt="Table of transitional devices grouped by function: addition, contrast, cause/effect and conclusion, with example words." width={780} height={470} />

### Transitional devices

Transitional devices connect paragraphs so the essay reads as a whole, not as isolated chunks. Use them
at the start of a new paragraph to signal the relationship to the previous one:

- **Addition**: *in addition, furthermore, moreover, another*
- **Contrast**: *however, on the other hand, in contrast, yet*
- **Cause/effect**: *as a result, therefore, consequently, because of this*
- **Conclusion**: *in conclusion, overall, to summarize*

Choose the transition that matches the logical relationship. "However" between two paragraphs that agree
with each other confuses the reader.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think every body paragraph must begin with a transition word.
Not so. The first body paragraph follows the introduction directly - no transition needed. Transitions
appear between paragraphs (paragraphs 2, 3, 4...) to show how each connects to what came before.

## Activity: Build a body paragraph from a thesis

Work in pairs. About 25 minutes.

Take this thesis: **"A well-managed classroom gives pupils a sense of safety, encourages participation
and supports learning."**

You will build the body paragraph for the second point (encourages participation).

1. Write a topic sentence that connects to the thesis and states the point about participation.
2. Add two pieces of evidence (an example and a reason).
3. After each evidence sentence, write one sentence explaining how it supports your point.
4. End with a transition sentence that leads to the next paragraph (about supporting learning).
5. Share with another pair. Check: does every sentence connect to the point? Is the evidence explained?

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the components of a body paragraph and state the job of each.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why a body paragraph's topic sentence should connect to the thesis, not just
   state any point about the topic.
3. *(Apply)* A pupil writes: "Practical work helps pupils. For example, a science demo. It is fun." Using
   what you learned, identify two things missing from this paragraph and explain why they matter.

## Summary

Each body paragraph develops one point from the thesis. It needs a topic sentence that connects to the
thesis, evidence that supports the point and an explanation of how the evidence connects. Transitional
devices link paragraphs logically. The first body paragraph needs no transition; later paragraphs use
transitions that match the logical relationship.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can write a topic sentence that connects to the thesis.
- [ ] I can support a point with evidence and explain how the evidence connects.
- [ ] I can choose a transitional device that matches the relationship between paragraphs.

## Try this at your practicum school

Take a textbook chapter (any subject, Grades 4-8). Find three consecutive paragraphs. Identify: (a) the
topic sentence of each, (b) the evidence type used and (c) any transitional devices between paragraphs.
Note whether the paragraphs connect to each other or feel like isolated chunks.

## Summative task

Using the thesis "A well-managed classroom gives pupils a sense of safety, encourages participation
and supports learning," write one complete body paragraph (80-120 words) developing the first point
(safety). Your paragraph must have: a topic sentence connected to the thesis, two pieces of evidence
with explanation, and a transition to the next paragraph.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Topic sentence* | 0-2: connects to thesis and states the point clearly / connects weakly / no connection. |
| *Evidence and explanation* | 0-3: two pieces of evidence each with explanation / one explained / evidence without explanation. (**Analyze**: explanation explicitly links the evidence to the point, not just repeats it.) |
| *Transition* | 0-2: uses a transition that matches the logical relationship / transition present but mismatched / missing. |
