## A real classroom situation

A B.Ed student writes an essay on the importance of play in early learning. Her first sentence reads:
"In this essay I will write about the importance of play in early learning." Her tutor circles it and
writes: "Your reader already knows the topic from the title. Why should they keep reading? Give them a
reason."

<Figure id="fig-U3-1" kind="diagram" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-03/fig-U3-1.svg" alt="A diagram showing the funnel shape of an introduction from hook (wide) through background to thesis statement (narrow)." width={780} height={470} />

Her introduction did its job of stating the topic, but it failed at the more important job: making the
reader want to read on. This topic teaches you how to start an essay so the reader leans in, not
tunes out.

## Explanation

### The hook

A <Glossary term="Hook">hook</Glossary> is the first sentence (or two) of an essay. Its job is to
capture the reader's attention. Four common types work well for expository essays:

1. **A question** the essay will answer: "What if half the school day were spent outside the classroom?"
2. **A short anecdote** that illustrates the topic: "A Grade 3 teacher in Lahore decided to drop
   homework for one term. Here is what happened to her pupils' results."
3. **A surprising fact or statistic**: "According to UNESCO (2022), over 250 million children worldwide
   cannot read a simple sentence by age ten."
4. **A bold statement** the essay will unpack: "The most powerful teaching tool in a primary school
   costs nothing and fits in a teacher's pocket."

Choose a hook that fits your topic and audience. A formal academic essay may use a statistic; a
reflective piece may use an anecdote.

### The introduction's funnel structure

An introduction moves from wide to narrow - like a funnel. Start with the hook (wide, engaging), then
provide a sentence or two of context (narrowing), and end with your <Glossary term="Thesis statement">thesis statement</Glossary> (narrow, specific). The thesis statement tells the reader exactly
what your essay will argue or explain.

Bullock, Goggin and Weinberg (2016) describe this as "moving from the general to the specific." A good
introduction makes the reader think, "I see where this is going, and I want to follow."

<Figure id="fig-U3-6" kind="table" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-03/fig-U3-6.svg" alt="Table comparing four hook types with examples and guidance on when to use each." width={780} height={470} />

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think the introduction should list every point the essay will
cover: "In this essay I will discuss A, B, C and D." That is a table of contents, not an introduction.
The introduction sets up the thesis; the body paragraphs develop A, B, C and D. A reader who knows
the thesis already knows what to expect.

## Activity: Write three hooks

Work in pairs. About 15 minutes.

Take the topic: **"The case for mother-tongue instruction in early primary school."**

1. Write three different hooks for this topic - one question, one statistic and one anecdote.
2. Share your hooks with your partner. Discuss: which hook makes you most want to read on, and why?
3. Now write a two-sentence introduction that follows your best hook: hook + one sentence of context
   leading to a thesis statement.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name four types of hooks and give one example of each.
2. *(Understand)* Explain the funnel structure of an introduction in one or two sentences.
3. *(Apply)* A pupil writes: "This essay is about homework. I will talk about its pros and cons." Using
   what you learned about hooks and funnel structure, explain what is missing and why it matters.

## Summary

A hook captures the reader's attention. An introduction follows a funnel: hook → context → thesis
statement. Four hook types work for expository essays: question, anecdote, statistic and bold
statement. The introduction sets up the thesis; it does not list every point in the essay.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can write a hook that makes a reader want to continue.
- [ ] I can structure an introduction from hook to thesis using the funnel shape.
- [ ] I understand the difference between an introduction and a table of contents.

## Try this at your practicum school

Take an English textbook used in Grade 6 or 7. Find three different essay introductions. Identify the
hook type in each and evaluate whether the funnel structure is clear. Note one strong and one weak
introduction.

## Summative task

Write a complete introduction (60-90 words) for an essay on **"Why every school should have a green
space."** Your introduction must include: (1) a hook (state which type you chose), (2) one or two
sentences of context, and (3) a thesis statement. Label each part.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Hook* | 0-2: hook is present and fits the type stated / hook present but weak / no hook. |
| *Funnel structure* | 0-3: clear move from hook through context to thesis / partial structure / no clear progression. (**Analyze**: the funnel narrows from general to specific in a way that leads naturally to the thesis.) |
| *Thesis statement* | 0-2: thesis is specific and arguable / vague or merely topical. |
