## A real classroom situation

A pupil hands in an essay with this thesis statement: "Homework is a very important thing in education."
The teacher's response: "Important how? For whom? Under what conditions? I cannot tell what your essay
will explain or argue because this thesis is too vague."

The pupil's thesis was not wrong - it was just too vague to be useful. A good thesis does more than
name the topic. It tells the reader exactly what the essay will explain or argue.

## Explanation

### What a thesis statement does

A <Glossary term="Thesis statement">thesis statement</Glossary> is usually one sentence at the end
of the introduction. It does three things:

1. **States the topic** - what the essay is about.
2. **States the controlling idea** - the one main point the essay explains or argues about that topic.
3. **Signals the scope** - what the essay will and will not cover.

The open textbook *Writing for Success* (2015, section 9.1) defines a thesis statement as "a sentence
that presents the controlling idea of an essay" and adds that it "must concentrate on a specific area
of a general topic."

A thesis can do this in two ways, depending on the kind of essay:

- An **explanatory thesis** sets out what the essay will explain: "Pupils in Grade 4 learn to write
  a paragraph in three stages: planning, drafting and checking." Nobody needs to disagree with it; it
  is strong because it is clear, focused and limited in scope.
- An **argumentative thesis** states a claim the essay will defend: "Mother-tongue instruction in the
  first three years of school gives pupils a stronger foundation for learning English later than
  English-only instruction." Here a reader could disagree, and the essay must give evidence.

Most essays in this course are expository, so you will write many explanatory theses. Both kinds are
judged by the same tests: is the main idea clear, is it focused on one point, and is the scope specific?

### Strong vs weak thesis statements

A weak thesis is too broad, too vague or only names the topic. A strong thesis is clear, specific
and focused.

| Weak thesis | Why it is weak |
|---|---|
| "Education is important." | Too broad - could cover anything in education. No clear main idea. |
| "This essay will discuss homework." | Just announces the topic. No controlling idea. |
| "Some people like homework and some do not." | Names two sides but no focus - the reader cannot tell what the essay will explain. |

| Strong thesis | Why it is strong |
|---|---|
| "Effective homework in Grades 3-5 has three features: it is short, it practises one skill and it is marked quickly." | Explanatory. Specific scope (Grades 3-5) and a clear plan of three points. |
| "Mother-tongue instruction in the first three years of school gives pupils a stronger foundation for learning English later than English-only instruction." | Argumentative. Specific, focused, and the claim can be supported with evidence. |

A useful test: ask "After reading only this sentence, can a reader say exactly what the essay will
explain or argue, and what it will leave out?" If yes, the thesis is clear and focused. If no, narrow
it.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think the thesis statement should appear at the very beginning
of the essay. It usually appears at the *end* of the introduction - after the hook and context - so the
reader is prepared for it. Placing it first (before any context) can feel abrupt.

## Activity: Strengthen weak thesis statements

Work individually. About 15 minutes.

Rewrite each of these weak thesis statements to make them strong (clear, specific, focused). You may
write an explanatory or an argumentative thesis; say which kind you chose.

1. "Games are good for children."
2. "This essay is about teacher training."
3. "Some schools have libraries and some do not."

For each rewrite, explain in one sentence what you changed and why it is stronger. Then swap with a
partner and compare.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State the three jobs of a thesis statement.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why "Education is a good thing" is a weak thesis. Use two features of a
   strong thesis in your explanation.
3. *(Apply)* Check this thesis against the four criteria in the thesis checklist: "Group work helps pupils learn
   because it gives them a chance to explain ideas to each other, which deepens understanding." Which
   criteria does it meet?

## Summary

A thesis statement states the topic, states the controlling idea and signals the scope. It should be
clear, specific and focused - not broad, vague or a mere announcement. It may explain (explanatory
thesis) or claim (argumentative thesis). A strong thesis usually appears at the end of the
introduction. Test your thesis: can a reader tell exactly what the essay will cover?

## Summative task

Take this weak thesis: "Punishment is bad for pupils." Rewrite it as a strong thesis statement (one
sentence). Then write two sentences explaining what was wrong with the original and what makes your
rewrite stronger.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|

| *Explanation* | 0-2: identifies specific weaknesses in the original and specific strengths in the rewrite / vague. |
