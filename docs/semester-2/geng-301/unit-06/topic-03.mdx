## A real classroom situation

A pupil's essay is full of direct quotes - every second sentence is in quotation marks. Her tutor
writes: "This reads like a patchwork of other people's words, not your own writing. Paraphrase more,
quote only when the exact wording matters."

<Figure id="fig-U6-3" kind="table" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-06/fig-U6-3.svg" alt="Table comparing quoting, paraphrasing and summarizing by definition, when to use each and the key rule for each." width={780} height={470} />

The pupil over-quoted. This topic teaches you three ways to integrate sources and when each is right.

## Explanation

### Three integration techniques

When you use a source, you have three choices:

1. **<Glossary term="Quoting">Quoting</Glossary>** - use the author's exact words, in quotation marks,
   with a citation. Use sparingly: when the exact wording is powerful, precise or memorable.
   Over-quoting makes your essay feel like a collection of other people's voices.

2. **<Glossary term="Paraphrasing">Paraphrasing</Glossary>** - restate a specific idea or passage in
   your own words, with a citation. This is the most common technique in academic writing. A good
   paraphrase changes the wording AND the sentence structure, not just a few words.

3. **<Glossary term="Summarizing">Summarizing</Glossary>** - condense a longer passage or argument into
   a few sentences in your own words, with a citation. Use when you need the main point but not the
   detail.

<Figure id="fig-U6-7" kind="table" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-06/fig-U6-7.svg" alt="Table showing a paraphrasing example: original, poor paraphrase and good paraphrase." width={780} height={470} />

### Paraphrasing done right

A poor paraphrase swaps a few words but keeps the original sentence structure - this is mosaic
plagiarism. A good paraphrase:

1. Reads the original and sets it aside.
2. Writes the idea from memory in your own words and sentence structure.
3. Checks against the original to ensure the meaning is preserved and the wording is genuinely
   different.
4. Adds a citation.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think changing a few words in a sentence (using synonyms) is a
valid paraphrase. It is not. True paraphrasing changes both wording AND structure. If your sentence
follows the same sequence as the original, it is too close.

## Activity: Paraphrase a passage

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Paraphrase this passage in your own words. Then write a one-sentence summary.

> Original: "Formative assessment, when practised consistently, allows teachers to identify gaps in
> pupil understanding before those gaps become entrenched. Unlike summative assessment, which measures
> learning at the end of a unit, formative assessment provides ongoing feedback that shapes teaching in
> real time." (Channa, 2023, p. 78)

1. **Paraphrase**: Restate the key idea in your own words and sentence structure. Do NOT just swap
   synonyms. Add an APA in-text citation.
2. **Summary**: Condense the passage into one sentence capturing the main point.
3. Exchange with another partner. Check: does the paraphrase follow the same sentence sequence as the
   original, or has the structure genuinely changed?

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the three integration techniques and state when each is appropriate.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why a paraphrase that only swaps synonyms is not a true paraphrase.
3. *(Apply)* A pupil copies a sentence and changes "important" to "significant." Using what you learned,
   explain why this is not a valid paraphrase and what they should do instead.

## Summary

Three integration techniques: quoting (exact words, sparingly), paraphrasing (own words, own structure,
most common) and summarizing (condensed main point). A true paraphrase changes both wording and
sentence structure. Always cite, regardless of technique.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can paraphrase a passage in my own words and sentence structure.
- [ ] I can distinguish a good paraphrase from a poor one.
- [ ] I know when to quote, paraphrase and summarize.

## Try this at your practicum school

Find a short passage from a textbook or article. Write one direct quote (with quotation marks and
citation) and one paraphrase (in your own words with citation). Compare: which better serves your
purpose as a writer?

## Summative task

Take this passage: "The CRAAP test helps writers evaluate the credibility of sources by examining five
criteria: currency, relevance, authority, accuracy, and purpose." (Johnson & Paine, 2016, p. 215).

Write: (1) a paraphrase in your own words and sentence structure (with APA citation), and (2) a
one-sentence summary (with APA citation). Then write one sentence explaining what you changed in your
paraphrase to avoid mosaic plagiarism.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Paraphrase quality* | 0-3: wording and structure both genuinely changed / wording changed but structure similar / too close to original. (**Analyze**: the paraphrase reads as the writer's own words, not a synonym swap.) |
| *Summary* | 0-2: captures the main point in one sentence / partial or too long. |
