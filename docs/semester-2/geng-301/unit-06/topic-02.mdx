## A real classroom situation

A B.Ed student writes an essay and includes a reference list at the end with five sources. But in the
body of the essay, there are no in-text citations - no way for the reader to know which idea came from
which source. Her tutor writes: "A reference list without in-text citations is a bibliography, not a
citation system. The reader needs to see which idea belongs to which source."

<Figure id="fig-U6-2" kind="flowchart" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-06/fig-U6-2.svg" alt="A flowchart showing when to cite: if it is not common knowledge and uses someone's ideas, data or words, you must cite." width={780} height={470} />

The student had a reference list but no in-text citations. This topic teaches you both halves of a
citation system.

## Explanation

### When to cite

<Glossary term="Citation">Citation</Glossary> is required whenever you use someone else's ideas, data
or words. You do NOT need to cite common knowledge (e.g. "Pakistan gained independence in 1947") or
your own original observations.

A simple test: ask "Did I know this before I read it, or did I learn it from a source?" If you learned
it from a source, cite it. When in doubt, cite - over-citing is a minor error; under-citing is
plagiarism.

### What to cite

You must cite:
- Direct quotations (someone else's exact words).
- Paraphrases (someone else's idea in your own words).
- Summaries (someone else's argument condensed).
- Data, statistics, graphs or images from a source.
- Specific theories or frameworks attributed to a particular author.

### APA vs MLA

Two citation styles are common in education:

| Feature | APA (American Psychological Association) | MLA (Modern Language Association) |
|---|---|---|
| Used in | Education, psychology, social sciences | Humanities, literature, languages |
| In-text citation | (Author, Year, p. page) | (Author page) |
| Reference list name | References | Works Cited |
| Year position | After author name | Near the end of the entry |

<Figure id="fig-U6-6" kind="table" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-06/fig-U6-6.svg" alt="Table comparing APA and MLA citation styles side by side across four features." width={780} height={470} />

For this course, either style is acceptable, but use one consistently throughout an essay. Do not mix
APA in-text citations with an MLA reference list.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think a reference list at the end is enough. It is not. Every
source in your reference list must be cited in the text, and every in-text citation must appear in the
reference list. They are two halves of one system.

## Activity: Format a reference

Work in pairs. About 15 minutes.

Format this source in BOTH APA and MLA styles. Note the differences.

Source: A book titled *Teaching and Learning in Pakistan* by Muhammad Channa, published in 2023 by
Oxford University Press, Karachi.

1. Write the APA reference list entry.
2. Write the MLA reference list entry.
3. Write one APA in-text citation and one MLA in-text citation for a quote from page 45.

Compare with another pair. Identify one difference between the two styles.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State the five types of content that require citation.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why a reference list alone is not enough - why in-text citations are also
   needed.
3. *(Apply)* A pupil writes: "Research says homework is important." No citation. Using what you learned,
   explain what is wrong and what the pupil should add.

## Summary

Cite when you use someone else's ideas, data or words - not for common knowledge. APA and MLA are both
acceptable; use one consistently. Every in-text citation must appear in the reference list and vice
versa. When in doubt, cite.

## Summative task

Format this source in APA style: "A journal article by S. B. Hussain and F. Ali titled 'The impact of
teacher training on classroom practice,' published in 2022 in the Journal of Research and Reflections in
Education, volume 16, issue 2, pages 112-130." Then write one APA in-text citation for a paraphrase from
page 118.
