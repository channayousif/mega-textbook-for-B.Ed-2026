## A real classroom situation

A pupil writes in an essay: "Our school is very good. It has many facilities and the teachers are
very nice." Her teacher writes: "I still cannot picture your school. What does it look like? Give me
one concrete detail I can see."

<Figure id="fig-U4-1" kind="diagram" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-04/fig-U4-1.svg" alt="A diagram comparing description (sensory detail) and illustration (concrete examples) as two ways to make abstract ideas concrete." width={780} height={470} />

The pupil was not wrong - her school may be very good. But she told the reader instead of showing.
This topic teaches you two ways to show: description and illustration.

## Explanation

### Description

<Glossary term="Description">Description</Glossary> uses sensory detail - what you can see, hear,
smell, touch or taste - to help the reader picture a person, place or object. In expository writing,
description is not the main point; it supports the point. A paragraph about overcrowded classrooms
might describe "thirty-six pupils sharing eighteen desks, the back row pressed against the wall" to
make the problem real for the reader.

Good description is specific. "The classroom was hot and uncomfortable" tells. "Sweat darkened the
collars of shirts by mid-morning and the single fan pushed warm air from one side of the room to the
other" shows.

### Illustration

<Glossary term="Illustration">Illustration</Glossary> supports a point with concrete examples. If your
claim is "many government schools lack basic facilities," illustration names specific cases: "In one
school in Shikarpur, pupils sit on jute sacks because there are no chairs." One vivid example can be
more persuasive than a list of statistics.

The difference: description paints a picture; illustration gives instances. But both make abstract
claims concrete and believable.

<Figure id="fig-U4-5" kind="table" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-04/fig-U4-5.svg" alt="Table comparing six expository types by signal words and the writing purpose each serves best." width={780} height={470} />

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think "descriptive writing" means decorating sentences with
adjectives: "The big, beautiful, wonderful school." Description is not decoration - it is concrete,
specific detail that helps the reader see. "The school had one cracked blackboard and thirty-six
desks for sixty pupils" is description without a single adjective.

## Activity: Show, do not tell

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

1. Take this claim: "Morning assembly builds school spirit." Write one descriptive sentence (what you
   can see or hear at assembly) and one illustrative sentence (a concrete example of assembly building
   spirit).
2. Now rewrite these "telling" sentences as "showing" sentences using description or illustration:
   - "The classroom was very crowded."
   - "Pupils were happy about the new library."
3. Share with another pair. Discuss: which version makes the point more real for the reader?

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Explain the difference between description and illustration in one sentence each.
2. *(Understand)* Why is "the school was very good" less effective than a specific detail?
3. *(Apply)* A pupil writes: "Discipline is important for learning. For example, discipline is needed
   in the classroom." Using what you learned about illustration, explain why this example does not work
   and what a better example would be.

## Summary

Description uses sensory detail to paint a picture. Illustration uses concrete examples to make a
point believable. Both make abstract claims real for the reader. Specific detail matters more than
adjectives.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can write a descriptive sentence that uses specific sensory detail.
- [ ] I can write an illustrative sentence with a concrete example.
- [ ] I understand the difference between telling (vague claim) and showing (specific detail).

## Try this at your practicum school

Visit a classroom during a lesson. Write three descriptive sentences about what you observe (what you
see, hear, notice). Then write one illustrative sentence that supports the claim "This teacher uses
questioning effectively."

## Summative task

Take the claim: "A well-organized classroom helps pupils learn." Write two sentences - one descriptive
(what a well-organized classroom looks like) and one illustrative (a concrete example of how
organization helped learning). Then write one sentence explaining how the two types work together.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Description* | 0-2: uses specific sensory detail / vague description / no description. |
| *Illustration* | 0-2: gives a concrete example tied to the claim / weak or unrelated example / no example. |
| *Integration* | 0-2: explains how description and illustration complement each other / vague. (**Analyze**: the explanation shows understanding of why showing matters more than telling.) |
