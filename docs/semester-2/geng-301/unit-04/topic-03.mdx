## A real classroom situation

A pupil writes an essay comparing government and private schools. She writes everything about government
schools in the first half and everything about private schools in the second half. The tutor writes:
"Paragraph two tells me nothing new because it just repeats paragraph one with a different subject.
Compare point by point, not subject by subject."

<Figure id="fig-U4-3" kind="flowchart" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-04/fig-U4-3.svg" alt="A flowchart showing process analysis structure from goal through sequential steps to result, with warnings at each step." width={780} height={470} />

The pupil used the block comparison structure (all of A, then all of B) without a clear point-by-point
organization. This topic teaches you two comparison structures and how to explain a process step by step.

## Explanation

### Process analysis

<Glossary term="Process analysis">Process analysis</Glossary> explains how to do something or how
something works - step by step. A process analysis paragraph must:

1. **State the goal** - what the reader will be able to do or understand after reading.
2. **List the steps in order** - chronological or logical sequence.
3. **Include warnings or transitions** - what to watch out for at each step, how steps connect.

An example: "To set up a science demonstration for Grade 4, first gather all materials and test the
yourself before class. Next, explain what pupils should observe. Then carry out the demonstration
slowly, pausing for questions. Finally, connect the result to the day's learning objective."

Steps must be complete and in order. Skipping a step or jumping ahead loses the reader.

### Comparative analysis

<Glossary term="Compare and contrast">Comparative analysis</Glossary> examines similarities and
differences between two or more things. Two structures work:

- **Block structure (subject by subject):** Discuss all points for A, then all points for B. Works for
  short comparisons but can make the reader work hard to see connections.
- **Point-by-point structure:** Discuss one point for both A and B, then the next point for both, and
  so on. Clearer for longer comparisons because the reader sees the comparison directly.

> "On cost, government schools are free while private schools charge fees. On class size, government
> schools often have 50+ pupils per class while private schools may have 25-30. On facilities..."

Each paragraph compares one point across both subjects.

<Figure id="fig-U4-4" kind="table" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-04/fig-U4-4.svg" alt="Table comparing block and point-by-point comparison structures with guidance on when to use each." width={780} height={470} />

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think a comparison must give equal weight to similarities and
differences. Not so. Your purpose determines the balance. If you want to show that two things are more
alike than expected, emphasize similarities. If you want to choose between them, emphasize differences.

## Activity: Process and compare

Work in pairs. About 25 minutes.

1. **Process analysis (12 min):** Write a process analysis (60-80 words) explaining "How to prepare
   for a teaching practicum lesson." Include at least four steps in order, one warning and a clear
   goal statement.
2. **Comparative analysis (13 min):** Compare two things using point-by-point structure: "A textbook
   lesson vs a hands-on activity" on three points (pupil engagement, preparation time, learning
   retention). Write three sentences, each comparing one point across both.
3. Swap with another pair and give feedback: are the steps in order? Is the comparison clear?

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State the three requirements of process analysis and the two comparison structures.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why point-by-point comparison is often clearer than block comparison.
3. *(Apply)* A pupil writes a process analysis that jumps from step 1 to step 4, skipping steps 2 and
   3. Using what you learned, explain what is wrong and what the writer should add.

## Summary

Process analysis explains steps in order with a goal, warnings and transitions. Comparative analysis
uses block or point-by-point structure. Choose the structure that serves your purpose. Both types need
clear logical organization - steps in sequence, comparisons point by point.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can write a process analysis with steps in order and a clear goal.
- [ ] I can write a comparison using point-by-point structure.
- [ ] I understand when to use block vs point-by-point comparison.

## Try this at your practicum school

Observe a teacher preparing for a lesson. Write down the steps they take (materials, review, new
content, practice, assessment). Write a short process analysis (50 words) describing the preparation
process you observed.

## Summative task

Write a point-by-point comparison (80-120 words) of "learning from a textbook" vs "learning from a
field trip" on three points: preparation required, pupil engagement and long-term retention. Name the
structure you are using and explain in one sentence why it suits this comparison.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Comparison structure* | 0-3: clear point-by-point with three points / partial point-by-point / block or unstructured. (**Analyze**: each point compares both subjects directly, not separately.) |
| *Process explanation* | 0-2: names the structure and explains why it suits the topic / vague or missing. |
