## A real classroom situation

A pupil writes an essay about types of assessment. In one paragraph she mixes formative assessment,
summative assessment, classroom tests, national exams and oral questioning - jumping from one to the
another without any order. The teacher writes: "I cannot follow how these categories relate to each
other. Can you sort them?"

<Figure id="fig-U4-2" kind="concept-map" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-04/fig-U4-2.svg" alt="A concept map showing types of assessment sorted into formative and summative categories with sub-types under each." width={780} height={470} />

The pupil had information but no organizing principle. Classification gives that principle.

## Explanation

### Classification

<Glossary term="Classification">Classification</Glossary> sorts items into categories based on a
single organizing principle. The key word is "single." If you classify Pakistani schools, you might
use one principle (government vs private) or another (by level: primary, secondary, higher secondary)
- but not both at once. Mixing principles confuses the reader.

A classification paragraph names the principle, states the categories and gives examples of each:

> "Primary schools in Pakistan can be classified by management type into three categories:
> government schools (managed by the district education authority), private schools (managed by
> private owners or trusts) and community schools (managed by local organizations)."

### Cause and effect

<Glossary term="Cause and effect">Cause and effect</Glossary> explores why something happened (cause)
and what resulted (effect). It answers "Why?" and "What happened because of it?"

A cause-and-effect paragraph identifies the causal relationship clearly:

> "When a school introduces daily reading periods (cause), pupils' vocabulary improves within one term
> (effect), because they encounter new words in context rather than through memorization (mechanism)."

Be careful: correlation is not causation. Just because two things happen together does not mean one
caused the other. A writer must show the actual link, not just the sequence.

<Figure id="fig-U4-6" kind="table" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-04/fig-U4-6.svg" alt="Table comparing classification, cause/effect, process and comparison by organizing principle, signal words and structure." width={780} height={470} />

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think cause and effect means one cause leads to one effect, in
a straight line. Real causation is often multiple and complex: several causes lead to one effect, or
one cause produces a chain of effects. Good cause-and-effect writing acknowledges this complexity.

## Activity: Classify and connect

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

1. **Classification:** Take these items and classify them using one principle: {textbook, blackboard,
   homework, library, exam, group play, science lab, parent-teacher meeting}. Name your principle,
   state your categories and list the items under each.
2. **Cause and effect:** Identify one cause-effect relationship in a Pakistani school context (e.g.
   "When a school provides free textbooks..." or "When class sizes exceed fifty pupils..."). Write one
   cause-and-effect paragraph (40-60 words) that names the cause, the effect and the mechanism.
3. Compare with another pair. Discuss: is the classification principle consistent? Is the causal link
   shown, not just assumed?

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Explain what classification requires (single principle) and what cause and effect
   requires (causal link).
2. *(Understand)* Why is it wrong to mix two organizing principles in one classification?
3. *(Apply)* A pupil writes: "Test scores went down. It rained that week. Therefore rain caused lower
   scores." Using what you learned about causation, explain what is wrong with this reasoning.

## Summary

Classification sorts items into categories using a single organizing principle. Cause and effect explores
why something happened and what resulted. Both require clear logical relationships - classification needs
consistent categories, cause and effect needs a real causal link, not just correlation.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can classify a set of items using a single consistent principle.
- [ ] I can write a cause-and-effect paragraph that shows the causal mechanism, not just the sequence.
- [ ] I understand the difference between correlation and causation.

## Try this at your practicum school

Identify three "causes" your cooperating teacher mentions for a common classroom problem (e.g. low
attendance, poor results). For each, ask: is this a real cause or just something that happens at the
same time? Note one cause-effect relationship where the mechanism is clear.

## Summative task

Write one classification paragraph (50-70 words) classifying "types of homework" using one organizing
principle. Name the principle, state the categories and give one example of each. Then write two
sentences explaining why your principle is consistent (it does not mix two different ways of sorting).

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Classification structure* | 0-3: principle named, categories clear, examples given / missing one element / disorganized. (**Analyze**: the principle is applied consistently - no mixing of two different sorting criteria.) |
| *Explanation* | 0-2: explains why the principle is consistent / vague or missing. |
