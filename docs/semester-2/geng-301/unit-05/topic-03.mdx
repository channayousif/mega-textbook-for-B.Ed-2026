## A real classroom situation

A teacher writes a notice for parents about a school event. She copies the style of her B.Ed essays:
long sentences, formal vocabulary, passive voice. Half the parents do not read past the first line.
A colleague tells her: "Parents need to understand this in thirty seconds. Short sentences. Simple
words. Tell them what, when and where."

<Figure id="fig-U5-3" kind="diagram" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-05/fig-U5-3.svg" alt="A diagram showing the audience-awareness spectrum from academic to public, with key differences in language, tone and structure." width={780} height={470} />

The teacher used the wrong style for her audience. This topic teaches you how to write for the general
public - parents, community members, newspaper readers.

## Explanation

### What public audiences need

Public readers are not specialists. They may read quickly, on a phone, in a noisy environment. They
need writing that is:

- **Accessible**: Short sentences, common words, no jargon. If a word has a simpler synonym, use it.
- **Engaging**: Open with something that matters to the reader. A question, a short story, a surprising
   fact.
- **Scannable**: Headings, short paragraphs, key information first. A busy parent scanning a newsletter
   should find the key point in the first line.

### Public writing forms

Public writing takes several forms, each with its own conventions:

- **Opinion columns**: The writer's view on a current issue, supported by reasoning and examples.
   Usually 400-800 words. Opens with a strong hook.
- **School newsletters**: Short, factual, welcoming tone. Bullet points, clear headings, dates and
   times prominent.
- **Blog posts or articles**: Informal but informative. Conversational tone, short paragraphs, subheadings.
   May include a personal voice.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think "accessible" means "simplified to the point of being
childish." It does not. Accessible writing respects the reader's time and attention - it is clear and
direct without being condescending. You can write about complex topics accessibly by using short
sentences and concrete examples.

## Activity: Rewrite for a public audience

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Rewrite this academic paragraph for a parent newsletter. Keep the key information but make it
accessible: short sentences, simple words, scannable.

> "Research conducted by the Sindh Education Foundation (2023) indicates that regular parental
> engagement in domestic pedagogical activities, particularly shared reading practices, correlates
> positively with enhanced literacy outcomes in children aged six to ten years. The study recommends
> that caregivers dedicate a minimum of fifteen minutes daily to structured reading interaction."

1. Identify the key information parents need (what, how much, why).
2. Rewrite in no more than 50 words, using simple language and short sentences.
3. Add a heading that makes the key point scannable.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name three features of public writing (accessibility, engagement, scannability).
2. *(Understand)* Explain why a parent newsletter should not use academic vocabulary.
3. *(Apply)* A school writes in a notice: "The institutional timetable undergoes modification
   effective the first of next month." Rewrite this in language a parent would understand immediately.

## Summary

Public writing is accessible, engaging and scannable. Use short sentences, simple words and clear
headings. Forms include opinion columns, newsletters and blogs. Accessible does not mean childish -
it means clear and direct for a non-specialist reader.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can rewrite an academic text in accessible public style.
- [ ] I understand the conventions of opinion columns, newsletters and blogs.
- [ ] I can identify when a text is too formal for its intended audience.

## Try this at your practicum school

Collect one example of school-home communication (a notice, a newsletter, a circular). Evaluate it for
accessibility: is the language clear? Is the key information easy to find? Note one strength and one
improvement.

## Summative task

Write two versions of the same message - one for an academic reader (your B.Ed tutor) and one for a
parent newsletter. Topic: "The school is introducing a daily reading period." Each version should be
30-50 words. Then write two sentences explaining the key differences between your two versions.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Academic version* | 0-2: formal tone, appropriate vocabulary / partially formal / informal. |
| *Public version* | 0-2: accessible, scannable, simple language / partially accessible / still academic. |
| *Comparison* | 0-2: identifies specific differences in tone, vocabulary or structure / vague. |
