## A real classroom situation

A pupil writes an essay about school libraries. She includes emotional stories, persuasive appeals and
entertaining jokes. Her tutor writes: "What is this essay trying to do? Inform me? Persuade me?
Entertain me? Choose one purpose and stick with it."

<Figure id="fig-U5-1" kind="concept-map" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-05/fig-U5-1.svg" alt="A concept map placing 'Writing purposes' at the centre with four branches - inform (present facts), analyze (break down), persuade (argue a case), entertain (engage the reader)." width={780} height={470} />

The pupil mixed purposes and the result was unfocused. This topic helps you choose one purpose and
follow it.

## Explanation

### Four writing purposes

Every piece of writing has a purpose - the effect the writer wants to have on the reader. Four purposes
are common in academic and professional writing:

1. **To inform** - present facts, explain a topic, help the reader understand something new. Strategy:
   clarity, logical organization, evidence.
2. **To analyze** - break a topic into parts and examine how they relate. Topic 4.2 classification and
   cause/effect are analytical strategies.
3. **To persuade** - argue for a position and convince the reader to accept it. Strategy: clear thesis,
   strong evidence, acknowledgment of counter-arguments.
4. **To entertain** - engage the reader through storytelling, humour or vivid description. Strategy:
   anecdotes, varied sentence rhythm, concrete detail.

Most academic writing is informative or analytical. But even an informative essay needs a clear thesis -
so analysis is always present.

### Matching purpose to task

When you receive a writing task, identify the purpose before you start. Key words signal the purpose:

- "Explain," "describe," "outline" → inform
- "Analyze," "compare," "evaluate" → analyze
- "Argue," "defend," "recommend" → persuade
- "Reflect," "narrate," "share" → may include entertainment

<Figure id="fig-U5-5" kind="table" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-05/fig-U5-5.svg" alt="Table comparing four writing purposes by strategy and the signal words teachers use to set each type of task." width={780} height={470} />

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think a longer essay with more information is automatically
better. Not so. A focused essay that serves one purpose well is better than a long essay that tries to
inform, persuade and entertain at once and does none of them well.

## Activity: Identify the purpose

Work in pairs. About 15 minutes.

Read these writing task instructions. For each, identify the primary purpose (inform, analyze,
persuade or entertain) and one strategy the writer should use.

1. "Explain three reasons why some pupils struggle with reading in Grade 3."
2. "Argue whether homework should be banned in primary school."
3. "Compare government and private schools in terms of facilities and results."
4. "Write a short story about a memorable day at school."

Compare your answers with another pair. Where you disagree, discuss: what makes one purpose more
primary than another?

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name four writing purposes and state one strategy for each.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why most academic writing is informative or analytical rather than persuasive.
3. *(Apply)* A teacher writes: "Evaluate whether group work improves learning." What is the primary
   purpose and what strategy should the writer use?

## Summary

Every piece of writing has a purpose: to inform, analyze, persuade or entertain. Identify the purpose
before you start. Match your strategy to the purpose - facts for informing, breaking down for analyzing,
arguing for persuading. A focused essay serving one purpose is better than an unfocused essay trying to
do everything.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can identify the purpose of a writing task from its key words.
- [ ] I can match a strategy (evidence, analysis, argument) to the purpose.
- [ ] I understand why mixing purposes weakens an essay.

## Try this at your practicum school

Look at three different writing tasks in a Grade 6 or 7 English textbook. Identify the primary purpose
of each. Note which purposes the textbook uses most and which it uses least.

## Summative task

Take the topic: "The effects of mobile phones on pupil learning." Write two opening sentences - one
that informs (presents facts) and one that persuades (argues a position). Label each. Then write one
sentence explaining the difference in strategy between the two.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Inform sentence* | 0-2: presents facts neutrally / partly factual / not informative. |
| *Persuade sentence* | 0-2: argues a position clearly / weakly argued / not persuasive. |
| *Explanation* | 0-2: explains the difference in strategy / vague. |
