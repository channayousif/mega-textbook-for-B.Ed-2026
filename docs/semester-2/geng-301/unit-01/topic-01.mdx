## A real classroom situation

A student-teacher in her first semester of B.Ed is given her first written assignment: "Explain the
role of assessment in teaching and learning." She sits down and writes a long story about a teacher
she once had who gave too many tests. Her tutor returns the paper with a note: "You have written a
personal narrative. The task asked for expository writing. Please revise." The student is puzzled.
She thought she had answered the question. She did not yet understand that different writing tasks
call for different *modes* of writing, and that the mode her tutor wanted was one that explains and
informs, not one that tells a story.

<Figure id="fig-U1-1" kind="concept-map" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-01/fig-U1-1.svg" alt="A concept map placing 'Modes of Writing' at the centre with four branches - narration tells a story, description paints a picture, exposition explains and informs, argumentation persuades; the exposition branch is highlighted." width={780} height={470} />

Her confusion is common. Most pupils in Pakistani schools are asked to write without being taught
the difference between one mode of writing and another. This topic gives you that picture so that you
can both produce and teach expository writing with confidence.

## Explanation

### The four modes of writing

Writers commonly distinguish four broad <Glossary term="Modes of writing">modes of writing</Glossary>:

- **Narration** tells a story or recounts events in time order. A diary entry, a news report of an
  incident, a short story - all are narration.
- **Description** paints a picture with words. It helps the reader see, hear, smell or feel a
  person, place or object. A paragraph about the view from Mohenjo-daro is description.
- **Exposition** explains, informs or clarifies. It answers questions like "What is it?", "How does
  it work?" or "Why does it matter?" A textbook section, a how-to guide, a report - these are
  expository.
- **Argumentation** tries to persuade the reader to accept a position. It makes a case and supports
  it with evidence. An editorial, a debate speech, a letter to a school board - these are
  argumentative.

The guide for this course focuses on <Glossary term="Expository writing">expository writing</Glossary>:
writing that explains and informs in a clear, organized way. That does not mean the other modes never
appear in your essays - a well-placed example may be described, a short anecdote may open an
introduction - but the dominant mode in this course is exposition.

### Purpose and applications

<Figure id="fig-U1-4" kind="table" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-01/fig-U1-4.svg" alt="Table comparing four expository writing purposes - academic essay, professional report, textbook section and how-to guide - by their purpose, audience and key feature." width={780} height={470} />

Expository writing is the mode you will use most often as a student and as a teacher. Consider where
it shows up in your B.Ed programme and in a Pakistani school:

- **Academic essays** explain a concept or analyze an issue for a tutor or examiner.
- **Professional reports** (lesson evaluations, practicum reports) inform a reader who needs facts and
  analysis, not a story.
- **Textbook and handout sections** explain a topic to pupils in clear, structured language.
- **How-to guides** (how to set up a science demonstration, how to run a parent-teacher meeting) walk
  a reader through a procedure.

Graff and Birkenstein (2011) describe academic expository writing as a conversation: writers respond
to what others have said, making their own contribution clear. That picture reminds us that expository
writing is not just listing facts - it is entering a discussion with a reader in mind.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think "expository writing" means long, formal, bookish
writing full of difficult words. It does not. A good expository paragraph about how to plant a seed
can be three clear sentences. The defining feature is not length or formality - it is the purpose:
to explain and inform clearly.

## Activity: Sort the passages

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Below are four short passages. For each one, decide whether its dominant mode is narration,
description, exposition or argumentation. Write down your answer and one sentence explaining why.

1. "The school bell rang at 8:00. By 8:05, thirty-two pupils were seated in rows, waiting."
2. "Assessment is the process of gathering evidence about pupils' learning to make informed
   decisions about next steps."
3. "Every government school in Sindh should have a functioning library by 2027."
4. "The classroom had cracked plaster, a single ceiling fan, and a blackboard so worn the chalk
   barely showed."

Compare your answers with another pair. Where you disagree, read the passage aloud and discuss which
purpose it serves. Agree on a final classification for each.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* In your own words, name the four modes of writing and give one example of each.
2. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between expository writing and narration in one or two sentences, using the idea of purpose.
3. *(Apply)* A pupil writes, "I think homework should be banned because it causes stress." Is this exposition, argumentation or description? Give one reason for your answer.

## Summary

Expository writing is the mode that explains and informs. It is distinct from narration (telling a
story), description (painting a picture) and argumentation (persuading). It is the dominant mode in
academic essays, professional reports, textbook sections and how-to guides. Its defining feature is
not length or formality but a clear purpose: to help a reader understand something.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can name the four modes of writing and give an example of each.
- [ ] I can explain the difference between exposition and narration in terms of purpose.
- [ ] I can classify a short passage as exposition or another mode and justify my answer.

## Try this at your practicum school

Collect two examples of writing from your school environment: one that is clearly expository (a
textbook section, a notice, a report) and one that is clearly not (a story, a poem, a persuasive
letter). Bring both to your next session and be ready to explain which mode each one uses and why.

## Summative task

Read this passage and decide whether it is expository, narrative, descriptive or argumentative. Then
rewrite it in a *different* mode while keeping the same topic.

Passage: **"A lesson plan is a teacher's written outline of what will happen in a class period. It
lists the objectives, the activities and the materials needed."**

Write your new version in 40-60 words. State which mode you chose and one reason your rewrite fits that
mode.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Mode identified* | 0-2: correctly identifies the original mode and the new mode / identifies one correctly / neither. |
| *Rewrite fits the new mode* | 0-3: rewrite clearly shows the features of the chosen mode / partly shows them / does not show them. (**Analyze**: the rewrite demonstrates the defining feature of the new mode - e.g. a narrative version tells a story about a teacher planning a lesson.) |
| *Justification* | 0-2: gives a specific reason tied to the mode's defining feature / vague or missing reason. |
