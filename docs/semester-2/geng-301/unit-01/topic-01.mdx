## A real classroom situation

A student-teacher in her first semester of B.Ed is given her first written assignment: "Explain the
role of assessment in teaching and learning." She sits down and writes a long story about a teacher
she once had who gave too many tests. Her tutor returns the paper with a note: "You have written a
personal narrative. The task asked for expository writing. Please revise." The student is puzzled.
She thought she had answered the question. She did not yet understand that different writing tasks
call for different *modes* of writing, and that the mode her tutor wanted was one that explains and
informs, not one that tells a story.

A writing task can be misunderstood when its purpose is unclear. This topic helps you choose
an expository type that explains your subject for a reader.

## Explanation

### The six expository types

<Glossary term="Expository writing">Expository writing</Glossary> explains and informs in a clear,
organized way. The course guide introduces six types. Each offers a way to organize an explanation:

- **Description** explains what something is like by selecting relevant features. A factual
  description of a classroom's layout helps a reader understand how the space is used.
- **Illustration** explains a general point through examples. A paragraph about useful feedback
  might give examples of comments that tell pupils what to improve.
- **Classification** groups things by a stated rule. A report might group assessment tasks into
  oral, written and practical tasks, explaining what each group includes.
- **Cause and effect** explains why something happens or what follows from it. A paragraph might
  explain how blocked drains lead to standing water in a school yard.
- **Process analysis** explains how something works or how to do it, step by step. Instructions
  for planting a seed explain the sequence and why each step matters.
- **Comparative analysis** explains similarities and differences using shared criteria. A report
  might compare two classroom layouts by visibility, movement and space for group work.

These types can work together. A report can describe a classroom, classify its resources and compare
its layout with another classroom. Choose the type that best serves the reader's question.

Narration mainly recounts events; argumentation mainly tries to persuade a reader to accept a
position. Exposition mainly helps the reader understand. Description can serve exposition when it
presents relevant features to inform, rather than being a separate, excluded category.

### Purpose and applications

<Figure id="fig-U1-4" kind="table" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-01/fig-U1-4.svg" alt="Table comparing four expository writing purposes - academic essay, professional report, textbook section and how-to guide - by their purpose, audience and key feature." width={780} height={470} />

Expository writing is the mode you will use most often as a student and as a teacher. Consider where
it shows up in your B.Ed programme and in a Pakistani school:

- **Academic essays** explain a concept or analyze an issue for a tutor or examiner.
- **Professional reports** (lesson evaluations, practicum reports) inform a reader who needs facts and
  analysis, not a story.
- **Textbook and handout sections** explain a topic to pupils in clear, structured language.
- **How-to guides** (how to set up a science demonstration, how to run a parent-teacher meeting) walk
  a reader through a procedure.

For these tasks, begin with a reader's question: what needs explaining, and which type will
make it easiest to understand? The examples here are teaching examples, not findings about schools.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think "expository writing" means long, formal, bookish
writing full of difficult words. It does not. A good expository paragraph about how to plant a seed
can be three clear sentences. The defining feature is not length or formality - it is the purpose:
to explain and inform clearly.

## Activity: Sort the passages

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

For each passage, name its expository type and explain which feature supports your choice.

1. "The classroom has two doors and wide aisles so pupils can move between desks."
2. "Useful feedback names a next step; for example, 'Add a reason after your claim.'"
3. "Assessment tasks can be grouped as oral, written or practical."
4. "Blocked drains leave standing water because rainwater cannot flow away."
5. "First fill the pot with soil, then plant the seed and water it."
6. "Rows and circles both seat a class, but circles let pupils see one another more easily."

Compare your answers with another pair. Where you disagree, read the passage aloud and discuss which
purpose it serves. Agree on a final classification for each.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* In your own words, name the six expository types and give one example of each.
2. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between expository writing and narration in one or two sentences, using the idea of purpose.
3. *(Apply)* A pupil writes, "I think homework should be banned because it causes stress." Is its main purpose to explain or to persuade? Give one reason for your answer.

## Summary

Expository writing explains and informs through description, illustration, classification, cause
and effect, process analysis and comparative analysis. Narration recounts events and argumentation
aims to persuade; description can be part of exposition. It is the dominant mode in
academic essays, professional reports, textbook sections and how-to guides. Its defining feature is
not length or formality but a clear purpose: to help a reader understand something.

## Try this at your practicum school

Collect two examples of writing from your school environment: one that is clearly expository (a
textbook section, a notice, a report) and one that is clearly not (a story, a poem, a persuasive
letter). Bring both to your next session and be ready to explain which purpose each one serves and why.

## Summative task

Read this passage and identify its expository type. Then rewrite it using a *different* expository
type while keeping lesson planning as the topic. Compare how the two types help a reader understand.

Passage: **"A lesson plan is a teacher's written outline of what will happen in a class period. It
lists the objectives, the activities and the materials needed."**

Write your new version in 40-60 words. State which type you chose and one reason your rewrite fits that
type.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Types identified* | 0-2: correctly identifies the original type and the new type / identifies one correctly / neither. |
| *Rewrite fits the new type* | 0-3: rewrite clearly shows the features of the chosen type / partly shows them / does not show them. (**Analyze**: the rewrite demonstrates the defining feature of the new type, for example a process version orders the steps for planning a lesson.) |
| *Justification* | 0-2: gives a specific reason tied to the type's defining feature / vague or missing reason. |
