## Unit summary

This unit gave you a working picture of expository writing and its basic building blocks.

- Topic 1.1 defined expository writing as the mode that explains and informs, distinct from
  narration, description and argumentation.
- Topic 1.2 named three characteristics of effective expository writing: clarity (readable on the
  first pass), coherence (ideas connect logically) and organization (planned and structured).
- Topic 1.3 gave the three components of a well-structured paragraph (topic sentence, supporting
  details, concluding sentence) and the principles of unity (one idea per paragraph) and completeness
  (the idea is adequately developed).

Together these give you a practical lens: when you read or write expository text, you can ask what
mode it uses, whether it is clear, coherent and organized, and whether each paragraph is unified and
complete.

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

Choose the single best answer for each question.

1. *(Remember)* Which of the following best describes the dominant mode of expository writing?
   - A) Telling a story in time order
   - B) Painting a picture with sensory detail
   - C) Explaining and informing clearly
   - D) Persuading the reader to accept a position

2. *(Remember)* How many components does a well-structured expository paragraph have?
   - A) Two
   - B) Three
   - C) Four
   - D) Five

3. *(Understand)* A pupil writes a paragraph about the benefits of homework. One sentence is about
   homework. Another is about his favourite cricket match. Another is about the school canteen. Which
   principle is broken?
   - A) Completeness
   - B) Unity
   - C) Organization
   - D) Coherence

4. *(Understand)* Which of the following is the best reason a paragraph full of "moreover" and
   "therefore" can still be incoherent?
   - A) The transition words are misspelled
   - B) Coherence comes from the logic of the ideas, not from transition words alone
   - C) The paragraph is too short
   - D) The sentences use simple words

5. *(Apply)* A teacher says, "I want you to write an expository paragraph." Which instruction would
   help a pupil understand what that means?
   - A) "Write a story about your last holiday"
   - B) "Describe what your classroom looks like"
   - C) "Explain one reason why reading every day helps learning"
   - D) "Argue that homework should be banned"

6. *(Apply)* Which of these topic sentences is the most specific?
   - A) "Schools are good places"
   - B) "Teaching is a noble profession"
   - C) "A weekly spelling quiz gives a teacher quick feedback on what pupils have learned"
   - D) "Education is important for everyone"

7. *(Apply)* A paragraph ends with this sentence: "So in many ways, morning assembly builds school
   spirit." What is the function of this sentence?
   - A) Topic sentence
   - B) Supporting detail
   - C) Concluding sentence
   - D) Transitional device

8. *(Apply)* A B.Ed student writes, "The teacher did a thing in the class that was good." What is
   the main clarity problem?
   - A) The sentence uses specific words
   - B) The sentence is too short
   - C) The words "thing" and "good" are vague - the reader cannot picture what happened
   - D) The sentence has too many commas

9. *(Apply)* Which of these best shows a writer has organized before writing?
   - A) The first draft has no spelling errors
   - B) The writer has a rough outline of main points before drafting
   - C) The writer uses transition words in every sentence
   - D) The paragraph is ten sentences long

10. *(Apply)* A paragraph has a clear topic sentence and two supporting details, but the concluding
    sentence introduces a brand-new idea about a different topic. What is the main problem?
    - A) The topic sentence is missing
    - B) The supporting details are too short
    - C) The concluding sentence breaks unity by introducing a new idea
    - D) There are too many supporting details

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

Answer each question in the space indicated.

1. *(Understand)* In two sentences, explain the difference between expository writing and argumentation, using the idea of purpose.

2. *(Remember)* Name the three characteristics of effective expository writing and, for each, give one strategy a writer can use to improve it.

3. *(Apply)* A pupil writes: "Group work helps pupils. I like football. My school is in Karachi." Identify the paragraph component that is missing and explain why the paragraph lacks unity.

4. *(Apply)* Rewrite this vague sentence to make it clear in no more than one sentence: "The teacher did something good and the pupils were happy about it."

5. *(Apply)* Identify the topic sentence in this paragraph and explain why it works as a topic sentence.
   > "A library corner in a primary classroom encourages independent reading. A small shelf of graded
   > books within arm's reach means pupils can browse during free minutes. Pupils who choose their
   > own books read more often than those who are only given class texts. A quiet corner with a mat
   > and a few cushions makes the space inviting. Together these small changes turn a classroom into
   > a place where reading happens every day."

6. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between coherence and organization in one sentence each.

7. *(Apply)* A paragraph about the benefits of lesson planning has these sentences in this order:
   (1) "Teachers who plan their lessons tend to cover the syllabus on time." (2) "Lesson planning also
   helps teachers anticipate where pupils may struggle." (3) "A plan acts as a roadmap for the class
   period." (4) "Without a plan, a teacher may spend too long on one activity and run out of time for
   others." Rearrange the sentence numbers into a logical order and explain your reasoning.

8. *(Apply)* Take this topic sentence: "Timely feedback helps pupils improve their work." Write two
   supporting sentences that develop it with specific details.

9. *(Analyze)* A paragraph has six sentences. The topic sentence says the paragraph is about
   "morning assembly". Three sentences are about assembly. One sentence is about the school uniform.
   One sentence is about lunch break. Identify the unity problem and explain what the writer should do
   about it.

10. *(Analyze)* A student writes a paragraph about classroom management. The ideas are logically
    ordered, but many sentences use vague words like "thing", "good" and "nice". Explain which
    characteristic is weak, which is strong, and what the student should do to improve the weak one.

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

Answer each question in a short paragraph (80-140 words unless stated otherwise).

1. *(Analyze)* This unit defined expository writing as "writing that explains and clearly informs"
   and said it is distinct from narration, description and argumentation. Compare expository writing
   with *one* of these other modes and explain, with a concrete example, why the distinction matters
   when a teacher gives a writing task to pupils.

2. *(Apply)* Draft a unified, complete paragraph (60-90 words) on this topic: "Why a teacher should
   mark attendance every day." Your paragraph must have a topic sentence, at least two supporting
   details and a concluding sentence. Then explain, in two sentences, how your paragraph shows unity
   and completeness.

3. *(Analyze)* Read this paragraph and analyse it against the three characteristics of effective
   writing (clarity, coherence, organization).
   > "Homework is a thing that teachers give. It is good for pupils. Some pupils do not like it.
   > Teachers should give less homework because it causes stress. But some homework is necessary.
   > The thing about homework is that it is complicated."
   For each characteristic, state whether the paragraph meets it and give one piece of evidence from
   the text.

4. *(Evaluate)* A B.Ed student writes: "A paragraph is good when it has five sentences: one topic
   sentence, three supporting sentences and one concluding sentence." Evaluate this claim using what
   you learned in Topic 1.3. Explain why sentence count alone is not the best test of a good paragraph
   and what the better test is.

5. *(Create)* A Grade 4 teacher wants to teach her pupils the difference between a description and an
   explanation. Write a short expository paragraph (50-70 words) that *explains* the difference between
   description and exposition. Then write two sentences you would use to introduce the example to the
   class, showing how you would help pupils see the difference in purpose.

## Answers and marking guidance

This section is for self-study. Try every question above before you look back at the keys below.

### MCQ answer key

1. **C** - Expository writing explains and clearly informs. A is narration, B is description, D is argumentation.
2. **B** - Three: topic sentence, supporting details, concluding sentence.
3. **B** - Unity. The cricket and canteen sentences wander away from the main idea about homework.
4. **B** - Coherence comes from the logic of the ideas themselves. Transition words cannot fix a paragraph whose ideas are out of order.
5. **C** - "Explain one reason" calls for expository writing. A is narration, B is description, D is argumentation.
6. **C** - It is specific: it names the activity (weekly spelling quiz), the actor (teacher) and the outcome (quick feedback on learning). A, B and D are vague.
7. **C** - "So in many ways" signals a concluding sentence that rounds off the paragraph.
8. **C** - "Thing" and "good" are vague. The reader cannot picture what the teacher did or why it was good.
9. **B** - Organization means planning structure before writing. A rough outline is the clearest evidence of organization.
10. **C** - A concluding sentence should round off the main idea, not introduce a new one. The new idea breaks unity.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. **Model:** Expository writing aims to explain and inform; argumentation aims to persuade the reader to accept a position. The difference is in purpose: one clarifies, the other convinces. **Marks (2):** 1 - names the purpose of exposition; 1 - names the purpose of argumentation.

2. **Model:** Clarity (use specific words, short sentences), Coherence (logical order, transitions), Organization (outline before writing). **Marks (3):** 1 per characteristic with a matching strategy.

3. **Model:** The topic sentence is missing. The first sentence ("Group work helps pupils") could be one, but it is never developed - the football and Karachi sentences are off-topic, breaking unity. **Marks (2):** 1 - identifies the missing/unclear topic sentence; 1 - explains the unity problem.

4. **Model:** "The teacher used think-pair-share for ten minutes, and the pupils said it helped them understand the passage." **Marks (2):** 1 - replaces "thing" with a specific activity; 1 - replaces "good" with a specific outcome.

5. **Model:** The topic sentence is sentence 3 ("A library corner in a primary classroom encourages independent reading"). It works because it states one specific, arguable main idea that the rest of the paragraph develops. **Marks (2):** 1 - correctly identifies the topic sentence; 1 - explains why it works (specific, main idea).

6. **Model:** Coherence is the logical connection between ideas within a text. Organization is the planned structure of the whole text before writing. **Marks (2):** 1 per correct distinction.

7. **Model:** A logical order is 3-1-2-4 or 1-3-2-4. Sentence 3 ("A plan acts as a roadmap") is the general idea (topic sentence); sentences 1 and 2 give specific benefits; sentence 4 shows the consequence of not planning (concluding idea). **Marks (2):** 1 - logical order; 1 - reasoning about the role of each sentence.

8. **Model:** Any two specific supporting sentences. E.g. "When a teacher writes a short comment on an essay, the pupil knows exactly what to fix next time." and "Quick feedback within a day helps pupils while the work is still fresh in their minds." **Marks (2):** 1 per supporting sentence that is specific and connects to the topic sentence.

9. **Model:** The unity problem is the sentences about school uniform (one sentence) and lunch break (one sentence) - they do not relate to the topic of morning assembly. The writer should cut them or move them to separate paragraphs. **Marks (2):** 1 - identifies the off-topic sentences; 1 - explains what to do.

10. **Model:** Coherence is strong (ideas are logically ordered) but clarity is weak (vague words). The student should replace vague words like "thing" and "good" with specific ones. **Marks (2):** 1 - correctly identifies which characteristic is strong and which is weak; 1 - suggests a specific improvement.
