## A real classroom situation

A Grade 5 English teacher in Hyderabad gives her pupils this instruction: "Write a paragraph about
waste management." One pupil writes seven sentences. The first is about waste in the school, the second
is about his favourite cricket team, the third is about his mother's cooking, the fourth returns to
waste, the fifth is about traffic in the city, the sixth is about a festival, and the seventh repeats
the first. When the teacher asks what the paragraph is about, the pupil says, "I wrote everything I
think about." The paragraph has sentences but it does not have a point.

<Figure id="fig-U1-3" kind="flowchart" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-01/fig-U1-3.svg" alt="A flowchart showing the paragraph-writing process in four steps: choose a single idea, write a topic sentence, add two to four supporting details, write a concluding sentence." width={780} height={470} />

That pupil's paragraph is not unusual. Without a clear picture of what a paragraph is supposed to
do, many learners pile sentences together and hope the reader will find the point. This topic gives
you the structure that prevents that.

## Explanation

### The three components of a paragraph

A well-structured expository paragraph has three parts:

1. **<Glossary term="Topic sentence">Topic sentence</Glossary>** - one sentence that states the main
   idea or point of the paragraph. It usually comes first. It tells the reader what the paragraph is
   about. A good topic sentence is specific: not "Education is important" but "Weekly quizzes help
   teachers identify which pupils need more support."

2. **<Glossary term="Supporting details">Supporting details</Glossary>** - two to four sentences that
   develop, explain or prove the topic sentence. These may give examples, reasons, facts or a short
   explanation. Each supporting sentence must connect back to the topic sentence. If a sentence does
   not relate to the main idea, it does not belong in the paragraph.

3. **<Glossary term="Concluding sentence">Concluding sentence</Glossary>** - one sentence that rounds
   off the paragraph. It may restate the main idea in different words, summarize the points made, or
   point forward to what comes next. It should not introduce a brand-new idea.

Here is a short example. Notice how each part does its job.

> **[Topic sentence]** A weekly spelling quiz is a simple but effective formative check in a primary
> classroom. **[Supporting detail 1]** It takes only five minutes and covers the week's target words.
> **[Supporting detail 2]** Pupils mark each other's papers, so the teacher gets immediate feedback
> without adding to her workload. **[Supporting detail 3]** The results show which words need reteaching
> before the next week begins. **[Concluding sentence]** In this way a short quiz helps a teacher adjust
> her pace to what the class actually needs.

### Unity and completeness

<Figure id="fig-U1-6" kind="table" src="/img/figures/geng-301/unit-01/fig-U1-6.svg" alt="Table comparing a weak paragraph and a strong paragraph by sentence role." width={780} height={470} />

Two principles keep a paragraph strong:

- **Unity**: every sentence in the paragraph relates to one main idea. The cricket sentence and the
  cooking sentence in the opening vignette break unity - they wander away from the point. To check
  for unity, read each sentence and ask: "Does this connect to the topic sentence?" If it does not,
  cut it or move it to another paragraph.

- **Completeness**: the paragraph develops its main idea fully. A paragraph with a topic sentence
  and only one short supporting sentence feels thin. A paragraph with no supporting evidence feels
  unconvincing. Completeness does not mean long - it means the point is adequately supported for the
  reader to understand it.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees were taught that "a paragraph must have five sentences". It
must not. A paragraph must have enough sentences to develop its main idea - sometimes three, sometimes
six. The test is not sentence count: ask whether the main idea is clear and whether it is adequately
supported. A three-sentence paragraph that is unified and complete is stronger than a ten-sentence
paragraph that wanders.

## Activity: Build a paragraph together

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Take this topic sentence:

> **"A classroom display board can be a powerful learning tool when it is used well."**

1. Brainstorm three supporting details that could develop this idea. Each detail should be one
   sentence and must connect to the topic sentence.
2. Write a concluding sentence that rounds off the paragraph.
3. Combine your topic sentence, three supporting sentences and concluding sentence into one paragraph.
4. Now check your paragraph against two questions: (a) Does every sentence relate to the main idea
   (unity)? (b) Is the main idea developed enough for a reader who knows nothing about display boards
   (completeness)?
5. Swap your paragraph with another pair and give feedback on unity and completeness.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the three components of a well-structured paragraph and state what each one does.
2. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between unity and completeness in one sentence each. Use your own words, not definitions copied from this topic.
3. *(Apply)* Read this paragraph and identify the unity problem: "Morning assembly builds discipline. Pupils stand in rows and listen quietly. My school has a large playground. The head teacher gives announcements every Monday." Which sentence breaks unity, and why?

## Summary

A strong expository paragraph has three components: a topic sentence that states the main idea,
supporting details that develop it, and a concluding sentence that rounds it off. Unity means every
sentence relates to one main idea. Completeness means the idea is adequately supported. Neither
principle depends on sentence count - a short, unified, complete paragraph is better than a long,
wandering one.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can write a topic sentence that is specific and states a clear main idea.
- [ ] I can add supporting details that connect to the topic sentence.
- [ ] I can check a paragraph for unity by asking whether every sentence relates to the main idea.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask a Grade 4 or 5 pupil to write a paragraph about "My favourite festival." Read the paragraph and
identify: (a) does it have a clear topic sentence? (b) are all the sentences on topic (unity)? (c) is
the idea developed enough (completeness)? Note one strength and one thing to improve, and bring your
notes to the next session.

## Summative task

Draft a paragraph (60-90 words) on one of these topics: "Why punctuality matters in school" or "One
way to keep a classroom clean." Your paragraph must have a topic sentence, at least two supporting
details and a concluding sentence. After writing it, underline the topic sentence, number the
supporting details and circle the concluding sentence. Then write two sentences evaluating your
paragraph for unity and for completeness.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | Levels |
|---|---|
| *Structure* | 0-2: has a clear topic sentence, supporting details and a concluding sentence / missing one component / missing two or more. |
| *Unity and completeness* | 0-3: all sentences are on topic (unity) and the idea is developed with at least two relevant details (completeness) / minor gaps / major gaps or off-topic sentences. (**Analyze**: if there is a unity problem, names the off-topic sentence and explains why it does not belong.) |
| *Self-evaluation* | 0-2: identifies specific evidence for both unity and completeness / partial evidence / generic. |
