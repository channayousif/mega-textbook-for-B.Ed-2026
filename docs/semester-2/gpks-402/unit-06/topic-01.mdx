## A real classroom situation

Bilal is a B.Ed trainee conducting his teaching practicum at a government boys' secondary school in Hyderabad. During a Class 10 Pakistan Studies lesson on regional geography, a pupil named Farhan asks an unexpected question: "Sir, whenever we watch the television news, our foreign relations seem to be entirely about our disputes with India. Does Pakistan have an actual foreign policy beyond reacting to our neighbours?"

Before Bilal can answer, another student remarks, "My uncle says foreign policy is only decided behind closed doors by diplomats, so citizens do not need to understand it." Bilal looks around the classroom and notices that most pupils nod in agreement. They view Pakistan's diplomatic relations either through the lens of a single adversarial conflict or as an abstract puzzle detached from domestic life.

Bilal realises that his pupils have never been taught the constitutional principles that govern Pakistan's external conduct. He needs to help them see that foreign policy touches their everyday lives - from the prices of imported fuel and fertilizer to regional trade corridors and international peacekeeping - and that it is anchored in clear constitutional directives.

## Explanation

A country's <Glossary term="Foreign policy">foreign policy</Glossary> is its official strategy for managing relations, trade, security, and diplomatic interactions with other nations and multilateral organisations. It reflects a nation's core values, historical experience, geographical reality, and economic needs.

### Principles of Pakistan's foreign policy

Pakistan's external relations are not arbitrary; they are grounded in constitutional law, international commitments, and the founding vision of the nation. In a broadcast speech in February 1948, Quaid-e-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah articulated the guiding ethos of Pakistan's diplomacy: "Our foreign policy is one of friendliness and goodwill towards all the nations of the world. We do not cherish aggressive designs against any country or nation. We believe in the principle of honesty and fair play in national and international dealings."

At the constitutional level, Article 40 of the 1973 Constitution sets out the state's obligations regarding international relations. It states that Pakistan shall endeavour to preserve and strengthen fraternal relations among Muslim countries based on Islamic unity, support the common interests of the peoples of Asia, Africa, and Latin America, promote international peace and security, foster goodwill among all nations, and encourage the peaceful settlement of international disputes.

Scholars of Pakistani diplomacy, such as Khan (2015) and Burki (1999), highlight five enduring principles that structure this constitutional mandate:

1. **Protection of sovereignty and territorial integrity:** The foremost objective of foreign policy is safeguarding the nation's political independence and physical borders against external aggression or coercion.
2. **Peaceful coexistence and non-interference:** Pakistan upholds the principles of the United Nations Charter, affirming mutual respect for the sovereign equality of all states and non-interference in their domestic affairs.
3. **Peaceful settlement of disputes:** Pakistan advocates resolving bilateral and multilateral disputes through negotiated settlements, international arbitration, and adherence to UN resolutions rather than unilateral military force.
4. **Solidarity with the Muslim world:** Grounded in Article 40, Pakistan actively champions cooperation among Muslim-majority nations, extending moral, political, and diplomatic support to self-determination struggles such as those in Palestine and Kashmir.
5. **Economic and commercial diplomacy:** In the twenty-first century, diplomacy increasingly focuses on geoeconomics: attracting foreign direct investment, expanding trade access, securing energy supplies, and building regional transport connectivity.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Foreign policy is only about relations with India."** While the unresolved Jammu and Kashmir dispute and military security have historically consumed immense attention, Pakistan's foreign policy is far wider. Pakistan maintains active diplomatic and economic partnerships across North America, Europe, China, Central Asia, the Middle East, and Africa. Focusing exclusively on India blinds pupils to vital global issues, such as international climate finance, diaspora remittances, overseas employment, and multilateral trade treaties.

A worked example illustrates how these principles operate in practice:

* **Analytical move:** Evaluating a proposed bilateral transit trade agreement between Pakistan and a Central Asian republic.
* **Criterion 1 (Sovereignty and non-interference):** Does the agreement respect both nations' customs laws and sovereign border control? Yes.
* **Criterion 2 (Peaceful cooperation):** Does it resolve previous customs disputes through a joint consultative committee? Yes.
* **Criterion 3 (Geoeconomic benefit):** Does it grant Pakistani agricultural producers access to new markets while using Gwadar and Karachi ports for Central Asian exports? Yes.
* **Conclusion:** The agreement directly aligns with Pakistan's constitutional goal of promoting regional peace and economic development.

### Relations with neighboring countries: India, Afghanistan, China, Iran

Geopolitics begins at a nation's borders. Pakistan shares land borders with four distinct neighbours, each presenting unique strategic opportunities and historical complexities.

#### 1. China: All-weather strategic partnership

Pakistan was among the first Muslim nations to recognise the People's Republic of China in 1950. The border agreement signed in 1963 established a stable boundary in the Karakoram range. Over the following six decades, the relationship evolved into what both nations term an "all-weather strategic partnership."

In recent years, bilateral cooperation expanded dramatically through the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC), a flagship project of the global Belt and Road Initiative. CPEC has invested billions of dollars in modernising Pakistan's energy infrastructure, highways, railway networks, and the deep-sea port of Gwadar. For Pakistan, China provides dependable defense cooperation, substantial economic investment, and consistent diplomatic support in international forums.

#### 2. Afghanistan: Shared culture and border security

Pakistan shares a 2,600-kilometre border with Afghanistan, historically known as the Durand Line. The two nations share deep linguistic, ethnic, religious, and economic ties, with millions of Pashtuns living on both sides of the frontier. Pakistan has hosted millions of Afghan refugees since the Soviet invasion of 1979, representing one of the longest protracted refugee situations in modern history.

However, relations have faced persistent challenges. Key issues include border regulation, unauthorized crossings, cross-border militant sanctuaries, and transit trade disputes. Pakistan maintains that long-term peace and economic prosperity require an inclusive, stable government in Kabul and formal, biometric border management to facilitate legitimate trade while halting illegal militant movement.

#### 3. Iran: Cultural affinity and energy cooperation

Pakistan and Iran share an ancient cultural and linguistic affinity; Persian was the official court language of the Mughal empire for centuries, and Pakistan's national anthem contains largely Persian vocabulary. Iran was the very first country to recognise Pakistan upon its independence in August 1947.

Bilateral relations focus on border security in the shared Balochistan borderland, bilateral commerce, counter-smuggling efforts, and energy collaboration. The proposed Iran-Pakistan gas pipeline reflects the potential for economic partnership, although international financial sanctions on Iran have created significant hurdles for project completion. Both states cooperate through the Economic Cooperation Organization (ECO) to enhance regional transport and trade links.

#### 4. India: The quest for durable peace and the Kashmir dispute

Relations with India have been troubled since partition in 1947, marred by three major wars (1948, 1965, 1971) and the Kargil conflict in 1999. The core impediment to normalisation remains the unresolved dispute over the former princely state of Jammu and Kashmir. Pakistan's official stance is that the future of Jammu and Kashmir must be determined by its people through a free and impartial plebiscite conducted under United Nations supervision, as mandated by UN Security Council resolutions.

Despite severe political hostility, bilateral treaties have achieved notable successes. The Indus Waters Treaty of 1960, brokered by the World Bank, successfully regulated the sharing of river waters through wars and geopolitical crises. Pakistan has consistently advocated for a structured, composite dialogue addressing all outstanding issues, including Kashmir, water security, trade, and nuclear risk reduction. However, dialogue remains stalled following unilateral political changes in Indian-administered Kashmir in 2019, underscoring the urgent need for international mediation and renewed diplomatic engagement.

### Role in international organizations: UN, OIC, SAARC

Pakistan has participated actively in multilateral organisations to amplify its diplomatic voice, build coalitions, and support global peace.

<Figure id="fig-U6-2" src="/img/figures/gpks-402/unit-06/fig-U6-2.svg" alt="Table comparing Pakistan's role, contributions, and national benefits in four international organisations: UN, OIC, SAARC, and SCO." />

#### 1. United Nations (UN)

Pakistan was admitted to the United Nations on 30 September 1947. Since then, it has been elected seven times as a non-permanent member of the UN Security Council and has repeatedly presided over the Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC).

Pakistan's most celebrated contribution to the United Nations is in peacekeeping. Over the past six decades, more than 200,000 Pakistani military and police personnel have served in 46 UN peacekeeping missions across Africa, Asia, and the Balkans, often operating in dangerous conflict zones to protect civilian populations. Pakistani diplomats have also played leading roles in drafting human rights instruments and advocating for international climate compensation funds.

#### 2. Organisation of Islamic Cooperation (OIC)

Formed in 1969 following the arson attack on the Al-Aqsa mosque in Jerusalem, the OIC is the second-largest intergovernmental organisation after the UN, comprising 57 member states. Pakistan is a founding member and hosted the historic Second Islamic Summit in Lahore in 1974, which brought together heads of state from across the Muslim world.

Within the OIC, Pakistan consistently advances the collective rights of Muslim minorities, counters Islamophobia, and mobilises political support for the Palestinian and Kashmiri peoples. Through the Islamic Development Bank (IsDB), Pakistan secures funding for vital social and infrastructure projects.

#### 3. South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation (SAARC)

Established in Dhaka in 1985, SAARC brings together eight South Asian nations: Pakistan, India, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, Maldives, and Afghanistan. It was created to accelerate economic growth, social progress, and cultural development in one of the world's most populous and poverty-dense regions.

Pakistan hosted the 4th SAARC Summit in 1988 and the 12th Summit in 2004, where member states signed the landmark South Asian Free Trade Area (SAFTA) agreement. Unfortunately, deep-seated political tensions between Pakistan and India have repeatedly stalled SAARC summit meetings and prevented the region from realising the high level of economic integration seen in ASEAN or the European Union.

#### 4. Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO)

In June 2017, Pakistan became a full member of the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation, a trans-regional Eurasian security and economic body founded by China, Russia, and Central Asian states. Participation in the SCO allows Pakistan to deepen counter-terrorism cooperation through the Regional Anti-Terrorist Structure (RATS), expand trade connectivity into Central Asia, and participate as an equal partner in Eurasian security dialogues.

## Activity: Mapping Pakistan's Diplomatic Ties

* **Format:** Small groups of 3 or 4 students.
* **Time:** Approximately 20 minutes.
* **Context:** A secondary school civics or Pakistan Studies classroom.

**Instructions:**
1. Examine the four neighbouring countries discussed in this topic (China, Afghanistan, Iran, India).
2. On a large sheet of paper, sketch a simple diagram placing Pakistan at the centre.
3. For each of the four countries, write down:
   - One primary mutual interest (for example, economic infrastructure, cultural trade, or border stability).
   - One ongoing challenge or dispute that requires careful diplomatic negotiation.
4. Conclude by identifying which constitutional foreign policy principle (from Article 40) is most relevant to resolving each identified challenge.
5. Share your group's diagram with the neighbouring pair, noting how balanced foreign relations support national development.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State the core requirement of Article 40 of the 1973 Constitution regarding Pakistan's international relations.
2. *(Understand)* Explain why Quaid-e-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah described Pakistan's foreign policy as "friendliness and goodwill towards all nations" and how this contrasts with aggressive nationalism.
3. *(Apply)* A school pupil asserts: "CPEC only benefits China because they supply the construction machinery." Using the concepts taught in this topic, provide two concrete counter-arguments showing how CPEC addresses Pakistan's domestic economic and energy needs.

## Summary

Pakistan's foreign policy is guided by constitutional mandates, particularly Article 40, which commits the state to international peace, non-interference, sovereignty, and solidarity with the Muslim world. Regional diplomacy is anchored by a close strategic partnership with China, historical and border ties with Afghanistan, cultural affinities with Iran, and the enduring quest for a peaceful, negotiated settlement of the Kashmir dispute with India. Globally, Pakistan plays a constructive role through extensive UN peacekeeping missions, active leadership in the OIC, and participation in regional organisations such as SAARC and the SCO.
