## A real classroom situation

During a practicum observation at a government higher secondary school in Sukkur, a student teacher named Tariq begins a Grade 9 Pakistan Studies class on the Pakistan Movement. He opens by writing "1940: The Start of Pakistan" on the chalkboard. A student in the front row, Zainab, raises her hand and asks: "Sir, did the Pakistan Movement really start out of nowhere in 1940 with Quaid-e-Azam, or were our people doing something before that?" Tariq hesitates. He realizes that reducing history to a single resolution hides nearly two centuries of struggle, educational institution-building, and social reform that made political unity possible.

## Explanation

### Shah Waliullah: reform and revival

The intellectual roots of Muslim self-awareness in South Asia trace back to the 18th-century scholar Shah Waliullah Dehlawi (1703–1762). Following the death of Emperor Aurangzeb in 1707, the Mughal Empire rapidly fragmented under administrative corruption, regional rebellions, and the rising power of the Marathas and the British East India Company. As political power slipped away, Muslim society suffered from moral decline, intellectual stagnation, and bitter sectarian divisions between different schools of thought (Rabbani, 2020).

Shah Waliullah diagnosed that political collapse was merely a symptom of a deeper moral, educational, and intellectual decay. He argued that Muslims had abandoned direct engagement with the core teachings of Islam and had become entangled in sectarian disputes and rigid dogma. His reform program focused on three decisive interventions:

1. **Accessibility of Scripture:** In an era when religious texts were restricted to Arabic scholars, Shah Waliullah took the revolutionary step of translating the Holy Quran into Persian, the literary language of educated South Asians. This broke the monopoly of conservative interpreters and allowed ordinary educated Muslims to understand divine guidance directly. His sons, Shah Rafi-ud-Din and Shah Abdul Qadir, later translated the Quran into Urdu, expanding this access even further.
2. **Reconciliation of Juristic Differences (*Tatbiq*):** Deep divisions existed between different schools of Islamic jurisprudence (*fiqh*) and between jurists and Sufi orders. Shah Waliullah formulated the principle of *tatbiq* (synthesis and reconciliation), demonstrating that differences among the four major schools of jurisprudence were matters of secondary interpretation rather than contradictory fundamental principles. In his magnum opus, *Hujjat Allah al-Baligha* (The Conclusive Argument from God), he integrated spiritual ethics with rational legal philosophy.
3. **Institutional and Social Revival:** Through the Madrasah-i-Rahimiyah in Delhi, established by his father, Shah Waliullah trained a generation of scholars committed to social justice, ethical governance, and public welfare. He also addressed regional political instability directly, writing letters to regional leaders to halt the territorial fragmentation of Muslim communities.

In modern Pakistani classrooms, Shah Waliullah's work offers an enduring pedagogical model: when a society faces crisis, renewal begins with educational accessibility, critical inquiry, and reconciliation across sectarian divides.

### Sir Syed Ahmad Khan and the Aligarh Movement

A century after Shah Waliullah, the Muslims of South Asia faced their most acute existential crisis following the defeat of the 1857 War of Independence. The British colonial authorities held Muslims primarily responsible for the uprising, subjecting them to severe political repression, confiscation of properties, and exclusion from public employment (Khan & Khan, 2020). Traditional Muslim elites responded with profound despair and cultural withdrawal, rejecting modern Western education and the English language as threats to religious identity.

Sir Syed Ahmad Khan (1817–1898) recognized that cultural isolationism was suicidal. If Muslims refused modern education while other communities embraced it, Muslims would be permanently relegated to an impoverished, disenfranchised underclass. Sir Syed embarked on a comprehensive mission known as the **Aligarh Movement**, aimed at reconciling South Asian Muslims with modern scientific knowledge without abandoning their religious values.

Sir Syed implemented his educational vision through three strategic steps:

1. **Reconciliation and Critical Inquiry:** In 1859, Sir Syed wrote *Asbab-e-Baghawat-e-Hind* (The Causes of the Indian Revolt), courageously pointing out British administrative miscalculations, lack of communication with native populations, and the exclusion of Indians from legislative councils. In 1864, he founded the **Scientific Society** at Ghazipur (later shifted to Aligarh), which translated seminal works of European science, history, and agriculture into Urdu.
2. **Social and Cultural Reform:** In 1870, after visiting England and studying Cambridge and Oxford universities, Sir Syed launched the influential journal *Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq* (Social Reformer). The journal attacked fatalism, prejudice, and outdated customs, advocating rationalism, personal integrity, social responsibility, and scientific hygiene.
3. **Institutional Foundation:** In 1875, Sir Syed established the **Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental (MAO) High School** at Aligarh, upgraded to MAO College in 1877 (and later becoming Aligarh Muslim University in 1920). The institution combined modern Western science and humanities with Islamic studies and character building, creating a residential collegiate culture that fostered mutual solidarity and leadership skills.

**Common Misconception.** A frequent misconception among students is that Sir Syed advocated the Two-Nation Theory directly from the beginning of his public career. In reality, Sir Syed spent much of his early life championing joint Hindu-Muslim national cooperation, famously describing Hindus and Muslims as "two beautiful eyes of a bride." However, his outlook changed decisively during the **Hindi-Urdu Controversy of 1867** in Benares, when Hindu revivalist leaders demanded the elimination of Urdu as the court language in favor of Hindi in Devanagari script. Sir Syed realized that language was a vital marker of cultural identity, observing to his colleague Shakespeare that the two communities would likely walk divergent paths in the future.

### Role of Muslim intelligentsia in shaping political consciousness

The Aligarh Movement was far more than a single college; it was a cultural renaissance that generated a modern Muslim intelligentsia. Before Aligarh, South Asian Muslim leadership consisted almost entirely of feudal landlords and traditional theologians, neither of whom possessed the linguistic or analytical tools to engage with British colonial constitutional structures (Khan & Khan, 2020).

Aligarh produced an educated middle class of lawyers, teachers, journalists, civil servants, and authors. This intelligentsia bridged the gap between traditional heritage and modern governance:

- **Literary Renaissance:** Maulana Altaf Hussain Hali published his monumental poem *Musaddas-e-Hali* (The Flow and Ebb of Islam) in 1879 at Sir Syed's request. Hali vividly contrasted the historical intellectual and scientific achievements of Muslims with their contemporary backwardness, galvanizing public sentiment toward education and self-reliance. Similarly, Maulana Shibli Nomani established the *Darul Musannefin* (Academy of Authors) and enriched historical scholarship.
- **Journalism and Public Opinion:** Aligarh graduates established newspapers and literary periodicals that discussed civic rights, education, and constitutional developments, fostering an informed community across distant provinces.
- **Educational Conferences:** In 1886, Sir Syed founded the **All-India Muhammadan Educational Conference**. Meeting annually in major cities across South Asia, this conference provided a unified platform where Muslim educators and community leaders gathered to discuss school openings, female education, and social welfare.

**Worked Example.** Consider how an educator analyzes Hali's *Musaddas* in the classroom. Instead of merely asking students to memorize verses, the teacher presents two paired passages: one depicting historical Arab scholars translating Greek medicine and astronomy, and the other depicting 19th-century youth idling without vocational or scientific training. The teacher asks students: "What cause does Hali identify for Muslim decline, and what remedy does he demand?" Students analyze the text to conclude that Hali identifies the abandonment of knowledge (*ilm*) as the root cause of downfall, and active pursuit of modern learning as the only viable remedy.

By the turn of the 20th century, this intellectual class realized that educational achievement alone could not protect Muslim rights under emerging democratic institutions; it required dedicated political representation.

**Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.**

Review the comparison table below contrasting Shah Waliullah's 18th-century revival with Sir Syed Ahmad Khan's 19th-century reform:

| Dimension | Shah Waliullah (18th Century) | Sir Syed Ahmad Khan (19th Century) |
|---|---|---|
| **Historical Context** | Mughal administrative collapse; rise of regional conflicts | Post-1857 colonial subjection; severe socio-economic decline |
| **Primary Diagnosis** | Moral corruption, religious dogma, and sectarian division | Educational isolationism and lack of scientific knowledge |
| **Key Strategic Action** | Translation of Quran into Persian; doctrinal reconciliation | Founding of Scientific Society (1864) and MAO College (1875) |
| **Intended Outcome** | Religious purification and spiritual unity | Socio-economic modernization and political capacity |

3. Which elements of Sir Syed's educational philosophy are most urgently needed to improve public schools in your district today?

## Summary

The foundation of the Pakistan Movement rested on over a century of deliberate intellectual, religious, and educational reform. Shah Waliullah diagnosed the moral and sectarian roots of South Asian Muslim decline, breaking scholarly monopolies by translating the Quran into Persian and promoting reconciliation among juristic schools. In the post-1857 era, Sir Syed Ahmad Khan recognized that Muslims faced socio-economic extinction unless they embraced modern scientific education and rational discourse. Through the Scientific Society, *Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq*, and the MAO College at Aligarh, Sir Syed nurtured a confident Muslim intelligentsia. This educated class transitioned from cultural self-defense to proactive civic and political mobilization, laying the indispensable social groundwork for the Pakistan Movement.
