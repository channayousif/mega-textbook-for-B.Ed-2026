## A real classroom situation

At a government high school in Larkana, a teacher named Saima asks her Grade 10 students: "Why do we celebrate 14 August 1947?" A student named Bilal answers quickly: "Because the British decided to divide India and leave." Saima notices that several students nod in agreement, believing that the emergence of Pakistan was simply an arbitrary colonial decision made over a few months. She realizes that her students do not understand the decades of constitutional struggle, democratic elections, and negotiations through which South Asian Muslims articulated their collective identity and democratic will.

## Explanation

### Formation of the All-India Muslim League

By the early 20th century, the educational renaissance initiated by Sir Syed Ahmad Khan had produced a politically conscious generation of Muslim professionals. Two events catalyzed the formal transition into organized politics: the **Partition of Bengal in 1905** and the anticipation of new British constitutional reforms.

The administrative partition of the unwieldy Bengal presidency created a new province of Eastern Bengal and Assam with a Muslim majority. For the impoverished Muslim peasantry and emerging middle class of East Bengal, this provided administrative access, educational opportunities, and economic relief. However, the Indian National Congress launched an aggressive anti-partition agitation (*Swadeshi* movement), involving boycotts and strikes. This intense reaction demonstrated to Muslim leaders across South Asia that general majoritarian organizations would not protect Muslim socio-economic interests when they conflicted with majority privileges (Khan, 2017).

Anticipating constitutional reforms by the British government, a delegation of 35 prominent Muslim leaders led by Sir Aga Khan III met Viceroy Lord Minto at Simla on 1 October 1906. Known as the **Simla Deputation**, the leaders presented two fundamental constitutional demands:

1. **Separate Electorates:** Muslims should elect their own representatives through exclusive Muslim constituencies, rather than competing in joint electorates where a 25% minority would consistently be outvoted.
2. **Weightage in Representation:** In provinces where Muslims were in a minority, their legislative representation should reflect not merely their numerical proportion but also their historical and political importance.

Encouraged by the Viceroy's receptive response, delegates attended the twentieth annual meeting of the All-India Muhammadan Educational Conference at Dhaka. On **30 December 1906**, under the leadership of Nawab Salimullah of Dhaka and chaired by Nawab Viqar-ul-Mulk, delegates formally founded the **All-India Muslim League**. Its foundational objectives were:

- To promote loyalty to the British government among Muslims while removing misunderstandings regarding administrative measures.
- To protect and advance the political and civil rights of South Asian Muslims by respectfully submitting their needs and aspirations to the government.
- To prevent the rise of animosity between Muslims and other South Asian communities without compromising Muslim interests.

In 1909, the British government incorporated separate electorates into the Indian Councils Act (Morley-Minto Reforms), legally recognizing Muslims as a distinct political entity in the constitutional structure of British India.

### Key events and milestones of the Pakistan Movement

The political trajectory of the Muslim League between 1906 and 1940 moved through three distinct phases: constitutional cooperation, disillusionment, and ideological transformation (Hassan, 2020).

1. **The Era of Constitutional Cooperation (1913–1920):** Quaid-e-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah joined the Muslim League in 1913, having already established himself as a leading constitutional lawyer in the Congress. Committed to Hindu-Muslim unity, Jinnah brokered the historic **Lucknow Pact of 1916** between Congress and the Muslim League. Congress formally conceded the Muslim League's core demands: separate electorates for Muslims and provincial weightage. For this landmark achievement, Jinnah was celebrated across the subcontinent as the "Ambassador of Hindu-Muslim Unity."
2. **Disillusionment and the Parting of the Ways (1928–1929):** The constitutional compromise collapsed in 1928 with the publication of the **Nehru Report**, drafted by a committee chaired by Motilal Nehru. The report repudiated separate electorates, rejected guaranteed Muslim quotas in the central legislature, and proposed a centralized unitary government rather than a loose federation with provincial autonomy. In response, Jinnah presented his famous **Fourteen Points in March 1929**, demanding a genuinely federal constitution with residuary powers vested in the provinces, one-third Muslim representation in the central legislature, full religious liberty, and separation of Sindh from Bombay Presidency. When Congress rejected these minimum safeguards, Jinnah declared: "This is the parting of the ways."
3. **Ideological Articulation (1930):** In December 1930, philosopher-poet Allama Muhammad Iqbal delivered his historic **Presidential Address at Allahabad**. Iqbal diagnosed that European concepts of secular nationalism could not be mechanically superimposed on a multinational continent like South Asia. He proposed that the consolidation of the Muslim-majority provinces in northwestern India (Punjab, NWFP, Sindh, and Balochistan) into a self-governing state was the final destiny of South Asian Muslims.
4. **The Crisis of Congress Ministries (1937–1939):** Provincial elections held under the Government of India Act 1935 saw Congress form ministries in seven provinces. In power, Congress leaders refused coalition governance with the Muslim League unless League members dissolved their own party. Congress ministries implemented policies that alarmed Muslim communities: enforcing the recitation of the *Bande Mataram* anthem in state schools, introducing the *Vidya Mandir* educational scheme centered on majoritarian cultural symbolism, and prioritizing Hindi over Urdu. When Congress ministries resigned in October 1939 over Britain's entry into World War II without consulting Indian leaders, the Muslim League observed a nationwide **Day of Deliverance on 22 December 1939**.
5. **The Watershed: Lahore Resolution (1940):** The experience of 1937–1939 convinced Muslim leaders that majoritarian democracy in a centralized, undivided India would reduce Muslims to a permanent, disenfranchised minority. On **23 March 1940**, at the annual session of the Muslim League in Minto Park, Lahore, Bengal Premier A. K. Fazlul Huq presented the historic resolution, unanimously passed by delegates from every province. The resolution declared that no constitutional plan would be workable or acceptable to Muslims unless geographically contiguous units were demarcated into regions in which the Muslims were numerically in a majority (the North-Western and Eastern zones of India) and grouped into independent sovereign states.

**Common Misconception.** A frequent error is assuming that the demand for Pakistan was born out of unprovoked communal hostility. As historical documents demonstrate, Muslim leaders spent more than twenty-five years (from the 1916 Lucknow Pact to the 1929 Fourteen Points) attempting to construct power-sharing federal frameworks within an undivided India. It was only after repeated rejections of genuine provincial autonomy and the exclusionary practices of the 1937–1939 Congress ministries that sovereign self-determination became the non-negotiable objective.

### Creation of Pakistan: partition and independence

Following the 1940 Lahore Resolution, the Muslim League under Quaid-e-Azam's disciplined leadership mobilized widespread public support across all social classes: students, women, peasants, professionals, and religious scholars (Khan, 2017).

- **Wartime Negotiations:** In 1942, the British sent the **Cripps Mission** to secure Indian cooperation during World War II. For the first time, the British government officially conceded the principle that provinces could opt out of an Indian union, providing indirect recognition to the Pakistan concept.
- **The Decisive Democratic Mandate (1945–1946):** The turning point occurred in the General Elections of 1945–46. The Muslim League fought the election on a single-point manifesto: that the Muslim League was the sole representative body of Indian Muslims, and Pakistan was their non-negotiable goal. The results were overwhelming:
  - In the Central Legislative Assembly, the Muslim League won **100% of all Muslim seats** (30 out of 30).
  - In the provincial assemblies, the Muslim League captured **87% of all Muslim seats** (446 out of 495), sweeping elections across Punjab, Bengal, Sindh, Assam, and the minority provinces.
- **Cabinet Mission Plan (1946):** In a final attempt to preserve Indian administrative unity, the British Cabinet Mission proposed a three-tier constitutional structure with a weak center and three compulsory groups of provinces: Group A (Hindu-majority provinces), Group B (North-Western Muslim provinces), and Group C (Bengal and Assam). To preserve peaceful transition while securing autonomy, Jinnah and the Muslim League accepted the plan. However, Congress leaders issued statements claiming Congress would enter the constituent assembly unfettered by grouping commitments. Recognizing that Congress intended to dismantle the safeguards once in power, Jinnah withdrew the League's acceptance and called for **Direct Action Day** on 16 August 1946.
- **3 June Plan and Partition:** With civil order breaking down and constitutional deadlock complete, Viceroy Lord Mountbatten announced the **3 June 1947 Partition Plan**. Both Congress and the Muslim League accepted the plan. The British Parliament passed the **Indian Independence Act in July 1947**. A Boundary Commission headed by Sir Cyril Radcliffe demarcated the borders in Punjab and Bengal.

On **14 August 1947** (corresponding to 27 Ramadan 1366 AH), Pakistan emerged as an independent sovereign state, with Quaid-e-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah sworn in as its first Governor-General.

**Worked Example.** When teaching the 1945–46 elections, a teacher should present a bar chart comparing the Muslim League's 1937 electoral performance (capturing less than 25% of Muslim votes) with its 1945–46 landslide (winning 87% to 100% of Muslim seats). The teacher asks: "What factors explain this dramatic transformation within eight years?" Students analyze how the organizational reorganization under Jinnah, the tangible experience of Congress rule (1937–1939), and the clear ideological vision of the Lahore Resolution galvanized widespread grassroots consensus.

## Activity: Analyzing constitutional turning points

**Work in pairs. About 25 minutes.**

Consider the constitutional deadlock that occurred between the **Nehru Report (1928)** and **Jinnah's Fourteen Points (1929)**:

| Issue | Nehru Report (1928) Position | Jinnah's Fourteen Points (1929) Position |
|---|---|---|
| **Electoral System** | Joint electorates without reservation of seats for majorities | Separate electorates retained until communities choose otherwise |
| **Federal Structure** | Strong unitary center with residuary powers vested in Delhi | Federation with residuary powers vested in autonomous provinces |
| **Central Representation** | Representation strictly proportional to population | At least one-third Muslim representation in central legislature |
| **Provincial Reorganization** | Conditional administrative adjustments | Separation of Sindh from Bombay; constitutional reforms in NWFP & Balochistan |

With your partner, answer the following questions:

1. Why was genuine provincial autonomy so vital for Muslims in the 1920s and 1930s?
2. If the authors of the Nehru Report had accepted Jinnah's Fourteen Points in 1929, how might the constitutional history of South Asia have developed differently?
3. What lessons does this historical negotiation offer to contemporary Pakistani educators regarding inclusivity, minority rights, and power-sharing in democratic governance?

## Summary

The Pakistan Movement progressed through clear, interconnected constitutional phases. Catalyzed by the Partition of Bengal and the Simla Deputation, the All-India Muslim League was established at Dhaka in 1906 to safeguard Muslim political interests and achieve separate electorates. For two decades, Muslim leaders under Quaid-e-Azam pursued constitutional power-sharing within a united India, culminating in the 1916 Lucknow Pact. However, the centralized, majoritarian vision of the 1928 Nehru Report and the exclusionary governance of the 1937–1939 Congress ministries closed avenues for coexistence within an undivided state. Allama Iqbal provided the intellectual vision in 1930, and on 23 March 1940, the Lahore Resolution formally established the demand for sovereign Muslim statehood. The overwhelming democratic mandate of the 1945–46 general elections decisively proved the popular will of South Asian Muslims, leading directly to the partition of British India and the independence of Pakistan on 14 August 1947.
