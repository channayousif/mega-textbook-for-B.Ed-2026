# The 1973 Constitution: Pakistan's enduring framework

## A real classroom situation

At a teacher education college in Larkana, Bilal prepares a lesson on the 1973 Constitution. His group finds Article 25A in a 2012 copy and labels it "education right introduced in 1973". Another member says Islamic provisions mean that an advisory religious council passes the country's laws. Their tutor asks them to locate the articles, read the amendment notes and identify the institution that actually holds each power. The task is to explain a living constitutional framework without treating every later provision as original.

## Explanation

The 1973 Constitution supplies an enduring framework because later governments and amendments have continued to refer to that constitutional text. **Enduring does not mean uninterrupted or unchanged.** Its history includes suspension, restoration and substantial amendment. A document can remain the basis of the constitutional order while its operation is interrupted or its provisions altered. The National Assembly's *Parliamentary History* records these developments; the official 2012 edition provides amendment notes that help distinguish original provisions from later additions.

For this topic, use the **2012 reading edition** identified in the unit opening. A consolidated edition combines the original text with amendments adopted by a stated date. It is convenient because a reader can see the text as it stood at that date. It is also easy to misuse: a sentence printed in the edition may have entered the Constitution long after 1973. Always ask both "Where is the provision?" and "When did it enter this framework?" This unit is an introduction to constitutional development, not legal advice about the latest amended law.

### Background and formation of the 1973 Constitution

The background was the crisis of 1971 and the need to rebuild an agreed constitutional order in the remaining federation. Topic 3.1 showed that the 1956 and 1962 arrangements had ended in military-rule transitions. The 1970 election had not produced an agreed transfer of power across the two wings. Following the creation of Bangladesh, Pakistan needed institutions that could command wider political acceptance and address relations between the centre and provinces. The formation of the 1973 framework must be read against this experience rather than presented as an isolated drafting exercise.

The National Assembly met in April 1972, adopted an **Interim Constitution on 17 April**, and appointed a committee to prepare a permanent text. The committee's draft was presented at the end of 1972. Negotiation among political parties was important because a constitution needs more than the preferences of the government holding office. Opposition participation can help make a framework a common agreement about authority rather than only a programme for one party. This does not mean that every disagreement disappeared or that all people outside the Assembly were directly involved in drafting.

The Assembly passed the Constitution on **10 April 1973**. It was authenticated on **12 April** and came into force on **14 August 1973** (National Assembly, *Parliamentary History*). These dates answer different questions. Passage marks legislative agreement on the text. Authentication marks formal confirmation. Commencement marks its operation as the new governing framework. If a pupil learns only "1973", the pupil may know the year but still be unable to explain what happened at each stage.

The National Assembly describes unanimous passage. This shows broad agreement among the representatives participating in adoption; it should not be enlarged into a claim that every citizen held the same view. **Consensus** means an agreement broad enough to support a shared course of action. It need not mean that participants have identical beliefs on every detail. For constitution-making, the important point is that political rivals can accept common rules for future disagreement. They can continue to oppose each other's policies while agreeing about how governments are chosen and limited.

A classroom example makes this distinction visible. Suppose a student council debates library hours. Some members favour a longer day and others a shorter one. They agree on a procedure: collect requests, discuss costs and vote at an announced meeting. Agreement on that procedure does not settle the library question. It makes the disagreement manageable. A constitution similarly establishes rules within which policy disputes should continue. National constitutional agreement is much more complex, but the example explains why shared rules do not require shared opinions about every policy.

The 1973 settlement responded to earlier design problems. It adopted a **parliamentary** executive and a **bicameral** Parliament at the federal level. Bicameral means two chambers. The National Assembly linked representation mainly to population; the Senate gave provinces equal representation as provinces. This combination sought to give a place to both the people's numbers and the provinces' status within the federation. The precise membership of the chambers has changed over time, so learning the design principle is more useful here than treating one edition's seat totals as permanent.

A worked example of historical explanation has three steps. First state the earlier challenge: how could a federation represent both population and its constituent provinces? Next identify the institutional response: two federal chambers with different bases of representation. Finally explain the link: the Senate gives smaller provinces an institutional voice that a population-based chamber alone cannot supply in the same way. This is an explanation of the design's purpose. It does not prove that every later dispute between provinces was resolved. Purpose, constitutional mechanism and observed success are separate claims.

Consider a fictional student from a less populous province asking whether population-based representation means that the province has no voice. The teacher should not answer with a slogan that every province has exactly the same role in all decisions. Instead, she distinguishes the chambers and explains what each basis of representation contributes. A federal arrangement can combine equal provincial representation in one place with population-based representation in another. The same design may still leave disputes over resources, policy and influence. Those disputes do not make the distinction between the two chambers meaningless.

The term **formation** therefore covers several activities: preparing a draft, debating institutions, negotiating agreement, adopting the text and bringing it into force. It should not be reduced to a single signature or the name of one leader. Zulfikar Ali Bhutto and his government played a central role, but an account of constitution-making also needs the Assembly, committee work and agreement across parties. A personality-only explanation prevents learners from seeing the institutions they are meant to understand.

### Salient features of the 1973 Constitution

**Salient features** are the main characteristics that help explain how a framework works. For this unit, organise them under parliamentary responsibility, federalism, representative law-making, constitutional limits and rights. Keep Islamic provisions visible as a major feature, then study their specific articles in the next section. This organisation avoids presenting fifteen isolated facts without showing how they relate.

**Parliamentary responsibility.** The President is head of state and represents the unity of the Republic. The Prime Minister leads the federal government with the cabinet. In the 2012 edition, Articles 90 and 91 explain the federal executive and cabinet's responsibility; Article 48 addresses presidential action on advice, with the constitutional qualifications. The central idea is that the government depends on its parliamentary relationship rather than a President independently directing a cabinet as under the 1962 framework. Do not translate "parliamentary" into "the President does nothing". A head of state has constitutional functions even where government is led by the Prime Minister.

**Federalism.** Article 1 identifies Pakistan as a federal republic. Authority is distributed between the federation and provinces through the Constitution. Articles 141 and 142 and the Fourth Schedule help explain law-making competence in the 2012 edition. **Competence** here means lawful authority to act in a defined area. It does not mean personal ability or intelligence. A highly capable official may still lack constitutional authority to decide a matter assigned elsewhere.

Federalism includes both divided responsibilities and shared arrangements. A province needs authority over its own sphere, while national institutions handle matters assigned to the federation. Coordination is needed where interests cross boundaries. The Council of Common Interests is one constitutional mechanism for such coordination (Articles 153-154 in the reading edition). Its presence shows that a federation cannot be understood as separate governments never speaking to one another. It also cannot be understood as a single centre merely lending powers that it may withdraw without constitutional limits.

Education provides a useful dated example for a trainee in Sindh. The **Eighteenth Amendment of 2010** removed the Concurrent Legislative List and widened provincial legislative responsibility. Article 25A, also added in 2010, recognised free and compulsory education for children aged five to sixteen, in the manner determined by law. These changes appear in the 2012 edition. They are **later developments within the 1973 framework**, not evidence that Article 25A or the post-2010 allocation of responsibilities existed in precisely that form from the start. Hussain and Kokab (2012) examine the amendment's parliamentary and provincial changes and the debates around them.

The example does not mean that the federal level has no educational role of any kind. Federal territories, coordination, standards in specified areas and other constitutional provisions complicate such a sweeping statement. For this introductory unit, the sound claim is narrower: the 2010 amendment substantially changed the distribution of authority and added an express education right. Unit 4 examines governance and rights in more detail. Here the example demonstrates why date and article should travel together in a constitutional explanation.

**Bicameral representation.** Article 50 identifies Parliament as comprising the President and the two Houses, the National Assembly and Senate. This wording is important: the President is a constitutional component of Parliament, but is not a third legislative chamber. A **legislature** is an institution that makes laws. Saying that the federal legislature is bicameral counts the Houses, not every office participating in the law-making process. The distinction corrects an error that often appears when pupils count the three names in Article 50 and conclude there are three chambers.

The National Assembly and Senate have different representative bases and do not have identical powers in every matter. For example, the reading edition distinguishes the Money Bill procedure in Article 73. A Money Bill concerns constitutionally specified financial matters. At this level, you need not learn every procedural detail. You should understand that "two Houses" does not mean "two completely identical institutions". Before claiming equal authority in a particular decision, check the article that governs that decision.

**Constitutional limits.** The Constitution establishes offices and places rules around them. An officeholder's authority comes from the framework and does not depend only on personal popularity. The existence of an election therefore does not make every later action lawful. A mandate to govern operates within constitutional limits. This is one meaning of **constitutional government**: public authority is exercised under the governing rules rather than whatever the officeholder wishes at the moment.

**Fundamental rights.** Part II, Chapter 1 provides rights protections. Article 8 addresses laws inconsistent with fundamental rights, while particular articles set out particular protections and their qualifications. Article 20 concerns religious freedom and religious institutions; Article 25 concerns equality before law and equal protection. Explain the article's scope rather than assuming a right has no conditions or that any official may remove it for convenience. Detailed legal disputes require fuller interpretation, but a trainee can still learn to locate a provision and paraphrase it carefully.

For a classroom example, imagine a teacher preparing an equal-participation lesson for pupils of different backgrounds. Article 25 can help frame an explanation of equal citizenship, while Article 20 helps distinguish religious freedom from compulsory agreement in belief. The teacher should not tell a pupil that a classroom disagreement has automatically proved a constitutional violation or predict a court's decision. The teaching move is to connect general constitutional principles to respectful conduct while keeping the difference between an educational example and a legal finding clear.

**Principles of Policy** appear separately in Part II, Chapter 2. They guide state action on social and other goals. Their constitutional treatment differs from the enforceable rights chapter. Article 30 explains that the validity of a law or action is not called into question merely because it is inconsistent with these principles. This distinction matters when reading Article 31 on the Islamic way of life. The principles are constitutional commitments, but their mode of operation is not identical to that of each fundamental right. Unit 4 develops the distinction; here it helps you read the text without giving every article the same legal effect.

**Judicial institutions.** The constitutional framework provides courts and legal processes for addressing disputes and limits on public power. A **constitutional remedy** is a legal means of seeking protection where a constitutional requirement is at issue. The detailed court structure and jurisdiction have been amended over time. This lesson therefore uses the broad feature and the dated reading edition rather than offering an undated account of every court's current powers. Judicial independence is a constitutional aim; the presence of courts alone does not prove that independence has always been secure in practice.

**A written framework that can be amended.** Written does not mean incapable of change. An amendment changes the constitutional text through the prescribed process. Articles 238-239 in the 2012 edition describe that process, including the two-thirds-of-total-membership requirement in each House for a constitutional amendment. For a proposal altering provincial boundaries, the relevant provincial assembly also has a specified approval role. These are special requirements for constitutional change, not the procedure for every ordinary law. Never apply a constitutional-amendment threshold to a routine school rule or ordinary parliamentary bill merely because it sounds more serious.

A worked reading task connects these features. Bilal's group says, "The Constitution guarantees education, so Article 25A was part of it in 1973." First locate the article. Next read the amendment footnote identifying the Eighteenth Amendment of 2010. Then split the original statement into two: "The 2012 edition contains Article 25A" and "Article 25A entered the 1973 framework through a later amendment." The corrected explanation preserves the right while fixing the historical claim. It also models how to correct an error without discarding everything a pupil has understood.

**Constitutional continuity and political practice.** The 1973 framework was interrupted under military rule, notably after 1977 and 1999, and later restored with amendments. The National Assembly's history records those disruptions. A pupil who says the Constitution has operated unchanged every day since 1973 overlooks them. A pupil who says Pakistan received an entirely new constitution with every restoration also misunderstands continuity. The more precise account identifies a continuing constitutional framework whose operation and balance of power have changed over time.

Hussain and Kokab (2012) treat the Eighteenth Amendment as a major effort to restore parliamentary features and strengthen provincial autonomy, while discussing disagreements it raised. Their article is an interpretation of a particular reform, not proof that all earlier or later disputes were settled. Use its central analysis alongside the constitutional text. Where an article number, meeting requirement or legal rule matters, the primary constitutional provision is the controlling reference for this lesson. Scholarly commentary helps explain significance; it does not replace checking the rule itself.

### Islamic provisions in the 1973 Constitution

**Islamic provisions** are constitutional rules and commitments concerning the relationship between Islam and public life. They occur in more than one place, not only in the Part titled "Islamic Provisions". To understand them, identify the article, the institution or person it addresses, and what kind of obligation it creates. Then read related rights and citizenship provisions. This approach is more accurate and respectful than trying to decide the meaning of the entire constitutional order from a single label.

**Article 2** declares Islam the state religion. This is a statement about the constitutional identity of the state. By itself, it does not explain which institution passes laws, how the cabinet is accountable or what rights citizens hold. Those questions require other articles. The declaration is therefore a starting point for inquiry rather than a substitute for examining the rest of the framework.

**Article 2A** gives substantive status to the Objectives Resolution reproduced in the Annex. As Topic 3.1 explained, it was inserted in **1985**. The Preamble already contained the Resolution's commitments in 1973. The two locations are connected, but their chronology and constitutional function must be distinguished. A prompt asking for "Islamic provisions of the 1973 Constitution as amended to 2012" may include Article 2A with that date. A prompt asking for "the original provisions adopted in 1973" must not silently include it as original.

**Article 31**, in the Principles of Policy, concerns enabling Muslims to order their lives according to Islam and providing relevant facilities and education. It also refers to promoting unity and observance of Islamic moral standards and organising zakat, ushr, auqaf and mosques. For this unit, the important distinction is its location and purpose. It directs state policy; it is not an instruction for any individual teacher to impose a personal interpretation on every pupil. Read it with Article 30's treatment of the Principles of Policy and the specific protections elsewhere in the Constitution.

**Article 227**, in Part IX, requires laws to be brought into conformity with the injunctions of Islam as laid down in the Quran and Sunnah, and bars laws repugnant to those injunctions. **Repugnant** here means inconsistent or in conflict. Article 227 also states that effect is to be given in the manner provided in that Part. Its clause (3) protects the personal laws of non-Muslim citizens and their status as citizens. An account that omits that clause turns a qualified constitutional provision into a broader claim than the text supports.

**Personal law** concerns matters such as family relationships governed by the applicable legal system. You do not need to learn the details of each community's personal law for this unit. You do need to recognise the explicit safeguard in Article 227(3). It is a strong example of why reading only a heading or the first clause is insufficient. The same article can state a general requirement and specify its limits.

**Articles 228-230** provide for the **Council of Islamic Ideology** and its advisory work. Article 228 concerns its composition; Article 229 concerns references for advice; Article 230 sets out its functions. The Council makes recommendations and advises relevant constitutional bodies on questions about conformity of laws with Islamic injunctions. It is **not Parliament** and does not replace Parliament as the law-making institution. Article 231 concerns the Council's procedure. The article sequence shows an institution, a route for asking it questions, its functions and its procedural framework.

A worked example tests the institutional distinction. Suppose a fictional news summary says, "The Council recommended a change, so the law changed that afternoon." Locate the action: a recommendation. Identify the actor: the Council. Next ask whether the relevant legislature completed a law-making process. Without that evidence, the conclusion does not follow. A recommendation may be important and may create duties of consideration under the constitutional arrangements, but recommendation and enactment are different actions. The Constitution also allows specified circumstances in which proposed legislation proceeds before advice is received, with reconsideration if the Council later finds it repugnant (Article 230).

**Qualifications for offices** also form part of the Islamic constitutional framework. In the 2012 edition, Article 41(2) requires the President to be Muslim, and Article 91(3) provides for election of the Prime Minister from the Muslim members of the National Assembly. State the article and edition when explaining such rules. A requirement for a particular office is not a general description of every citizenship right or every public job. Keep the scope of a rule as narrow as the text makes it.

**Religious freedom and citizenship must be read alongside these provisions.** Article 20 recognises the right of citizens to profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to law, public order and morality, and addresses religious institutions. Article 22(1) protects a person attending an educational institution from being required to receive religious instruction, or take part in a ceremony or worship, relating to a religion other than their own. Article 25 addresses equality before law. Reading these provisions alongside Articles 2, 31 and 227 helps a teacher avoid presenting religious identity and minority protections as if only one can exist in the constitutional text.

Use an invented school example carefully. A practicum teacher in Tharparkar prepares a lesson for a class including Muslim and Hindu pupils. She explains Article 31's policy purpose concerning Muslims and Article 22(1)'s protection concerning another religion's instruction or worship. She can teach constitutional literacy without requiring pupils to disclose private beliefs or defend their community before classmates. The example shows how separate provisions answer separate questions. It does not turn the teacher into a court deciding the application of every clause to a particular dispute.

**Correcting the theocracy misconception.** A theocracy, in the sense used here, is government by religious officeholders as the holders of ruling authority. The existence of Islamic provisions does not by itself show that constitutional authority belongs to such officeholders. The framework also establishes representative institutions, a parliamentary executive, courts and an advisory Council. The appropriate response is an institutional explanation: who legislates, who governs, who advises and what rights are stated? Avoid replacing the misconception with the opposite claim that religion has no constitutional role. Both shortcuts ignore the text's combination of Islamic commitments and representative arrangements.

There can be debate about how these commitments should be interpreted and how well institutions have upheld them. A constitutional lesson can acknowledge that debate without asking trainees to settle all questions of political or religious theory. Begin with the shared evidence: article, function, institutional role and date. Distinguish what the text requires from what you think good policy should be. That distinction allows respectful disagreement because participants can see whether they are disputing a fact about the document or an evaluation of it.

Return to Bilal's group. Their Article 25A card needs a 2010 date, not deletion. Their Council card needs the action "advises" rather than "passes laws". Their overview can describe the 1973 framework as enduring while marking interruptions and amendments. These repairs preserve useful learning and remove overclaims. The same method will help you teach other historical documents: locate the evidence, identify its date and scope, then write a claim no wider than that evidence supports.

## Activity: An article-reading jigsaw

**Work in groups of four. Allow about 35 minutes.** Use this topic and, where available, printed pages from the official 2012 edition. No pupil or trainee needs to disclose religious identity or political affiliation.

- Assign an initial focus to each member: formation dates; parliamentary and federal design; rights and policy; Islamic provisions and Council advice. Each member first recalls two points without looking.
- Each member then prepares a source card with four fields: article or official history reference, plain-English meaning, actor or institution, and date or version warning. The formation card uses historical dates rather than inventing an article for the drafting process.
- Teach your card to the group. Other members must ask one question about scope: who does the rule address, what does it require, or what does it not establish by itself?
- Together inspect three invented claims: "Article 25A began in 1973"; "the President is Parliament's third chamber"; "a Council recommendation immediately becomes law". Identify the evidence needed to repair each.
- Write one accurate replacement for each claim. Exchange replacements with another group. The receiving group checks whether each has an institution, an action and a date where required.
- Finish with an individual 80-word note connecting one historical challenge from Topic 3.1 to one feature of the 1973 settlement. Partners compare their links and explain differences.

The joint product is four source cards and three repaired claims. The individual note makes each member retrieve and use the group's learning. A well-presented card without an accurate institutional relationship is still unfinished work.

## Check your understanding

1. Recall the three principal dates of the Constitution's passage, authentication and commencement in 1973. State which event belongs to each. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why two federal chambers can represent population and provinces in different ways. Use the National Assembly and Senate in your explanation. *(Understand)*
3. A lesson slide lists Article 2A and Article 25A as original 1973 provisions. Explain how you would use the reading edition's notes to correct it. *(Apply)*
4. A classmate says that Council advice is the same action as enacting a law. Use an institutional distinction and a relevant article range to explain the difference. *(Apply)*

## Summary

The 1973 Constitution emerged from committee work, political agreement and formal adoption after the 1971 crisis. It combines parliamentary government, federal institutions, two federal chambers, constitutional limits, rights and Islamic commitments. Its continuing identity does not mean that it has operated without interruption or amendment. Accurate explanation names the relevant institution and article and distinguishes the original settlement from later additions.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can explain how agreement on constitutional rules differs from agreement on every policy.
- [ ] I can connect parliamentary responsibility and the two-chamber design to earlier constitutional challenges.
- [ ] I can use an article and its amendment note to make a dated constitutional claim.
- [ ] I can distinguish Islamic Council advice from law-making and explain a related minority safeguard.

## Try this at your practicum school

With your cooperating teacher, prepare a one-page reading sheet containing paraphrases of Article 50, Article 22(1), Article 227(3) and Article 230 from the dated reading edition. Include article numbers and a link or printed reference. Ask a small group of pupils, in a supervised lesson, to sort the paraphrases by function: institution, protection or advice. The aim is textual understanding, not a survey of beliefs.

Record which distinction needed explanation and revise one sentence to make it clearer. Collect no names or private religious information. If you cannot teach the sheet on placement, ask peers to try the sort and label your reflection as a peer simulation. Bring the revised sheet and a short reflection to your tutor; do not claim that a short activity measures pupils' citizenship or loyalty.

## Summative task

Prepare a **500-600 word** explanation for a trainee who says: "The 1973 Constitution was the first constitution, has never changed, and gives law-making power to the Islamic Council." Correct the three claims with evidence. Connect one feature of the 1973 settlement to an earlier challenge, and include one dated later amendment. Refer to a safeguard concerning non-Muslim citizens or religious education without asking anyone to disclose personal beliefs. *(Analyze / Evaluate)*

| Criterion | Strong evidence | Partial evidence | Limited evidence | Marks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Historical and edition accuracy | Corrects first-constitution and unchanged-text claims with dated evidence | Corrects claims but leaves one date or distinction unclear | Repeats either major error | 3 / 1 / 0 |
| Institutional analysis | Explains law-making versus advice and links a design feature to an earlier challenge | Describes institutions but develops only one relationship | Lists names without explaining powers or relationships | 4 / 2 / 0 |
| Textual evidence and scope | Uses relevant articles, a dated amendment and a correctly scoped safeguard | Gives usable evidence but omits one required element | Gives unsupported assertions or generalises beyond the text | 3 / 1 / 0 |

The institutional criterion requires analysis of relationships. Merely naming the Prime Minister, Parliament and Council cannot receive its highest score.
