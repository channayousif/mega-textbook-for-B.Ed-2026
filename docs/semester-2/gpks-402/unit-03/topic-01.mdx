# The early constitutions and the Objectives Resolution

## A real classroom situation

At a government secondary school in Hyderabad, trainee teacher Ayesha asks pupils to prepare a constitutional timeline. One pupil begins in 1973 because that is the constitution named in the textbook. Another places the Objectives Resolution in 1940 beside the Lahore Resolution. A third writes that Pakistan had no rules of government before 1956. Ayesha needs to correct the dates and explain what each document actually did.

## Explanation

A **constitution** sets the basic rules of a state. It establishes institutions, distributes authority and states limits on its use. A government is the group holding office for a period; a constitution provides the framework within which governments should act. Changing a prime minister is therefore different from replacing a constitution. A new prime minister may take office under exactly the same constitutional rules.

A school comparison helps, but has limits. A school committee may agree that a head teacher manages daily work while a council approves the budget. The next head teacher should follow the same arrangement. However, a national constitution deals with public power, citizenship and rights across a whole country. It has a legal status that a classroom agreement does not have. Use the school example to understand roles, not to reduce national constitutional conflict to a simple disagreement among pupils.

Keep three questions beside every document in this topic. What principles did it announce? What institutions did it establish? What happened to those institutions in practice? The first asks about aims, the second about design and the third about history. An explanation becomes weak when it answers one question with evidence that belongs to another.

### The Objectives Resolution: content and significance

The first Constituent Assembly was responsible for framing Pakistan's constitution. A **constituent assembly** is a representative body charged with making a constitution. While that work continued, an adapted Government of India Act, 1935 provided the governing framework. Independence in 1947 did not mean that every rule disappeared until 1956. It meant that the new state inherited an interim arrangement while seeking an agreed permanent one. The National Assembly's *Parliamentary History* describes this sequence and records adoption of the Objectives Resolution on **12 March 1949**, after its introduction by Prime Minister Liaquat Ali Khan.

The **Objectives Resolution** stated principles to guide constitution-making. It was not a complete constitution. It did not by itself supply the detailed structure of Parliament, every public office or a full procedure for passing laws. Its significance lay in setting the direction in which a later constitutional settlement should develop. Distinguishing a guiding resolution from a complete framework prevents the mistaken statement that the 1949 document was simply the first full constitution under another name.

The Resolution brought several commitments into one statement. It declared the sovereignty of Allah and treated the authority exercised through the people as a trust within prescribed limits. It called for the state to exercise authority through chosen representatives. It linked democracy, freedom, equality, tolerance and social justice with Islam. Muslims were to be enabled to live according to the Quran and Sunnah. It also promised religious and cultural freedom for minorities, fundamental rights, an independent judiciary and a federation with autonomous units. These themes appear in the Constitution's Preamble and the Resolution reproduced in its Annex (National Assembly, 2012 edition, Preamble and Annex).

**Sovereignty** concerns ultimate authority. **Representation** concerns how people take part in public decision-making through persons chosen to act for them. Reading the Resolution carefully means attending to both ideas. A pupil who notices only sovereignty and omits chosen representatives gives an incomplete account. A pupil who mentions representation but leaves out the Islamic framing also leaves out a central part of the document. The educational task is to explain the combination accurately before evaluating how it worked.

**Fundamental rights** are basic protections recognised by a constitution. The Resolution's commitments included equality and freedoms of belief and expression, subject to its stated qualifications. A **judiciary** is the system of courts. Independence of the judiciary means that judges should decide according to law rather than instructions from those whose actions they examine. The promise of an independent judiciary matters because a limit on government is weak if the government alone decides whether it has crossed that limit.

A **federation** is a constitutional arrangement that distributes authority between a national level and provincial or other constituent levels. **Autonomy** means a sphere of decision-making of one's own within that arrangement. It does not mean that each province is a separate sovereign country. Nor does a federation mean that the national government may decide every provincial matter whenever it wishes. Later constitutions had to turn the broad promise of autonomy into workable rules about powers and institutions.

Consider an invented lesson in a Mirpurkhas classroom. The teacher writes two statements: public authority is a trust, and minorities should be able to practise their religions. She asks whether only the first belongs in an explanation of the Resolution. A careful explanation keeps both. It shows that the document combines a view of authority with obligations towards people. This classroom exercise describes the text's commitments; it does not claim that those commitments were always fulfilled in later practice.

A useful worked example begins with a claim: "The Objectives Resolution gave every province a complete list of its powers in 1949." Identify the type of document first. It was a statement of guiding principles. Next, identify the relevant commitment: a federation with autonomous units. Finally, ask whether the claim adds detail the Resolution did not provide. It does. A better sentence is: "The Resolution committed constitution-makers to provincial autonomy, while later constitutional arrangements had to specify its scope." This is more accurate because it separates a principle from its detailed implementation.

The Resolution also matters as a point of continuity across constitutional change. Pakistan replaced constitutional frameworks, yet the Resolution remained a reference point in the constitutional tradition. Continuity of an idea does not mean continuity of every institution. A document may repeat a commitment to representation while establishing a different method of choosing representatives. This distinction will help you compare 1956 with 1962 rather than assuming that similar introductory language makes their government systems identical.

**A common confusion needs two corrections.** The Lahore Resolution of 1940 and the Objectives Resolution of 1949 are different documents serving different purposes. The former belongs to the demand for a political settlement before Pakistan's creation; the latter guided constitution-making after independence. Also, the Objectives Resolution was already reflected in the **Preamble of the 1973 Constitution from the outset**. It did not first become a preamble in 1985. **Article 2A**, inserted in 1985, gave the principles and provisions reproduced in the Annex substantive constitutional status. A preamble introduces purposes; a substantive provision forms part of the operative constitutional rules. The 2012 edition's Article 2A and its amendment footnote establish the distinction.

The approved course plan alerts us to confusion about the Resolution's place in 1973. The accurate sequence is therefore 1949 adoption, inclusion in the 1973 Preamble, and 1985 substantive status through Article 2A. When you make revision cards, use separate lines for these events. Combining them into a single date would hide the very change you are trying to explain.

### Constitution of 1956: key features

Pakistan's first full constitution came into force on **23 March 1956**. The National Assembly's history records that the second Constituent Assembly passed it on 29 February, followed by assent on 2 March. **Passage**, **assent** and **commencement** are separate steps: approval by the law-making body, formal approval by the head of state, and the date when the framework takes effect. A date question should make clear which step it asks about. The first constitution did not arrive nine years after independence because the country previously had no administration. The problem was achieving an agreed permanent settlement within a functioning but disputed interim system.

The Constitution established the **Islamic Republic of Pakistan** and a **parliamentary** system. In a parliamentary system, the prime minister and cabinet govern through their relationship with the representative assembly. The head of state and the head of government have different roles. Under the 1956 framework, the President was head of state, while the Prime Minister led the government. The National Assembly's history describes the President as normally acting on prime-ministerial advice, subject to specified areas of discretion. Calling the framework parliamentary does not imply that the President had no powers.

The Constitution was **federal** in form. It organised the country at national and provincial levels. At that time Pakistan had eastern and western wings separated geographically by India. One Unit had combined the western provinces and territories into West Pakistan in 1955. The federal question in 1956 must therefore be understood in that historical setting. It is misleading to picture the same provincial arrangement that exists in a later edition of the 1973 Constitution and simply place it on a 1956 map.

The central legislature was **unicameral**, meaning that it had one legislative chamber. The two wings were given equal representation in the National Assembly. This arrangement is often described as **parity**, meaning equality between the two wings in representation. Parity between wings is not the same as distributing every seat strictly by population. A representation rule can balance territorial units while raising questions about the weight of individual votes. That tension became a major constitutional issue because East Pakistan had a larger population.

Here is a worked comparison using imaginary numbers rather than historical seat totals. Suppose one region has 60 people and another has 40. If both receive ten seats, each region has equal representation. However, each eastern seat would represent six people while each western seat would represent four. Equal seats do not mean equal population per seat. The example shows the logic of parity, not the actual population or seat figures of Pakistan. Whenever you use a simplified numerical example, label it clearly so pupils do not mistake a teaching aid for a historical statistic.

The 1956 framework included fundamental rights and Islamic provisions (historical text, Parts II and XII, linked by the Council of Common Interests). These features should not be reduced to the country's official name. Rights concern protections and limits on public authority. Islamic provisions concern the relationship between the constitutional order and Islam. In comparing constitutions, ask what the provisions required and what institutions were meant to give them effect. Do not assume that the word "Islamic" answers every question about law-making, voting or executive responsibility. The constitutional title and the system of government are different comparison categories.

A trainee in Sukkur might prepare four cards: President, Prime Minister, National Assembly and provincial government. Placing Prime Minister beside "head of government" and President beside "head of state" corrects a frequent confusion. Adding the word "federal" beside the national-provincial relationship answers a different question. Finally, placing "one chamber" beside National Assembly completes the institutional picture. This exercise works because the categories are kept separate rather than all being treated as synonyms for democracy.

The Constitution's short life does not make its design unimportant. It was abrogated, or formally set aside, on **7 October 1958**, when President Iskander Mirza declared martial law. General Ayub Khan subsequently removed Mirza and assumed the presidency. The National Assembly's history distinguishes these events. Saying only "Ayub cancelled it in 1958" loses the sequence and the role of the President who first abrogated it. Chronology helps explain institutional action rather than merely test memory.

No general election under the 1956 Constitution took place before its abrogation. This is relevant when judging its record. We can study the parliamentary design, but we cannot evaluate a completed national electoral cycle under that framework because there was none. Its failure should therefore not be explained solely as the voters rejecting parliamentary government. Political instability, executive intervention and the interruption of constitutional institutions must be considered. A constitutional arrangement can be cut short before its planned representative process is fully tested.

### Constitution of 1962: key features

The next constitution was promulgated under President Ayub Khan in **1962**. The National Assembly's history describes a Constitution Commission appointed in 1960 and the subsequent introduction of the new framework. It replaced the parliamentary arrangement with a **presidential** one. In a presidential system, the chief executive is the President rather than a prime minister sustained by assembly confidence. Under this framework, cabinet members were responsible to the President. The key comparison concerns the executive's constitutional base, not whether a country happens to use the title President.

The presence of a President therefore does not prove that a system is presidential. The 1956 parliamentary framework also had a President. Ask instead who headed government and to whom the cabinet was responsible. For 1956, identify Prime Minister and parliamentary responsibility. For 1962, identify the President and a cabinet responsible to that office. This test is more reliable than looking at the most familiar title on a photograph or a list of leaders.

The 1962 Constitution retained a **federal form**, with national and provincial institutions, but concentrated considerable authority in the presidency. Provincial governors were appointed by the President. It is consequently inaccurate to call it simply unitary because the centre was strong. A **unitary** arrangement locates ultimate constitutional authority in one central system, while a federal arrangement recognises constituent levels within the constitutional framework. In this comparison, the careful description is "formally federal, with a strong centre and presidency" (National Assembly, *Parliamentary History*).

The central legislature remained **unicameral**. Indirect election was another important feature. Under the Basic Democracies system, a body of local representatives, called Basic Democrats, served as an **electoral college** for the presidency and assemblies. An electoral college is a body that chooses officeholders on behalf of a wider electorate. This differs from direct national voting for those offices. Explaining the route from voter to local representative to national institution is more useful than memorising the term without knowing what it means.

Use a clearly labelled classroom analogy. In one student council arrangement, every pupil votes directly for council representatives. In another, pupils choose class delegates and those delegates choose the council. Both involve a choice somewhere in the chain, but the route and the distance between the voter and the final officeholder differ. This analogy helps explain indirect election; it does not establish that the Basic Democracies system had the same powers or conditions as a school council. Historical evaluation still requires evidence about the actual system.

Rights and Islamic provisions also require attention to **version**. The 1962 constitutional text was amended during its lifetime. Safeguards for fundamental rights were restored through the Constitution (First Amendment) Act, 1963, which received assent on 10 January 1964 and took effect at once. Its title contains 1963, but its legal enactment belongs to 1964. The original scheme had not provided the same enforceable rights chapter. The original title "Republic of Pakistan" was also changed to "Islamic Republic of Pakistan" through that amendment. Do not describe every feature of the amended text as if it appeared unchanged on the first day. Compare the historical constitutional text linked by the Council of Common Interests with sections 3-4 of Act I of 1964 in the Ministry of Law's official collection. The latter records both assent and the rights chapter.

This distinction matters when using revision charts. A chart that says "1962: no rights" without a date is too broad. A chart that says "1962: rights chapter throughout" is also misleading. The more accurate entry is that the original scheme differed from 1956, while the First Amendment, effective in 1964, introduced enforceable fundamental-rights safeguards. A teacher need not turn an introductory lesson into a complete legal commentary. However, even a short comparison should avoid a statement that becomes false because it merges different versions.

The same caution applies to Islamic features. The original 1962 scheme included Islamic advisory arrangements, and its title later changed. It is incorrect to infer that Islam had no place in the constitutional framework simply because the first title omitted the word "Islamic". A title is one piece of evidence. Provisions and institutions supply other evidence. Compare the constitution on those dimensions rather than allowing one word to stand for the entire text.

The 1962 Constitution ended in **March 1969**, when Ayub Khan transferred power to General Yahya Khan and martial law was imposed again. This was another interruption of the constitutional framework, not an ordinary change of cabinet within it. A new constitution had not automatically prevented conflict or assured a settled transfer of power. Institutional durability depends on what political actors do as well as on the rules a document sets out. The next section connects this distinction to the transition towards 1973.

### Constitutional challenges and the gap between 1956 and 1973

The interval between the first and third constitutions was not an empty gap without constitutional arrangements. It included the 1956 framework, the martial-law period after 1958, the 1962 framework, another martial-law period after 1969, the 1971 crisis and the Interim Constitution of 1972. Use the word "gap" to ask about interruptions in agreed constitutional government, not to erase the documents that existed. The National Assembly's history provides the sequence; explaining the challenges requires relating that sequence to representation, authority and trust.

**Representation was a central challenge.** A common political community needs an accepted way of turning votes into authority. East-West parity and representation by population addressed different concerns. Parity sought a balance between the two wings, while population-based representation reflected the larger eastern electorate. A fair account explains both concerns without treating all people in either wing as holding identical views. Regional leaders, political parties and citizens could disagree within a wing as well as across the country.

**Provincial autonomy was another challenge.** Different regions sought a voice over matters affecting their own lives. A strong centre could claim that national coordination required concentrated power; critics could argue that concentration excluded provincial needs. For a Sindh classroom, a useful analogy is deciding which matters belong to an entire school and which a class can settle for itself. A shared examination timetable needs school coordination. A classroom reading corner may need local choice. The analogy shows that distributing powers requires reasons. It does not settle the historical constitutional question by itself.

**The relationship between elected institutions and unelected authority also mattered.** The dissolution of the first Constituent Assembly in 1954 and the abrogations of 1958 and 1969 interrupted constitution-making or constitutional government. A prime minister losing legislative support within a constitution is one kind of change. An officeholder setting aside the framework is another. If pupils place both in the same category, they miss the difference between working through a rule and removing the rule. This is why institutional verbs such as dissolved, abrogated and amended should be explained rather than memorised without meaning.

A **constitutional amendment** changes the text through a recognised procedure. **Abrogation** sets the framework aside. **Suspension** interrupts its operation without necessarily replacing it formally. The terms identify different events. Calling every political change an amendment makes it impossible to see whether actors used a constitutional process or displaced it. When your source uses one of these words, note the actor, the action and the effect. That three-part note often explains more than an isolated date.

The **1970 general election** brought the question of representation into sharp focus. It was held on the basis of adult franchise and population under the Legal Framework Order. Adult franchise means voting rights for adults who meet the electoral conditions. Failure to achieve an agreed transfer of power, conflict over autonomy and the subsequent military conflict formed part of the crisis. East Pakistan became Bangladesh in 1971.

Explain this history with care. It cannot be reduced to "the eastern wing chose to leave after an election" or "one leader caused everything". Representation, negotiation, state force, regional grievances and war all matter to the context. The constitutional lesson here is narrower: an election alone cannot secure a stable settlement if political actors do not agree on how its outcome will become governing authority. For deeper historical interpretation, use Hamid Khan's *Constitutional and Political History of Pakistan* as the guide-recommended further reading; the official parliamentary chronology establishes the sequence used in this lesson.

After the 1971 rupture, constitution-making concerned the remaining federation. Provincial questions within it still required agreement. The National Assembly adopted an **Interim Constitution in April 1972** and appointed a Constitution Committee. Interim means temporary: it provided an arrangement while a permanent text was prepared. A temporary framework can be legally significant even though its authors expect it to be replaced. It is not the same as having no constitution or waiting with no rules at all.

A worked explanation of the whole period can now connect evidence to a conclusion. Start with the question: "Why was writing a constitution not enough to secure continuity?" Select three pieces of evidence: the 1956 parliamentary constitution was abrogated in 1958; a presidential constitution followed in 1962; that framework was also displaced in 1969. The evidence supports the conclusion that changing executive design alone did not resolve constitutional instability. It does not prove that the two designs were identical, or that written rules were useless. A stronger conclusion is that rules require accepted representation, accountable institutions and observance by those who wield power.

The classroom tension at the start can now be resolved. Pakistan had constitutional arrangements before 1973, including two earlier full constitutions. The Objectives Resolution belongs to 1949 and guided later constitution-making. The country's history between 1956 and 1973 contains both institutional experiments and serious interruptions. Ayesha can ask pupils to add a second line below each date: "What changed in authority or representation?" That turns the timeline into an explanation rather than a memory exercise.

## Activity: Build an evidence-based comparison

**Work in groups of four. Allow about 30 minutes.** Use paper cards; internet access is not required if one group member has printed this topic. The aim is to explain the choices and interruptions in the early frameworks, not to rank classmates' political opinions.

- Assign roles: chronology reader, institutional comparer, evidence checker and presenter. Rotate the roles after the first round so every person explains a concept.
- Close the text for two minutes. Each member recalls one feature of the Resolution or an early constitution and gives a reason it mattered. Other members ask a clarifying question before the group checks the text.
- Make columns for 1949, 1956, 1962 and the 1969-1972 transition. Put a document or event in each column. Under each, identify whether it states principles, establishes institutions, interrupts a framework or prepares a replacement.
- Compare 1956 and 1962 on the executive, legislature and route of representation. Require the evidence checker to reject a cell saying only "democratic" or "strong" without explaining the institution concerned.
- Add a separate strip for a change within a framework, such as the 1964 rights amendment. Keep it distinct from abrogation. This tests whether the group has understood constitutional versions.
- Exchange the chart with another group. That group must find one accurate distinction and one statement needing a date, a qualification or a source. Revise the statement and report how the revision improves it.

Your shared product is a comparison chart and a 100-word explanation of why constitutional continuity remained difficult. A group succeeds when each member can explain one comparison without reading the chart. Agreement without a reason is not enough.

## Check your understanding

Answer from memory first, then use the explanation to repair omissions.

1. State the year and purpose of the Objectives Resolution, and name two commitments beyond its statement about sovereignty. *(Remember)*
2. Explain, in three sentences, why the presence of a President does not make both the 1956 and 1962 systems presidential. *(Understand)*
3. A pupil writes, "The 1962 Constitution was unitary because the centre was powerful." Rewrite the sentence using the distinction between federal form and central concentration. *(Apply)*
4. A classroom timeline labels the 1958 abrogation "an amendment". Explain how you would move or relabel that entry and why. *(Apply)*

## Summary

The Objectives Resolution supplied guiding commitments in 1949, while the 1956 and 1962 constitutions established different systems of government. The first was parliamentary and the second presidential; both had a federal form and one central legislative chamber. Disputes over representation and autonomy, together with interventions that displaced constitutional rules, prevented a stable constitutional sequence. A reliable account distinguishes principles, institutional design, later amendments and actual political events.
