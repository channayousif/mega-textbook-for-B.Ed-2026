## A real classroom situation

Zainab is a B.Ed trainee teaching Class 7 Social Studies at a government school in Mithi, in Tharparkar. She asks the class to describe Pakistan in one word. Most pupils write "desert", "sand" or "hot". One boy writes "drought". When Zainab shows a photograph of snow on Nanga Parbat, a girl laughs and says, "That is not Pakistan, Miss. That is Switzerland." Later, a pupil whose uncle drives trucks to Karachi port says, "My uncle says our country is important because ships and trucks from China pass through it. Is that true?"

Zainab sees two things. Her pupils know their own landscape very well, but they think the whole country looks like Thar. And one pupil has noticed something real about Pakistan's position in the world without knowing why it matters. Zainab needs to widen their picture of the land, and to show why Pakistan's location makes it important.

## Explanation

### Physical features: mountains, plains, deserts and the Indus system

Geographers describe a country's **relief**: the shape and height of its land. Pakistan's relief is among the most varied in the world. Its land rises from sea level on the Arabian Sea coast to more than 8,000 metres in the north. Five physical regions are usually described (Rabbani, n.d.).

**1. The northern and western mountains.** Three great ranges meet in the far north: the Himalaya, the Karakoram and the Hindu Kush. They contain some of the highest mountains on Earth. K2, in the Karakoram, is the second highest mountain in the world, and Nanga Parbat stands at the western end of the Himalaya. These mountains hold large glaciers, which feed the rivers in summer. Further south, lower ranges run along the western side of the country: the Safed Koh, the Sulaiman Range and the Kirthar Range, which forms the boundary between Sindh and Balochistan. Passes through these ranges, such as the Khyber Pass, have been routes for trade and invasion for thousands of years.

**2. The plateaus.** The Potohar Plateau, around Rawalpindi and Chakwal, is a high, broken upland between the mountains and the plain. It holds Pakistan's main oil fields and the salt range, with the Khewra salt mine. The Balochistan Plateau, in the south-west, is a large, dry upland of ranges and basins. It covers a large share of the country's area but holds only a small share of its people.

**3. The Indus Plain.** The <Glossary term="Indus River System" /> is the heart of Pakistan. The Indus rises in Tibet, flows through Gilgit-Baltistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and runs the length of the country to the Arabian Sea. In the Punjab it is joined by its eastern tributaries, the Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi and Sutlej. Over thousands of years these rivers have laid down rich soil, forming the Indus Plain. The **upper Indus Plain** in the Punjab is crossed by five rivers and the areas between them, called doabs. The **lower Indus Plain** in Sindh is flatter, and the river ends in a wide delta south of Thatta. Most of Pakistan's people, cities and farms are on this plain.

**4. The deserts.** Pakistan has four main deserts: the Thal in the Punjab between the Indus and the Jhelum; the Cholistan in southern Punjab, near Bahawalpur; the Thar in south-eastern Sindh; and the Kharan in Balochistan. The Thar is a sandy desert with dunes, but it is not empty: it supports villages, livestock and farming in good rain years.

**5. The coastal belt.** Pakistan's coastline on the Arabian Sea runs for about a thousand kilometres from the Indus Delta near Karachi to the Iranian border. The western part, the Makran coast in Balochistan, is dry and rocky. The eastern part includes the creeks and mangrove forests of the delta. The ports of Karachi, Port Qasim and Gwadar are on this coast.

**Where people live.** The physical regions explain the map of population. Most people live on the Indus Plain, where water, soil and flat land support farming, roads and cities: Lahore, Faisalabad, Multan, Hyderabad and, at the edge of the delta, Karachi. The mountains and the Balochistan Plateau are thinly populated, because steep or dry land supports fewer farms. This uneven spread of people matters for government: it is why the Punjab has the most seats in the National Assembly and why Balochistan, with the largest area, depends on the Senate's equal provincial seats for an equal voice (Topic 4.1).

**Correcting a common misconception: "Pakistan's geography is only desert."** Pupils in Tharparkar or Cholistan meet desert every day, and textbook photographs of camels add to the idea. Deserts are an important part of Pakistan, but they cover only part of it. Look again at the map of physical regions at the start of this topic: the same country holds glaciers and some of the world's highest mountains, forested valleys in Swat and Murree, the irrigated Indus Plain, which is one of the largest farming regions in South Asia, plateaus, and a long sea coast with mangroves. The accurate statement is: *Pakistan has deserts, but it is a country of mountains, plateaus, river plains, deserts and coast, all linked by the Indus.*

**Worked example: tracing one drop of water.** One way to see how the regions connect is to follow the water.

1. **Mountains.** Snow falls on the Karakoram. In summer, a glacier melts and feeds a stream that joins the Indus near Skardu.
2. **Storage.** The river flows south and is held behind the Tarbela Dam in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, which stores water and produces electricity.
3. **Plain.** Released from the dam, the water flows to barrages such as Sukkur, which divert it into canals.
4. **Farm.** A canal carries it to a wheat field in Khairpur.
5. **Sea.** What remains reaches the delta, where fresh water keeps the mangroves and the fisheries alive.

One drop has crossed four of the five physical regions. This is why geographers describe Pakistan as a country built around one river system.

### Climate, water and natural resources

**Climate.** <Glossary term="Climate" /> is the usual pattern of weather in a place over many years. Pakistan's climate is mostly dry, but it changes greatly from place to place and season to season.

- **Temperature.** Summers on the plains are very hot. Jacobabad in Sindh is one of the hottest places in Asia. The northern mountains have long, freezing winters. The coast is milder, because the sea keeps temperatures more even.
- **Rainfall.** Most of the country receives little rain. The main rainy season is the summer **monsoon**, from about July to September, when moist winds from the Indian Ocean bring rain, mostly to the north-east (Punjab, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Kashmir). In winter, weather systems from the west bring rain and snow to the north and west. Sindh and Balochistan receive the least rain.
- **Four seasons.** On the plains, people experience a cold season (about December to February), a hot dry season (March to June), the monsoon (July to September) and a short post-monsoon season.

**Worked example: comparing four places.** A comparison of four towns shows how relief, distance from the sea and the monsoon shape climate. The descriptions are general patterns, not exact measurements.

| Place | Region | Summer | Winter | Main source of rain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jacobabad | Lower Indus Plain, Sindh | Extremely hot and dry | Mild | Little rain; occasional monsoon showers |
| Karachi | Coast | Hot and humid, eased by sea breezes | Mild | Little rain; some monsoon rain in some years |
| Murree | Foothills of the Himalaya | Cool and wet | Cold, with snow | Heavy monsoon rain; winter snow |
| Quetta | Balochistan Plateau | Warm and dry | Cold, often below freezing | Light winter rain and snow from the west |

Read the table as a geographer would. Height makes Murree and Quetta cooler than Jacobabad. The sea keeps Karachi's temperatures more even than Jacobabad's, though both are on low ground in Sindh. And the monsoon brings the most rain to the north-east, which is why Murree is so much wetter than Quetta. A pupil who can explain one row has understood the three main controls of Pakistan's climate: height, distance from the sea, and the monsoon.

**Climate is changing.** In 2022 extreme monsoon rainfall caused the worst flooding in Pakistan's history. Otto and colleagues (2023) found that intense rainfall over Pakistan has become heavier as the country has warmed, though they could not measure exactly how much of the change was caused by human activity. They also showed that the damage was made worse by people living close to rivers, weak infrastructure, poverty and limited preparation. The lesson is that a natural hazard becomes a disaster through human and governance factors as well as climate.

**Water.** The Indus basin supplies water to the largest connected irrigation system in the world. Qureshi (2011) estimates that it provides water for 90% of Pakistan's food production. Water flows from the rivers through dams, barrages and thousands of kilometres of canals to the fields. Under the Indus Waters Treaty of 1960 with India, Pakistan received the waters of the western rivers (the Indus, Jhelum and Chenab), and India the eastern rivers (the Ravi, Beas and Sutlej). Pakistan then built large works, including the Mangla and Tarbela dams, to move water to where it was needed.

The system faces serious pressure. Qureshi (2011) shows that Pakistan can store only a small amount of water per person compared with countries such as the United States and China, that reservoirs are losing capacity as they fill with silt, and that poor drainage has brought waterlogging and salt to farmland. Groundwater, pumped by tube wells, is being used faster than it is replaced in many areas.

**Natural resources.** <Glossary term="Natural resources" /> are anything from nature that people use: soil, water, minerals, forests, fish and sources of energy.

<Figure id="fig-U4-7" src="/img/figures/gpks-402/unit-04/fig-U4-7.svg" alt="Table of Pakistan's natural resources in four sectors (agriculture, water, minerals and energy), showing the main resources in each, where they are found, such as Thar coal, Sui gas and Reko Diq copper and gold in Balochistan, and why they matter for food, industry, jobs and energy." />

Three points about resources are worth teaching clearly:

1. **Land and water are the most important resources.** Agriculture on the Indus Plain feeds the country and supplies cotton for the textile industry, Pakistan's largest manufacturing sector.
2. **Many mineral and energy resources are in less developed areas.** Natural gas was discovered at Sui in Balochistan in 1952; the coal of Thar lies under one of Sindh's poorest districts; and the copper and gold of Reko Diq and Saindak are in western Balochistan. Who benefits from these resources, the area where they are found or the whole country, is a question for the federal system (Topic 4.1). The Constitution addresses it directly: Article 161 pays the federal excise duty and royalty on natural gas to the province where the gas well is situated, and the net profits of a hydro-electric station to the province where it stands (Government of Pakistan, 1973/2012).
3. **Some resources are under strain.** Forests cover only a small part of the country and have been reduced by cutting. Groundwater is falling in many areas. Using resources carefully is part of good citizenship (Topic 4.2).

**Worked example: who benefits from Thar coal?** The coal under Tharparkar is the kind of case Zainab's own pupils live beside. Analyse it with three questions.

1. **What is the resource, and where?** A very large deposit of lignite, a low-grade coal, lies under the Thar Desert in Sindh. Since the 2010s it has been mined to fuel power stations that feed electricity into the national grid.
2. **Who gains, and who pays?** The country gains electricity from a resource of its own instead of imported fuel. Sindh gains investment and royalties. Local people may gain jobs, roads and water schemes, but some villages have had to move, and mining and power stations raise concerns about water, air and grazing land in a fragile desert.
3. **Who decides?** Minerals other than nuclear materials, oil and natural gas are a provincial subject, so the Government of Sindh has a central role. Electricity crosses provincial lines through the national grid, so federal bodies are involved too. Local councils and citizens can press for fair compensation and safeguards.

The pattern is common across Pakistan: a resource lies in a poor and remote area, the benefits flow mostly to the wider country, and governance decides whether local people share in them. Pupils in Mithi can study the national question by starting from their own district.

### Strategic importance of Pakistan's location

**What strategic importance means.** A place has <Glossary term="Strategic Importance" /> when its location makes it important to other countries for trade, transport, energy or security. A country's strategic importance can bring opportunities, such as trade and investment, and also pressures, such as conflict on its borders.

**Pakistan's neighbours.** Pakistan sits where South Asia, Central Asia, West Asia and China meet.

- **China** lies to the north-east, across the Karakoram. The Karakoram Highway crosses the Khunjerab Pass to link the two countries by road.
- **Afghanistan** lies to the west and north-west, along a long border. Through Afghanistan, Pakistan is the nearest sea outlet for the landlocked countries of Central Asia.
- **Iran** lies to the south-west, linking Pakistan to West Asia.
- **India** lies to the east.
- A narrow strip of Afghan territory, the Wakhan Corridor, separates Pakistan from Tajikistan in the far north.
- To the south is the **Arabian Sea**, close to the entrance of the Persian Gulf, through which a large share of the world's oil is shipped.

**Strategic importance in history.** Pakistan's position mattered long before the country existed. Routes of the old Silk Road crossed its northern mountains, and armies and traders entered South Asia through the passes of the north-west. After 1947, the same position shaped Pakistan's foreign relations. Pakistan joined Western-sponsored defence alliances in the 1950s, and after the Soviet military intervention in Afghanistan in December 1979 it became a front-line state and renewed its ties with the United States (Rizvi, 2004). After 2001, Pakistan was again at the centre of international attention because of the war in Afghanistan. In each period, location brought both support from outside powers and serious costs at home.

**Why the location matters: four reasons.**

1. **Trade and transit.** Pakistan's ports are the shortest sea route for western China and for Central Asia. The <Glossary term="China-Pakistan Economic Corridor" /> (CPEC), part of China's Belt and Road Initiative, is a programme of roads, railways, energy projects and the port of Gwadar to link western China with the Arabian Sea through Pakistan. Wolf (2017) describes it as a project hailed as a "game changer" for Pakistan's economy.
2. **Energy routes.** Pakistan lies between energy-rich West and Central Asia and energy-hungry South Asia and China, which makes it a possible route for pipelines.
3. **Security.** Pakistan's long borders have placed it at the centre of regional conflicts, including tension with India and decades of war in Afghanistan. This has brought international attention and aid, and also refugees, insecurity and high defence spending.
4. **A bridge between regions and the Muslim world.** Pakistan's location and history link it to South Asia, Central Asia, West Asia and the Muslim world, a role reflected in Article 40 of the Constitution.

**Worked example: geography shapes governance.** This unit began with government and ends with the land. The two are linked. Take the question the truck driver's nephew raised in Zainab's class, and analyse it in four steps.

1. **The geographic fact.** Pakistan lies between western China and the Arabian Sea, so it offers China a short route to the sea (CPEC).
2. **The opportunity.** New roads, power plants and a port can bring investment and jobs, including in Balochistan.
3. **The governance challenge.** Many CPEC routes pass through remote and insecure areas. Wolf (2017) argues that the need to guarantee security for CPEC has expanded the military's role, which affects civil-military relations (Topic 4.1). Decisions about where routes go and who benefits also raise federal questions between the centre and the provinces, especially Balochistan.
4. **The judgement.** Location is an advantage only when governance turns it into one: secure routes, fair sharing of benefits, and civilian institutions able to manage large projects.

The same method works for water. The Indus flows through several provinces, so sharing its water is a federal question, which is why the Constitution gives the Council of Common Interests a role in water disputes (Article 155). Rizvi (2004) shows that a sense of insecurity on Pakistan's borders helped enlarge the military's role in politics. Geography does not decide a country's future, but it sets many of the problems its government must solve.

**Returning to Zainab.** Zainab can now show her class the whole country: the mountains and glaciers of the north, the plateaus, the great Indus Plain, the deserts including their own Thar, and the sea coast. She can trace a drop of water from K2 to the delta. And she can tell the boy that his uncle is right: Pakistan's position between China, Central Asia and the Arabian Sea makes it strategically important, but turning that importance into prosperity depends on how well the country is governed.

## Activity: Map, resource and problem

Work in groups of four, each member taking one role: mapper, resource expert, climate expert and reporter. About 35 minutes.

1. On a blank outline map of Pakistan (or one drawn on chart paper), the **mapper** marks the five physical regions, the Indus and its four main tributaries, and the four neighbouring countries.
2. The **resource expert** adds at least six resources from the table of natural resources in the right places, using simple symbols with a key.
3. The **climate expert** shades or labels the areas of highest and lowest rainfall, marks where the monsoon brings most rain, and marks one place affected by the 2022 floods.
4. Together, choose one place on your map where geography creates a problem for government (for example, water sharing between provinces, a resource in a poor district, floods, or a border route). Write three sentences: the geographic fact, the problem it creates, and which institution from Topic 4.1 should deal with it.
5. The **reporter** presents the map and the three sentences to another group, which must ask one question about the evidence.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the five physical regions of Pakistan and give one feature of each.
2. *(Understand)* A pupil says, "Pakistan is just a desert." Write the two or three sentences you would say in reply, with examples from at least three regions.
3. *(Apply)* Using a map, explain to a Class 7 pupil in Gwadar why Gwadar port matters to China and to Central Asia.

## Summary

Pakistan's land rises from the Arabian Sea coast to the high mountains of the north, and it has five main regions: the northern and western mountains, the Potohar and Balochistan plateaus, the Indus Plain, the deserts (Thal, Cholistan, Thar and Kharan) and the coastal belt. The Indus River system links them all and supports the largest connected irrigation system in the world. The climate is mostly dry, with hot summers on the plains, cold winters in the north and most rain from the summer monsoon; the 2022 floods showed how climate and governance together turn hazards into disasters. Pakistan's resources include fertile land, water, minerals and energy, many of them in less developed areas. Its location between China, Central Asia, West Asia, India and the Arabian Sea gives it great strategic importance, which brings both opportunities, such as CPEC, and governance challenges, such as security and the fair sharing of benefits.

## Summative task

Write an analysis of 300-350 words in answer to this question:

*"Pakistan's geography is both its greatest asset and its greatest challenge." Analyse this statement using one physical feature and one aspect of Pakistan's location.*

In your answer: (a) describe the physical feature and the aspect of location accurately, with places; (b) analyse one opportunity and one challenge that each creates; (c) explain which level or branch of government must deal with one of the challenges, and why; and (d) reach a justified judgement on the statement.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | 3 - strong | 2 - adequate | 1 - weak |
|---|---|---|---|
| Geographic knowledge | Accurate description with named places, rivers or neighbours | Mostly accurate, few named places | Vague or inaccurate |
| Analysis of opportunity and challenge (**Analyze**) | Explains *how* each feature creates an opportunity and a challenge, with evidence (for example Qureshi, 2011, or Otto et al., 2023) | Names opportunities and challenges without explaining the link | Lists features only |
| Link to governance | Names the right institution or level and explains why, using Topic 4.1 | Names an institution without reasons | No link to government |
| Judgement (**Evaluate**) | A clear, balanced judgement that follows from the analysis | A judgement only partly supported | No judgement |
