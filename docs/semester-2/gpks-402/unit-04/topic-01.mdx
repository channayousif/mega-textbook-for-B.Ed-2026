## A real classroom situation

Bilal is a B.Ed trainee placed at a government boys' high school in Sukkur. In a Class 10 Pakistan Studies period, he asks the class who makes the laws in Pakistan. Hands go up at once. "The Prime Minister, sir." "No, the President." "The Chief Minister makes laws for Sindh." A boy at the back says, "It depends who is in power. Sometimes it is the army." Another pupil adds, "My uncle says Pakistan has always been run by the army anyway."

Bilal sees that the class has many pieces of the answer, but no picture that joins them. Some pupils mix up who makes laws, who runs the government and who judges disputes. Some are unsure whether Sindh has its own government or simply follows Islamabad. And the comment about the army is half right and half wrong, but Bilal is not sure how to explain it without either avoiding the subject or turning the lesson into an argument.

By the end of this topic you will be able to give Bilal's class one clear picture of how Pakistan is governed, and a fair, evidence-based answer about the army.

## Explanation

### The federal system: centre and provinces

**What a federation is.** <Glossary term="Federalism" /> is a system of government in which power is divided by the constitution between a central government and the governments of regions, such as provinces. Each level has its own powers, its own elected bodies and its own officials. Neither level can simply abolish the other. The alternative is a unitary system, in which all power belongs to the centre and regional offices only carry out its orders.

Article 1 of the Constitution states that Pakistan "shall be Federal Republic" (Government of Pakistan, 1973/2012). The federation is made up of four provinces (Balochistan, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, the Punjab and Sindh) and the Islamabad Capital Territory. The former Federally Administered Tribal Areas were merged into Khyber Pakhtunkhwa in 2018. Gilgit-Baltistan and Azad Jammu and Kashmir have their own administrative arrangements and are not provinces under Article 1.

**Why Pakistan is federal.** Pakistan is a large and diverse country. Its provinces differ in language, history, size, population and economy. A federal system lets each province manage matters close to its people, such as schools, health and the police, while the centre manages matters that concern the whole country, such as defence, foreign affairs, currency and national highways. This idea goes back to the Pakistan Movement itself: the Lahore Resolution of 1940 asked for Muslim-majority regions whose units would govern themselves (see Unit 2).

**How the Constitution divides power.** The Constitution lists subjects in the Federal Legislative List in its Fourth Schedule. Under Article 142, Parliament has exclusive power to make laws on any matter in that list, and a Provincial Assembly has power to make laws on any matter *not* in it (Government of Pakistan, 1973/2012). In other words, the provinces hold the remaining, or residual, powers.

This changed in an important way in 2010. Until then the Constitution also had a Concurrent List of subjects on which both levels could make laws, and federal law usually won. The Eighteenth Amendment of 2010 abolished the Concurrent List, so most of those subjects, including education, passed to the provinces. Hussain and Kokab (2012) describe this as the amendment's main step towards provincial autonomy, and they also record the debate it caused: some experts feared the federation would lose the power to set national standards, while some provincial parties said the change still did not go far enough.

**Worked example: who is responsible for the school curriculum?** Ask the question the way Bilal's pupils might: "Does Islamabad decide what Sindh's children learn?"

1. Look for the subject in the Federal Legislative List. School education is not a federal subject there.
2. Before 2010, education appeared in the Concurrent List, so both the centre and the provinces could legislate on it.
3. The Eighteenth Amendment abolished the Concurrent List.
4. So, under Article 142, school education is now mainly a provincial subject. Sindh's curriculum and textbooks are a matter for the Government of Sindh and the Provincial Assembly of Sindh.

The method is the point: find the subject, check which list it is in, and apply Article 142. Pupils can use the same method for the police, health or highways.

**Institutions that hold the federation together.** Dividing power creates disputes, so the Constitution provides forums for the centre and the provinces to settle them together.

- The **Council of Common Interests** (Article 153) is chaired by the Prime Minister and includes the four Chief Ministers. It makes policy on shared matters, such as railways, electricity and major water questions.
- The **National Finance Commission** (Article 160) recommends how the money collected through federal taxes is shared between the federation and the provinces.
- Article 155 lets a province complain if its water supply from a natural source is harmed by another government's action. Water is a federal question in a very real sense, as Topic 4.3 shows.

Each province also has its own government, built on the same pattern as the centre: an elected **Provincial Assembly**; a **Chief Minister** and provincial cabinet who govern with the Assembly's confidence (Article 130); a **Governor** who is the formal head of the province; and a **High Court**.

**The third tier: local government.** Below the provinces sits local government: elected councils for districts, towns, union councils and cities. Article 140A, added in 2010, requires each province, by law, to establish a local government system and to devolve "political, administrative and financial responsibility and authority" to its elected representatives (Government of Pakistan, 1973/2012). Elections to local governments are held by the Election Commission of Pakistan. The principles of policy also ask the State to encourage elected local institutions with special representation for peasants, workers and women (Article 32; see Topic 4.2).

Local government matters to a teacher because it is the level closest to the school. Street lights outside the school, the drain that floods the playground and the water supply in the village are usually the work of the local council, not of Islamabad. In practice, local elections have often been delayed, and local councils have been suspended for long periods. This is itself a lesson in governance: a constitutional promise needs provincial laws, elections and active citizens before it works.

### The legislature: National Assembly and Senate

The <Glossary term="Legislature" /> is the branch of government that makes laws. Pakistan's federal legislature is called the Majlis-e-Shoora (Parliament). Under Article 50 it consists of the President and two Houses: the National Assembly and the Senate (Government of Pakistan, 1973/2012). A legislature with two Houses is called bicameral.

**The National Assembly.** Its members are elected directly by the people. Every citizen aged eighteen or over whose name is on the electoral roll may vote (Article 51). The number of seats depends on population, so the Punjab, the most populous province, has the most seats. Some seats are reserved for women and for non-Muslims, so that both groups are represented. The Assembly sits for five years unless dissolved earlier (Article 52). Its most important powers are:

- **Making laws.** A bill becomes a law when both Houses pass it and the President assents.
- **Controlling money.** A Money Bill, including the annual budget, must begin in the National Assembly. The Senate may only make recommendations on it (Article 73).
- **Choosing and removing the government.** The National Assembly elects the Prime Minister, and it can remove the Prime Minister through a vote of no-confidence.

**The Senate.** The Senate is the House of the federating units. Each province elects the same number of senators through its Provincial Assembly, whatever its population (Article 59). This balances the National Assembly: a province with fewer people, such as Balochistan, has an equal voice with the Punjab in the Senate. The Senate cannot be dissolved. Senators serve six years, and half of them retire every three years, so the House always holds experienced members.

**Worked example: how a bill becomes a law.** Follow an imagined bill to set a national standard for the safety of school buses, a subject assumed here to be federal.

1. **Drafting.** A ministry prepares the bill and the Cabinet approves it. (A private member of either House may also introduce a bill.)
2. **First House.** A bill on a Federal Legislative List subject may begin in either House (Article 70). Suppose it begins in the National Assembly. It is read, sent to a standing committee for detailed study, debated, and passed by a majority of members present and voting.
3. **Second House.** The bill goes to the Senate, which may pass it, amend it or reject it. If the two Houses cannot agree, the bill may be considered at a joint sitting of both Houses.
4. **Assent.** The bill is presented to the President, who must assent within ten days or, for a bill other than a Money Bill, return it to Parliament for reconsideration. If Parliament passes it again in a joint sitting, the President must assent within ten days, and if he does not, assent is deemed to have been given (Article 75).
5. **Law.** The bill becomes an Act of Parliament. It is published and carried out by the executive, and its validity can be tested in the courts.

Notice how many people share in the process: ministers, committee members, two Houses and the Head of State. No single office makes law alone.

**Correcting a common confusion: "The Prime Minister makes the laws."** The Prime Minister leads the government and usually proposes most bills, but the law is made only when Parliament passes it. A Prime Minister who cannot win a majority in Parliament cannot make a law, however powerful the office seems.

### The executive: Prime Minister, President and the Cabinet

The <Glossary term="Executive" /> is the branch that carries out the laws and runs the government day to day: ministries, the civil service, the police and public services.

**The Prime Minister and the Cabinet.** Under Article 90, the executive authority of the Federation is exercised in the name of the President by the Federal Government, which consists of the Prime Minister and the Federal Ministers. Article 91 says there shall be a Cabinet of Ministers "with the Prime Minister at its head" (Government of Pakistan, 1973/2012). After an election, the National Assembly elects one of its members as Prime Minister by the votes of a majority of its total membership. The Prime Minister then chooses ministers and leads the Cabinet. The Cabinet is collectively answerable to the National Assembly. This is what makes Pakistan a **parliamentary** system: the government comes from Parliament and stays in office only while it keeps Parliament's confidence.

**The President.** Article 41 makes the President the Head of State, who "shall represent the unity of the Republic". The President is elected by an electoral college of both Houses of Parliament and all four Provincial Assemblies. In most matters the President acts on the advice of the Cabinet or the Prime Minister (Article 48).

The balance between the two offices has changed over time. Unit 3 showed that the Eighth Amendment of 1985 gave the President power under Article 58(2)(b) to dissolve the National Assembly on his own judgement. Presidents used that power to dismiss elected governments in 1988, 1990, 1993 and 1996. The Eighteenth Amendment of 2010 removed it. Hussain and Kokab (2012) explain that after 2010 the President must follow the Prime Minister's advice to dissolve the Assembly, which returned the 1973 Constitution to its parliamentary form.

**Worked example: comparing two systems.** A useful way to fix the difference is a side-by-side comparison.

| Question | Parliamentary system (Pakistan under the 1973 Constitution) | Presidential system (Pakistan under the 1962 Constitution) |
|---|---|---|
| Who leads the government? | The Prime Minister | The President |
| Where does the government come from? | Elected from and by the National Assembly | Elected separately from the legislature |
| Who can remove the head of government? | The National Assembly, by a vote of no-confidence | Only by impeachment, which is very difficult |
| Role of the Head of State | Mostly formal; acts on advice | Holds real executive power |

The comparison shows why the same title, "President", can mean very different things in different constitutions.

### The judiciary: Supreme Court, High Courts and the judicial system

The <Glossary term="Judiciary" /> is the branch that interprets the law and settles disputes, including disputes between citizens and the government.

**The structure of the courts.** Article 175 establishes a Supreme Court of Pakistan, a High Court for each province and for the Islamabad Capital Territory, and such other courts as the law creates (Government of Pakistan, 1973/2012). Below the High Courts are the district and sessions courts and the civil and magistrates' courts that most people deal with. A Federal Shariat Court examines whether laws are in line with the Injunctions of Islam (see Unit 3).

| Court | Main work |
|---|---|
| Supreme Court | Highest court of appeal. Can hear cases of public importance on fundamental rights directly (Article 184(3)). Its decisions on law bind all other courts (Article 189). |
| High Courts | Hear appeals from lower courts. Can issue writs to government officials who act outside the law (Article 199). |
| District and subordinate courts | Hear most civil and criminal cases in the first instance. |

**Independence of the judiciary.** Judges must be free to decide cases against the government when the law requires it. Article 175(3) required the judiciary to be separated from the executive. Since 2010, judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts have been appointed through a Judicial Commission led by the Chief Justice (Article 175A), with a parliamentary committee also involved. Hussain and Kokab (2012) note that this new appointment process was one of the most debated parts of the Eighteenth Amendment.

**Separation of powers and checks and balances.** We now have all three branches. The <Glossary term="Separation of Powers" /> is the principle that the legislature, the executive and the judiciary should be separate, so that no single person or body holds all power. In Pakistan's parliamentary system the separation is not complete: the Prime Minister and ministers sit in Parliament. What matters most is that each branch can check the others:

- Parliament checks the executive: it passes or rejects the budget, questions ministers, and can remove the Prime Minister.
- The executive checks Parliament: the Prime Minister can advise the President to dissolve the National Assembly and call an election.
- The courts check both: under Article 8 a law that takes away a fundamental right is void, and the courts can declare such a law, or an unlawful government action, to have no effect.
- Parliament and the executive check the courts: Parliament makes the laws the courts apply, can amend the Constitution by a two-thirds majority, and takes part in appointing judges.

**Worked example: who deals with the problem?** Bilal can give his class short cases and ask which branch acts.

1. *A new tax on school bags is proposed.* A tax needs a law, and a Money Bill must start in the National Assembly. **Legislature.**
2. *Textbooks have not reached schools in Khairpur by May.* Delivering textbooks is running a public service, and education is a provincial subject. **Provincial executive** (the Sindh education department, answerable to the Provincial Assembly).
3. *A family says a new rule bars their daughter from school because of her religion.* That is a claim that a fundamental right (Article 22 or 25) has been violated. **Judiciary** (a High Court can hear a writ petition).

### Civil-military relations: patterns and consequences

**What the term means.** <Glossary term="Civil-military relations" /> means the relationship between a country's elected civilian government and its armed forces: who controls defence policy, who decides on war and peace, and how far the military takes part in politics. Every country with an army has civil-military relations. The question is what form they take.

The Constitution sets out the intended form. Article 245 says the Armed Forces shall, "under the directions of the Federal Government", defend Pakistan against external aggression and act in aid of civil power when called upon (Government of Pakistan, 1973/2012). This is the principle of **civilian supremacy**: elected leaders decide policy, and the military carries it out. Article 6 makes it high treason to abrogate or suspend the Constitution by force. Article 39 asks the State to let people from all parts of Pakistan join the Armed Forces, so that the military belongs to the whole nation.

**The pattern in practice.** Hasan Askari Rizvi (2004) traces how the military's role grew from this starting point. At independence the military inherited the British tradition of civilian supremacy and aloofness from politics. Its role then expanded in stages:

1. **1947-1958.** The military became an important voice in defence and security decisions while weak civilian governments changed often.
2. **1958-1969.** General Ayub Khan took power in October 1958 and ruled under <Glossary term="Martial law" /> until June 1962, when he introduced the presidential Constitution of 1962. He resigned in March 1969 after mass protests.
3. **1969-1971.** General Yahya Khan abolished the 1962 Constitution and ruled under martial law until December 1971, when he handed power to Zulfikar Ali Bhutto after the separation of East Pakistan.
4. **1971-1977.** A civilian government made the 1973 Constitution. Bhutto asserted civilian control for a time.
5. **1977-1988.** General Zia ul Haq took power in July 1977 and ruled under martial law until 1985, followed by a controlled civilian system until his death in August 1988.
6. **1988-1999.** Elected governments returned, but none completed its term. Presidents dismissed them, and Rizvi records that the military continued to watch and comment on their performance.
7. **1999-2008.** General Pervez Musharraf took power in October 1999 and ruled until 2008 (Taj, Shah & Ahmad, 2016).
8. **Since 2008.** Elected governments have ruled. In 2013, for the first time, an elected government completed its full term and handed power to another elected government. Debate about the military's influence on politics has continued.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Pakistan has always been governed by the military."** Count the years on the timeline. Taj, Shah and Ahmad (2016) put the total period of military domination at about 32 years: 1958-1971, 1977-1988 and 1999-2008. That is a very large part of Pakistan's history, and it should be taught honestly. But it is not the whole history. For most of the years since independence, and for every year since 2008, Pakistan has been governed by civilian governments under a constitution. Elections, parliaments, courts and a free press have all shaped the country. The accurate statement is this: *Pakistan has had long periods of military rule, and the military has often had strong influence even under civilian governments, but Pakistan has not always been governed by the military.*

**Why intervention happened: the causes.** Rizvi (2004) identifies several causes, which work together rather than alone:

- **Weak political institutions.** Quaid-e-Azam died in 1948 and Liaquat Ali Khan was assassinated in 1951, before strong democratic traditions could take root. The Muslim League did not turn itself from a national movement into a stable national party, and other parties suffered from similar division.
- **A sense of insecurity.** Tension with India from the first years of independence, and later the war in Afghanistan, made defence the government's first priority. This gave the military a large share of the budget and a central voice in policy.
- **The gap in strength.** As a result, a disciplined, well-organised military faced weak and divided civilian institutions. Rizvi notes that when the military took power in 1958, 1969 and 1977 it met little opposition, and some groups welcomed it.
- **Interests of the institution.** Rizvi also lists the military's own concerns: the size of the defence budget, security and foreign policy, and its autonomy in its own affairs.

**What intervention led to: the consequences.** Military rule had real effects that pupils can trace in the units of this course:

- **Constitutional breaks.** The 1956 Constitution was abrogated in 1958 and the 1962 Constitution in 1969. The 1973 Constitution was suspended or held in abeyance in 1977 and 1999 and amended by military rulers.
- **Weaker political institutions.** Parties and parliaments had less time to learn how to govern, which in turn made future intervention easier. This is a cycle.
- **National unity.** Long periods without elected national government added to the grievances in East Pakistan before 1971 (see Unit 3).
- **Stability and development.** Supporters of military governments point to periods of economic growth and order, especially in the 1960s. Critics reply that growth without participation did not last and did not build institutions.

**Correcting a second misconception: "Civil-military relations are only a problem, never a subject of study."** Some trainees think the topic should be avoided in school, or that it can only be discussed as complaint. In fact civil-military relations is a recognised field of political science, studied in every country that has armed forces. It asks questions that can be answered with evidence: What does the Constitution say? What happened, and when? What caused it, and with what results? How have other countries built stable relations between elected leaders and their armed forces? Studying the subject in this way respects both the soldiers who defend the country and the citizens who elect its governments. It also helps pupils understand why the Constitution insists on civilian direction of the Armed Forces.

**Worked example: analysing a cause with evidence.** A pupil asks, "Why did the army take over in 1958?" A weak answer is a single cause: "Because the generals wanted power." A strong answer follows three steps.

1. **State the event accurately.** In October 1958 the 1956 Constitution was abrogated and General Ayub Khan took power under martial law.
2. **Name more than one cause, each with evidence.** Political institutions were weak: there had been frequent changes of government since 1951, and no national election had been held under the new Constitution. Security fears had given the military a large role in policy. The military was organised and united; the parties were not.
3. **Weigh the causes.** Rizvi's (2004) evidence suggests that the weakness of civilian institutions was what made the takeover possible, while security concerns had already enlarged the military's role. Neither cause alone explains the event.

**Teaching the topic in school.** Civil-military relations can be taught calmly if the teacher follows three rules. First, keep to evidence: dates, constitutional articles and the views of named historians, not rumour or party slogans. Second, separate the institution from its political role: pupils can respect the soldiers who defend the borders, help after floods and serve in United Nations peacekeeping missions, and still ask whether military rule was good for the country's institutions. Third, let pupils weigh more than one view. A good question for Class 10 is not "Was military rule bad?" but "What reasons have historians given for military rule in 1958, and how strong is the evidence for each?"

**Returning to Bilal.** Bilal can now draw the three columns on the board: Parliament makes laws, the Prime Minister and Cabinet carry them out, and the courts interpret them, with Sindh's own Assembly, Chief Minister and High Court beneath. He can show that education is a provincial subject. And he can answer the boy at the back fairly: the army has ruled Pakistan directly for about 32 years, which matters a great deal, but for most of its history Pakistan has been governed by civilians under a constitution, and the Constitution itself says the Armed Forces act under the elected government's direction.

## Activity: Which branch, which level?

Work in groups of four. About 30 minutes.

1. Each group receives (or writes on slips) these eight situations:
   - a. A road between Karachi and Hyderabad needs repair.
   - b. A pupil in Larkana is refused admission to a government school because her father is in prison.
   - c. Sindh and Punjab disagree over how much water from the Indus each should receive.
   - d. The government wants to raise the price of petrol through a new tax.
   - e. A provincial government wants to change the Class 9 Pakistan Studies textbook.
   - f. Pakistan is asked to send troops to a United Nations peacekeeping mission.
   - g. A citizen believes a new law takes away the freedom of assembly.
   - h. The National Assembly has lost confidence in the Prime Minister.
2. For each slip, decide together: (i) which branch acts (legislature, executive or judiciary); (ii) which level (federal or provincial); and (iii) which article or institution from this topic supports your decision.
3. Two members of your group then join another group and compare answers. Wherever you disagree, both groups must point to the evidence in the Constitution.
4. Return to your group and choose the one slip that caused the most argument. Prepare a one-minute explanation of how you settled it.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the two Houses of Parliament and state how the members of each are chosen. Then name the body, chaired by the Prime Minister, in which the centre and the provinces decide shared matters.
2. *(Understand)* Explain, in two or three sentences, why Pakistan's system is called "parliamentary" rather than "presidential", and give one example of a check that one branch holds over another.
3. *(Apply)* A pupil says, "Pakistan has always been ruled by the army." Using the timeline in this topic, write the three or four sentences you would say in reply.

## Summary

Pakistan is a federation: the Constitution divides power between the federal government and four provinces, and since the Eighteenth Amendment of 2010 the provinces hold most powers not on the Federal Legislative List, including school education. Bodies such as the Council of Common Interests and the National Finance Commission help the two levels work together. The legislature (National Assembly and Senate) makes laws and controls the government; the executive (Prime Minister and Cabinet, with the President as Head of State acting on advice) carries them out; and the judiciary interprets the law and protects rights. Each branch checks the others. The Constitution places the Armed Forces under the direction of the elected government, but Pakistan has experienced about 32 years of military rule. Its causes, chiefly weak civilian institutions and a strong sense of insecurity, and its consequences, including broken constitutions and weakened institutions, can be studied with evidence.

## Summative task

Write an analysis of 300-350 words in answer to this question:

*"The Eighteenth Amendment of 2010 strengthened both the provinces and Parliament." Analyse this statement.*

In your answer: (a) explain two changes the amendment made, one to federalism and one to the balance between the President and the Prime Minister; (b) analyse how each change was meant to prevent a problem from Pakistan's earlier history, including the history of civil-military relations; (c) describe one concern that critics raised about the amendment; and (d) reach a justified judgement on how far the statement is true.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | 3 - strong | 2 - adequate | 1 - weak |
|---|---|---|---|
| Knowledge of the changes | Two changes explained accurately with the relevant articles (for example 142, 58) | Two changes named, one explained | Changes vague or wrong |
| Analysis of purpose (**Analyze**) | Links each change to a specific earlier problem (for example dismissals of governments under Article 58(2)(b), or central control of provincial subjects) and explains the link | Links one change to an earlier problem | No link to history |
| Balance | States a real criticism and weighs it against the benefits | Mentions a criticism without weighing it | One-sided |
| Judgement (**Evaluate**) | A clear judgement that follows from the evidence given | A judgement that is only partly supported | No judgement, or one not supported |
