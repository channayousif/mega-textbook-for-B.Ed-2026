## A real classroom situation

Ayesha is a B.Ed trainee teaching Class 8 at a government girls' elementary school on the edge of Hyderabad. During a lesson on "our rights", a pupil named Rukhsana says that her younger sister, aged eleven, has stopped coming to school. The family needs her to work at home, and there is no school for older girls close by. Another pupil says, "But we watched an American film where the police said, 'You have the right to remain silent.' Are our rights the same as theirs?" A third pupil asks, "If we have rights, what do we have to do in return?"

Ayesha realises that the class is asking three serious questions. What rights does a child in Pakistan actually hold? What can be done when a right is not met? And what does a citizen owe in return? She also knows that Rukhsana's sister is not a textbook example. She is a real child, eleven years old, and out of school.

By the end of this topic you will be able to answer all three of the class's questions, and to say what can be done for Rukhsana's sister.

## Explanation

### Fundamental rights under the 1973 Constitution

**What a fundamental right is.** <Glossary term="Fundamental Rights" /> are basic rights that the Constitution guarantees to people and that the State may not take away by ordinary law. They are set out in Chapter 1 of Part II of the 1973 Constitution, Articles 8 to 28 (Government of Pakistan, 1973/2012). "The State" here has a wide meaning: Article 7 defines it to include the federal and provincial governments, Parliament, the Provincial Assemblies, and local authorities that can levy taxes. So a district education office is bound by the fundamental rights just as the federal Cabinet is.

**Why rights are "fundamental": Article 8.** Article 8 is the key that makes the other rights strong. It says that any law, custom or usage that is inconsistent with the fundamental rights is void to the extent of that inconsistency, and that the State shall not make any law that takes away or abridges them. Article 8(5) adds that the rights "shall not be suspended except as expressly provided by the Constitution". The only such provision is Article 233, which allows certain freedoms (movement, assembly, association, trade, speech and property) to be restricted while a Proclamation of Emergency is in force.

**The main groups of rights.** It helps to sort Articles 9 to 28 into groups.

1. **Life, liberty and justice.** Security of person (Article 9); safeguards on arrest, including the right to be told the grounds and to see a lawyer, and to be produced before a magistrate within twenty-four hours (Article 10); the right to a fair trial and due process (Article 10A); no slavery or forced labour, and no child below fourteen in a factory, mine or hazardous work (Article 11); no retrospective punishment (Article 12) and no double punishment (Article 13); the dignity of the person and privacy of the home, and no torture to obtain evidence (Article 14).
2. **Freedoms.** Movement (Article 15), peaceful assembly (Article 16), association, including forming political parties (Article 17), trade and profession (Article 18), speech and the press (Article 19), and access to information in matters of public importance (Article 19A).
3. **Religion.** Freedom to profess, practise and propagate one's religion and to manage religious institutions (Article 20); no tax for the benefit of another religion (Article 21); and, in educational institutions, no one shall be required to receive religious instruction in a religion other than their own (Article 22).
4. **Property.** Articles 23 and 24.
5. **Equality and non-discrimination.** All citizens are equal before law, and there shall be no discrimination on the basis of sex, though the State may make special provision for women and children (Article 25); non-discrimination in public places (Article 26) and in public service (Article 27).
6. **Education and culture.** The State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children aged five to sixteen (Article 25A); and any section of citizens with a distinct language, script or culture has the right to preserve it (Article 28).

Three of these rights were added by the Eighteenth Amendment of 2010: fair trial (10A), information (19A) and education (25A). Hussain and Kokab (2012) count these additions among the amendment's main achievements.

**Rights have limits set by law.** Most freedoms in Articles 15 to 19 are granted "subject to any reasonable restrictions imposed by law". Article 19, for example, allows restrictions on speech in the interest of the glory of Islam, the integrity, security or defence of Pakistan, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, or in relation to contempt of court. Note who sets the limit: it must be a *law*, and a court can examine whether the restriction is *reasonable*. An official's personal dislike of a speech is not a lawful restriction.

**Worked example: is a restriction reasonable?** Pupils often think a right is either absolute or worthless. A short case shows the middle ground that the Constitution actually draws.

*Case.* A group of college students in Larkana plans a peaceful march to the district office about a shortage of teachers. On the morning of the march the district administration bans it.

1. **Which right?** Freedom of assembly: every citizen has the right to assemble "peacefully and without arms" (Article 16).
2. **Is there a limit?** Yes. Article 16 allows reasonable restrictions imposed by law in the interest of public order.
3. **Was the limit imposed by law?** The administration must point to a law that gives it the power to ban a gathering, and it must use that power for the stated purpose. A ban with no legal basis fails at this step.
4. **Was it reasonable?** A court would ask questions such as: was there real evidence of a threat to public order, or did the officials simply dislike the message? Could a smaller step, such as changing the route or time, have met the concern? A ban that silences a peaceful protest because it is embarrassing is not a reasonable restriction; a temporary change of route because of a festival crowd on the same road may be.

The method, *right, limit, law, reasonableness*, works for speech, movement and association as well. It also teaches pupils that rights and public order are both protected, and that the balance between them is decided by law and courts, not by whoever holds power that day.

**Who holds the right?** Read each article carefully. Some rights belong to "every person", including foreigners in Pakistan: security of person, fair trial, freedom from forced labour. Others belong to "every citizen": movement, assembly, speech, information, equality. This matters in class: a refugee child in Karachi is protected by Article 9 and Article 11 even though she is not a citizen.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Fundamental rights are the same as in the US Constitution."** Pupils who watch foreign films and dramas often assume that rights are the same everywhere. There are real similarities: both constitutions protect life and liberty, speech, religion and a fair trial. But the differences are important and easy to show:

| Feature | United States Bill of Rights | Pakistan's 1973 Constitution |
|---|---|---|
| Form | Amendments added in 1791, written mostly as limits on government ("Congress shall make no law...") | A chapter of the original Constitution, written as rights held by persons and citizens |
| Limits on rights | Mostly developed later by the courts | Written into the text: "reasonable restrictions imposed by law", with the grounds listed |
| Religion | Separation of church and state | Islam is the State religion (Article 2), with freedom of religion for all (Article 20) and protection for minorities |
| Education | No right to education in the federal Constitution | A right to free and compulsory education for ages 5 to 16 (Article 25A) |
| Language and culture | Not listed | A right to preserve one's language, script and culture (Article 28) |
| Guiding goals for the State | None in the text | A separate chapter of Principles of Policy (Articles 29 to 40) |

So the honest answer to the pupil is: some rights look alike, but Pakistan's rights grew from its own history and values, and they must be read in their own text. The famous "right to remain silent" has a counterpart in Article 13, which protects a person accused of an offence from being compelled to be a witness against himself, but it is worded differently.

**Enforcing a right: the route to a remedy.** A right is only as strong as the remedy behind it. Pakistan's Constitution gives citizens a direct route to the courts.

- **Article 199 (High Courts).** Where no other adequate remedy is provided by law, a High Court may, on the application of an aggrieved party, order a person performing public functions to do what the law requires or to stop doing what the law does not permit. On the application of any aggrieved person, it may also give directions to any person or authority, including any government, for the enforcement of any fundamental right. Such an application is called a <Glossary term="Writ petition" />.
- **Article 184(3) (Supreme Court).** If a question of public importance about the enforcement of a fundamental right is involved, the Supreme Court can make such an order directly. Courts have used this power in cases affecting large numbers of people, such as public health and environmental cases.
- **Appeals.** A party unhappy with a High Court decision can appeal to the Supreme Court (Article 185).

Before going to court, a family will usually try the simpler routes: a written complaint to the office concerned, then to a senior officer, or to an ombudsman (Mohtasib), who deals with complaints of maladministration by government offices.

**Worked example: what can be done for Rukhsana's sister?** Apply the flowchart to the case in the opening situation.

1. **Which right?** The sister is eleven. Article 25A says the State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children aged five to sixteen "in such manner as may be determined by law". Sindh passed such a law in 2013, the Sindh Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act. Article 25 also forbids discrimination on the basis of sex.
2. **Who has the duty?** The State, which here means the Government of Sindh and its education department, because education is a provincial subject (Topic 4.1).
3. **What is the first step?** Talk to the family with respect. The family's needs are real; the head teacher and the school management committee can explain that the education is free and may help with practical problems.
4. **If the problem is that there is no school place nearby,** the head teacher or parents can write to the district education office asking for a school or a place for older girls, citing Article 25A and the provincial law.
5. **If nothing happens,** the matter can go to the provincial ombudsman, and in the last resort a High Court can be asked under Article 199 to direct the education department to perform its duty.

Ayesha's role as a teacher is not to file a court case. It is to know that Rukhsana's sister holds a constitutional right, to tell the class so accurately, and to help the school take the first, simple steps.

### Principles of policy and their relationship to fundamental rights

**What the principles are.** Chapter 2 of Part II, Articles 29 to 40, sets out the <Glossary term="Principles of Policy" />. These are goals that the State should work towards. Article 29 says it is the responsibility of each organ and authority of the State to act in accordance with them in so far as they relate to its functions (Government of Pakistan, 1973/2012). In respect of each year, the President and the Governors must lay a report before Parliament and the Provincial Assemblies on how the principles have been observed (Article 29(3)).

The principles include:

- the Islamic way of life, including, for Muslims, making the teaching of the Holy Quran and Islamiat compulsory (Article 31);
- promotion of elected local government institutions, with special representation for peasants, workers and women (Article 32);
- discouraging parochial, racial, tribal, sectarian and provincial prejudices (Article 33);
- full participation of women in national life (Article 34);
- protection of the family, the mother and the child (Article 35);
- safeguarding the rights and interests of minorities (Article 36);
- social justice, including removing illiteracy, providing free and compulsory secondary education "within minimum possible period", making higher education accessible on merit, and inexpensive and speedy justice (Article 37);
- the economic well-being of the people, including raising the standard of living and preventing the concentration of wealth in the hands of a few (Article 38);
- participation of people from all parts of Pakistan in the Armed Forces (Article 39);
- strong relations with the Muslim world and the promotion of international peace (Article 40).

**The key difference: enforcement.** Article 30(2) says that the validity of an action or a law shall not be called in question on the ground that it is not in accordance with the Principles of Policy, and that no legal action lies against the State on that ground. This is the heart of the relationship:

| | Fundamental rights (Articles 8-28) | Principles of policy (Articles 29-40) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Guarantees held by persons and citizens | Goals the State must work towards |
| Can a court enforce it? | Yes: a conflicting law is void (Article 8), and courts can order a remedy (Articles 199, 184(3)) | No: an action cannot be challenged in court on this ground (Article 30(2)) |
| How is the State held to it? | Through the courts | Through Parliament, the annual report, elections and public opinion |
| Example | Equality before law (Article 25) | Full participation of women in national life (Article 34) |

**But the two are not separate worlds.** The principles help explain what the rights are for, and courts and governments read them together. Most importantly, a principle of policy can *become* an enforceable right when Parliament decides the country is ready.

**Worked example: from principle to right.** Follow the history of education in the Constitution.

1. **1973.** Education appeared only as a principle of policy. Article 37(b) asked the State to "remove illiteracy" and provide free and compulsory secondary education within the minimum possible period. A child out of school could not go to court on this ground (Article 30(2)).
2. **2010.** The Eighteenth Amendment added Article 25A to the chapter of fundamental rights. Now the State "shall provide free and compulsory education to all children of the age of five to sixteen years".
3. **After 2010.** Because 25A is a fundamental right, a law that conflicts with it is void, and a court can be asked to enforce it. Provinces passed their own laws to say how the right would be provided, as Sindh did in 2013.
4. **What remains a principle.** Article 37 still contains goals that are not rights, such as making technical and professional education generally available.

The lesson for pupils is that a constitution is not frozen. A goal written in 1973 became a promise that can be enforced in 2010. This also shows why the principles matter even though courts cannot enforce them directly: they set the direction in which rights grow.

### The role of citizens in a democratic Pakistan

**Rights and duties go together.** A <Glossary term="Citizen" /> is a full member of a State, who holds rights from it and owes duties to it. The Constitution names two duties directly. Article 5 says that loyalty to the State is "the basic duty of every citizen", and that obedience to the Constitution and law is the inviolable obligation of every citizen and of every other person in Pakistan (Government of Pakistan, 1973/2012).

From the rest of the Constitution we can draw a fuller picture of what a citizen in a democracy does:

1. **Votes, and votes with knowledge.** Every citizen aged eighteen or over may vote (Article 51). Voting is the main way citizens choose and remove governments.
2. **Takes part between elections.** Citizens can join parties and associations (Article 17), meet peacefully (Article 16), stand for local government (Article 32), and serve on bodies such as school management committees.
3. **Seeks information and holds officials to account.** Article 19A lets citizens ask for information on matters of public importance, such as how a school's funds were spent.
4. **Respects the rights of others.** Every right has a matching duty on others: my freedom of religion means I must respect yours. Article 33 asks the State to discourage provincial, sectarian and racial prejudice, and citizens make that real in daily life.
5. **Pays taxes and obeys the law,** because public services such as schools are paid for by the public.
6. **Protects public property and the environment,** from the school's furniture to the canal and the forest.

**Being an informed citizen.** Every one of these roles depends on accurate information. A citizen who votes on the basis of rumour, or forwards a false message about another community on a phone, can harm the rights of others without meaning to. Teachers can build the habits of an informed citizen in any subject: asking "How do we know?", checking a claim against an official source such as the Constitution or the Election Commission, and listening to people who disagree. These habits are part of what the Constitution means when it asks the State to discourage prejudice (Article 33).

**Citizens and local government.** The level of government most citizens can reach directly is the local council (Article 140A; see Topic 4.1). A citizen can attend a union council meeting, stand as a candidate, raise a problem with an elected councillor, or join the school management committee. Women, peasants and workers have special representation in local institutions under the principles of policy (Article 32), so local government is also where many citizens first take part in public life.

**Correcting a confusion: "Citizenship means only obeying."** Some pupils, and some textbooks, present the role of citizens as a list of duties of obedience. Obedience to law is a real duty under Article 5. But in a democracy citizens are also the source of the government's authority: the Preamble of the Constitution says the State shall exercise its powers "through the chosen representatives of the people". A good citizen obeys the law *and* takes part in making and checking it.

**Worked example: turning a rights problem into citizen action.** Pupils in a Class 9 in Thatta notice that the girls' toilets at their school have been locked for months. Analyse the problem as active citizens would:

1. **Identify the right and the duty.** Pupils have a right to education and to equal treatment (Articles 25A and 25). A locked toilet keeps girls away from school in practice.
2. **Find the responsible office.** The school and the district education office, under the provincial government.
3. **Choose lawful, peaceful action.** The class writes a polite letter to the head teacher; the school management committee raises it; if needed, a parent requests information under Article 19A on the school's maintenance budget.
4. **Respect others' rights.** No damage to property, no blocking of the road, no insult to staff.

This is what "role of citizens" means in practice: knowing the right, finding the responsible body, and acting lawfully until the problem is solved.

## Activity: Rights in our school

Work in groups of three. About 30 minutes.

1. Each group receives one of these four case cards:
   - a. A Christian pupil in Class 6 is told to stay in the Islamiat period and take the test.
   - b. A teacher beats a pupil in front of the class for coming late.
   - c. A 13-year-old boy misses school three days a week to work in a mechanic's workshop.
   - d. Parents ask the school how the annual development grant was spent and are told it is "none of their business".
2. Using the table of rights at the start of this topic and the Constitution, decide: (i) which right or rights are involved, with article numbers; (ii) who holds the duty to protect the right; and (iii) whether a principle of policy also applies.
3. Use the flowchart on the route to a remedy to plan the first two steps the pupil or family should take. Write them as two numbered sentences.
4. Add one sentence: what should *other pupils* do, as citizens, in this situation?
5. Pass your card and answer to the next group. They write one question or improvement on your answer and pass it back.

## Summary

The 1973 Constitution guarantees fundamental rights in Articles 8 to 28, including security of person, fair trial, freedom of speech and religion, equality before law, and since 2010 the right to free and compulsory education for children aged five to sixteen. Article 8 makes any law that conflicts with these rights void, and citizens can seek a remedy through a writ petition in a High Court or, on questions of public importance, the Supreme Court. Pakistan's rights are not copies of another country's; they carry their own limits and reflect Pakistan's own values and history. The principles of policy in Articles 29 to 40 are goals for the State that courts cannot enforce directly, but they guide the State and can grow into rights, as the right to education did. Citizens owe loyalty and obedience to law, and in a democracy they also vote, take part, seek information and respect the rights of others.

## Summative task

Read the case below, then write an analysis of 300-350 words.

*In a village school near Badin, the head teacher tells parents of Hindu pupils that their children must attend the Islamiat class "because this is a Muslim country". The same school refuses to admit two girls aged fourteen, saying "older girls do not need school". When a parent asks to see the school's attendance register, the head teacher refuses.*

In your answer: (a) identify every fundamental right involved, with articles; (b) analyse whether each action is a violation of a right, a failure to follow a principle of policy, or both, and explain why the difference matters; (c) describe the route to a remedy for one of the problems; and (d) explain what the other parents, as citizens, could lawfully do.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | 3 - strong | 2 - adequate | 1 - weak |
|---|---|---|---|

| Rights vs principles (**Analyze**) | Explains for each action why it is (or is not) enforceable, using Articles 8 and 30(2), and why the difference matters to the families | Distinguishes rights from principles but without linking to enforcement | Treats rights and principles as the same |
