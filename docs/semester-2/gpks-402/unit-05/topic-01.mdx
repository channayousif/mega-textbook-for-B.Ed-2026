# Topic 5.1: The economy: sectors, challenges and the role of education

## A real classroom situation

In a fictional Grade 7 class near Hyderabad, a trainee asks who helped produce a cotton school uniform. One pupil names a farmer; another names a factory worker; a third remembers the driver who brought it to the shop. When transport costs rise, the shopkeeper raises the uniform price, and one family delays buying it. The class asks whether this is an agricultural problem, an industrial problem or a school problem.

## Explanation

### Agriculture: the backbone of Pakistan's economy

An <Glossary term="Economy">economy</Glossary> is the system through which people produce, exchange and use goods and services. Goods are physical items, such as rice, cloth and a desk. Services are useful activities, such as teaching, transporting rice or repairing a desk. An economic **sector** is a group of related activities. We use three broad sectors here: agriculture, industry and services. The Ministry of Finance's *Pakistan Economic Survey 2024-25*, Chapter 1, Table 1.2, uses these groups to describe production.

Agriculture includes growing crops and raising livestock, alongside forestry and fishing in the national accounts. A wheat farm, a dairy herd and fishing activity are different ways of producing from living resources. Industry includes activities such as manufacturing, mining and construction. Services include transport, shops, banking, education and health care. These categories describe the main activity. They do not rank the dignity of workers or tell us how much an individual earns.

Pakistan's agriculture matters because it supplies food, raw materials and livelihoods. The familiar phrase **agriculture-based economy** highlights these connections. Cotton can supply a textile factory; milk can supply a processor; grain can supply a flour mill. Calling agriculture a backbone is a way to stress its supporting role. It does not mean that agriculture is the only sector or even the largest measured sector. Do not turn a useful image into a false statistical statement.

The following dated snapshot makes the distinction visible. It uses the provisional FY2024-25 shares reported in the Ministry of Finance survey, Chapter 1, Table 1.2, based on the FY2015-16 constant-price series. These shares may later be revised. They are a teaching baseline, not a claim about the latest year.

| Sector | Share in the provisional FY2024-25 sectoral GDP table | Illustrative activity | A possible production difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agriculture | 23.54% | Crops, milk production, fishing | Unreliable water or crop damage |
| Industry | 18.07% | Cloth manufacturing, construction | Interrupted power or costly machinery |
| Services | 58.39% | Transport, retail, teaching | Poor roads or limited access to skills |

**Gross domestic product**, or **GDP**, measures the value of production within a country's borders during a period. It avoids counting the same intermediate product repeatedly. An intermediate product is something used to make another product. Cotton used in a shirt is an example. GDP is a measure of production, not a list of everything that makes life good. It does not, on its own, show how safely people work, how income is shared or how much unpaid care takes place.

Read the table in three steps. First, identify the year and whether the estimates are provisional. Second, identify what is being shared: the value of production, rather than people or jobs. Third, compare like with like. Services have the largest share in this table. That does not make agricultural food supply unimportant. A sector can have a smaller output share and still be essential to another sector's work.

A common misconception is that **Pakistan's economy depends only on agriculture**. The table directly challenges that claim. A second mistake is to read 23.54% as the percentage of households that farm. The table gives no household count. A third is to mistake a sector's share for its growth rate. A sector could grow while its share falls if another sector grows faster. Keep the question “share of what?” beside every percentage.

Consider a fictional cotton route in Sindh. A farmer sells cotton, a processor prepares it, a mill makes yarn and cloth, a stitching workshop makes uniforms, and a shop sells them. Drivers, mechanics and bookkeepers support several stages. A water shortage at the farm can therefore affect work beyond farming. Likewise, a transport delay can leave a farmer with produce that cannot reach buyers. The connection runs in more than one direction.

Here is a worked example of avoiding double counting. Suppose a teaching case records cotton sold for Rs 100, cloth sold for Rs 250 and a finished uniform sold for Rs 400. Adding all sales gives Rs 750, but some of the same value passes through all three transactions. If these are the only purchased inputs in the simplified case, the farmer adds Rs 100, the cloth stage adds Rs 150 and the final stage adds Rs 150. Total **value added**, meaning each stage's new contribution, is Rs 400. Real production has many more inputs, but the principle is the same.

A farm's total sales are also different from its profit. **Profit** is the amount left after relevant costs are subtracted from sales. Suppose a fictional vegetable grower sells Rs 30,000 of produce and spends Rs 22,000 on counted inputs and transport. The recorded difference is Rs 8,000. If family labour or equipment wear has not been valued, that difference is not a complete account of the grower's return. State which costs the example includes instead of treating cash left over as a full measure of well-being.

In a Sindh classroom, this matters when children discuss farming households. A pupil may say that a large harvest makes every farmer rich. Ask what happened to selling prices, input costs, land access and crop losses. Two growers with similar harvests can face different costs. A tenant who rents land has a different payment arrangement from an owner. These are reasons to investigate a case, not permission to guess a family's wealth from its occupation.

Farming also has a calendar. Work, costs and income do not always occur in the same month. In a fictional household, money is spent on seed before the harvest earns income. A school expense due during that gap can be difficult even when annual sales look adequate. The appropriate classroom response is to understand a possible timing barrier and offer accessible learning. It is not to approve child labour or assume that every rural absence has the same cause.

A practical explanation therefore links land, water, knowledge, labour and markets. A farmer needs more than physical effort. Reading instructions can support safe use of materials; measurement can help estimate quantities; information can help compare buyers. Yet knowledge cannot manufacture missing water or repair a public road by itself. This balance prepares us to examine both economic challenges and the role of education.

### Industry and trade: sectors and challenges

The **industrial sector** brings together activities that extract materials, make products or build structures. Manufacturing changes materials into goods. A textile mill turns fibre into cloth; a food processor packages produce; a construction enterprise combines materials and labour to make a building. Size varies. A small stitching workshop and a large factory both undertake production, though they have different equipment, costs and working arrangements.

Services connect production to people. A wholesaler buys and sells goods, a driver moves them, a bank provides financial services and a teacher supports learning. Trade is buying and selling goods or services. **Domestic trade** takes place within a country. **International trade** crosses national borders. An **export** is sold to another country; an **import** is bought from another country. The same product can be an export for one country and an import for the other.

In a fictional route, a rice producer in Sindh sells to a mill, the mill sells packaged rice to a Karachi business and that business sells rice overseas. The first transactions are domestic; the final sale is an export. If the mill purchases a machine from another country, that machine is an import into Pakistan. This shows why “local industry” and “imports” need not be opposites. A producer may use imported equipment to make an exported product.

A useful analysis asks four questions: What is produced? What inputs does it need? Who buys it? What can interrupt the connection? Do not begin with a slogan such as “imports are harmful” or “exports solve everything.” Imported medicine or equipment can be useful. Exporting can widen a firm's market, but firms still need reliable production, safe work and buyers willing to pay. Benefits and difficulties depend on the specific product and conditions.

The World Bank's *Pakistan Development Update, October 2025*, especially Section 4, identifies export competitiveness, access to finance, energy and transport conditions, skills and trade rules as linked reform concerns. It treats stronger exports as part of a wider growth strategy. This topic uses those concerns to frame the following original teaching cases. They are illustrations of mechanisms rather than evidence about any particular Pakistani business.

Consider a fictional workshop that promises fifty uniforms to a school. Its workers know how to sew, but power interruptions reduce machine time. The owner borrows to buy a backup supply, which increases costs. A late cloth delivery delays stitching further. Blaming the workers' effort alone misses two constraints. A constraint is a condition that limits what a person or enterprise can do. Good analysis identifies the limiting condition before proposing a solution.

Now compare a second workshop with steady power but repeated measurement errors. Cloth is cut incorrectly, and finished uniforms do not fit. In this case, a training session on measuring and checking patterns may address the immediate problem. The same training would not solve the first workshop's power interruption. Both cases concern productivity, but the cause differs. **Productivity** means output in relation to an input, such as useful uniforms per hour of work. More hours do not guarantee better productivity if the process wastes material.

Use this worked comparison before judging a new case:

| Evidence in the fictional case | Likely immediate barrier | A response linked to that barrier | What the response cannot guarantee |
|---|---|---|---|
| Correctly trained workers wait during repeated outages | Unreliable power | More reliable energy arrangements | Enough orders or fair pay |
| Workers have power but repeatedly misread measurements | A specific skill gap | Demonstration, supervised practice and checking | Lower fabric prices |
| Finished goods remain unsold after a buyer cancels | Weak or uncertain demand | Seek other buyers and review order risks | A buyer at the desired price |

Trade also involves standards. A buyer may require a product to meet a specified size, quality or delivery time. A worker who can read a specification and record a quality check can help a firm meet it. However, a certificate saying someone attended training is not proof that the person can perform the check. Evidence of learning might be several correctly inspected samples, including an explanation of why a faulty sample should be rejected.

The economy is connected through costs as well as products. A rise in transport costs may affect the delivered price of farm produce, industrial inputs and school supplies. Whether a seller raises prices depends on competition, demand and the ability to absorb costs. Avoid the claim that every cost increase is passed on fully and immediately. A fictional shopkeeper may reduce profit for a week to keep customers; another may change suppliers; another may raise the price.

A school can teach these links without turning the lesson into a business survey. Give groups a short fictional route and ask them to label activities and explain one interruption. This protects private information while providing common evidence. When learners disagree, return to the actual activity. A person may work on a farm in one season and drive a vehicle in another. Classify the work described rather than assigning the person a permanent sector identity.

### Economic challenges: poverty, inequality and debt

<Glossary term="Poverty">Poverty</Glossary> is a lack of resources needed to meet basic needs and take part in ordinary life. A household may lack adequate food, shelter or the means to reach a service. **Inequality** concerns differences in resources, opportunities or outcomes between people or groups. Poverty and inequality overlap, but they are not interchangeable. Everyone in a small example could have enough to meet a chosen basic-needs threshold while still having very unequal incomes.

**Debt** is money owed that must be repaid under agreed terms. Households, enterprises and governments can all borrow. The borrowed amount is the principal. Interest is a charge for borrowing. Debt can fund a useful investment or smooth a difficult period, but repayment uses future resources. A useful judgment asks what the loan finances, what it costs and whether repayment is manageable. “All debt is bad” and “borrowing creates free money” are both poor explanations.

Consider two fictional monthly budgets. Household A has Rs 24,000 after necessary work expenses and faces Rs 27,000 in basic costs. Household B has Rs 60,000 and faces the same Rs 27,000 in this simplified exercise. A has a shortfall of Rs 3,000. The income difference between A and B illustrates inequality. This exercise illustrates resource pressure; it does not establish an official poverty line or measure actual poverty in Pakistan. An official estimate needs a defined method, date and data.

Next suppose both incomes increase by Rs 5,000 while the stated costs stay fixed. A can now meet those listed costs, but the Rs 36,000 income gap remains. This worked example shows why reducing a shortfall is not identical to removing inequality. If prices rise at the same time, even the improvement in meeting costs may be smaller than the increase in money income suggests. Always check both resources and relevant needs.

**Inflation** is an increase in the general level of prices over time. One shop raising one price is not sufficient evidence of general inflation. Also, lower inflation means prices are rising more slowly; it does not necessarily mean that prices have fallen. In a teaching case, a basket costing Rs 1,000 rises to Rs 1,100, then to Rs 1,155. The increases slow from 10% to 5%, but the basket still becomes more expensive. This is why a household may continue to feel pressure after inflation slows.

An economic **shock** is a sudden change that disrupts expected income or costs. A flood may damage a crop; an illness may interrupt paid work; an unexpected repair may use savings. These cases show why a household just above a basic-needs threshold can still be vulnerable. Vulnerability means exposure to possible harm. It is different from proving that harm has already occurred. A teacher should respond to an observed learning barrier rather than assigning a public label to a child.

The World Bank's October 2025 update links Pakistan's recovery prospects with flood risks, limited public resources and financing needs. Its analysis reminds us to distinguish better national indicators from conditions facing individual households. National growth is an increase in production over time. It does not establish that every family has more secure work or that every school has better facilities. We need evidence at the level of the question we ask.

A fictional household borrows Rs 10,000 for a damaged water pump and agrees to repay Rs 11,000 after the harvest. The additional Rs 1,000 is the stated borrowing charge in this simplified example. If the pump restores useful production, the loan may help. If the harvest fails, repayment becomes harder. The analysis changes with the purpose, terms and outcome. Do not infer that the borrower was careless merely because a risk later occurred.

Government debt works at a different scale and has different institutions. Governments may borrow from domestic or external lenders. Public debt is not the same as a household's loan, and a classroom budget analogy cannot explain every public policy choice. It can show one narrow point: paying interest and repaying debt requires resources that have other possible uses. **Debt service** means these payments. Its burden depends on borrowing terms, public revenue and wider economic conditions.

To see a budget trade-off, use a fictional public budget of one hundred tokens. Suppose twenty tokens are committed to debt service, thirty to other existing services and fifty remain for other decisions. If debt service rises to twenty-five while total resources stay fixed, five fewer tokens remain elsewhere. This is an arithmetic illustration, not Pakistan's actual budget. It does not prove which service must be reduced, since revenue, priorities and borrowing decisions can also change.

Economic difficulty affects education through several routes. Costs may include travel, materials and time away from paid work or household duties. A child with no safe route to school faces an access barrier. A child present in class but unable to follow the lesson faces a learning barrier. A response should match the route. Extra worksheets do not remove transport costs, and a transport arrangement does not automatically improve weak teaching.

Consider a fictional pupil who misses two mornings each week because the available bus arrives late. The observed pattern supports checking travel arrangements. It does not justify a conclusion about the family's values. The teacher can share missed learning in a manageable form and raise the timetable problem through the school. If the pupil instead attends regularly but struggles with unfamiliar vocabulary, language support is a more direct teaching response. The evidence determines the first step.

A fair explanation of poverty also avoids blaming whole communities. Land access, employment, disability barriers, public services and sudden losses may affect opportunity. Individual decisions matter, but they occur under unequal conditions. A trainee can acknowledge effort while asking what choices were actually available. This gives a more useful basis for school action than dividing families into those who “care” and those who “do not care.”

### The role of education in economic development

**Economic development** means improvement in productive capacity and living conditions, including wider opportunities. It is broader than a rise in total output alone. Education can contribute through literacy, numeracy, technical knowledge, health knowledge and the ability to learn new tasks. <Glossary term="Human capital">Human capital</Glossary> refers to knowledge and skills people can use in productive activity. People also have rights and purposes beyond their economic contribution; education should not reduce their worth to earnings.

Here is the basic pathway: useful learning develops a capability; the capability improves a task or decision; an opportunity allows the improvement to be used; better results can support income or public resources; part of those resources can support further learning. Each step needs a condition. Attending school is not identical to learning. Learning is not identical to obtaining suitable work. Growth does not automatically produce fair reinvestment in schools.

<Figure id="fig-U5-2" src="/img/figures/gpks-402/unit-05/fig-U5-2.svg" alt="Education can build skills that support productive work income and reinvestment, while access jobs infrastructure and public decisions condition each link in the cycle." caption="Original teaching schematic, textbook.com.pk." />

UNESCO's 2025 *Languages matter: Global guidance on multilingual education* argues for learning in languages learners understand. That educational principle helps us examine the first link in the pathway. A lesson delivered in unfamiliar language may not produce the expected understanding. Giving access to meaningful learning is therefore an economic concern as well as a matter of inclusion. The case below applies the principle; it is not a measured estimate of a national return to schooling.

In a fictional adult numeracy class in rural Sindh, learners practise measuring quantities and comparing unit prices. Before instruction, one learner compares two seed packets only by total price. After guided practice, the learner compares the price per kilogram and checks that the products are suitable for the same purpose. The learning result is a demonstrated comparison, not simply a completed attendance register. Whether the choice improves farm income also depends on seed quality, water and other costs.

A second case concerns a stitching workshop. Learners practise measuring a uniform pattern, identifying a faulty measurement and explaining the correction to a partner. Their assessed skill could reduce wasted cloth. For that skill to improve production, the workshop needs functioning equipment and orders. For improved production to improve the worker's well-being, pay and working conditions also matter. Name these conditions instead of promising that training alone removes poverty.

A third case concerns a girl who wants to continue secondary school. A fee reduction may help, but it will have limited effect if safe travel is unavailable. A safe route may help, but it will have limited effect if she cannot access the learning materials. This is why a proposal should identify the first serious barrier rather than list every possible benefit of education. Ask what would change for the learner and how that change would be observed.

Use the following worked plan. A fictional school finds that pupils can copy a price calculation but cannot explain which of two equal-quality packets is better value. The learning barrier is weak understanding of unit cost. A suitable lesson uses familiar containers, one demonstrated comparison and paired practice with different quantities. The check asks pupils to solve a new example and explain their choice. The teacher records whether they can transfer the method, not whether they repeated the original numbers.

The proposal's limit must be explicit. Success in that lesson would show improved numeracy for a specific task. It would not demonstrate a rise in national GDP or prove that the learners will find work. A reasonable follow-up might check the same skill a few weeks later. Claims should stay close to the evidence. Small, honest evidence is more useful than an impressive promise that cannot be checked.

The misconception **education has no direct link to economic growth** overlooks these skills and decisions. The opposite misconception, that a degree guarantees a well-paid job, overlooks demand, infrastructure and unequal access. A balanced explanation says that learning can strengthen capability, while the wider economy affects how that capability is used. It also recognises education's social role: learners gain ways to understand public issues, cooperate across differences and question unfair treatment.

As a future teacher, you can make economic ideas understandable without asking children to behave like workers. Use safe, age-appropriate cases. Teach them to read a label, explain a comparison, distinguish an assertion from evidence and work fairly with others. Never require children to reveal household debts or perform unpaid production for a lesson. The aim is understanding and agency within a shared society.

## Activity: Trace a uniform and test a solution

**Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.** Use the fictional uniform route from the opening. Assign a route mapper, a cost checker, a questioning partner and a reporter. Rotate roles halfway through so that each person explains a link.

- From memory, sketch farming, processing, stitching, transport and selling. Label the activity at each point and explain where services appear.
- Use these case facts: workers make measurement errors, some machine time is lost during outages and one buyer cancels an order. Decide which problem a measurement lesson could address. State which problems need another response.
- Plan one demonstrated example, one paired practice task and one new check of the measurement skill. Use paper strips rather than real cutting tools.
- Exchange plans with another group. The questioning partner asks for the evidence that would show learning and for a condition that might prevent an economic benefit.
- Revise one claim that is too broad. Each member then gives a one-sentence explanation of a different sector link without looking at the sketch.

Do not gather household earnings or contact actual employers. The case provides enough common evidence for comparison.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the three sectors and give one different example of each from Pakistan. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why an economy can depend on agriculture without agriculture being its largest GDP sector. Distinguish an output share from a job count. *(Understand)*
3. A fictional school offers skills training where machines are usable but learners repeatedly misread measurements. Describe a teaching response and one observation that would show learning. *(Apply)*
4. Using a fictional household or public budget, explain how a repayment obligation can limit another choice. State one limit of your example. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Agriculture, industry and services depend on one another through products, work and exchange. Poverty, inequality and debt describe different problems that require different evidence. Education can improve useful capability, but access, suitable opportunities and public decisions affect whether learning improves economic well-being.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can trace a local product through more than one sector without confusing workers with activities.
- [ ] I can read a dated GDP share without treating it as a household or employment percentage.
- [ ] I can distinguish poverty, inequality and debt with a bounded example.
- [ ] I can explain a pathway from learning to productive activity and name a condition that can interrupt it.

## Try this at your practicum school

During one visit, look at an ordinary learning task that uses measurement, reading instructions or comparing quantities. With the cooperating teacher, identify the useful capability it develops. Observe the task rather than collecting children's financial information. Write a short note on the learning evidence and one change that would help a learner who struggles. Separate what you observed from what you would still need to know about later economic use.

## Summative task

Write a 350–450 word analysis of a fictional local enterprise linked to farming, industry and services. Choose one economic barrier and one education response. Explain the route from the proposed learning to a possible economic benefit. Distinguish that route from a barrier requiring a non-school response. Include a dated source for any national figure; the fictional case needs no national statistic.

**Mini-rubric: 12 marks.** Award each criterion separately.

| Criterion | 3 marks | 2 marks | 1 mark | 0 marks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sector relationships | Correctly explains all three sectors and their links | Explains two links with a minor gap | Names sectors without explaining links | No relevant classification |
| Barrier analysis (Analyze) | Uses specific case evidence to distinguish the learning barrier from another constraint | Distinguishes barriers with limited evidence | Names a barrier without tracing its effect | No relevant barrier |
| Response and learning check | Matches instruction and a new performance check to the barrier | Matches instruction but the check is weak | Gives a general call for training | No relevant response |
