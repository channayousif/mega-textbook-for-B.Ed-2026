# Topic 5.2: Society and culture: structure, diversity and identity

## A real classroom situation

In a fictional practicum class in Karachi, pupils prepare a display about Pakistan's cultures. One group wants every province to have one language, one costume and one food. A pupil whose family speaks two languages asks which box she belongs in, while another asks why a city family cannot also keep strong village ties. The trainee needs a display that makes differences visible without making children fit fixed boxes.

## Explanation

### Social structure: rural, urban and tribal dimensions

**Social structure** means the patterned relationships, roles and institutions through which people organise life. A <Glossary term="Family">family</Glossary>, a school, a workplace and a local government connect people in different ways. A <Glossary term="Role">role</Glossary> is a set of expectations linked to a position, such as pupil, teacher or parent. The distinction between social relationships and learned culture follows Conerly, Holmes and Tamang (2021), Sections 3.1 and 4.2. These relationships influence who can make decisions, obtain support or reach a service. They do not determine every person's choices.

A <Glossary term="Social institution">social institution</Glossary> is an established arrangement that meets recurring social needs. For example, education organises learning, while families organise care and relationships in many forms. A school is therefore more than a building: it has roles, routines and expectations. To understand a learning barrier, ask how these arrangements work together. A school timetable, transport route and household care responsibilities may interact even though they belong to different parts of daily life.

**Rural** usually describes village and countryside settings; **urban** describes towns and cities. Official statistics use administrative definitions, so everyday impressions do not replace the statistical category. The Pakistan Bureau of Statistics' *7th Population and Housing Census 2023* detailed results distinguish rural and urban populations and provide tables on education and language. These categories are useful for comparing patterns at a stated scale. They cannot tell us everything about an individual pupil.

**Tribal dimensions** refer here to social relationships organised partly through kinship, lineage and community affiliations. Kinship concerns family connections; lineage concerns a claimed line of descent. These relationships may influence mutual support, identity or local authority. They can exist in rural settlements and among people living in cities. Tribal is therefore not a third location category that sits neatly beside rural and urban, and it is not a synonym for poor, violent or uneducated.

This distinction is essential. Rural and urban describe settlement settings, while tribal describes a dimension of social organisation. A person may live in a city, maintain tribal affiliations and work in a modern service enterprise. Another may live in a village without identifying through a tribal structure. A three-column classroom comparison can help us ask questions, but it becomes misleading if it turns overlapping dimensions into three sealed kinds of people.

The following table gives questions to investigate, not fixed descriptions of every community. Family arrangements, livelihoods and services vary within each setting. Economic conditions and personal choices also change over time.

| Dimension | Rural setting: possible question | Urban setting: possible question | Tribal relationships: possible question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Family and support | How do relatives and neighbours share care across a settlement? | How do nearby and distant relatives support a household? | What support follows kinship ties, including across places? |
| Livelihood | How do farming and non-farm work connect? | How do industry, services and other work connect? | How do community networks connect people to different livelihoods? |
| Education access | What distances, transport and facilities shape access? | What costs, crowded schools or work schedules shape access? | How do support networks, geography and unequal authority affect access? |
| Traditions | Which practices are shared, adapted or debated locally? | How do different communities exchange and adapt practices? | How are inherited affiliations maintained, reinterpreted or questioned? |

<Figure id="fig-U5-4" src="/img/figures/gpks-402/unit-05/fig-U5-4.svg" alt="Rural and urban settlement questions are compared with overlapping tribal relationships across family livelihood education access and traditions, without assigning fixed traits to people." caption="Original teaching schematic, textbook.com.pk." />

A <Glossary term="Joint family">joint family</Glossary> includes relatives beyond a couple and their children sharing important household arrangements. A <Glossary term="Nuclear family">nuclear family</Glossary> centres on parents and their children. Neither label gives a complete account of support. Relatives may live apart and still share money or care. A household can also change as people migrate, marry or take up work. Do not assume that a city address proves a nuclear family or that a village address proves a joint family.

Work through this fictional case. A pupil in a Hyderabad neighbourhood lives with parents and a grandmother. An aunt in a village helps with care during a family illness, and a parent travels for seasonal work. The city address describes where the pupil lives. The shared household and wider care network describe relationships. Seasonal work describes a livelihood arrangement. The case cannot be explained by selecting only one label and treating all other details as exceptions.

Now compare two fictional pupils. One travels a long distance from a village to a secondary school. Another lives close to an urban school but cannot afford the daily transport fare needed to cross a difficult route. The first case highlights distance; the second highlights cost and route conditions. Both are access barriers. The urban pupil's address does not prove easy access, and the rural pupil's difficulty does not prove lack of family interest.

This is a worked comparison because it moves from evidence to a limited conclusion. Identify the stated fact, name the barrier and propose the first response. In the first case, a school might investigate safe travel arrangements. In the second, it might examine the route and available support. In neither case should the teacher announce that a whole community rejects education. The explanation stays with the mechanism that the facts support.

**Authority** is the recognised ability to make or influence decisions. Within any community, influence can be unequal. Age, wealth, gender, occupation and access to institutions may affect whose voice is heard. A description of cultural practice should therefore ask who speaks and who may be missing. One confident adult does not automatically represent all women, young people or minority households in a settlement.

Mutual support and unequal power can exist together. A family network may help a learner attend school while also limiting that learner's choices in another matter. We do not need to idealise every local practice or condemn every inherited relationship. We need to examine the actual effects. A rights-respecting classroom treats safety, dignity and equal participation as standards for everyone, regardless of the form of local organisation.

The misconception that “rural people farm, urban people work in offices and tribal people reject change” fails on each part. A village can contain shops, transport work and teaching. A city can contain manual work, informal enterprises and strong village ties. People with tribal affiliations can pursue many occupations and educational paths. **Stereotyping**, assigning a simplified trait to everyone in a group, hides these differences and can lead teachers to lower expectations unfairly.

As a trainee, describe conditions before assigning causes. “The pupil arrived after the lesson began on two observed days” is an observation. “The family does not value education” is an interpretation that the observation does not establish. There could be several causes. A respectful conversation and school records might clarify the issue, but a practicum note should not turn an untested guess into a finding. Fictional cases let us practise this distinction safely.

This way of reasoning also connects society to the economy. A transport link can support trade and school attendance. A family care arrangement can affect an adult's opportunity for paid work and a child's opportunity to study. An economic shock can place extra pressure on existing support networks. These are possible pathways, not fixed rules about a particular social group. Topic 5.1 provides the economic terms; this topic adds the relationships through which effects may pass.

### Cultural diversity across provinces and regions

<Glossary term="Culture">Culture</Glossary>, as introduced by Conerly, Holmes and Tamang (2021, Section 3.1), includes learned ways of life, meanings and practices that people share and change. It includes language, stories, arts, food practices and ideas about appropriate conduct. **Cultural diversity** is the presence of different cultural experiences and expressions within and across communities. Diversity exists between regions and within the same street, school or household. It does not require each person to display a single, unchanging cultural identity.

**Identity** concerns how people understand and describe their belonging. A learner may belong to a family, neighbourhood, language community, province, religion, profession and country at the same time. These identities can support one another. National belonging does not require a learner to stop speaking a home language or stop valuing a local tradition. Shared civic life depends on equal respect and cooperation across differences, not on a test of cultural sameness.

For an accessible example, consider an ajrak pattern discussed in a Sindh classroom. Pupils might examine how a repeated design is made, how an artisan learns the skill and how a family uses the cloth. The object is not a certificate showing that every Sindhi person dresses alike. A pupil who does not own an ajrak is not less entitled to belong. Focus on the practice and its meanings rather than policing a child's identity.

A regional display should work in the same way. It can offer examples associated with places while showing connections and variation. Punjab includes Punjabi and Saraiki language experiences, among others. Sindh includes Sindhi and Urdu language experiences, among others. Khyber Pakhtunkhwa includes Pashto and Hindko, among others. Balochistan includes Balochi, Brahui and Pashto, among others. People migrate, and language communities cross provincial boundaries. These are starting points for inquiry rather than exclusive provincial lists.

Northern regions also contain varied language and cultural experiences. Examples include Shina and Balti in Gilgit-Baltistan and different language communities in Azad Jammu and Kashmir. A table about four provinces is therefore not a complete account of the country or of every region's heritage. Nor should a national census table be extended beyond its stated geographical coverage. When using a source, read what area and population it actually covers before drawing a larger conclusion.

The PBS Census 2023 mother-tongue tables list multiple language categories and separate rural and urban results. They support the simple claim that language experience is diverse and cannot be reduced to one language for all residents of a province. They do not directly measure every language a person can speak or the strength of that person's national identity. Topic 5.2 uses examples without assigning current population shares, since that would require careful attention to coverage and category definitions.

Traditions include repeated practices that people pass on, such as ways of telling stories, making crafts, preparing food or marking important occasions. A tradition is **living** when people continue to practise and adapt it. It is not necessarily unchanged. A craftsperson may use a new tool, teach a younger learner and sell through a new route while keeping valued knowledge. Change does not automatically make a practice false or worthless.

UNESCO Pakistan's July 2026 account, *From the Desert to the Mountains: A Project Comes Full Circle*, describes work with Thari communities in Sindh and Kalasha communities in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. The work connects community documentation, learning tools and artisan livelihoods. Its example helps us see heritage as knowledge held and practised by people, rather than a collection of objects separated from their makers. It does not make one participant the voice of an entire province.

A classroom can apply that insight through a fictional comparison. One display shows an embroidery sample with only a price label. Another explains how an artisan learns a stitch, how designs are selected and what permission is needed before sharing a personal story. The second display gives the maker a role as a knowledgeable person. The teaching difference is whose knowledge the class recognises, not how expensive the object is.

Food examples require similar care. A class might compare ingredients, preparation methods or the stories attached to a dish. Avoid making every learner bring food from home. That could create costs, expose household differences or conflict with dietary requirements. A drawn recipe card or a spoken description can provide equal participation. A pupil may also choose an unfamiliar practice to research rather than be required to perform a supposed family identity.

Arts provide another route. A teacher can use an approved short example of a poem, story, pattern or rhythm and ask what knowledge it carries. Credit the source or maker and use material the school may share. Do not turn a living religious practice into a compulsory performance. Respectful learning can involve describing a practice or listening to an account without requiring children to take part in it. Equal belonging includes the ability to decline personal disclosure.

The misconception **all of Pakistan shares one uniform culture** confuses shared national life with identical everyday practice. A common school routine can sit alongside different home languages and traditions. A learner can share responsibility for classmates while celebrating different family occasions. The opposite error also matters: diversity does not mean that groups can never understand one another. Translation, exchange and cooperation make shared understanding possible without erasing differences.

The misconception **cultural diversity is a weakness** treats difference as a cause without examining conditions. Misunderstanding or exclusion can create tension, but difference can also widen the knowledge available to a group. A class with several ways to explain a word can compare meaning. A group with different practical experiences can ask better questions. The benefit is not automatic. It depends on fair participation, accurate representation and a willingness to listen and challenge prejudice.

Here is a worked response to the opening problem. Replace the display's four closed boxes with a map of connected examples. Label each item “one example associated with…” and leave space for more than one language or practice in a place. Add a shared centre on cooperation and equal respect. Invite learners to contribute a researched example without saying it represents their household. This changes the task from sorting children into cultural boxes to examining relationships among practices.

Then test the revised design. Can a bilingual pupil contribute without choosing only one identity? Can someone who recently moved contribute knowledge from more than one place? Can a child use a drawn or spoken contribution without buying anything? If the answer is no, the design still creates an unnecessary barrier. Inclusion is visible in the choices a learner can make, not only in a welcoming sentence on the display.

Cultural description should also permit disagreement within a community. A learner may respect a tradition and question a particular expectation linked to it. Another may interpret its meaning differently. The teacher can ask for reasons and evidence while maintaining dignity. Respecting culture does not mean accepting humiliation or unsafe treatment. It means discussing practices accurately and listening to people without making their background a reason to deny equal rights.

### Languages and traditions: unity in diversity

A **mother tongue** is a language learned early in home or community life, though people's language histories can be complex. A **multilingual** person uses more than one language. A census answer about mother tongue is not a complete inventory of everything a person understands, reads or speaks. A learner may use one language at home, another with friends and another in school. Competence can also differ across speaking, reading and writing.

Urdu can connect people from different language communities, while regional and local languages carry knowledge and relationships. English is also used in parts of education and work. These roles need not make languages rivals in a child's mind. The educational question is how learners gain access to meaning and develop additional language abilities. A home language is a learning resource, not evidence that a pupil is less capable.

UNESCO's *Languages matter* (2025) supports education in languages learners understand and values multilingualism in education policy. For a trainee, the practical implication is to distinguish understanding of the topic from familiarity with the lesson's language. A pupil may understand a farming process but not yet know the textbook term for it. Another may repeat the term without understanding the process. A good check makes space to demonstrate meaning before judging the language form separately.

In a fictional Grade 5 class near Sukkur, a pupil explains in Sindhi how a shop compares quantities but pauses over the English phrase “unit price.” The teacher asks the pupil to show the comparison with counters, then supplies the term and invites a short explanation in the school's teaching language. The sequence recognises existing understanding and builds a bridge to new vocabulary. It does not abandon the goal of learning the required term.

Contrast that with a teacher who accepts only an immediate English sentence and marks silence as lack of mathematical understanding. The evidence does not support that conclusion. Silence could reflect vocabulary, confidence, processing time or uncertainty about the concept. A second form of evidence, such as a demonstration or an explanation through a familiar language, helps identify the barrier. The teacher can then choose a response linked to what the learner actually needs.

Language support must be practical and fair. A teacher may not know every home language in a class. Useful options include picture sequences, objects, gestures, a short partner discussion and a board list of key terms with meanings. Learners can volunteer words they know, while the teacher checks rather than invents translations. A bilingual peer may help during a low-stakes task, but should not become a permanent unpaid interpreter responsible for another child's access.

The target is shared understanding. Suppose pairs first discuss a production route using languages they understand, then label the stages with the agreed course terms. Each learner subsequently explains one stage independently through words, a sketch or a demonstration. This final individual check matters. A group product alone may hide that only one confident speaker did the reasoning. Good inclusion combines support with evidence of each learner's learning.

A **tradition** is not only a festival or a costume. Ways of telling a story, learning a craft, offering hospitality or sharing seasonal knowledge can also be transmitted across generations. People can value these practices while adjusting them to new circumstances. A classroom should explore who teaches a practice, how it is learned and how its meaning is explained. The inquiry then connects generations rather than displaying culture as something distant from everyday life.

Work through a fictional oral-history lesson. The teacher supplies a short story that the school has permission to share. Pupils identify a practical problem in the story, compare possible responses and discuss what the storyteller values. They do not need to reveal family beliefs or perform a religious ceremony. A pupil may respond through a drawing and explanation. This supports access while keeping the common intellectual task: understanding how a story communicates knowledge and meaning.

“Unity in diversity” can become an empty phrase if it is taught only as a slogan. Give it an observable meaning. Pupils cooperate on a shared task, use fair turns, credit each other's contributions and challenge a claim that ranks one language community as less intelligent. They are not required to agree about every preference. They are required to treat others as equal participants and use reasons rather than insults when they disagree.

Consider this worked exchange. A pupil says, “People who speak that language cannot explain science.” The trainee does not ask a child to defend a whole community. Instead, the trainee separates language from capability, states that the claim is an unsupported generalisation and returns to a common science demonstration. Pupils then explain the same process using available words, diagrams and the agreed scientific terms. The teacher checks the explanation, not the prestige attached to an accent.

A teacher also needs a fair response when a cultural example is unfamiliar. It is reasonable to say that a detail needs verification and return with a source. It is not reasonable to invent a tradition because a display has an empty space. Learners can label an uncertain claim as a question. This models respect for evidence and for communities whose knowledge should not be replaced by a teacher's guess. The skill is relevant to every subject, not only Pakistan Studies.

To evaluate an inclusive lesson, look for evidence beyond a colourful display. Who had a meaningful turn? Could learners choose a low-cost way to contribute? Did each learner explain something independently? Did the teacher correct an unfair generalisation? Were examples presented as partial and varied? These questions help test whether cultural recognition changed participation. They do not claim that one lesson removes every wider inequality.

The link back to Topic 5.1 is clear. Language access can affect learning; learning can affect future opportunities; social relationships can support or restrict access. Economic resources can affect which learning materials are available. A good school response recognises both routes. It neither explains every difficulty as a cultural defect nor assumes that money alone creates belonging. Evidence about the specific learning barrier should guide the first practical action.

## Activity: Rebuild the cultural display

**Work in pairs, then groups of four. About 25 minutes.** Use the fictional display in the opening. No one must describe their own household.

- Each pair sketches the original closed-box display from memory and identifies two assumptions it makes about identity.
- One partner proposes connected examples; the other tests whether a bilingual pupil, a recent migrant and a child unable to buy materials can participate. Exchange roles.
- Join another pair. Produce a shared paper design showing overlapping examples and a clear note that a province contains internal diversity.
- Plan a two-minute language bridge for explaining one new term. Include a way to check each pupil's meaning independently.
- Each member explains one design choice and the barrier it addresses. Another group challenges one unsupported claim and suggests what source would help verify it.

Keep the focus on teaching decisions and evidence. No group is asked to assign classmates a culture or rank regional practices.

## Check your understanding

1. Define social structure and name two institutions that can shape school participation. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why rural, urban and tribal should not be treated as three mutually exclusive types of people. Use one possible case. *(Understand)*
3. A pupil understands a process but lacks the classroom term for it. Describe one support and one independent check that would help distinguish conceptual understanding from vocabulary difficulty. *(Apply)*
4. Rewrite “every resident of a province has the same culture” as a careful statement, then give one way a classroom display could show internal variation. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Social structure concerns relationships and institutions, while culture concerns learned meanings and practices. Rural and urban settings can overlap with tribal relationships and varied family networks. Shared national belonging can grow through equal participation across languages and traditions, with teaching that makes understanding visible and corrects stereotypes.

## Try this at your practicum school

During one ordinary lesson, observe how the teacher explains a new word or invites a contribution. Record the teaching move and the response without names or inferred family identities. Note whether a picture, object, short discussion or familiar-language explanation helps reveal meaning. Propose one practical change that the cooperating teacher could use with available materials. Do not ask pupils to disclose religion, income or a household language history.

## Summative task

Design a 20-minute Pakistan Studies lesson for a fictional class with varied home languages and family backgrounds. Choose a cultural example, explain its limits as a representation of a region, and plan participation without requiring purchases or personal disclosure. In 350–450 words, justify the design through social structure, cultural diversity and a language bridge. State what independent learning evidence you would seek and what one lesson cannot establish.
