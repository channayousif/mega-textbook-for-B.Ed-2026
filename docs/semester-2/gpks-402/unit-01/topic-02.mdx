## A real classroom situation

Bilal is a trainee teaching Class 10 Pakistan Studies at a government boys' secondary school in Umerkot, a district where many Hindu families live. He has just written "Two-Nation Theory" on the board. A pupil named Rahul, whose family runs a shop in the main bazaar, speaks quietly: "Sir, if Hindus and Muslims are two nations, does that mean my family should not be here?" A Muslim pupil sitting next to him looks uncomfortable. Another pupil calls out, "Sir, Pakistan was made for Islam, so it is an Islamic state, like being ruled by the ulema, isn't it?"

Bilal knows the dates by heart. But the pupils are not asking about dates. They are asking what the founding ideas of Pakistan *mean*, and what they mean for people living together today. If Bilal gets this wrong, he could hurt a child in his class and teach the whole class something false about their country.

This topic gives Bilal, and you, the understanding needed to answer these pupils accurately and kindly.

## Explanation

### Two-Nation Theory: origin and significance

**What the theory says.** The <Glossary term="Two-Nation Theory" /> is the idea that the Muslims and the Hindus of South Asia were two separate nations, not one. A nation, in this sense, is a large group of people who share a religion, a culture, a history, a set of values and a way of life, and who feel that they belong together. The theory argued that because Muslims were a separate nation, they had the right to govern themselves, and that in a single united India they would always be a permanent minority under a Hindu majority. From this argument came the demand for a separate homeland.

**Origin: how the idea grew.** The theory did not appear suddenly in 1940. It grew over about ninety years, in steps. The timeline above shows the main milestones.

1. **After 1857.** The War of Independence of 1857 ended Mughal rule. The British blamed Muslims heavily for the rising, and Muslims lost power, jobs and status. Reformers asked how the community could recover. Sir Syed Ahmad Khan, whose work you will study in Unit 2, urged Muslims to take up modern education. In the late nineteenth century he also spoke of Hindus and Muslims as two distinct communities with different interests, especially after the Urdu-Hindi controversy. He did not demand a separate state, but his view that Muslims had their own interests to protect is often seen as an early root of the theory.
2. **1906.** The All-India Muslim League was founded at Dhaka to protect the political rights of Muslims. Soon after, the British accepted separate electorates for Muslims, which meant Muslims would elect their own representatives. This recognised Muslims as a distinct political community.
3. **1930.** In his presidential address to the Muslim League at Allahabad, Allama Muhammad Iqbal argued that the Muslims of India were "a nation in the modern sense of the word". He said he would like to see the Punjab, the North-West Frontier Province, Sind and Baluchistan "amalgamated into a single State" (Iqbal, 1930/1977). Historians still discuss whether he meant a fully independent country or a large self-governing Muslim unit within India. Either way, he gave the idea of Muslim nationhood a territory in the north-west.
4. **1940.** At the Muslim League session in Lahore in March 1940, Quaid-e-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah stated the theory in its clearest form: "The Mussalmans are a nation by any definition." He argued that Hindus and Muslims "belong to two different civilisations" (Jinnah, 1940/1983). The session then passed the Lahore Resolution, which asked that the areas in the north-west and east of India where Muslims were in a majority should be grouped to form independent states.
5. **1947.** The theory became a political reality. Pakistan became independent on 14 August 1947.

**Significance.** The Two-Nation Theory matters for four reasons.

- **It was the basis of the demand for Pakistan.** Without the claim that Muslims were a nation, there would have been no argument for a separate homeland. Hassan (2020) shows how the theory, and the question of Muslim identity behind it, was central to the partition of 1947.
- **It united a scattered people.** Muslims in Bengal, Punjab, Sindh, the Frontier and many other regions spoke different languages and lived very different lives. The theory gave them one shared political identity and one goal.
- **It explained the need for constitutional safeguards.** It showed why ordinary majority rule could leave a large minority without real power.
- **It remains part of Pakistan's founding ideology.** It is the answer to the question "Why is Pakistan a separate country?"

**Correcting a common misconception: "The Two-Nation Theory means Hindus and Muslims cannot live together."** This is the misconception that worried Rahul in the opening situation, and it is wrong. The theory was a claim about *political nationhood*: about who should govern whom, and how a minority's rights could be protected. It was not a claim that the followers of two religions must hate each other or cannot be neighbours. Consider the evidence:

- Hindus and Muslims had lived side by side in South Asia for centuries, and many of the theory's own supporters had Hindu friends, colleagues and clients.
- Quaid-e-Azam, the leading voice of the theory, promised equal citizenship to non-Muslims in Pakistan in his address of 11 August 1947, as you will read below.
- Pakistan's Constitution protects the freedom of every citizen to profess and practise their religion (Article 20) and requires the State to safeguard the rights and interests of minorities (Article 36) (Government of Pakistan, 1973/2012).
- Hindu, Christian, Sikh and Parsi Pakistanis have served the country as citizens, soldiers, judges, teachers and legislators.

So the honest reply to Rahul is: no. The Two-Nation Theory explains why Pakistan was created as a separate state. It does not say that he, a Hindu citizen, does not belong in it. In Pakistan he is a full and equal citizen.

### Islamic concept of state and its role in Pakistan's ideology

The Two-Nation Theory answers the question *why* Pakistan should exist. The Islamic concept of state answers a different question: *how* should Pakistan be run? It sets out the principles on which public life and government should rest. Its main features are these.

1. **Sovereignty belongs to Allah.** <Glossary term="Sovereignty" /> means the highest authority, the final source of power and law. In the Islamic concept of state, final sovereignty belongs to Allah alone. No ruler, party or assembly is the final master.
2. **Authority is a trust.** The power that people and their rulers hold is a trust (*amanat*) from Allah. It must be used within the limits Allah has set, and those who use it are accountable for how they use it.
3. **Consultation.** Decisions about public affairs should be made through consultation (*shura*). In a modern state, this principle supports an elected assembly and government through the chosen representatives of the people.
4. **Justice and equality.** All people are equal before the law. Rulers must govern with justice (*adl*), and no person is above the law.
5. **Welfare.** The state must care for the poor and weak and work to remove injustice and exploitation.
6. **Rights of non-Muslims.** Non-Muslim citizens have the right to their faith, their places of worship, their property and the protection of the state.

**How these ideas entered Pakistan's constitution.** On 12 March 1949, the first Constituent Assembly of Pakistan passed the <Glossary term="Objectives Resolution" />, moved by Prime Minister Liaquat Ali Khan. It set out the principles on which the constitution would be built. It became the preamble of the constitutions of 1956, 1962 and 1973, and in 1985 it was made a substantive part of the 1973 Constitution as Article 2A. You can trace each of the six features above in the Constitution's text (Government of Pakistan, 1973/2012):

| Feature of the Islamic concept of state | Where it appears in the Constitution |
|---|---|
| Sovereignty belongs to Allah | Preamble: "sovereignty over the entire Universe belongs to Almighty Allah alone" |
| Authority is a trust | Preamble: authority exercised by the people "is a sacred trust" |
| Consultation | Preamble: the State acts "through the chosen representatives of the people" |
| Justice and equality | Article 25: equality of citizens before law |
| Islamic principles in public life | Article 2 (Islam the State religion); Article 31 (Islamic way of life); Article 227 (laws to conform with the Injunctions of Islam) |
| Rights of non-Muslims | Preamble; Article 20 (freedom of religion); Article 36 (protection of minorities) |

**Not a theocracy.** This is where the second pupil in Bilal's class went wrong. A <Glossary term="Theocracy" /> is a state ruled by a class of religious leaders who claim to govern in God's name, without the consent of the people. The Islamic concept of state, as Pakistan's Constitution expresses it, is different. Authority is a trust held by the *people*, and it is exercised by their *elected* representatives. Pakistan has a parliament, elections, courts and fundamental rights. Islam provides the principles; the people, through democratic institutions, govern. So "Islamic state" in Pakistan's sense does not mean rule by the ulema. It means a democratic state guided by Islamic principles of justice, equality, consultation and welfare.

**Its role in the ideology.** The Islamic concept of state gives the Ideology of Pakistan its moral content: the values the state is meant to serve. The Two-Nation Theory explains who the nation is and why it needed a home; the Islamic concept of state explains what kind of home it should be.

### Vision of Quaid-e-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah

<Glossary term="Quaid-e-Azam" /> ("the Great Leader") is the title by which Pakistanis remember Muhammad Ali Jinnah (1876-1948), who led the Muslim League to independence and became Pakistan's first Governor-General. His *vision* means his picture of what Pakistan should become. The best evidence for it is his own words, read in context. Here are its main points, each tied to a source.

1. **A homeland for a nation.** In March 1940 at Lahore he argued that the Muslims of South Asia were a nation and needed their own homeland to develop "to the fullest our spiritual, cultural, economic, social, and political life" in their own way (Jinnah, 1940/1983). This is the Two-Nation Theory in his own voice.
2. **A democratic, constitutional state.** He was a lawyer and a constitutionalist. He wanted Pakistan's constitution to be made by an elected Constituent Assembly, not imposed by one person.
3. **Equal citizenship and freedom of religion.** On 11 August 1947, three days before independence, he told the Constituent Assembly: "You are free; you are free to go to your temples," and went on to name mosques and every other place of worship. He said that "we are all citizens, and equal citizens, of one State" (Jinnah, 1947/1969). This is the clearest statement of his vision for minorities.
4. **Rule of law and honest government.** In the same address he said "the first duty of a government is to maintain law and order", and he called bribery and corruption "a poison" (Jinnah, 1947/1969).
5. **Unity, Faith, Discipline.** He gave the nation this three-word motto, asking citizens to stand together across provinces, languages and sects.
6. **Islamic principles of justice and equality.** He often spoke of Islam as a source of democracy, equality and social justice. His vision joined Islamic values with democratic government, which is the same balance you saw in the Objectives Resolution.

**A worked example: reading a source.** When you teach the Quaid's vision, do not hand pupils a list of slogans. Teach them to read his words. Here are four steps, applied to the 11 August 1947 address.

1. **Who and when?** Quaid-e-Azam, as President of the Constituent Assembly, on 11 August 1947, three days before independence.
2. **Who was listening?** The members of the Assembly, who were about to write the constitution, and through the newspapers, the people of the new country, including millions of non-Muslims.
3. **What does it say?** Every citizen is free to worship as they choose; all are equal citizens of one State; the government's first duty is law and order; corruption is a poison.
4. **What does it tell us about his vision?** He wanted a state in which religion would not decide a person's rights as a citizen, and in which government would be honest and lawful.

Notice that step 4 is an inference from the evidence, not a slogan. Some people today stress this address; others stress his many speeches about Islam. A careful reader keeps both in view: the Quaid's vision was of a state shaped by Islamic values of justice and equality, governed democratically, in which every citizen was equal. When you teach it, let pupils read his words and draw conclusions from them.

**Bringing the three ideas together.** You can now see how the three sub-topics fit into one ideology. Compare the two foundations side by side:

| Question | Two-Nation Theory | Islamic concept of state |
|---|---|---|
| What question does it answer? | Why should Pakistan exist as a separate country? | How should Pakistan be governed? |
| Main idea | South Asian Muslims are a separate nation | Sovereignty belongs to Allah; authority is a trust exercised by the people |
| Main evidence | Iqbal 1930; Jinnah 1940; Lahore Resolution | Objectives Resolution 1949; Preamble and Articles 2, 2A, 31 and 227 |
| What it gives the ideology | The nation and its right to a homeland | The values and principles of the state |

Quaid-e-Azam's vision is the bridge between them. He argued for the separate nation, and he described the kind of state that nation should build: democratic, lawful, Islamic in its values, and equal for all citizens.

## Activity: Source detectives

Work in groups of four, as a jigsaw. About 30 minutes.

1. Each member takes one source: (A) Iqbal's Allahabad address, 1930; (B) Jinnah's Lahore address, 1940; (C) the Objectives Resolution, 1949, as it appears in the Preamble of the Constitution; (D) Jinnah's address of 11 August 1947. Use the short quotations and descriptions in this topic. If your tutor provides fuller extracts, use those.
2. Working alone for 10 minutes, apply the four source-reading steps to your source: who and when, who was listening, what it says, and what it tells us.
3. Take turns to teach your source to the group in two minutes each.
4. Together, sort the four sources: which ones mainly support the Two-Nation Theory, which mainly express the Islamic concept of state, and which ones show Quaid-e-Azam's vision? One source may fit more than one place. Agree on a reason for each decision.
5. As a group, write one answer of three or four sentences to Rahul's question from the opening situation, using at least two of the sources as evidence.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* List the five milestones on the timeline from 1857 to 1947 and state in one line what happened at each.
2. *(Remember)* Name four features of the Islamic concept of state.
3. *(Understand)* Explain, in two or three sentences, why the Two-Nation Theory does not mean that Hindus and Muslims cannot live together.
4. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between an Islamic state in the sense of Pakistan's Constitution and a theocracy.
5. *(Apply)* A pupil says, "Quaid-e-Azam never said anything about minorities." Using the four source-reading steps, show the pupil the evidence that answers this claim.

## Summary

Pakistan rests on three connected foundations. The Two-Nation Theory held that the Muslims of South Asia were a separate nation; it grew from the reform era after 1857 through 1906, Iqbal's address of 1930 and the Lahore Resolution of 1940, and it explains why Pakistan exists. It is a claim about political nationhood, not a claim that people of different faiths cannot live together. The Islamic concept of state holds that sovereignty belongs to Allah and that authority is a trust exercised by the people's chosen representatives with justice, consultation, equality and protection for non-Muslims; through the Objectives Resolution it entered the Constitution, and it is not a theocracy. Quaid-e-Azam's vision joined these ideas: a homeland for a nation, governed democratically under the rule of law, guided by Islamic values, with equal citizenship for all.
