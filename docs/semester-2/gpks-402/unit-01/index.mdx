# Introduction and Ideological Foundations

Every pupil in Pakistan studies Pakistan Studies at some point, and every teacher will one day be asked a question about it. "Why was Pakistan made?" "What does the Ideology of Pakistan mean?" "Why do we study this when we already know our own country?" This unit gives you clear, honest answers to questions like these.

The unit has two parts. The first part explains what Pakistan Studies is, what it covers, and why it matters for a teacher. It also introduces two ideas that run through the whole course: national ideology and national identity. The second part goes back to the roots. It explains the three ideas on which Pakistan was founded: the Two-Nation Theory, the Islamic concept of state, and the vision of Quaid-e-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah. Units 2 to 6 build on these foundations, so take your time here.

## Unit learning outcomes

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. Explain the meaning, scope and importance of Pakistan Studies, and show that it is more than a history subject.
2. Explain what a national ideology and a national identity are, and describe how a person can hold several identities at once.
3. Describe how Pakistan Studies is taught in schools and what this asks of a teacher.
4. Explain the origin and significance of the Two-Nation Theory, and correct the common belief that it means Hindus and Muslims cannot live together.
5. Explain the main features of the Islamic concept of state and show where they appear in the Constitution of Pakistan.
6. Describe the main points of Quaid-e-Azam's vision for Pakistan, using his own speeches as evidence.
7. Analyse how the Two-Nation Theory and the Islamic concept of state each contributed to the Ideology of Pakistan.

## Prerequisite knowledge

None beyond HSC-level general knowledge. This is the opening unit of the course. You will have met some of these ideas in Pakistan Studies at matric and intermediate level, perhaps as dates and names to memorise. This unit asks you to go further: to understand the ideas, to use evidence, and to think about how you will teach them.

## How to use this unit

Each topic is one complete learning cycle. Start with the classroom situation and ask yourself how you would respond. Then read the explanation, which answers that situation step by step. Do the activity with a partner or a small group, because talking an idea through is the fastest way to find the gaps in your understanding.

The "Check your understanding" questions are retrieval practice. Answer them from memory first, then look back to check. Use the self-assessment checklist honestly: if you cannot tick a statement, return to that part of the explanation. The practicum task takes the idea into a real school.

When you have finished both topics, attempt the unit assessment. It has 10 multiple-choice questions, 10 short-answer questions and 5 extended questions. Answers and marking guidance are at the end of that page, so try every question before you look.
