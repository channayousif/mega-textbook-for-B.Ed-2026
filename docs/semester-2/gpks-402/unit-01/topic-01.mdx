## A real classroom situation

Sana is a B.Ed trainee on her first practicum at a government girls' high school in Mirpurkhas. Her cooperating teacher hands her the Class 9 Pakistan Studies period for the week. On the first day a pupil named Kavita, whose family is Hindu, raises her hand. "Miss, my brother says Pakistan Studies is just old dates for the exam. Why do we have to learn it?" Before Sana can answer, another pupil adds, "And is it only for Muslims? It keeps talking about the Ideology of Pakistan."

Sana realises she has never had to explain the subject itself. She studied it for her own exams by memorising answers. Now a pupil wants to know what it is for, and another wants to know whether it belongs to her at all. Sana needs answers that are honest, accurate and fair to every child in the room.

## Explanation

### Meaning, scope and importance of Pakistan Studies

**Meaning.** <Glossary term="Pakistan Studies" /> is the study of Pakistan as a whole: its history, its ideology, its constitution and government, its land and resources, its economy, and its society and culture. It brings these together into one subject because they are connected. The story of a country is not only what happened in the past. It is also where people live, how they earn a living, how they are governed, and what they believe and celebrate.

A useful way to hold the meaning is this: Pakistan Studies asks four questions about one country. *How did Pakistan come to be?* (history and ideology). *How is Pakistan governed?* (constitution and civics). *What is Pakistan's land like, and what does it produce?* (geography and economy). *Who are the people of Pakistan, and how do they live together?* (society and culture).

**Scope.** The scope of a subject is the range of topics it covers. Standard textbooks such as Rabbani's *Pakistan Studies* organise the subject around these main areas (Rabbani, n.d.):

- **History and ideology** - the Muslim rule and reform movements in South Asia, the Pakistan Movement from 1906 to 1947, and the ideas on which Pakistan was founded.
- **Constitution and politics** - the Objectives Resolution of 1949, the constitutions of 1956, 1962 and 1973, the federal system, and the rights and duties of citizens.
- **Geography** - physical features such as mountains, plains, deserts and rivers; climate; natural resources; and Pakistan's location in the region.
- **Economy** - agriculture, industry, trade, and the economic problems the country faces.
- **Society and culture** - social structure, the many languages and traditions of the provinces, and the shared culture that links them.
- **Pakistan and the world** - foreign policy and relations with neighbours and international organisations.
- **Contemporary issues** - population growth, poverty, extremism and environmental problems.

This is the same shape as this course. Units 2 and 3 take up history and the constitution; Unit 4 covers government, rights and geography; Unit 5 covers economy and society; Unit 6 covers foreign policy, current issues and the role of education. The Higher Education Commission sets the national curriculum for Pakistan Studies at undergraduate level, and its outline follows the same broad areas (Higher Education Commission, n.d.).

**Correcting a common misconception: "Pakistan Studies is only a history subject."** Many trainees arrive with this belief, because the history part is the part most often tested with dates and names. But look again at the list above. Only one of the seven areas is mainly history. A pupil who can explain why the Indus River matters to farmers in Sindh, or what Article 25 of the Constitution promises every citizen, is doing Pakistan Studies just as much as a pupil who knows the date of the Lahore Resolution.

Here is a worked example of how the areas connect. Take one place: the Sukkur Barrage on the Indus.

1. **Geography**: it sits on the Indus, Pakistan's main river, in the plains of Sindh.
2. **Economy**: its canals water a large area of farmland, so it supports crops such as wheat and cotton and the jobs that depend on them.
3. **History**: it was built during British rule, in the 1920s and early 1930s, as part of a large irrigation plan.
4. **Constitution and civics**: sharing river water between provinces is a federal question, decided through national institutions.
5. **Society**: villages, markets and family livelihoods along the canals depend on it.

One structure, five areas. That is why Pakistan Studies is taught as one subject rather than as separate pieces.

**Importance.** Why should anyone study Pakistan Studies? There are four main reasons.

1. **Informed citizenship.** A citizen who understands how the country is governed, what rights the Constitution guarantees, and what problems the country faces can take part in national life with knowledge rather than rumour.
2. **Understanding identity.** The subject helps people understand where the country came from and why. This gives them a grounded sense of belonging, based on understanding rather than slogans.
3. **National integration.** Pakistan is a diverse country. Learning about all its regions, languages and communities helps citizens see one another as members of the same nation.
4. **Critical thinking.** A good Pakistan Studies lesson teaches pupils to ask "How do we know?" and to look at evidence: a speech, a map, a constitutional article, a set of figures.

### National ideology and identity

Two terms appear throughout this course, and they are often confused. It helps to separate them clearly.

**Ideology.** An <Glossary term="Ideology" /> is a set of shared ideas and beliefs about how a group of people should live together and be governed. A national ideology answers questions such as: Why does this nation exist? What does it stand for? What kind of society does it want to build? The <Glossary term="Ideology of Pakistan" /> is the set of ideas on which Pakistan was founded. In short, it holds that the Muslims of South Asia formed a separate nation and needed a homeland where they could live according to the principles of Islam, govern themselves democratically, and protect the rights of all citizens, including minorities. Topic 1.2 explains where these ideas came from.

**Identity.** <Glossary term="National identity" /> is a people's shared sense of who they are and what they belong to. Ideology is about *ideas*; identity is about *belonging*. A national identity is built from many things: a shared history, a homeland, symbols such as the flag and the national anthem, a national language, shared values, and shared hopes for the future.

**Layered identity.** A person does not have only one identity. A girl in Larkana may think of herself as a Sindhi, a Pakistani, a Muslim, a daughter, a pupil, and a future doctor, all at the same time. These identities are layered, like the rings of a tree. They do not cancel each other out. A strong national identity does not ask people to give up their language, province or community. It adds a shared layer that links them to everyone else in the country.

The Constitution of Pakistan reflects this layered view. Article 251 makes Urdu the national language, and the same article allows a provincial assembly to promote a provincial language alongside it. Article 33 asks the State to discourage "parochial, racial, tribal sectarian and provincial prejudices" among citizens (Government of Pakistan, 1973/2012). In other words, the Constitution protects both the national layer and the regional layers, and it treats prejudice between groups as the problem, not diversity itself.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Ideology and religion are the same thing."** They are related, but they are not the same. Religion is a faith: a set of beliefs about God, worship and moral life. An ideology is a set of ideas about how a society should be organised and governed. The Ideology of Pakistan *draws on* Islam: it takes Islamic principles such as justice, equality and consultation as guides for public life. But it also includes political ideas, such as democracy, the rule of law, the separate nationhood of South Asian Muslims, and the protection of minorities. A pupil who says "the Ideology of Pakistan just means Islam" has seen one important part of it and missed the rest. This difference matters for Kavita in the opening situation: the Ideology of Pakistan includes, from the start, a promise of equal citizenship to non-Muslims.

### Pakistan Studies in educational context

Pakistan Studies is not only an academic subject. It is also a school subject, and that changes how it must be taught.

**Where pupils meet it.** In most Pakistani schools, children first meet these ideas in Social Studies at primary and middle level. There they learn about their family, their community, their province and their country. At secondary level (Classes 9 and 10) and again at intermediate level, Pakistan Studies is taught as a separate, compulsory subject. At university level, students in most degree programmes take a course on Pakistan's ideology and constitution. So by the time a person becomes a teacher, they have studied the subject several times. The problem is that many people studied it mainly by memorising.

**What the subject asks of a teacher.** Because the subject is about the pupils' own country, teaching it carries special responsibilities.

1. **Accuracy.** Teach what the evidence shows. Use dates, documents and maps correctly. When historians disagree, say so in simple words.
2. **Fairness.** Every child in the class is a citizen of Pakistan. Muslim and non-Muslim pupils, pupils of every province and every language, must all be able to see themselves in the subject. Article 25 of the Constitution states that "All citizens are equal before law" (Government of Pakistan, 1973/2012). A Pakistan Studies teacher should model that equality in the classroom.
3. **Connection.** Link the national story to the pupils' own place. A lesson on the Indus can begin at the canal outside the school. A lesson on the Pakistan Movement can ask what pupils' grandparents remember.
4. **Thinking, not only remembering.** Pupils should learn to explain, compare and use evidence, not just reproduce notes.

**A worked example: two lessons on the same topic.** Here is how one Class 9 topic, "the importance of Pakistan Studies", can be taught in two ways.

*Lesson A.* The teacher writes five points on the board, pupils copy them, and the next day the teacher asks pupils to recite them. Pupils can repeat "Pakistan Studies creates national unity" without being able to say how.

*Lesson B.* The teacher shows a map of Pakistan and asks each pupil to mark where their family comes from. The class sees that their own room holds families from several districts, perhaps several provinces, and speakers of several languages. The teacher asks: "What do all of us share?" Pupils list the flag, the anthem, the Constitution, Quaid-e-Azam, cricket, the national language. The teacher then writes "national identity" on the board and asks pupils to write one sentence explaining it using their own map.

Now analyse the two lessons against the four responsibilities. Lesson A is accurate, but it does not connect to pupils' lives or ask them to think. Lesson B is accurate, it includes every pupil, it starts from the pupils' own place, and it asks them to build the idea themselves. Both cover the same syllabus point. Only Lesson B serves the aims of the subject.

**Correcting a common misconception: "Pakistan Studies has no relevance to classroom teaching."** Some trainees think the subject is a general education requirement that has nothing to do with their teaching career, especially if they plan to teach science, English or mathematics. This is not true, for three reasons. First, every teacher in Pakistan teaches citizens, and the values of the Constitution, such as equality and the rule of law, apply in every classroom. Second, pupils bring questions about the country into every subject, and a teacher needs accurate answers. Third, many primary teachers teach Social Studies alongside other subjects, so they will teach this content directly. The responsibilities above apply to every teacher, not only to the Pakistan Studies specialist.

**Returning to Sana.** Sana can now answer both pupils. To the first: Pakistan Studies is not just old dates. It is the study of the whole country (its land, its economy, its government, its people and its past) so that you can understand it and take part in it as a citizen. To Kavita: yes, it belongs to you. The Ideology of Pakistan and the Constitution both promise equal citizenship to every Pakistani, and the subject is about the country you share with everyone in this room.

## Activity: One place, five areas

Work in groups of three or four. About 25 minutes.

1. As a group, choose one real place that every member knows: a river, a market, a shrine, a factory, a railway station, a school, or a monument in your district. (Examples: Keenjhar Lake, the Hyderabad bangle market, the shrine of Shah Abdul Latif Bhitai at Bhit Shah, Thar coalfield.)
2. Draw a simple concept map with your place in the centre, like the worked example of the Sukkur Barrage.
3. Add at least one link for each of the five areas: history, geography, economy, constitution and civics, and society and culture. Each link needs one short, accurate fact. Where you are not sure of a fact, write a question mark and the question you would need to research.
4. Add one more link: "What a Class 9 pupil could learn from this place about national identity."
5. Swap maps with another group. Check their map: does every area have a link? Is each fact accurate, or is it marked as a question? Write one suggestion on their map.

Be ready to tell the class which area was hardest to link to your place, and why.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* In one or two sentences, state the meaning of Pakistan Studies, and name five of the areas it covers.
2. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between a national ideology and a national identity. Give one example of each from Pakistan.
3. *(Understand)* A classmate says, "The Ideology of Pakistan and Islam are the same thing." Explain, in three or four sentences, what is right and what is missing in this statement.
4. *(Apply)* A pupil in Quetta describes herself as "Baloch, Pakistani and Muslim". Using the idea of layered identity, explain to her in simple words why these identities do not conflict.
5. *(Apply)* You are teaching Class 9 in a school where some pupils are Christian. Using the Constitution, state one thing you will say and one thing you will do to make sure these pupils feel included in a lesson on national identity.

## Summary

Pakistan Studies is the study of Pakistan as a whole: its history and ideology, constitution and civics, geography, economy, society and culture, foreign relations and contemporary issues. It is far more than a history subject, because these areas are connected in every real place. A national ideology is a set of ideas about why a nation exists and what it stands for; a national identity is a shared sense of belonging. People hold layered identities, and the Constitution protects both the national and the regional layers. The Ideology of Pakistan draws on Islam but is not the same as religion; it includes democracy, separate Muslim nationhood and equal citizenship for minorities. In school, the subject asks every teacher for accuracy, fairness, connection to pupils' lives, and thinking rather than memorising.

## Summative task

Read the description of a lesson below, then write a short analysis of 200-250 words.

*Mr Arif teaches Pakistan Studies to Class 10 in a town school in Nawabshah. For the topic "national identity", he dictates a definition and five points from a guidebook. He tells the class that "a true Pakistani speaks only Urdu at school". When a pupil asks why her Sindhi poems are not part of the lesson, he says that is a matter for the Sindhi period.*

In your analysis: (a) identify what Mr Arif does well, if anything; (b) analyse two problems in his lesson, using the ideas of layered identity and the teacher's responsibilities from this topic; (c) use one article of the Constitution as evidence; and (d) propose two specific changes that would make the lesson serve the aims of Pakistan Studies.
