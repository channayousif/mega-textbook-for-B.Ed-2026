## A real classroom situation

A teacher in a government secondary school in Hyderabad notices that the girls in her class stop raising their hands after the first month of term. When she asks a colleague why, the colleague says, "That is just how girls are here - they become shy." The teacher is not sure that explanation is right. She wonders whether the school itself, the community's expectations, or the way the classroom is organised is shaping her pupils' behaviour. She does not have a name for the kind of thinking she is doing, but she is already asking a sociological question.

Her question is the starting point of this unit. Sociology gives her a way to move beyond "that is just how things are" and ask what social forces are at work.

## Explanation

### Definition of sociology

<Glossary term="Sociology" /> is the systematic study of human social life, groups, and societies. It examines how social structures, relationships, and institutions shape individual behaviour and collective life (Givens & White, 2023). Unlike casual observation, sociology relies on evidence, methods, and theories that can be tested and debated.

A simple way to hold the definition: sociology asks not just *what* people do, but *what social conditions* make them do it, and *what consequences* follow for the wider community.

### The scope of sociology

Sociology's scope is broad. It studies everything from a two-person conversation in a school corridor to the global forces that shape Pakistan's education system. The scope includes:

- **Social relationships** - how people relate to one another in families, classrooms, workplaces, and communities.
- **Social institutions** - the organized patterns of behaviour that meet society's needs (family, education, religion, economy, politics, healthcare).
- **Social structures** - the arrangements of statuses, roles, and groups that organise collective life.
- **Social processes** - cooperation, competition, conflict, socialization, and social change.
- **Social problems** - poverty, crime, inequality, and other conditions that affect large numbers of people.

The scope is not limited to any one country or culture. Sociologists study both the local (a village school in interior Sindh) and the global (how international policy shapes national curricula).

### The nature of sociology

Sociology is both a science and a social science. This distinction matters.

As a **science**, sociology uses systematic methods to collect and analyse evidence: surveys, interviews, observation, and statistical analysis. It does not rely on personal opinion or tradition. A sociologist studying teacher absenteeism in Punjab would gather data from multiple schools, not just report one teacher's experience.

As a **social science**, sociology studies people, who are more complex than atoms or cells. People have intentions, meanings, and cultures. This means sociology cannot use exactly the same methods as physics or chemistry, but it shares the commitment to evidence, logic, and testable claims (Henson, 2022).

### Sociology as a science

Some people question whether sociology can truly be a science because human behaviour is unpredictable. The response from the discipline is that patterns exist even in complex behaviour. For example, educational attainment in Pakistan correlates strongly with household income and parental education level. That correlation is a social fact - it is measurable, stable, and can be explained sociologically. Sociology's scientific character lies in its methods, not in a claim that people behave like machines.

### Importance of sociology

For a trainee teacher, sociology matters in at least four ways:

1. **It sharpens observation.** Instead of seeing a pupil's silence as "shyness", a sociologically trained teacher asks what classroom or community norms produce that silence.
2. **It reveals inequality.** Sociology shows that differences in attainment are not just about individual ability but are shaped by social class, gender, language, and access to resources.
3. **It improves practice.** Understanding how groups function, how institutions shape behaviour, and how social change happens makes a teacher more effective and more adaptable.
4. **It builds the sociological imagination** - the habit of connecting personal troubles to wider social forces. This is the single most useful habit a sociology course can develop.

Consider a concrete example: in many Pakistani schools, pupils from rural areas underperform compared to urban peers. Common sense might attribute this to "less intelligence". Sociology shows that the difference is partly structural: rural schools often have fewer qualified teachers, less materials, and a curriculum that assumes urban experiences. The problem is not the pupil; it is the social arrangement. That reframe changes what a teacher does about it.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees arrive thinking that sociology is just "common sense" - things we already know about people. It is not. Common sense says "people are poor because they are lazy"; sociology asks what social conditions produce and maintain poverty. Common sense says "girls are naturally shy"; sociology examines how gender norms are taught and enforced. Sociology replaces personal judgement with systematic evidence.

## Activity: Is this sociology?

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Below are five statements. For each one, decide whether it is a sociological claim (evidence-based and about social patterns) or a common-sense claim (based on personal belief or tradition). Write one sentence explaining your reasoning for each.

1. "Children from educated families always do better in school."
2. "Our village has always been peaceful because we are good people."
3. "Teacher absenteeism in rural Sindh is twice as high as in urban Sindh, according to a survey of 200 schools."
4. "Boys are naturally better at science than girls."
5. "Schools with parent-teacher committees have 15 per cent higher attendance, according to a district education report."

Compare your answers with another pair. Where you disagree, discuss what evidence would settle the question.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* In your own words, define sociology in one sentence that includes the words "systematic", "social life", and "evidence".
2. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between sociology and common sense, using the example of pupil underperformance.
3. *(Apply)* A friend says, "Sociology is just psychology with a bigger sample size." Using what you have learned about sociology's scope and nature, give two reasons that statement is wrong.

## Summary

Sociology is the systematic, evidence-based study of human social life, groups, and institutions. Its scope covers relationships, institutions, structures, processes, and problems. It is a social science: it uses scientific methods while recognising the complexity of human meaning and culture. For a teacher, sociology sharpens observation, reveals inequality, improves practice, and builds the sociological imagination. It is not common sense; it replaces personal judgement with systematic evidence.

At your practicum school, observe one moment that puzzles you - a pattern of behaviour, a rule, a difference between groups of pupils. Write down what you saw in two sentences, then write one sociological question about it (a question about social patterns, not about individual personality). Bring your notes to the next session.

## Summative task

Take the common-sense claim: **"Our school's results are poor because the pupils are not intelligent enough."** Write a short paragraph (80-120 words) explaining why a sociologist would reject that claim. In your paragraph, name at least two social factors that the claim ignores and explain why sociology looks for evidence rather than accepting common sense.
