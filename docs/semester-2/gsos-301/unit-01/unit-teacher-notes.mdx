## Teaching strategies for Unit 1

### Opening the unit

This is the trainees' first encounter with sociology. Many will arrive with the belief that sociology is "just common sense" or "psychology but for groups". Use the opening vignette (Topic 1.1) to surface these expectations directly. Ask the class: "Have you ever been told that a school problem is 'just the way things are here'? What would it mean to question that?" This establishes the unit's central move: replacing "that is just how it is" with "what social forces produce this pattern".

### Topic 1.1 - What sociology is

**Key pedagogical move:** Contrast sociology and common sense using a single concrete example. Use the "Is this sociology?" activity early. Trainees will struggle most with the idea that sociology is a *method*, not just a *subject matter*. Emphasize that the discipline is defined by how it gathers and tests claims, not by what it studies.

**Likely wrong answer:** "Sociology is the same as social work because both deal with social problems." Correct by distinguishing: social work intervenes to help individuals and communities; sociology studies the structures that produce problems. Both are valuable; they are different kinds of activity.

**Good discussion prompt:** "Think of a school in your neighbourhood. What is one pattern you have noticed that sociology could help explain?" Let trainees generate examples before introducing the definition. Then show how the definition fits their examples.

### Topic 1.2 - The founders

**Key pedagogical move:** Do not teach the founders as a list to memorize. Teach them as *four different ways of seeing*. Use the "Which founder would say that?" activity as the main learning event. Each scenario should make the founder's perspective visible through application.

**Likely wrong answer:** "Marx and Weber both studied conflict, so they said the same thing." Correct by distinguishing: Marx focused on economic conflict between classes; Weber focused on meaning, status, and the independent power of ideas. They overlap but are not the same.

**Good discussion prompt:** "Which founder would be most useful for understanding teacher absenteeism in rural Sindh? Why?" This forces trainees to choose and justify, not just recall.

### Topic 1.3 - The three perspectives

**Key pedagogical move:** Use a single school practice (morning assembly, school uniform rule, exam system, parent-teacher meeting) and analyse it from all three perspectives. The "Three lenses on one school" activity does this. The point is not to pick the "right" perspective but to see what each reveals and conceals.

**Likely wrong answer:** "Conflict theory is right because there is inequality everywhere." Correct by explaining that each perspective illuminates what the others miss. Functionalism shows what holds together; conflict theory shows who gains and loses; symbolic interactionism shows how meaning is made. A full analysis uses all three.

**Good discussion prompt:** "Can a practice serve a function AND reflect inequality at the same time?" The answer is yes, and this insight is the payoff of the topic.

## Sequencing and pacing

The unit is designed for approximately 6-8 hours of contact time, distributed as:

| Topic | Contact hours | Main activity |
|---|---|---|
| 1.1 What sociology is | 2 hours | "Is this sociology?" pairs activity |
| 1.2 The founders | 2-3 hours | "Which founder would say that?" group activity |
| 1.3 The three perspectives | 2-3 hours | "Three lenses on one school" group activity |

The unit-assessment is designed for independent study or a supervised examination session (approximately 90 minutes for the full bank, or 45 minutes for a selection).

## Practical work and assignments

The guide's "Practical Work" section lists: Hands-on Computer Exercises, Individual Assignments, Group Assignments, Presentations, and Internet-Research tasks. For Unit 1, these map naturally:

1. **Internet-research task (individual):** Each trainee finds one reliable source (UNESCO, World Bank, Pakistan Bureau of Statistics, or an open-access academic article) that provides evidence about a social pattern in Pakistani education. They write one paragraph (80-120 words) describing the pattern and citing the source. This develops the evidence-based reasoning Topic 1.1 introduces.

2. **Group assignment:** Small groups (3-4 trainees) choose one Pakistani school practice and analyse it from two of the three perspectives. They present their analysis in a 5-minute presentation. This directly applies Topic 1.3.

3. **Individual written assignment:** Each trainee writes a short essay (300-400 words) applying one founder's ideas to explain a social pattern they have observed in their community. This deepens Topic 1.2.

## Handling trainee misconceptions

Three misconceptions are especially common at this stage and should be addressed directly:

1. **"Sociology is common sense."** Reframe: common sense is a starting point; sociology tests common-sense claims against evidence and looks for patterns that common sense misses.

2. **"One perspective is right and the others are wrong."** Reframe: the perspectives are tools, not truths. A mechanic does not ask whether a spanner is "right" and a screwdriver "wrong" - they pick the tool for the job. The same applies here.

3. **"Sociology is depressing because it only talks about problems."** Reframe: sociology also explains cooperation, solidarity, social change, and the institutions that make collective life possible. Conflict theory is one lens among three.

The unit-assessment bank (10 MCQ / 10 RRQ / 5 ERQ) is designed for flexible use. For a formative check, select 3-4 MCQs and 2-3 RRQs.
