## A real classroom situation

A head teacher in a government school in Larkana is reviewing the year's exam results. She notices that pupils from landlord families pass at higher rates than those from tenant families, even when both groups are taught by the same teachers in the same classrooms. She wonders why. She does not use the word "stratification", but she has spotted a pattern that sociology has studied for over a century. This topic introduces the four thinkers whose ideas give her the tools to understand what she is seeing.

## Explanation

### Auguste Comte (1798-1857): the father of sociology

Auguste Comte, a French philosopher, coined the term "sociology" in 1838. He argued that society could be studied scientifically, just like the natural sciences study the physical world. Comte called this approach **positivism**: the idea that reliable knowledge comes from observing and measuring social facts, not from speculation or religion (Henson, 2022).

Comte believed that societies pass through three stages of thought: the theological (explaining things by God or spirits), the metaphysical (explaining things by abstract forces), and the positive (explaining things by observed evidence). He saw sociology as the highest achievement of the positive stage - the "queen of the sciences" that would guide social reform.

For a teacher, Comte's legacy is simple but important: social life has patterns that can be observed and measured. When a head teacher notices a difference in pass rates between social groups, she is doing what Comte would recognize - looking for a social fact, not an act of God or a matter of luck.

### Karl Marx (1818-1883): class conflict and capitalism

Karl Marx, a German philosopher and economist, saw society through the lens of **class conflict**. He argued that the key to understanding any society lies in who owns the means of production (land, factories, tools) and who must work for those owners (Givens & White, 2023).

In capitalist society, Marx identified two main classes: the **bourgeoisie** (those who own the means of production) and the **proletariat** (those who sell their labour). The relationship between these classes is inherently unequal and conflictual, because the owners profit from the workers' labour while paying them less than the value their labour produces. This arrangement, Marx argued, produces exploitation, alienation, and eventually revolution.

Marx also argued that the ruling ideas in any society are the ideas of the ruling class. Schools, media, religion, and law tend to reflect and reinforce the interests of those who hold economic power. This insight helps explain why curricula often reflect urban, middle-class experiences and why the knowledge of poorer communities is often treated as less valuable.

A Pakistani example: the large difference in educational resources between elite private schools and government schools is not an accident. It reflects the economic and political power of the families who attend each type of school. Marx would see this as a structural feature of the system, not a natural difference in quality.

### Emile Durkheim (1858-1917): social facts and solidarity

Emile Durkheim, a French sociologist, is often considered the first sociologist to make the discipline fully scientific. He argued that sociology should study **social facts** - patterns of behaviour that exist outside any individual and exercise constraint over them (Henson, 2022). Language, law, moral rules, and religious practices are all social facts. You did not invent Urdu, but it shapes how you think and communicate.

Durkheim's most famous study examined **suicide**. He showed that suicide rates are remarkably stable across time and differ predictably between groups (Protestants vs Catholics, married vs single, wartime vs peacetime). This proved that even the most personal decision is shaped by social forces - the degree of integration and regulation a person experiences in society.

Durkheim also distinguished between **mechanical solidarity** (the bonding of small, traditional societies where people share similar beliefs and tasks) and **organic solidarity** (the bonding of complex modern societies where people depend on each other's specialised roles). A village in Tharparkar, where most families farm and share the same customs, illustrates mechanical solidarity. A city like Karachi, where the electricity worker, the schoolteacher, the rickshaw driver, and the shopkeeper all depend on each other, illustrates organic solidarity.

For a teacher, Durkheim's key insight is that schools are powerful agents of social integration. They transmit shared norms and values, connect pupils to wider society, and give individuals a sense of belonging. When a school fails to do this - when pupils feel alienated or excluded - the consequences can be severe.

### Max Weber (1864-1920): meaning, culture, and bureaucracy

Max Weber, a German sociologist, agreed with Marx that economic structures matter, but he argued that ideas and culture also have independent power. Weber introduced the concept of **verstehen** - empathetic understanding, or the effort to grasp the meanings people attach to their own actions (Givens & White, 2023). Sociology is not just about counting; it is about understanding why people do what they do.

Weber's most famous argument links Protestantism to the rise of capitalism. He showed that certain Protestant beliefs (especially Calvinist ideas about hard work as a sign of salvation) created a cultural environment in which capitalism flourished. This was a direct challenge to Marx's claim that economic structure alone explains everything. Weber argued that ideas can also shape economic life.

Weber also studied **bureaucracy** - the rational, rule-based form of organisation that dominates modern life. He saw bureaucracy as efficient and predictable but also as an "iron cage" that traps individuals in impersonal rules and procedures. A teacher who must follow a rigid curriculum, file multiple reports, and teach to a standardised exam is working inside a Weberian bureaucracy. Weber would recognise both its benefits (fairness, consistency) and its costs (creativity suppressed, individual needs overlooked).

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think the four founders agreed with each other. They did not. Comte wanted a science of social order; Marx wanted to expose and change inequality; Durkheim wanted to understand social integration; Weber wanted to understand meaning and culture. Their disagreements are not a weakness of sociology; they are its strength. The discipline is richer because it contains multiple ways of seeing.

## Activity: Which founder would say that?

Work in groups of three. About 25 minutes.

Each group receives three scenario cards. For each scenario, discuss which founder's ideas best explain it and why. Write one sentence per scenario naming the founder and the key concept.

**Scenario A:** A factory owner in Faisalabad pays workers the minimum wage while earning large profits. Workers complain but fear they will be dismissed if they organise.

**Scenario B:** A village in Punjab has one school. Children walk 5 kilometres each way. The teacher notices that pupils who miss school for harvest season often drop out permanently. The whole village shares the same religious practices and expects children to follow their parents' occupations.

**Scenario C:** A government school in Karachi requires parents to submit five documents for admission. Many families from katchi abadis cannot provide all five and give up. The rule was designed to prevent fraud, but it excludes the poorest families.

Compare your answers across groups. Discuss which scenarios could be explained by more than one founder and why.

## Summary

Auguste Comte gave sociology its name and argued that society can be studied scientifically through positivism. Karl Marx centred class conflict and the exploitation of labour under capitalism, arguing that ruling ideas serve ruling-class interests. Emile Durkheim established sociology as the study of social facts and showed that even the most personal acts are shaped by social forces. Max Weber added that meaning, culture, and ideas have independent power, and he analysed the double-edged nature of bureaucracy. Together, these four thinkers built the foundations on which the three major perspectives rest.
