## A real classroom situation

A new government secondary school opens in a small town in southern Punjab. Within a year, patterns emerge: the science stream attracts mostly boys from landowning families; the arts stream has more girls and children of shopkeepers; the school's morning assembly reinforces a shared sense of pride and discipline; and a quiet hierarchy forms among pupils based on who can afford the recommended textbooks. Three different sociologists walk into this school and see three different things. This topic explains what each of them sees and why.

## Explanation

A sociological **perspective** is a set of assumptions about what society is like, what matters most, and what questions to ask. The three major perspectives introduced here - functionalism, conflict theory, and symbolic interactionism - each illuminate different aspects of social life. None is complete on its own; together they give a fuller picture.

### Functionalism

Functionalism sees society as a complex system whose parts work together to produce stability and order. It asks: what function does this institution, practice, or pattern serve for society as a whole?

**Core assumptions:**

1. Society is a system of interrelated parts. Change in one part affects the others.
2. Each part has a **function** - it contributes to the maintenance and stability of the whole.
3. Social patterns persist because they meet a need. Even practices that seem harmful often serve a function (though functionalists also recognize **dysfunctions** - patterns that disrupt social order).
4. Consensus is normal; shared values and norms bind society together.

Emile Durkheim is the key founding thinker. Later theorists such as Talcott Parsons and Robert Merton developed the perspective further. Merton distinguished between **manifest functions** (the intended, recognized consequences of a pattern) and **latent functions** (the unintended, often unrecognized ones) (Givens & White, 2023).

**A Pakistani example:** Consider the institution of the extended family in rural Sindh. Its manifest function is to provide care for children, the elderly, and the sick. Its latent functions include pooling economic resources, arranging marriages, and transmitting land ownership. From a functionalist viewpoint, the extended family persists because it meets these needs in a context where the state provides limited social services.

**Strengths and limits:** Functionalism is good at showing how institutions fit together and why they persist. Its weakness is that it can overstate consensus and understate conflict. Not every practice serves a beneficial function for everyone; some serve the powerful at the expense of others.

### Conflict Theory

Conflict theory sees society as an arena of inequality, competition, and struggle over scarce resources. It asks: who benefits from this arrangement, and who pays the cost?

**Core assumptions:**

1. Society is structured by inequality - of class, gender, ethnicity, religion, and other divisions.
2. Social arrangements benefit some groups and disadvantage others. What looks like "the way things are" is often the way powerful groups have made them.
3. Conflict is normal and often drives social change. Stability is not consensus; it is the result of one group's dominance over others.
4. Ideas, institutions, and culture tend to reflect and reinforce the interests of the dominant group.

Karl Marx is the key founding thinker. Later theorists extended the perspective to gender (feminist theory), race (critical race theory), and other dimensions of inequality (Henson, 2022).

**A Pakistani example:** Consider the school system itself. Elite private schools in Karachi and Lahore offer Cambridge curricula, air-conditioned classrooms, and foreign faculty. Government schools in the same cities often lack functioning toilets. From a conflict theory viewpoint, this is not a natural difference in quality; it is a product of how economic and political power is distributed. The education system sorts pupils by class, and the curriculum of elite schools confers cultural capital that reproduces privilege across generations.

**Strengths and limits:** Conflict theory is powerful at exposing inequality and showing that social arrangements serve particular interests. Its weakness is that it can overstate conflict and understate cooperation and shared values. Not every interaction is a struggle over power.

### Symbolic Interactionism

Symbolic interactionism sees society as the product of everyday interactions between people. It asks: what meanings do people attach to their actions, and how do those meanings shape social life?

**Core assumptions:**

1. Society is built from the bottom up, through face-to-face interactions.
2. People act toward things on the basis of the meanings those things have for them.
3. Meaning arises from social interaction and is modified through interpretation.
4. Symbols - language, gestures, objects - are the medium through which meaning is shared and negotiated.

George Herbert Mead and Herbert Blumer are the key thinkers. The perspective focuses on the micro-level: a classroom conversation, a playground interaction, a staffroom exchange (Givens & White, 2023).

**A Pakistani example:** Consider a teacher who says, "You are a bright girl - you should become a doctor." The words sound like encouragement. But the girl hears them against a backdrop of community expectations that prioritize her brother's education, family honour, and early marriage. The meaning of the teacher's words is not fixed; it is negotiated in the interaction between what the teacher intends, what the girl hears, and what the wider context suggests. Symbolic interactionism examines how that meaning is made and what consequences follow.

**Strengths and limits:** Symbolic interactionism is powerful at showing how meaning is created and how identities are performed. Its weakness is that it can miss the larger structural forces - class, institutions, policy - that constrain what is possible in any interaction.

### How the perspectives differ

The same school can be analysed from all three perspectives:

| Aspect | Functionalism | Conflict theory | Symbolic interactionism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Morning assembly | Builds shared identity and social cohesion | Reinforces state authority and dominant cultural norms | A ritual where pupils learn how to stand, when to be silent, what "discipline" means |
| The curriculum | Transmits knowledge and skills society needs | Reproduces class advantage; excludes subordinate knowledge | A text pupils interpret differently based on their backgrounds |
| Teacher-pupil relationship | A functional role pair that enables learning | Reflects power asymmetry between adult and child, state and citizen | A negotiated interaction where both parties interpret the other's words and gestures |

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think one perspective must be "right" and the others "wrong". They are not competing truths; they are complementary lenses. A functionalist sees what holds a society together; a conflict theorist sees who gains and who loses; a symbolic interactionist sees how meaning is made in daily life. The richest sociological analysis draws on more than one.

## Activity: Three lenses on one school

Work in groups of four. About 25 minutes.

Read this scenario: **A government girls' school in Quetta has a rule that pupils must wear a dupatta in the classroom. Most pupils comply. A few stop coming to school after the rule is enforced strictly. The head teacher says the rule builds discipline; a parent says it makes school feel unwelcoming to girls from certain families.**

Each group member takes one role:

1. **Functionalist** - explain what function the rule serves for the school as a system.
2. **Conflict theorist** - explain whose interests the rule serves and who it may disadvantage.
3. **Symbolic interactionist** - explain what the dupatta means to different people in this situation and how those meanings are negotiated.
4. **Chair** - lead the discussion and write a one-paragraph summary integrating all three views.

Present your summary to the class. Discuss: which perspective feels most useful for this situation, and why?

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the three major sociological perspectives and the key thinker associated with each.
2. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between functionalism's and conflict theory's view of social order in two or three sentences.
3. *(Apply)* A school introduces a merit scholarship for the top three matriculation results each year. Analyse this policy from the functionalist perspective in two sentences and from the conflict perspective in two sentences.

## Summary

The three major sociological perspectives offer different ways of seeing social life. Functionalism (Durkheim, Parsons, Merton) sees society as a system of interrelated parts working toward stability, asking what function each part serves. Conflict theory (Marx and later theorists) sees society as structured by inequality, asking who benefits and who pays the cost. Symbolic interactionism (Mead, Blumer) sees society as built through everyday interactions, asking what meanings people attach to their actions. Each perspective illuminates what the others miss. Together they form the analytical toolkit a sociologically trained teacher brings to the classroom.

## Summative task

A government school in rural Sindh has a high dropout rate among girls after primary school. Write a short paragraph (120-160 words) analysing this situation from **two** of the three perspectives introduced in this topic. For each perspective, name it, state its core assumption, and explain what it would focus on in this case. Then, in one sentence, state which perspective you find most useful and why.
