## A real classroom situation

A teacher in a government school in Karachi has pupils from several linguistic backgrounds: Urdu-speaking, Punjabi, Pashto, and two children from a Baloch family that recently migrated. During a group activity, one Urdu-speaking pupil says to a Pashto-speaking classmate, "You people are so loud and rough. We are more civilized." The Pashto-speaking pupil goes quiet. The teacher overhears and realizes that the comment is not just a personal insult - it reflects a cultural judgment, one culture measuring itself against another and finding itself superior. The teacher has a chance to turn this into a teaching moment, but first needs the vocabulary to explain what is happening.

## Explanation

### What culture is

<Glossary term="Culture" /> is the shared way of life of a group of people: their language, values, beliefs, norms, customs, symbols, and material objects that they pass from one generation to the next (Givens & White, 2023). Culture is learned, not biological. A child born in Punjab but raised in a household in London will learn the culture of that household, not the culture of Punjab.

Culture has two broad dimensions:
- **Material culture** - the physical objects a society produces and uses: clothing, food, buildings, tools, art, technology.
- **Non-material culture** - the intangible aspects: language, values, beliefs, norms, customs, ideas.

A teacher encounters both every day. The textbook is material culture; the expectation that pupils will stand when the teacher enters is non-material culture.

### The elements of culture

Sociologists commonly identify four key elements:

1. **Norms** - the rules and expectations that guide behaviour in a society. Norms range from informal customs (how to greet an elder) to formal laws (traffic rules). In Pakistani culture, offering tea to a guest is a norm; refusing it without a reason can cause offence. Norms can be **folkways** (informal customs, not strictly enforced), **mores** (strongly held norms with moral weight), or **laws** (formally enforced rules).

2. **Values** - the shared ideals about what is good, desirable, and worthwhile. In Pakistani society, values include respect for elders, the importance of family, hospitality, and religious devotion. Values are abstract; norms are how values translate into everyday action.

3. **Beliefs** - the specific ideas people hold to be true. Beliefs may be religious (the belief in one God), practical (the belief that hard work leads to success), or cultural (the belief that certain foods are appropriate for certain occasions). Values and beliefs are related but distinct: a value is what we hold important; a belief is what we hold to be true.

4. **Symbols** - anything that carries a particular meaning recognized by people who share a culture. Language is the most powerful symbol system. The crescent on Pakistan's flag, the call to prayer, a dupatta, a nikkah certificate - all are symbols that carry cultural meaning. The same symbol can mean different things in different cultures.

Consider a concrete example: the Pakistani tradition of **mehndi** at weddings. It is a symbol (the henna itself), a norm (it is expected at most weddings), a value (it reflects the importance of celebration and family), and a belief (it is associated with blessings and good fortune). Understanding all four elements gives a fuller picture than any one alone.

### Cultural diversity

Pakistan is culturally diverse. Differences exist across provinces (Punjabi, Sindhi, Pashtun, Baloch, and many smaller groups), across rural and urban areas, across social classes, and across religious communities. This diversity is visible in language, dress, food, music, marriage customs, and daily routines.

Cultural diversity is not unique to Pakistan, but Pakistan's diversity is shaped by its geography, history, and the coexistence of multiple ethnic and linguistic groups within one state. A teacher in a multilingual classroom - as most Pakistani classrooms are - must navigate this diversity daily.

**Cultural universals** are patterns found in every culture: family, language, religion, music, food preparation, mourning rituals, and humor. Every culture has these, but each expresses them differently. The universal is "family"; the Pakistani expression is often the extended family with specific obligations to kin.

### Ethnocentrism vs cultural relativism

This is the distinction that matters most for a teacher in a diverse society.

**Ethnocentrism** is the tendency to view one's own culture as superior and to judge other cultures by the standards of one's own (Henson, 2022). The Urdu-speaking pupil in the opening vignette was being ethnocentric: measuring the Pashto classmate's behavior against the norms of Urdu-speaking culture and finding it wanting.

Ethnocentrism is common and often unconscious. It appears when a teacher assumes that children from rural backgrounds are "less intelligent" because they do not know the references an urban curriculum assumes. It appears when a school treats one provincial language as the default and others as "less serious." It appears when a community dismisses another's wedding customs as "wasteful" or "backward."

**Cultural relativism** is the practice of understanding a culture on its own terms, rather than judging it by the standards of another culture. It does not mean agreeing with every practice; it means suspending judgment long enough to understand why a practice exists and what it means to the people who follow it.

For a teacher, cultural relativism is a professional necessity. When a pupil from a conservative household refuses to participate in mixed-gender activities, cultural relativism does not require the teacher to abandon the curriculum, but it does require understanding the cultural norm behind the refusal rather than labeling the pupil as "difficult."

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think cultural relativism means "anything goes" or that there are no universal standards. It does not. Cultural relativism is a method of understanding, not a moral stance. A teacher can understand why a practice exists while still upholding the values of equality, safety, and learning. The point is to understand first, then respond thoughtfully rather than react with prejudice.

### Culture and the teacher

A teacher is both a carrier of culture and a bridge between cultures. The classroom brings together pupils from different cultural backgrounds, and the teacher's choices - which examples to use, which stories to tell, which norms to enforce - either include or exclude. Teaching with cultural awareness means recognizing that a pupil's silence may reflect cultural norms around authority, not lack of understanding. It means choosing examples that reflect the diversity of the class. It means correcting ethnocentric assumptions when they surface, as in the opening vignette.

## Activity: Mapping a cultural practice

Work in pairs. About 25 minutes.

Choose one cultural practice familiar to you (for example: a wedding ritual, a food custom, a greeting practice, a religious observance, a coming-of-age ceremony). For the practice you choose, complete the following table:

| Element | What it looks like in this practice |
|---|---|
| Norms | What rules or expectations apply? |
| Values | What ideals does it reflect? |
| Beliefs | What beliefs does it assume? |
| Symbols | What objects, gestures, or words carry meaning? |

Then write two sentences answering: How might someone from a different cultural background misunderstand this practice? What would cultural relativism require of them?

Share with another pair. Discuss whether any of your examples involve practices that some people might view ethnically rather than relativistically.

3. *(Apply)* A teacher says, "These village children have no manners because they don't say 'please' and 'thank you' the way city children do." Using the concept of ethnocentrism, explain what is wrong with that statement in two or three sentences.

## Summary

Culture is the shared way of life of a group, made up of norms, values, beliefs, and symbols. It is learned, not innate, and it varies across groups and over time. Ethnocentrism is judging another culture by the standards of one's own; cultural relativism is understanding a culture on its own terms. For a teacher in Pakistan's diverse classrooms, cultural awareness is not optional - it is essential professional knowledge.
