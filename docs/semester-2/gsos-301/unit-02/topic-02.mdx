## A real classroom situation

A ten-year-old boy in a government school in Lahore is polite and obedient at school but uses rough language and aggressive behaviour with his neighbourhood friends. His teacher is surprised when a parent-teacher meeting reveals that the father frequently shouts at the mother and uses the same rough language at home. The boy's sister, who attends a different school, is quiet and anxious. Their mother says, "He is just like his father. His sister is like me." The teacher begins to see that the children's personalities are not just innate traits - they are being shaped by the people and environments around them.

This topic examines how that shaping happens.

## Explanation

### What socialization is

<Glossary term="Socialization" /> is the lifelong process through which individuals learn the values, norms, beliefs, and behaviours of their society (Givens & White, 2023). It is how a newborn becomes a member of a society - not just biologically, but socially. Without socialization, a child would not learn language, would not know how to interact with others, and would not develop a sense of self.

Socialization is not something that ends at a particular age. It continues through childhood, adolescence, and adulthood. A B.Ed student entering a new profession is being socialized into the role of a teacher - learning the norms of the profession, the expectations of colleagues, and the identity of "teacher."

### Agents of socialization

An **agent of socialization** is a person, group, or institution that transmits culture and shapes an individual's development. The five major agents are:

1. **Family** - the first and most influential agent. Family is where a child learns language, basic norms, values, and emotional bonds. In Pakistani society, the extended family (grandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins) often plays a significant role, transmitting cultural and religious values alongside the parents. Family socialization is powerful because it begins early, is emotionally intense, and often goes unquestioned - children absorb family norms as "the way things are."

2. **School** - the first major agent outside the home. School teaches not only academic knowledge but also punctuality, discipline, obedience to authority, cooperation with peers, and the norms of public life. In Pakistan, school is often where children first encounter people from different backgrounds and learn to navigate diversity. The hidden curriculum - the unspoken lessons about hierarchy, competition, and conformity - is as powerful as the formal one.

3. **Peer group** - people of roughly the same age and social status. Peers become increasingly important from middle childhood through adolescence. They teach belonging, negotiation, conflict resolution, and identity. In Pakistani schools, peer groups may form around language, neighborhood, or shared interests. Peer pressure can be positive (encouraging academic effort) or negative (discouraging study as "uncool" or pressuring early marriage).

4. **Media** - television, social media, mobile phones, films, and the internet. Media socialization has grown rapidly in Pakistan. Through media, young people encounter ideas about beauty, success, relationships, gender roles, and lifestyle that may differ from what family or school teaches. A teenager in a small town in Sindh may follow influencers in Karachi or Mumbai, absorbing aspirations and values that conflict with local norms.

5. **Religion** - mosques, madrasas, religious festivals, and religious instruction. In Pakistan, religion is a powerful agent of socialization, transmitting moral frameworks, rituals, identity, and community belonging. Religious socialization often reinforces family values but can also introduce norms that differ from secular school teachings.

### The role of society in personality development

Personality is not just inborn. While temperament has biological roots, the shape a personality takes is heavily influenced by social forces. The sociological view is that personality develops through interaction - a child learns who she is by seeing how others respond to her, by the roles she is expected to play, and by the values her community rewards and punishes.

George Herbert Mead (Unit 1) argued that the self develops through a process of taking the perspective of others. A child learns to see herself as others see her - first through specific others (mother, father, teacher), then through the "generalized other" (the wider community's expectations). A Pakistani girl who is consistently told "good girls are quiet" may internalize that expectation as part of her personality, not because she is naturally quiet but because socialization rewards that behaviour.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think personality is fixed at birth or determined entirely by biology. It is not. Biology provides raw material, but socialization shapes it. A child raised in a nurturing, stimulating environment will develop differently from one raised in neglect or violence, even if their biological potential is the same.

### Stages of socialization

Sociologists identify several stages:

1. **The preparatory stage** (birth to about age 3) - children imitate the people around them without fully understanding what they are doing. A toddler may hold a toy phone and say "Assalam-o-Alaikum" without knowing what the greeting means.

2. **The play stage** (about ages 3-5) - children begin to take on specific roles they observe. A child plays "mother," "teacher," or "shopkeeper," learning what each role requires. Role-playing is how children practice being someone else and learn social expectations.

3. **The game stage** (about ages 6-8 and beyond) - children learn to take on multiple roles simultaneously and understand the rules of complex interactions. A child playing cricket must understand not only her own role but also the roles of the bowler, the fielder, and the captain, and how they fit together. This is the stage where children learn to follow rules, cooperate, and compete.

4. **Adult socialization** - socialization continues throughout life. Starting a new job, getting married, becoming a parent, migrating to a new city, or retiring all require learning new roles and norms. A B.Ed student entering a practicum school is in adult socialization - learning the role of a teacher, not just the content of teaching.

In Pakistani society, the transition to adulthood is often marked by specific events: completing education, entering employment, marriage, and parenthood. Each transition involves new socialization - learning new roles, expectations, and responsibilities.

### When agents conflict

Socialization is not always smooth. Sometimes different agents teach different things. A school may teach gender equality while a family expects girls to prioritize domestic duties. A peer group may value academic achievement while media glamorizes wealth without education. A child caught between conflicting messages must negotiate, choose, or find a compromise.

For a teacher, recognizing conflicting socialization is important. A pupil who is aggressive at school may be mirroring family conflict. A pupil who seems "unmotivated" may be responding to a peer culture that devalues education. Understanding the social forces behind behaviour helps a teacher respond to the cause, not just the symptom.

## Activity: Socialization map

Work individually for 15 minutes, then share in pairs for 10 minutes.

Draw a simple map of your own socialization. For each of the five agents (family, school, peers, media, religion), write two things you learned from that agent during your childhood. Then write one sentence about a time when two agents taught you different things and how you resolved the conflict.

Share your map with a partner. Discuss: which agent was most powerful in your socialization, and why? Were there conflicts between agents? How do your socialization experiences compare with those of your pupils?

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name the five agents of socialization and give one example of what each teaches.
2. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between the play stage and the game stage, using your own childhood experience as an example.
3. *(Apply)* A pupil in a government school in Peshawar is bright but refuses to speak in class. His father told him, "Children should be seen and not heard." Which agent of socialization is most influencing this behaviour, and what stage of socialization does it reflect?

## Summary

Socialization is the lifelong process through which individuals learn the values, norms, beliefs, and behaviours of their society. The five major agents - family, school, peers, media, and religion - each contribute to personality development. Mead's stages (preparatory, play, game, and adult) show how socialization deepens as a child grows. Agents sometimes conflict, and understanding these conflicts helps a teacher respond to the causes of behaviour, not just the symptoms. Personality is shaped by social forces, not by biology alone.
