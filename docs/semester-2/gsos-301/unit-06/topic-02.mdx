## A real classroom situation

A trainee teacher on practicum in a government school in rural Sindh notices that almost a third of the pupils in her Class 4 section are absent on any given day. When she asks the head teacher, he says simply, "These are poor families. The children must work." She visits three homes. One child looks after younger siblings while both parents work in the fields. Another sells small items at a traffic junction in the nearby town. A third simply stopped coming after the family could not afford the uniform and the exam fee. The head teacher was right that the families are poor. But the trainee wants to know *why* the poverty persists, what it connects to, and whether anything other than individual effort could change it.

Poverty, crime, unemployment, media influence, human rights and the environment each look like separate issues. Sociology connects them by looking for the shared social structures underneath.

## Explanation

### Poverty as a social problem

<Glossary term="Poverty" /> is the condition in which individuals or households lack the material resources needed for a minimally decent life: adequate food, shelter, clothing, healthcare and education. In Pakistan, poverty is measured against a national poverty line, and by that measure a significant share of the population falls below it, with the rate considerably higher in rural areas and in certain provinces (Conerly et al., 2021).

The common-sense view is that poverty is an individual failing: people are poor because they are lazy, unskilled or unlucky. Sociology offers a structural explanation instead. Poverty persists because of the way social institutions distribute resources, opportunities and risks. A child born into a landless household in rural Sindh faces different life chances from a child born into a wealthy household in central Lahore, not because of any difference in effort or ability, but because of social arrangements that were in place before either child was born.

These arrangements include the distribution of land, the quality of government schools, access to healthcare, the availability of formal employment and the strength of social networks. Poverty, in this view, is produced and maintained by social structures, which means it can also be reduced by changing those structures. Land reform, investment in public education, a functioning healthcare system and labour regulation are all structural responses, and their absence or weakness is part of what keeps poverty in place.

### Crime and deviance

**Crime** is behaviour that breaks a society's formal laws. **Deviance** is a wider term: behaviour that violates social norms, whether or not a law has been broken. All crime is deviant, but not all deviance is crime. A teacher who publicly criticises a powerful local figure may be deviant by local standards but breaks no law. A young man who steals from a market stall is both deviant and criminal.

Sociologists study crime and deviance not just as individual choices but as social patterns. Rates of crime vary between neighbourhoods, between social classes and between countries in ways that cannot be explained by individual morality alone. A neighbourhood with high unemployment, weak policing, poor schooling and few legitimate opportunities typically has higher crime rates than a well-resourced one (Hammond & Cheney, n.d.). This does not mean poverty "causes" crime directly; it means the social conditions that accompany poverty create more opportunities and fewer deterrents.

Three perspectives from Unit 1 illuminate deviance differently. A functionalist notes that deviance can reinforce social solidarity - a shared outrage against a crime reminds everyone what the norms are. A conflict theorist asks whose laws define deviance: powerful groups may criminalise the survival strategies of the poor while leaving the harmful practices of the rich legally unchallenged. A symbolic interactionist studies how a person comes to be labelled "deviant" and how that label shapes their future behaviour and identity. All three angles are useful; none is the whole story.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think deviance is universally defined - that the same act is deviant everywhere. It is not. What counts as deviant varies across cultures, across historical periods and even across social groups within one society. Homosexuality was once criminalised in many societies and is no longer in others. Child marriage is legal and normative in some settings and criminalised in others. Sociologists study how definitions of deviance are made, enforced and changed, not just what breaks a rule at one moment.

### Unemployment

**Unemployment** is the condition of being without paid work while actively seeking it. In Pakistan, unemployment is a persistent concern, particularly among the young and the educated. Graduate unemployment - university degree holders who cannot find formal employment - is a recognised pattern in Pakistan as in many developing economies.

The structural view of unemployment asks why the economy fails to generate enough jobs for those who want them, rather than treating unemployment as a personal shortfall. Several structural factors are at work: the pace of economic growth, the mismatch between what the education system produces and what the labour market needs, the concentration of high-growth sectors in a few cities, and the barriers that certain groups - women, ethnic minorities, people with disabilities - face in entering the workforce.

Unemployment connects to other social problems. It is both a consequence of poverty (poor households cannot fund the education or connections that lead to employment) and a cause of it (unemployment deepens poverty). It is linked to crime (unemployed young men in some contexts turn to illegal livelihoods) and to social unrest. It is a social problem in its own right and a thread that runs through several other problems.

### Media and society

The media - television, print, social media and online platforms - play a growing role in shaping how people see the world, what they value and what they consider normal. In Pakistan, television and social media reach the majority of the population and are a daily presence in most households (Hammond & Cheney, n.d.).

Sociologists study media on several levels. At the simplest level, media transmit information and entertainment. At a deeper level, media shape public opinion: what people think about, what they consider important and what they believe is true. This power makes media a significant social institution, not just a source of content.

Three concerns recur in the sociological study of media. First, **representation**: whose voices and images appear, and whose are absent or stereotyped? Media that repeatedly show women in passive roles, or that treat rural communities as backward, or that ignore minority languages, shape how audiences see those groups. Second, **concentration of ownership**: when a few corporations or individuals own most of the media, the range of viewpoints narrows. Third, **the digital divide**: those without access to digital media are excluded from the conversations and opportunities it creates, a pattern that reinforces existing inequalities.

Social media adds a new dimension. It allows ordinary people to produce content, not only consume it. It has been used to expose injustice, organise social movements and give voice to groups that traditional media ignored. It has also been used to spread misinformation, harass individuals and deepen social divisions. As with technology generally, sociologists study both sides.

### Human rights

**Human rights** are the basic entitlements that belong to every person by virtue of being human: the right to life, to education, to freedom of expression, to freedom from torture, to equality before the law and to adequate living standards, among others. Pakistan has signed international human-rights conventions and has constitutional guarantees for many of these rights. The gap between guarantee and reality is itself a sociological topic.

From a sociological standpoint, human rights are not only a legal category but a social one. A right that exists on paper but cannot be claimed by those who most need it - because they are poor, because they lack information, because the justice system is slow or because local power structures block them - is a right in name only. The study of human rights, therefore, includes the study of the social conditions that make rights real or hollow.

The sociological perspective connects human rights to earlier units. Poverty denies the right to adequate living standards. Gender inequality denies the right to equality. Weak institutions - courts, police, schools - deny the right to justice and to education. Human rights, in this sense, are the standard by which the other social problems in this topic can be measured.

### Environmental issues

Environmental problems - air pollution, water contamination, deforestation, climate change and the degradation of farmland - are social problems because they are produced by social arrangements and because their effects fall unevenly on different groups. In Pakistan, air pollution in major cities regularly exceeds safe levels, access to clean drinking water is limited in many rural areas, and the country is among those most vulnerable to the effects of climate change, including glacial melt, flooding and heatwaves (Conerly et al., 2021).

The sociological point is that environmental damage is not "natural" in the sense of being nobody's fault. It results from policy choices about industry, energy, transport and land use, and it is driven by patterns of consumption that vary between wealthy and poor communities. Its effects are also unequal: poor communities are more likely to live near polluting industry, more likely to lack clean water, and less able to recover after a flood or a crop failure. Environmental issues, therefore, overlap with poverty, inequality and human rights, and they will shape Pakistan's social and economic life for decades to come.

**How the problems connect across the whole course.** One payoff of the GSOS-301 course is that it lets you trace a single problem through several units. Take poverty as an example. Unit 4 showed that stratification by class and gender produces persistent inequality. Unit 5 showed that institutions like family, education and the economy distribute resources unequally, which keeps poverty in place. Topic 6.2 now adds that poverty is also linked to unemployment, to crime (when illegal livelihoods become the only option), to media (when the poor are stereotyped or ignored) and to the environment (when the poor suffer most from flood and pollution). The same tracing works for gender, for education and for health. Sociology's contribution is to show that no social problem stands alone; each is produced by structures and each reinforces the others.

## Activity: Connecting the problems

Work in groups of three or four. About 25 minutes.

Each group picks one social problem from this topic: poverty, crime and deviance, unemployment, media, human rights or the environment. Your task is to draw a short "problem web" on a sheet of paper:

1. Write your chosen problem in the centre.
2. Draw arrows to at least three other problems from the list that it connects to, and label each arrow with a brief explanation (e.g., "poverty deepens because of unemployment").
3. Draw arrows to at least two institutions from Unit 5 (family, education, religion, economy, politics, healthcare) that are affected by or contribute to the problem.

Present your web to another group and compare. Where your webs differ, discuss whether the connections are equally strong and what evidence would support them.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Name three structural causes of poverty and explain briefly why they are structural rather than individual.
2. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between crime and deviance, and give one example of behaviour that is deviant but not criminal.
3. *(Apply)* A policymaker says, "If we punish crime more strictly, crime will disappear." Using the sociological perspective from this topic, explain what the policymaker is missing.
4. *(Analyse)* Choose one of the social problems in this topic and explain how it connects to at least two other problems from the list. Use a Pakistani example.

## Summary

Poverty, crime, deviance, unemployment, media, human rights and environmental issues are social problems because they are produced by social structures, not only by individual failings. A structural explanation asks how institutions, policies and distributions of resources create and maintain the problem, not only how individuals behave. These problems are interconnected: poverty deepens unemployment, unemployment can feed crime, environmental damage falls hardest on the poor, and human-rights guarantees are hollow without functioning institutions. Sociology does not just name the problems; it traces the connections between them.

## Summative task

Choose one social problem from this topic that affects a community you know (your own neighbourhood, your practicum school's area or a community you have observed). Write a short analysis (120-180 words) that does three things: names the problem, explains its structural cause using at least one concept from this topic, and proposes one structural response (not an individual one) that could reduce it. In your analysis, connect the problem to at least one other problem from the list.
