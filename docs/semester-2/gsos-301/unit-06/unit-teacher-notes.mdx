## Teaching strategies for Unit 6

### Opening the unit

This is the capstone unit of the GSOS-301 course, and it draws on every earlier unit. Open by reminding trainees of the thread: sociology's three perspectives (Unit 1), culture and socialization (Unit 2), structure and interaction (Unit 3), stratification and gender (Unit 4) and institutions (Unit 5). Social change and social problems are where all of those come together. Ask the class: "Think of one change you have seen in your community in the last five years. Did it solve an old problem, create a new one, or both?" This frames the unit's central idea: change is constant, and it is mixed in effect.

### Topic 6.1 - Why societies change

**Key pedagogical move:** Make abstract causes concrete. Trainees understand "technology drives change" only when they can trace it through a specific case. Use the opening vignette (mobile phones in a Badin classroom) as your anchor, then ask trainees to supply a parallel example from their own experience. The concept-map activity in the "Tracing one change" exercise is the main learning event.

**Likely wrong answer:** "Technology always improves society." Correct by naming the digital divide and the disruption of old livelihoods alongside the genuine benefits. The point is not that technology is bad; it is that it is *mixed* and *unequal* in effect.

**Good discussion prompt:** "Which has changed daily life in your community more in the last ten years: a new road, mobile phones, or television? Why?" This makes the abstract causes (technology, economic shifts, infrastructure) concrete and comparable.

### Topic 6.2 - Social problems and contemporary issues

**Key pedagogical move:** Train the structural explanation repeatedly. For every problem named (poverty, crime, unemployment, media, human rights, environment), ask the same three questions: What is the problem? What is the individual explanation? What is the structural explanation? The contrast is the unit's most important habit. The "Connecting the problems" activity makes the interconnections visible.

**Likely wrong answer:** "Poverty is the fault of poor people." Correct by walking through the structural causes one at a time: land distribution, school quality, healthcare access, labour markets. Then name a concrete case: two children born the same week in Pakistan, one in a wealthy Lahore household and one in a landless rural household, facing different life chances through no fault of their own.

**Good discussion prompt:** "If you could change one institution (family, education, religion, economy, politics, healthcare) to reduce poverty, which would you choose and why?" This forces trainees to think structurally rather than individually, and it links Topic 6.2 back to Unit 5.

**Sensitivity note.** Poverty, inequality and social problems touch trainees' own lives and the lives of their pupils. Some trainees come from the communities being discussed. Frame the structural analysis as an explanation, not a judgement of any group. Avoid examples that single out a particular caste, ethnicity or religion. Handle crime and deviance examples with care: the point is to explain patterns sociologically, not to stigmatise any community.

## Sequencing and pacing

The unit is designed for approximately 6-8 hours of contact time, distributed as:

| Topic | Contact hours | Main activity |
|---|---|---|
| 6.1 Why societies change | 3 hours | "Tracing one change" pairs activity |
| 6.2 Social problems and contemporary issues | 3-4 hours | "Connecting the problems" group activity |

The unit-assessment is designed for independent study or a supervised examination session (approximately 90 minutes for the full bank, or 45 minutes for a selection).

## Practical work and assignments

The guide's "Practical Work" section lists: Hands-on Computer Exercises, Individual Assignments, Group Assignments, Presentations, and Internet-Research tasks. For Unit 6, these map naturally:

1. **Internet-research task (individual):** Each trainee finds one reliable source (Pakistan Bureau of Statistics, State Bank of Pakistan, UNDP Pakistan, or an open-access academic article) that provides evidence about a named social problem in Pakistan. They write one paragraph (80-120 words) describing the scale of the problem and citing the source. This develops the evidence-based reasoning the course has built across all six units.

2. **Group assignment:** Small groups (3-4 trainees) choose one social problem and analyse it using at least two causes from Topic 6.1 and at least one institution from Unit 5. They present their analysis in a 5-minute presentation. This integrates the whole course.

3. **Individual written assignment:** Each trainee writes a short analysis (300-400 words) applying the structural explanation to a social problem they have observed in their community. This deepens Topic 6.2 and prepares trainees for the unit-assessment ERQs.

## Handling trainee misconceptions

Four misconceptions are especially common at this stage and should be addressed directly:

1. **"Social problems are individual failings."** Reframe: sociology asks what social structures produce and maintain the problem, not only what individuals do wrong. The question is not "why did this person make a bad choice?" but "what conditions make this outcome common?"

2. **"Technology always improves society."** Reframe: technology is a powerful driver of change, and it brings real benefits. It also creates new inequalities (the digital divide), disrupts old livelihoods and produces environmental costs. Analyse both sides.

3. **"Deviance is universally defined."** Reframe: what counts as deviant varies across cultures, periods and groups. The sociological interest is in how definitions of deviance are made, enforced and changed.

4. **"Poverty is inevitable."** Reframe: poverty is a product of social arrangements that can be changed. Other countries have reduced poverty through land reform, public investment in education and healthcare, and social protection. It is a social fact, not a natural law.

## Assessment guidance

The unit-assessment bank (10 MCQ / 10 RRQ / 5 ERQ) is designed for flexible use. For a formative check, select 3-4 MCQs and 2-3 RRQs. For a summative test, use the full bank or a representative sample. The ERQs are designed to integrate Topics 6.1 and 6.2 and should be reserved for summative or portfolio assessment.
