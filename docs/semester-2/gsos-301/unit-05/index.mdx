# Social Institutions

Every society faces the same basic needs: it must raise and socialise children, pass on knowledge, give its members a sense of meaning and purpose, produce and distribute goods, make collective decisions, and care for people when they are ill. Society meets these needs not through single acts but through stable, organised patterns of behaviour called **social institutions**. An institution is not a building or an office. It is a set of norms, roles, values and expectations that work together to meet a core social need. This unit examines the six major institutions - family, education, religion, economy, politics and healthcare - and shows how they connect, how they maintain social life, and how they change.

## Unit learning outcomes

By the end of this unit you will be able to:

1. Define a social institution and distinguish it from a single organisation or building.
2. Explain the core functions of family, education and religion as social institutions.
3. Explain the core functions of economy, politics and healthcare as social institutions.
4. Analyse how a change in one institution affects at least one other institution in a described Pakistani community.
5. Describe one example of institutional change in Pakistani society and explain which institutions drove it.

## Prerequisite knowledge

- **Unit 1, Topic 1.3:** the functionalist and conflict perspectives. This unit uses them to ask what each institution does for society and who gains or loses from it.
- **Unit 1, Topic 1.2:** Durkheim and Weber. Durkheim's view of religion and Weber's ideas about authority return here.
- **Unit 3, Topic 3.1:** groups and formal organisations. This unit shows that an institution is wider than any one organisation, such as a school or a hospital.

## In this unit

## How to use this unit

Each topic is one complete learning cycle: a real situation, an explanation with worked examples, a group activity, quick checks, and a task for your practicum school. Read Topic 5.1 first, because Topic 5.2 builds on its definition of an institution and its idea of interdependence. Keep one community you know well in mind as you read; most activities ask you to apply the ideas to it. When you finish both topics, use the unit assessment to test yourself, and try every question before you look at the answers section at the end.
