## Teaching strategies for Unit 5

### Opening the unit

This unit introduces the concept of a social institution. Many trainees will arrive with the idea that an institution is a building or an organization. Use the opening vignette (Topic 5.1) to surface this assumption directly. Ask the class: "When you hear the word 'institution', what comes to mind?" Write the responses on the board. Then introduce the sociological definition and show how it is broader than any single building or office.

### Topic 5.1 - Family, education and religion

**Key pedagogical move:** Use the "Mapping institutions in your community" activity to ground the abstract definition in trainees' own experience. The activity works best when trainees draw on real communities they know, not hypothetical ones.

**Likely wrong answer:** "The joint family is the natural and best form of family." Correct by explaining that family forms are shaped by economic and social conditions, not by nature. Joint families pool resources in contexts where the state provides little safety net; nuclear families become practical when jobs require mobility. Neither is inherently better.

**Good discussion prompt:** "In your own upbringing, which institution had the strongest influence on what you learned and believed - family, school or religious teaching? Can you give one specific example?" This makes the abstract idea personal and concrete.

**Teaching note on religion:** When teaching religion as a social institution, emphasize that the sociological approach describes social functions (meaning, cohesion, moral framework) without judging the truth of any belief. This is both methodologically correct and respectful in a diverse classroom. If a trainee asks whether the course is "for or against" religion, the answer is that sociology studies what religion does for society, not whether any particular faith is true.

### Topic 5.2 - Economy, politics and healthcare

**Key pedagogical move:** Use the "Tracing one event across institutions" activity to develop the habit of thinking in chains rather than in single causes. Trainees often default to explaining a problem through one institution (e.g., "the school is failing because the government does not fund it"). Push them to trace the chain further: "And why does the government not fund it? And what happens to families when the school fails?"

**Likely wrong answer:** "Politics is only about elections and political parties." Correct by explaining that politics is the whole system of collective decision-making. A head teacher negotiating with the district office, a union council meeting about a water supply, a mother deciding whether to vaccinate her child in the face of community pressure - all of these involve the distribution of power and resources, which is the domain of politics.

**Good discussion prompt:** "Think of a recent news story about a social problem in Pakistan - a flood, a factory closure, a new education policy. Which institutions are involved? Map them on the board." This develops the habit of institutional analysis in real time.

## Sequencing and pacing

The unit is designed for approximately 6-8 hours of contact time, distributed as:

| Topic | Contact hours | Main activity |
|---|---|---|
| 5.1 Family, education and religion | 3 hours | "Mapping institutions in your community" group activity |
| 5.2 Economy, politics and healthcare | 3 hours | "Tracing one event across institutions" pairs activity |
| Unit assessment | 1-2 hours | Independent or supervised assessment |

## Practical work and assignments

The guide's "Practical Work" section lists: Hands-on Computer Exercises, Individual Assignments, Group Assignments, Presentations, and Internet-Research tasks. For Unit 5, these map naturally:

1. **Individual written assignment:** Each trainee chooses one social institution and writes a one-page analysis (300-400 words) describing its core functions and explaining how it connects to at least two other institutions, using a concrete example from their own community or practicum school.

2. **Group assignment:** Small groups (3-4 trainees) take one event from current news (e.g., a factory closure, a flood, a new education policy) and map the institutional chain: which institutions are involved, how they connect, and what the consequences are for a specific community. The group presents its analysis in a 5-minute presentation.

3. **Internet-research task (individual):** Each trainee finds one reliable source (government report, newspaper article, or academic paper) that describes a change in one Pakistani institution over the past decade (e.g., the growth of private schooling, the expansion of the Benazir Income Support Programme, the rise of telemedicine). They write one paragraph summarizing the change and naming one other institution affected.

## Handling trainee misconceptions

Three misconceptions are especially common at this stage and should be addressed directly:

1. **"An institution is a building."** Reframe: an institution is a pattern of social behaviour. A school building houses the institution of education; the institution itself is the organized system of teaching, learning, credentials and shared expectations. The building is not the institution.

2. **"The family naturally looks one way."** Reframe: family forms vary across cultures and over time. Joint and nuclear families are both adaptations to economic and social conditions. Neither is natural or inevitable.

3. **"Social problems have a single cause."** Reframe: most social problems involve multiple institutions. Unemployment is not only an economic problem; it affects family life, health, education and politics. Solutions that target one institution often fail because they ignore the others.
