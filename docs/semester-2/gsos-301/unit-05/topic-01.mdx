## A real classroom situation

A trainee teacher on practicum at a government girls' primary school in Larkana notices that Sana, a bright Class 4 pupil, is absent for several days at the start of each month. A colleague says, "Her family does not value girls' education. That is just how these people are." The trainee is not satisfied. She speaks with Sana's mother at the school gate and learns more. The father's wage arrives late in the month, so the family cannot pay for transport in the first week. Sana also looks after her two younger brothers while her mother does paid stitching work at home. On Fridays, Sana goes with her grandmother to a Quran class in the mohalla, which the family values highly.

What looked like "a family attitude" is shaped by several things at once: who is expected to care for children, how wages are paid, how far the school is, and what the community expects of a girl. No single cause explains the absences. This topic gives you the idea that explains the pattern: the **social institution**.

## Explanation

### What is a social institution?

A <Glossary term="Social institution">social institution</Glossary> is a stable pattern of norms, roles, values and expectations that meets a basic need of society. OpenStax lists family, religion, education, government and the economy among the major institutions that sociologists study (Conerly, Holmes, & Tamang, 2021, chapters 14 to 18).

Four features help you recognise an institution:

1. **It meets a basic need.** A society must raise children, pass on knowledge, give life meaning, produce goods, make decisions and care for the sick.
2. **It is made of norms and roles.** A family is not only people under one roof. It is a set of expectations about who earns, who cares for children, who decides and who is responsible for elders.
3. **It lasts longer than any one person.** People are born into institutions and leave them, but the pattern continues.
4. **It is connected to other institutions.** A change in one usually moves the others. This is called <Glossary term="Institutional interdependence">institutional interdependence</Glossary>.

**Worked example: institution or organisation?** Take the sentence "The District Education Office is an institution." Test it against the four features. The office meets a need, but only as one part of a larger system. It has roles, but those roles belong to the wider pattern of schooling, examinations, certificates and shared ideas about what school is for. That wider pattern would continue even if this one office closed. So the office is an **organisation** inside the **institution** of education, in the sense used in Unit 3.

**Common misconception.** Many learners think an institution is a building or an office. It is not. A school building houses the institution of education, and a mosque houses religious practice, but each institution is the pattern of behaviour, not the bricks.

### Family as a social institution

The <Glossary term="Family">family</Glossary> is the institution through which a society raises children, gives emotional and economic support, passes on a name and social identity, and regulates marriage and reproduction. Some form of family exists in every known society, but its form varies widely across cultures and over time (Conerly, Holmes, & Tamang, 2021, sections 14.1 and 14.2; Hammond & Cheney, n.d., chapter 1.13).

In Pakistan, several family forms exist side by side:

- A <Glossary term="Joint family">joint family</Glossary> - grandparents, their married sons, the sons' wives and children - often shares one house, one kitchen and pooled income.
- A <Glossary term="Nuclear family">nuclear family</Glossary> - a married couple and their children - lives as a separate household.
- Single-parent households, often headed by a widowed woman, and households where the father works away in a city or in the Gulf.

**Worked example: why the form matters.** Picture two households in the same mohalla in Hyderabad. In the joint household, a grandmother looks after the young children, so both the son and his wife can work, and three earners pool their income. In the nuclear household next door, a young couple rent two rooms near the husband's new job. They have more privacy and make their own decisions, but there is no one to mind the baby, so the wife gives up paid work. The same institution, the family, meets the same needs in two different ways. The form depends on housing, jobs and income, not on nature.

**Common misconception.** Trainees often say the family "naturally" looks one way, usually the way their own family looks. A sociologist does not ask which form is right. A sociologist asks what conditions produce a given form and what each form makes easier or harder for its members.

### Education as a social institution

<Glossary term="Education">Education</Glossary> is the institution that passes on knowledge, skills and values in an organised way, and that places people into positions in society. OpenStax separates two kinds of function (Conerly, Holmes, & Tamang, 2021, section 16.2):

- A <Glossary term="Manifest function">manifest function</Glossary> is an intended and openly recognised result. For schooling these include socialisation, passing on culture, social control (teaching pupils to follow rules), **social placement** (sorting people into future roles through examinations and certificates) and cultural innovation.
- A <Glossary term="Latent function">latent function</Glossary> is an unintended or less recognised result. School also builds friendships and social networks, teaches group work and can bring together young people from different backgrounds.

**Worked example: one examination, two functions.** The Class 10 board examination has a manifest function: it measures what pupils have learned and gives them a certificate. It also sorts. A pupil from a well-resourced private school in Karachi and a pupil from a rural government school in Tharparkar may both pass, but their grades, their English, and the contacts their schools give them open different doors. The examination was not designed to reward family wealth, yet the sorting often does. This is why sociologists say education can reproduce inequality even when it aims to reduce it.

The conflict perspective from Unit 1 adds the idea of the <Glossary term="Hidden curriculum">hidden curriculum</Glossary>: the lessons pupils learn from the way school is organised, not from the textbook (Conerly, Holmes, & Tamang, 2021, section 16.2). The morning assembly, the uniform, standing when the teacher enters, and who is chosen as class monitor all teach punctuality, obedience and ideas about who leads.

**Common misconception.** "Education only transmits knowledge." It also sorts people and teaches norms. As a teacher, you take part in all three functions every day.

### Religion as a social institution

Religion is a social institution because it organises shared beliefs, rituals and moral rules into a lasting pattern of community life. Sociology studies what religion **does** in society, such as building community, guiding behaviour and marking the stages of life. It does not judge whether any belief is true (Conerly, Holmes, & Tamang, 2021, section 15.1; Hammond & Cheney, n.d., chapter 1.15). Following Durkheim from Unit 1, the functionalist perspective stresses that shared rituals bind people into a moral community.

In Pakistan, Islam is the faith of the large majority, and Christian, Hindu, Sikh and other communities have their own places of worship, festivals and leaders. Across these communities, <Glossary term="Religion">religion</Glossary> performs similar social functions:

- **Meaning:** it offers answers about life, death and suffering, and comfort at times of loss.
- **Moral guidance:** it sets norms for honesty, charity, modesty and care of parents.
- **Cohesion and identity:** shared occasions such as Eid prayers, Diwali or Christmas bring families and neighbours together and mark who belongs.
- **Life-course rituals:** naming ceremonies, marriages and funerals mark changes of status.

**Worked example: an occasion as a source of cohesion.** In many neighbourhoods, sharing sacrificial meat at Eid ul Adha with relatives, neighbours and the poor strengthens bonds between households, sets a clear norm of sharing, and reminds people of their shared identity. A sociologist describes these functions. The conflict perspective adds one more question: when religious identity is used to mark some people as outsiders, the same cohesion can become a source of division. Describing both effects is analysis, not disrespect.

### How family, education and religion shape one another

These three institutions overlap all the time, and the overlap is where many classroom realities come from.

- **Family shapes education.** A family's income, its views on girls' schooling and its need for children's help at home affect who enrols and who attends, as in Sana's case.
- **Education shapes family.** When more girls in a community finish school, families' expectations about marriage, work and their own children's schooling can change over a generation.
- **Religion shapes both.** Many families send children to a Quran class or a madrassa as well as to school, and religious values guide what parents expect a school to teach.

## Activity: Mapping institutions in your community

Work in groups of three or four. About 25 minutes.

1. Choose one community the whole group knows: a neighbourhood, a village, or your practicum school's catchment area.
2. On a large sheet, draw three boxes: family, education and religion. In each box write one **form** the institution takes there (for example, "most families are joint") and one **function** it performs.
3. Draw at least two arrows between the boxes. On each arrow, write one sentence explaining how one institution shapes the other. Use the words "manifest" or "latent" at least once.
4. Swap sheets with another group. Each group checks the other's arrows: is the link a real connection, or just two facts placed side by side? Return the sheet with one written suggestion.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* Define a social institution in one sentence, using the words "pattern", "norms" and "need".
2. *(Understand)* Explain why a madrassa building is not the same thing as the institution of religion.
3. *(Understand)* Give one manifest function and one latent function of a school, with an example of each.
4. *(Apply)* A friend says, "The joint family is the only natural family; nuclear families are a mistake." Give two reasons, from this topic, why a sociologist would call this claim too simple.

## Summary

A social institution is a lasting pattern of norms, roles and values that meets a basic need of society; it is not a building or a single organisation. The family raises and supports children, and its form changes with housing, work and income. Education transmits knowledge, but it also sorts people through examinations and teaches a hidden curriculum of norms. Religion gives meaning, moral guidance and community, and sociology studies these functions without judging belief. The three institutions constantly shape one another, so a pupil's attendance or achievement rarely has only one cause.
