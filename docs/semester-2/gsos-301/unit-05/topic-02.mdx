## A real classroom situation

A teacher in a government school in Jacobabad arrives one morning to find the school closed. A local factory has shut down without warning. Over the following weeks, the head teacher notices changes in the classroom: pupils whose parents worked at the factory stop coming; a few families leave the area entirely to look for work elsewhere. At the Basic Health Unit down the road, the staff report more patients with stress-related complaints and fewer mothers bringing children for routine vaccinations. The teacher realizes that a single economic event - the factory closure - is reshaping family life, health, and even the politics of the area, as local leaders demand government action. The institutions did not change one at a time; they shifted together.

This situation illustrates the question this topic answers: how do economy, politics and healthcare function as social institutions, and how do they connect to the family, education and religion institutions already covered?

## Explanation

### Economy as a social institution

The <Glossary term="Economy" /> is the institution that organizes how a society produces, distributes and consumes goods and services (Conerly et al., 2021). It answers three basic questions: what gets made, who makes it, and who gets what.

In Pakistan, the economy combines several sectors: agriculture (the largest employer, especially in rural Sindh and Punjab), industry (textiles, cement, sugar), and a large and growing services sector (banking, transport, telecommunications). The informal economy - daily wage labour, small street vendors, home-based work - employs a significant share of the workforce and is often invisible in official statistics.

The economy does more than produce goods. It shapes where people live (families move to cities for jobs), what they eat (the price of wheat and cooking oil affects every household), and how much education they can afford (schooling costs compete with other needs). Andersen et al. (2014) note that economic institutions also reproduce inequality: those who own capital or land accumulate wealth faster than those who sell their labour, and the gap widens across generations.

Consider one concrete example: a farming family in Khairpur district. When the price of cotton falls, the family's income drops. The father takes a loan from a local trader. The oldest son leaves school after Class 8 to work and help repay the debt. The mother's health suffers because the family cuts back on food and delays a clinic visit. One economic shift moves through education, family and health at the same time. This is institutional interdependence in daily life.

### Politics as a social institution

The <Glossary term="Politics" /> is the institution through which a society makes and enforces collective decisions, distributes power and authority, and governs itself. The state - with its legislature, executive, courts and bureaucracy - is the most visible political institution, but politics also operates at the local level: in union council meetings, in the jirga or panchayat traditions of many communities, and in the everyday negotiations between citizens and officials.

In Pakistan, political institutions include the federal and provincial governments, the judiciary, the election system, and the military as a political actor. Local government - the union council and the tehsil administration - is the level most citizens encounter directly. It is the level that decides where a road is repaired, how a government school is staffed, and whether a water supply project goes ahead.

Politics connects directly to every other institution. The state sets the education curriculum, regulates religious affairs through bodies like the Council of Islamic Ideology, funds healthcare through the provincial health departments, and shapes the economy through taxation, subsidies and labour laws. A change in government can redirect resources across all other institutions at once.

**Common misconception.** Many trainees think politics means only elections and political parties. In reality, politics is the whole system of collective decision-making. When a head teacher negotiates with the district education office for more teachers, that is a political act within the institution of politics. The everyday distribution of resources is political, even when no election is in sight.

### Healthcare as a social institution

The <Glossary term="Healthcare" /> institution is responsible for maintaining and restoring the population's health. It includes hospitals, Basic Health Units, rural health centres, private clinics, public health programmes (such as the Expanded Programme on Immunization), and the trained professionals who staff them.

In Pakistan, healthcare is delivered through a mix of public and private providers. Government facilities - from the Basic Health Union in rural areas to the teaching hospitals in major cities - are meant to serve everyone, but they are often understaffed and underfunded. Private clinics and hospitals offer an alternative for those who can pay. This two-tier system means that a child born in a wealthy Karachi household and a child born in a remote village in Balochistan have very different chances of surviving a treatable illness (Conerly et al., 2021).

Healthcare connects to every other institution in the unit. A family's economic position determines whether it can afford private care. The state's political decisions determine how much funding the health sector receives. Education affects health literacy - whether a mother knows to boil water or seek prenatal care. Religious beliefs may shape attitudes toward vaccination or family planning. No institution works alone.

**Worked example.** The government's Lady Health Worker programme, started in 1994, sends trained women into rural communities to provide basic maternal and child health services. By 2020, the programme covered much of the country. Its success depended on several institutions working together: politics (the government decided to fund it), economy (the programme paid the workers), education (the workers needed training), and family (the workers visited homes and spoke to mothers and grandmothers). When funding was cut in some years, the programme weakened. Institutional support is what keeps a health intervention alive.

### How economy, politics and healthcare interrelate with family, education and religion

The three institutions in this topic do not stand apart from those in Topic 5.1. They form a connected system:

- **Economy shapes family:** A family's form and functioning depend on its economic position. Migration for work splits joint families. Unemployment increases domestic tension.
- **Politics shapes education:** The state decides the curriculum, the language of instruction, and how resources are distributed between urban and rural schools. A new education policy can reshape what millions of pupils learn.
- **Healthcare shapes and is shaped by religion:** Religious authorities may endorse or resist public health measures. In Pakistan, some polio vaccination campaigns faced resistance in certain communities where religious leaders expressed distrust. Building trust required engagement with religious institutions, not only medical ones.
- **All three connect to institutional change:** The growth of private schooling in Pakistan over the last three decades is a clear example of institutional change. It was driven by the economy (rising middle class willing to pay), politics (the state's willingness to allow private providers), and religion (some private schools adding Islamic studies to attract enrolment). The change in education then reshaped the economy (a new sector of private school employment) and politics (new lobbying groups of private school operators).

## Activity: Tracing one event across institutions

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

Read the scenario below and then discuss the questions.

**Scenario:** A large textile mill in Faisalabad closes. Five hundred workers lose their jobs. Over the next six months, the local government school reports higher absenteeism. The nearby Basic Health Unit reports more patients with anxiety and high blood pressure. Local politicians hold a public meeting to demand that the provincial government reopens the mill or provides alternative employment. Two families send their teenage sons to work in Lahore.

Answer these questions together:

1. Which institutions are affected by the mill closure? List each one and explain how it is affected in one sentence.
2. Name one connection between any two of the institutions in this chain. Explain how a change in one caused a change in the other.
3. If you were the local union council councillor, what is one action you could take that would involve at least two institutions at the same time?

Be ready to share your answers with another pair.

## Summary

Economy, politics and healthcare are three social institutions that work alongside family, education and religion to organize social life. The economy produces and distributes resources; politics makes and enforces collective decisions; healthcare protects and restores wellbeing. None of these institutions operates alone. A change in one - a factory closure, a new law, a vaccination campaign - ripples through the others. Understanding these connections is what gives a sociologist the power to explain why social problems are so hard to solve with a single intervention.
