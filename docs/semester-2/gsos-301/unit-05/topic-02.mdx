## A real classroom situation

A trainee teacher on practicum in a government boys' middle school near Kotri notices that attendance in Class 7 has fallen sharply over two months. The head teacher explains that a large textile mill nearby has closed, and many pupils' fathers worked there. Some boys now sell vegetables in the market in the mornings. Two families have moved to Karachi to look for work. A Lady Health Worker who visits the area tells the school that fewer mothers are bringing babies for vaccination, because the families cannot spare the bus fare to the Basic Health Unit. Meanwhile, the local councillor has called a public meeting to demand help from the provincial government.

One economic event has changed classrooms, households, health and local politics at the same time. This topic explains why: the economy, politics and healthcare are institutions too, and they are tied to the family, education and religion institutions you met in Topic 5.1.

## Explanation

### Economy as a social institution

The <Glossary term="Economy">economy</Glossary> is the institution that organises how a society produces, distributes and uses goods and services. It answers three questions: what is made, who does the work, and who gets what (Conerly, Holmes, & Tamang, 2021, section 18.1). Its norms include ideas about fair wages, who should earn, and what counts as "real work".

Pakistan's economy combines farming, industry such as textiles and cement, and a large services sector. Much work is <Glossary term="Informal economy">informal</Glossary>: daily-wage labour, street vending, home-based stitching and farm work on a share of the crop. Informal work is often unregistered and unprotected, so a family can lose its income overnight. Many households also depend on money sent home by relatives working in Karachi, Lahore or the Gulf.

**Worked example: one price, many effects.** A cotton-growing family in Khairpur district has a poor season because prices fall. To cover costs, the father borrows from a local trader. The eldest son leaves school after Class 8 to work in a town workshop and help repay the loan. The family delays a clinic visit for the mother to save money. Follow the chain: an economic change (falling prices) altered family decisions (borrowing, the son's work), education (dropping out) and health (a delayed visit). The economy rarely acts alone.

The conflict perspective from Unit 1 asks who gains and who loses. Those who own land, capital or a business can build wealth over time, while those who sell daily labour have little to fall back on. Economic institutions can therefore pass inequality from one generation to the next (Conerly, Holmes, & Tamang, 2021, chapter 18).

### Politics as a social institution

Politics is the institution through which a society makes and enforces shared decisions and distributes <Glossary term="Political power">power</Glossary>. Following Max Weber, OpenStax defines power as the ability to exercise one's will over others, and <Glossary term="Authority">authority</Glossary> as power that people accept as rightful (Conerly, Holmes, & Tamang, 2021, section 17.1). Weber named three types of authority: **traditional** (accepted because it has always been so), **charismatic** (accepted because of a leader's personal qualities) and **legal-rational** (accepted because it rests on laws and official rules).

**Worked example: three kinds of authority in one district.** A village elder who settles a land dispute because families have always turned to his family holds traditional authority. A popular local leader whose speeches draw large crowds holds charismatic authority. The Deputy Commissioner, whose power comes from an appointment under law, holds legal-rational authority. All three may shape what happens in the same district.

In Pakistan, political institutions include the federal and provincial governments, the courts, elections and political parties, and local government such as union councils. The Council of Islamic Ideology advises Parliament on whether laws agree with Islamic teaching, which shows one formal link between politics and religion. Through budgets and laws, politics touches every other institution: it sets the school curriculum, funds hospitals, sets the minimum wage and decides where roads are built.

**Common misconception.** "Politics means elections and parties." Elections matter, but politics is the whole system of deciding who gets what. When a head teacher asks the district education office for two more teachers, or a union council decides which street gets a drain, power and authority are at work.

### Healthcare as a social institution

<Glossary term="Healthcare">Healthcare</Glossary> is the institution that organises how a society prevents illness, treats the sick and protects public health. It includes hospitals and clinics, Basic Health Units, vaccination programmes, pharmacies, trained professionals, and the norms about when and where people seek care. OpenStax stresses that health is also **socially shaped**: income, education, housing and gender affect who falls ill and who gets treatment (Conerly, Holmes, & Tamang, 2021, sections 19.1 and 19.2).

In Pakistan, care comes from a mix of providers: government hospitals and Basic Health Units, private clinics and hospitals, pharmacies, and traditional healers such as hakeems. A family's choice depends on cost, distance, trust and who in the family decides. Government facilities are meant to serve everyone, yet a family with money can buy faster private care, while a poor family may wait or go without.

**Worked example: the Lady Health Worker.** Pakistan's Lady Health Worker programme, started in 1994, trains local women to visit homes and give basic mother-and-child health services, such as advice on nutrition and family planning and support for vaccination. Look at what makes the job possible. Politics funds and organises the programme. The economy pays the workers. Education trains them. The family is where the service is delivered, often to mothers and mothers-in-law together. Religious leaders and community norms affect whether families welcome the visit. Healthcare works only when several institutions work together.

**Common misconception.** "Health is only a medical matter." Medicine treats disease, but whether a child is vaccinated may depend on bus fare, a mother's schooling, a father's permission and a community's trust in health workers.

### How institutions connect and change

The six institutions in this unit form one connected system. A change in one moves the others, as the mill closure shows: lost wages (economy) pulled boys out of school (education), split households (family), reduced vaccination (healthcare) and created demands on the government (politics).

<Glossary term="Institutional change">Institutional change</Glossary> means a lasting change in the norms and roles of an institution, not just a change of people inside it. **Worked example: the spread of private schooling.** Over recent decades, many low-fee private schools have opened in Pakistan, including in small towns and villages. Several institutions pushed the change. In the economy, families with some extra income began to pay fees, and educated young women found work as teachers. In politics, government policy allowed private providers to operate and register. In the family, parents' hopes for English and for better results grew. The change then fed back: new norms about what a "good school" is, new jobs for women, and new pressure on government schools. This is change in the institution of education, not just one new school.

## Activity: Tracing one event across institutions

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

**Scenario:** Heavy floods damage crops and roads in a district of Sindh. A government school is used as a relief camp for three months. Many families lose their harvest. A Basic Health Unit reports more cases of diarrhoea among children. Local leaders and the provincial government argue about who should pay for repairs.

1. Partner A lists every institution affected and writes one sentence on how each is affected. Partner B does the same without looking.
2. Compare lists. Agree on a chain of at least **three** institutions, in order, with an arrow and a reason for each link.
3. Decide together: if the district could fund only one response, which institution should it start with, and why? Use the word "interdependence" in your answer.
4. Join another pair and compare your chains. Note one link the other pair saw that you missed.

## Check your understanding

1. *(Remember)* State the main function of the economy, politics and healthcare in one sentence each.
2. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between power and authority, with one example from Pakistan.
3. *(Understand)* Explain why falling cotton prices can affect a child's schooling.
4. *(Apply)* A classmate says, "A new Basic Health Unit will solve the health problems of a poor village." Using the Lady Health Worker example, give two reasons why a building alone may not be enough.

## Summary

The economy produces and distributes resources, and informal, insecure work makes many Pakistani families vulnerable to sudden loss of income. Politics makes shared decisions and distributes power; authority is power that people accept, and Weber described traditional, charismatic and legal-rational types. Healthcare prevents and treats illness, but health is shaped by income, schooling, family decisions and community trust. All six institutions in the unit are interdependent, and institutional change, such as the spread of private schooling, happens when several institutions push in the same direction.

## Summative task

A provincial department proposes: **"Build one new Basic Health Unit in every union council, and child health will improve."** In 150-200 words, evaluate this proposal. Use institutional interdependence, name at least two institutions other than healthcare that would affect whether the units work, and recommend one addition to the plan with a reason.
