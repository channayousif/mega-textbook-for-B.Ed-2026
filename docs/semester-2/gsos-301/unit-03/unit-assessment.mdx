## Unit summary

This unit examined two connected ideas: how society is structured and how people interact within that structure.

- Topic 3.1 defined status, role and role strain; distinguished primary from secondary groups; and explained the features, strengths and limitations of bureaucracy as a form of organization.
- Topic 3.2 identified the three major social interaction processes (cooperation, competition and conflict), examined how interaction patterns differ between rural and urban settings, and explained the role of verbal and non-verbal communication in social life.

Together these give you a framework for understanding the invisible architecture of social life - the positions people occupy, the expectations that go with them, and the processes through which people work together, compete and sometimes clash.

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

Choose the single best answer for each question.

1. *(Remember)* A socially recognized position in a group or society - such as teacher, student, or mother - is called:
   - A) A role
   - B) A status
   - C) A group
   - D) A norm

2. *(Remember)* Which of the following is an example of a primary group?
   - A) A district education office
   - B) A national teachers' association
   - C) A close family
   - D) A university lecture hall

3. *(Understand)* A woman is expected to manage the household as a daughter-in-law and to be at work as a teacher, but she cannot do both at the same time. This is an example of:
   - A) Role strain
   - B) Role exit
   - C) Status inconsistency
   - D) Group conflict

4. *(Understand)* Weber argued that bureaucracy brings two important benefits. Which pair below names them?
   - A) Speed and flexibility
   - B) Predictability and fairness
   - C) Personal warmth and informality
   - D) Low cost and simplicity

5. *(Apply)* Two government schools are competing for the same sports trophy. They prepare separately, follow the tournament rules, and accept the referee's decisions. This is an example of:
   - A) Cooperation
   - B) Conflict
   - C) Competition
   - D) Coercion

6. *(Apply)* A community builds a well together, with each family contributing labour. This is an example of:
   - A) Competition
   - B) Cooperation
   - C) Conflict
   - D) Conformity

7. *(Apply)* A teacher frowns at a pupil who is talking during the lesson. The pupil stops talking. The frown is an example of:
   - A) Verbal communication
   - B) Bureaucratic procedure
   - C) Non-verbal communication
   - D) Cooperation

8. *(Apply)* Which of the following is a feature of bureaucracy according to Weber?
   - A) Decisions based on personal relationships
   - B) Hierarchy of authority
   - C) Rules that change with each case
   - D) Leadership based on family ties

9. *(Apply)* A pupil completes homework only because the teacher will punish them if they do not. The sociological term for this pattern is:
   - A) Exchange
   - B) Conformity
   - C) Coercion
   - D) Cooperation

10. *(Apply)* A new teacher starts dressing and speaking like the senior teachers, even though no one told them to. This is an example of:
    - A) Cooperation
    - B) Conformity
    - C) Competition
    - D) Conflict

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

Answer each question in the space indicated.

1. *(Remember)* Define status and role in your own words, and explain the difference between them in one sentence.

2. *(Remember)* Name two features of a primary group and two features of a secondary group.

3. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between competition and conflict, using the idea of "agreed rules" in your answer.

4. *(Apply)* A teacher says, "My school's transfer policy is just red tape." Using Weber's concept of bureaucracy, explain two benefits the policy provides, even though it is slow.

5. *(Apply)* Name two forms of communication and give one example of each from a Pakistani classroom.

6. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between verbal and non-verbal communication, and why both matter in a classroom.

7. *(Apply)* A rural community resolves a dispute through village elders rather than going to court. Explain why this pattern fits the description of rural social interaction in two sentences.

8. *(Analyze)* A head teacher gives instructions, and teachers follow them without question. Name the interaction pattern at work and explain one risk of this pattern in two sentences.

9. *(Apply)* Name the three major forms of social interaction and give one Pakistani example of each.

10. *(Apply)* A teacher holds a meeting where every staff member can raise concerns. Explain in one sentence how this practice improves communication within the school's bureaucracy.

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

Answer each question in a short paragraph (100-150 words unless stated otherwise).

1. *(Analyze)* A government school teacher in rural Punjab holds several statuses: class teacher, examination coordinator, wife, mother, and daughter-in-law. Analyse this situation using the concepts of status, role and role strain. Explain what causes the strain and suggest one realistic way the school could reduce it.

2. *(Evaluate)* A teacher says, "Conflict is always bad for a school." Evaluate this claim using evidence from this unit. Explain one situation in which conflict can be constructive and one situation in which it is destructive, drawing on Pakistani school examples.

3. *(Analyze)* Compare social interaction in a rural Pakistani community and an urban Pakistani school. Analyse how the setting shapes the dominant interaction patterns and the way conflicts are resolved. Give specific examples.

4. *(Evaluate)* "A school should be run like a family, not a bureaucracy." Evaluate this claim using the concepts of primary groups, secondary groups and bureaucracy. Explain what the school would gain and what it would lose.

5. *(Create/Analyse)* Describe a realistic situation in a Pakistani school where competition between two groups of pupils has turned into conflict. Analyse the situation using the concepts of competition, conflict and communication, then suggest what the teacher could do to restore constructive interaction.

## Answers and marking guidance

This section is for self-study. Try every question above before you look back at the keys below.

### MCQ answer key

1. **B** - A status. A role is the expected behaviour that goes with a status; a group is a collection of people; a norm is a rule of behaviour.
2. **C** - A close family. A, B, and D are all secondary groups because they are larger, more formal, and task-focused.
3. **A** - Role strain: the difficulty of meeting the demands of multiple roles at the same time. Role exit is leaving a role; status inconsistency is holding statuses of different ranks; group conflict involves groups, not individual roles.
4. **B** - Predictability and fairness. Weber argued that bureaucracy's written rules and hierarchy make outcomes predictable and reduce favouritism.
5. **C** - Competition. The schools are striving for a limited reward (the trophy) within agreed rules. Cooperation would be working together; conflict would be direct opposition without agreed rules.
6. **B** - Cooperation. The families are working together toward a shared goal (the well). Competition would be striving against each other; conflict would be direct opposition.
7. **C** - Non-verbal communication. The frown is a facial expression that carries meaning without words. Verbal communication uses spoken or written language.
8. **B** - Hierarchy of authority. Weber identified hierarchy as a key feature of bureaucracy. A, C, and D describe practices that bureaucracy is designed to prevent.
9. **C** - Coercion. The pupil complies because of a threat of punishment. Exchange involves mutual benefit; conformity is following group norms voluntarily; cooperation is working together willingly.
10. **B** - Conformity. The new teacher is following group norms to fit in. Cooperation would be working together; competition would be striving against others; conflict would be deliberate opposition.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. **Model:** Status is a socially recognized position in a group or society; role is the set of expected behaviours that goes with that status. The difference: a status is the position you hold; a role is what you are expected to do because of that position. **Marks (2):** 1 - defines status correctly; 1 - defines role correctly and distinguishes the two.

2. **Model:** Primary group - small, long-lasting, warm personal relationships. Secondary group - larger, formal, task-focused, impersonal relationships. **Marks (4):** 1 for each correct feature.

3. **Model:** Competition is striving against others for a limited reward within agreed rules; conflict is deliberate opposition where the parties are no longer bound by shared rules. **Marks (2):** 1 - identifies the "agreed rules" distinction; 1 - explains both terms.

4. **Model:** The policy ensures predictability (every transfer request follows the same procedure, so everyone knows what to expect) and fairness (the rules apply equally to all teachers, reducing favouritism). **Marks (2):** 1 - explains predictability; 1 - explains fairness.

5. **Model:** Verbal communication uses language (spoken or written) - for example, a teacher explaining a lesson. Non-verbal communication uses gestures, facial expressions, tone of voice - for example, a teacher raising an eyebrow to signal disapproval. **Marks (2):** 1 - names and explains both forms; 1 - gives a classroom example.

6. **Model:** Verbal communication is the exchange of meaning through words (spoken or written). Non-verbal communication is the exchange of meaning through facial expressions, gestures, tone and posture. Both matter because non-verbal cues often carry more emotional weight than words and can contradict or reinforce the spoken message. **Marks (2):** 1 - defines both; 1 - explains why both matter.

7. **Model:** In rural communities, social interaction is often face-to-face and shaped by long-standing relationships. Disputes are handled through trusted community elders rather than formal procedures, because personal relationships and community harmony matter more than bureaucratic rules. **Marks (2):** 1 - identifies the face-to-face, relationship-based pattern; 1 - explains why elders are used instead of courts.

8. **Model:** The pattern is coercion or one-way downward communication. The risk is that problems go unreported: if teachers never raise concerns, issues like resource shortages or unfair workloads stay hidden and get worse. **Marks (2):** 1 - names the pattern; 1 - explains a specific risk.

9. **Model:** Cooperation (working together toward a shared goal - for example, teachers planning a joint lesson). Competition (striving against others within rules - for example, schools competing for a sports trophy). Conflict (deliberate opposition - for example, parents arguing over school admissions). **Marks (3):** 1 for each correct term with an example.

10. **Model:** It opens a communication channel that goes upward through the hierarchy, allowing concerns to reach the head teacher instead of staying hidden, which makes the bureaucracy more responsive. **Marks (2):** 1 - identifies upward communication; 1 - explains why it improves the school's functioning.
