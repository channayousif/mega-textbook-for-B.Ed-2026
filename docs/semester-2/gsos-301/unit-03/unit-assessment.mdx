## Unit summary

This unit examined two connected ideas: how society is structured and how people interact within that structure.

- Topic 3.1 defined status, role, role strain and role conflict; distinguished primary from secondary groups; and explained the features, strengths and limitations of bureaucracy as a form of organization.
- Topic 3.2 identified the three major social interaction processes (cooperation, competition and conflict), examined how interaction patterns differ between rural and urban settings, and explained the role of verbal and non-verbal communication in social life.

Together these give you a framework for understanding the invisible architecture of social life - the positions people occupy, the expectations that go with them, and the processes through which people work together, compete and sometimes clash.

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

Choose the single best answer for each question.

1. *(Remember)* A socially recognized position in a group or society - such as teacher, student, or mother - is called:
   - A) A role
   - B) A status
   - C) A group
   - D) A norm

2. *(Remember)* Which of the following is an example of a primary group?
   - A) A district education office
   - B) A national teachers' association
   - C) A close family
   - D) A university lecture hall

3. *(Understand)* A woman is expected to manage the household as a daughter-in-law and to be at work as a teacher, but she cannot do both at the same time. This is an example of:
   - A) Role conflict
   - B) Role exit
   - C) Status inconsistency
   - D) Role strain

4. *(Understand)* Weber argued that bureaucracy brings two important benefits. Which pair below names them?
   - A) Speed and flexibility
   - B) Predictability and fairness
   - C) Personal warmth and informality
   - D) Low cost and simplicity

5. *(Apply)* Two government schools are competing for the same sports trophy. They prepare separately, follow the tournament rules, and accept the referee's decisions. This is an example of:
   - A) Cooperation
   - B) Conflict
   - C) Competition
   - D) Coercion

6. *(Apply)* A community builds a well together, with each family contributing labour. This is an example of:
   - A) Competition
   - B) Cooperation
   - C) Conflict
   - D) Conformity

7. *(Apply)* A teacher frowns at a pupil who is talking during the lesson. The pupil stops talking. The frown is an example of:
   - A) Verbal communication
   - B) Bureaucratic procedure
   - C) Non-verbal communication
   - D) Cooperation

8. *(Understand)* A school has a clear chain of command, written policies for leave and transfers, and promotes teachers based on qualifications rather than personal connections. This school most closely follows which organizational model?
   - A) Primary group structure
   - B) Bureaucracy
   - C) Extended family structure
   - D) Community elder system

9. *(Apply)* A pupil completes homework only because the teacher will punish them if they do not. The sociological term for this pattern is:
   - A) Exchange
   - B) Conformity
   - C) Coercion
   - D) Cooperation

10. *(Apply)* A new teacher starts dressing and speaking like the senior teachers, even though no one told them to. This is an example of:
    - A) Cooperation
    - B) Conformity
    - C) Competition
    - D) Conflict

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

Answer each question in the space indicated.

1. *(Understand)* Explain why teacher is a status whereas preparing a lesson is part of a role. Use the position/expected-behaviour distinction.

2. *(Analyze)* A staff friendship circle provides emotional support; the district office processes transfer requests. Compare the groups using purpose and relationships. Explain why size alone cannot classify them.

3. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between role strain and role conflict, using one Pakistani example to illustrate each.

4. *(Apply)* A teacher says, "My school's transfer policy is just red tape." Using Weber's concept of bureaucracy, explain two benefits the policy provides, even though it is slow.

5. *(Apply)* A new teacher notices that some decisions in her school are made through formal written procedures, while others are settled informally over tea. Using the concepts of bureaucracy and primary groups, explain in two sentences why both patterns exist side by side.

6. *(Understand)* Explain the difference between competition and conflict. In your answer, explain what makes competition a form of rivalry for a reward, and what makes conflict a form of direct opposition.

7. *(Apply)* Name two forms of communication and give one example of each from a Pakistani classroom.

8. *(Analyze)* A head teacher sends instructions but provides no channel for teachers to respond. Identify the direction of communication and explain one risk. Is this evidence alone enough to infer coercion? Explain.

9. *(Apply)* In an invented village case, neighbours choose elders they trust to mediate a dispute. Explain how personal relationships shape this choice, and why this case does not show that all rural communities avoid formal procedures.

10. *(Apply)* A teacher holds a meeting where every staff member can raise concerns. Explain in one sentence how this practice improves communication within the school's bureaucracy.

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

Answer each question in a short paragraph (100-150 words unless stated otherwise).

1. *(Analyze)* A government school teacher in rural Punjab holds several statuses: class teacher, examination coordinator, wife, mother, and daughter-in-law. Analyse this situation using the concepts of status, role, role strain and role conflict. Suppose marking and lesson preparation compete for her time, while a parent-care appointment clashes with school hours. Distinguish the two pressures and suggest one realistic structural adjustment.

2. *(Evaluate)* A teacher says, "Conflict is always bad for a school." Evaluate this claim using evidence from this unit. Explain one situation in which conflict can be constructive and one situation in which it is destructive, drawing on Pakistani school examples.

3. *(Analyze)* Compare the two invented village and city-school settings from Topic 3.2, without treating them as national patterns. Analyse how the setting shapes the dominant interaction patterns and the way conflicts are resolved. Give specific examples.

4. *(Evaluate)* "A school should be run like a family, not a bureaucracy." Evaluate this claim using the concepts of primary groups, secondary groups and bureaucracy. Explain what the school would gain and what it would lose.

5. *(Create/Analyse)* Describe a realistic situation in a Pakistani school where competition between two groups of pupils has turned into conflict. Analyse the situation using the concepts of competition, conflict, communication and the role of the teacher's status and authority in the school's hierarchy. Then suggest what the teacher could do to restore constructive interaction.

## Answers and marking guidance

This section is for self-study. Try every question above before you look back at the keys below.

### MCQ answer key

1. **B** - A status. A role is the expected behaviour that goes with a status; a group is a collection of people; a norm is a rule of behaviour.
2. **C** - A close family. A, B, and D are all secondary groups because they are larger, more formal, and task-focused.
3. **A** - Role conflict: the clash between the demands of two roles attached to different statuses (daughter-in-law and teacher). Role strain is pressure within a single role; role exit is leaving a role; status inconsistency is holding statuses of different ranks.
4. **B** - Predictability and fairness. Weber argued that bureaucracy's written rules and hierarchy make outcomes predictable and reduce favouritism.
5. **C** - Competition. The schools are striving for a limited reward (the trophy) within agreed rules. Cooperation would be working together; conflict would be direct opposition with incompatible goals.
6. **B** - Cooperation. The families are working together toward a shared goal (the well). Competition would be striving against each other for a limited reward; conflict would be direct opposition.
7. **C** - Non-verbal communication. The frown is a facial expression that carries meaning without words. Verbal communication uses spoken or written language.
8. **B** - Bureaucracy. The school displays hierarchy, written policies, and merit-based promotion - the defining features of Weber's bureaucratic model. A primary group is small and personal; an extended family and a community elder system are not formal organizational models.
9. **C** - Coercion. The pupil complies because of a threat of punishment. Exchange involves mutual benefit; conformity is following group norms voluntarily; cooperation is working together willingly.
10. **B** - Conformity. The new teacher is following group norms to fit in. Cooperation would be working together; competition would be striving against others; conflict would be deliberate opposition.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. **Model:** Teacher names a recognized position; lesson preparation is expected behaviour attached to it. **Marks (2):** 1 for explaining position; 1 for linking the expected behaviour to that position.

2. **Model:** The friendship circle centres on close relationships and support; the office centres on formal tasks. Size is typical rather than decisive: a small office can still be a secondary group. **Marks (3):** 1 for each comparison (purpose and relationships); 1 for explaining the limitation of size.

3. **Model:** Role strain is competing demands within one role, such as a teacher balancing marking and lesson preparation. Role conflict is incompatible demands across statuses, such as a teacher's school meeting clashing with a parent's childcare duty. **Marks (4):** 1 for each correct distinction; 1 for each corresponding Pakistani example.

4. **Model:** Written procedures aim at predictability and equal treatment, although actual practice may depart from the ideal. **Marks (2):** 1 for explaining each benefit in relation to transfers.

5. **Model:** Written procedures coordinate formal tasks in a bureaucracy; personal ties provide support through primary-group relationships. Both can coexist among the same staff. **Marks (2):** 1 for explaining the formal pattern; 1 for explaining the informal pattern.

6. **Model:** Competition is rivalry for a scarce reward; conflict is direct opposition to another party's demands. A formal grievance can be conflict within agreed rules. **Marks (3):** 1 for each definition; 1 for explaining why rules alone do not distinguish them.

7. **Model:** Verbal communication uses words, such as explaining a lesson. Non-verbal communication uses cues such as a raised eyebrow. **Marks (4):** 1 for each correctly identified form; 1 for each matching classroom example.

8. **Model:** Communication flows downward without feedback. Resource problems may remain hidden. Coercion cannot be inferred without evidence of force or a threat. **Marks (3):** 1 for direction; 1 for a linked risk; 1 for the justified limit on inference.

9. **Model:** The neighbours choose mediators through trust built in personal relationships. This is one invented case; other villages may use courts or combine procedures. **Marks (2):** 1 for explaining trust; 1 for limiting the inference.

10. **Model:** Staff concerns can travel upward through the hierarchy, allowing the head to respond to problems. **Marks (2):** 1 for upward communication; 1 for explaining its benefit.
