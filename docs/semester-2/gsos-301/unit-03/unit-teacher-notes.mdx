## Teaching strategies for Unit 3

### Opening the unit

This unit examines the invisible architecture of social life: the positions people occupy, the expectations attached to them, and the processes through which people interact. Use the opening vignette (Topic 3.1) to surface the problem: every teacher holds multiple statuses and roles, and the tension between them is familiar. This makes the abstract concepts immediately relevant.

### Topic 3.1 - How society is structured

**Key pedagogical move:** Make the abstract personal. Use the "Mapping your statuses and roles" activity early. Trainees will grasp status and role more easily through their own experience than through definitions alone. The activity also surfaces role strain naturally: most trainees will find at least two statuses whose roles conflict.

**Likely wrong answer:** "Bureaucracy is just red tape and inefficiency." Correct by explaining: bureaucracy can be slow and frustrating, but Weber argued it brings predictability and fairness. Without it, organizations run on personal relationships and arbitrary decisions, which often harms the most vulnerable. Ask trainees to imagine a school where promotions depend on who you know rather than your qualifications.

**Good discussion prompt:** "Think of a time when you experienced role strain - when two of your statuses pulled you in different directions. What happened? How did you manage it?"

### Topic 3.2 - How people interact

**Key pedagogical move:** Use the opening vignette (the debate competition) as the running example throughout the topic. It contains cooperation, competition and conflict, so trainees can analyse the same story through multiple lenses. The "Interaction analysis" activity then asks them to apply the concepts to a new scenario.

**Likely wrong answer:** "Conflict is always destructive." Correct by explaining: conflict can drive positive social change. The struggle for girls' education in Pakistan involved conflict, and the outcome improved millions of lives. The key is to manage conflict constructively, not to pretend it does not exist.

**Good discussion prompt:** "Have you seen competition turn into conflict in your school or community? What caused the shift, and how was it resolved?"

## Sequencing and pacing

The unit is designed for approximately 5-6 hours of contact time:

| Topic | Contact hours | Main activity |
|---|---|---|
| 3.1 How society is structured | 2-3 hours | "Mapping your statuses and roles" pairs activity |
| 3.2 How people interact | 2-3 hours | "Interaction analysis" group activity |

The unit-assessment is designed for independent study or a supervised examination session.

## Practical work and assignments

1. **Status and role map (individual):** Each trainee maps their own statuses, identifies role strain, and suggests one structural change that would reduce it. This deepens Topic 3.1.

2. **Interaction observation (individual):** Each trainee observes one social interaction in their school or community and identifies whether it is cooperation, competition or conflict. They write a one-paragraph analysis. This deepens Topic 3.2.

3. **Group case analysis:** Small groups analyse a case of competition turning into conflict (for example, two sports teams accusing each other of cheating). Groups present their analysis and proposed resolution to the class.

## Handling trainee misconceptions

1. **"Roles are fixed and unchanging."** Reframe: people play multiple roles simultaneously, and those roles often conflict. Role strain is normal, not a sign of personal failure.

2. **"Bureaucracy is only red tape."** Reframe: bureaucracy brings predictability and fairness. Its slowness is a trade-off, not a defect. A school without bureaucracy would run on favouritism.

3. **"Conflict is always bad."** Reframe: conflict can drive positive change. The question is not whether conflict exists but how it is managed - through dialogue and fair processes rather than violence.
