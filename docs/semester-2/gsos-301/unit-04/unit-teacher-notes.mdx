# Unit 4 - Teacher notes

These notes are for the teacher. They do not carry assessment items. The practical, discussion-based and case-study activities that the course guide requires live here.

## Teaching strategies

Use a mix of the strategies the course guide names. The unit's content is abstract, so move from the concrete to the general in each topic.

- **Interactive discussion** works well for the classroom vignettes. Ask, "What do you notice about who gets access here?" before naming the sociological concept. Let learners surface the pattern, then attach the term.
- **Question-and-answer** fits the mobility classifications. Give one case, ask learners to classify it, then change one detail and ask whether the classification changes. This makes the dimensions (direction, time comparison) visible.
- **Demonstration** suits the literacy indicator comparison. Project or hand out the table from Topic 4.2 and walk through how to read one row (population, year, definition, unit) before asking learners to interpret another.
- **Case study analysis** is the spine of both summative tasks. Give a short fictional case, ask learners to apply one concept, then a second, then to compare the two readings.

## Sequencing

Topic 4.1 (stratification) before Topic 4.2 (gender): both status/role and the conflict perspective are prerequisites. Stratification gives the ranking language (class, caste, structural barrier); gender applies and extends it to learned roles. If you have limited contact hours, 4.1 is the heavier topic; do not skip the mobility classification exercise, it is the part learners most often confuse.

A workable four-session plan:

1. Session 1: Topic 4.1 opening vignette + Explanation through mobility. Activity: Follow the opportunity chain.
2. Session 2: Topic 4.1 inequality + Check your understanding + Summative task. Collect and return feedback before the next session.
3. Session 3: Topic 4.2 opening vignette + Explanation through gender inequality. Activity: Two lenses, one school decision.
4. Session 4: Topic 4.2 feminist perspectives + Summative task. Brief review of both topics, then assign the unit assessment.

## Common misconceptions and how to handle them

- **"Inequality is natural or inevitable."** Learners arrive with this belief more than any other in the unit. Do not argue it directly. Instead, walk through a rule that was changed (the printing requirement, a selection committee's criteria) and ask what changed as a result. The point is that arrangements can be examined and altered, not that one change removes all inequality.
- **"Gender is purely biological."** This resurfaces when learners first distinguish sex from gender. Return to the opening vignette: the task allocation is a learned routine, and it can be rotated. Keep the focus on the routine, not on anyone's body.
- **"A success story proves equal opportunity."** Learners narrate individual mobility stories fluently. The exercise "which people does this story leave out?" is the counterweight. Ask it each time a case is discussed.

## Discussion prompts

A good prompt is specific and evidence-based, not abstract. Use these or adapt them:

- "What is one school rule that looks the same for everyone but might not work the same for everyone? What would you check before deciding?"
- "If you wanted to know whether girls in this school have the same access to science equipment, what would you count? What would you NOT learn from that count?"
- "A pupil says, 'My family is poor, so I cannot become a teacher.' What part of that statement is true, and what part does the unit suggest examining?"

## Likely wrong answers and how to work with them

- **A learner conflates class and caste.** Do not correct by saying "wrong." Ask: "Is the advantage inherited at birth and tied to marriage rules, or is it tied to income and wealth right now?" Let the distinction emerge from the evidence.
- **A learner says the literacy gap proves girls are less able.** Ask: "What does the number actually measure, and what would we have to check before drawing a conclusion about ability?" Return to the source definition.
- **A learner says a woman in leadership means inequality is over.** Agree that the achievement is real, then ask: "What would we have to know about the other women who wanted that role to see whether the path was equally open?"

## Practical work

The course guide names individual assignments, group assignments, presentations, internet-research tasks and hands-on exercises. Options that fit this unit:

- **Individual assignment:** each learner writes a one-page analysis of a fictional mobility case, classifying it by direction and time comparison and naming one possible barrier.
- **Group assignment:** each group analyses the same fictional club or enrolment gap through both liberal and structural feminist lenses and presents a joint recommendation with one school-level and one wider action.
- **Presentation:** a five-minute summary of the evidence table from Topic 4.2, teaching the class how to read one indicator without making unsupported claims about individuals.
- **Internet-research task:** with appropriate safeguards, learners locate one published statistic about education or health in Pakistan and record its source, population, year and definition, then state one claim the statistic can support and one it cannot.

## Sensitivity notes

Stratification and caste examples can cause real harm if handled carelessly. Use invented groups (Group P, Group Q, a fictional village near [town]) rather than naming communities. Do not ask learners to identify their own caste, income or family standing. If a learner volunteers a personal experience, thank them and return to the invented case. The analytical goal is to examine the rule or arrangement, not to rank real families.

Gender discussion likewise needs a clear norm against ridicule. The unit's framework examines rules and patterns, not the worth of any individual or group. State this explicitly at the start of Topic 4.2 and restate it if the discussion drifts toward generalising about "all boys" or "all girls."
