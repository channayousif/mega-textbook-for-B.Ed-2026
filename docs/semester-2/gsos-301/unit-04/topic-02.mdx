# Topic 4.2 - How gender structures social life

## A real classroom situation

In a fictional school near Hyderabad, the teacher asks boys to carry science equipment and girls to tidy the room. A girl who wants to connect the circuit is told to write the report instead. A boy who enjoys caring for younger pupils is teased by classmates. During a staff discussion, the trainee asks whether pupils chose these tasks or learned to expect them. The question leads from everyday roles to access, power and opportunity.

## Explanation

### Gender roles and stereotypes

**Sex** refers to biological characteristics, including reproductive anatomy and related traits. **Gender** concerns socially learned meanings, identities and expectations associated with being a girl, boy, woman, man or another gender. A **gender role** is an expectation about what someone should do because of gender. A **stereotype** is an overgeneralised belief about a group, used as though it described every member. Gender expectations are learned through interaction and institutions, rather than being a direct instruction from biology (Conerly, Holmes, & Tamang, 2021, section 12.2).

The ability to lift a particular object can be checked by its weight and by safe handling requirements. It should not be assumed from a pupil's gender. Writing a careful science report and connecting a safe classroom circuit are learned skills. Giving different pupils different opportunities to learn them can create different experience. A later difference in confidence may then be mistaken for an original difference in ability.

Here is a **worked analysis** of the opening case. The statement "girls should tidy" assigns a role. The claim "boys understand equipment better" is a stereotype. The decision to keep girls away from circuit building limits practice. If a later test includes handling equipment, this allocation can affect preparation. The sequence is expectation, allocation, practice and possible outcome. It does not require us to infer anyone's private intentions. We can examine the routine and its consequences even if the teacher believes the routine is helpful.

Change the routine by rotating tasks. In one lesson, each member handles equipment, records an observation and reports a result. The teacher explains safety instructions and offers support according to experience. Rotation does not mean forcing a pupil to carry something unsafe. It means that reasons for support are tied to the task and the individual, rather than to a stereotype. A pupil with a physical access need may use an adapted role while retaining access to the scientific reasoning.

A second fictional example concerns collecting water before school. In one household, a daughter regularly helps while her brother studies. In another household, children share the work. The contrast illustrates that household arrangements can vary. It is not evidence about how every family in Sindh behaves. Ask who does a task, how much time it takes and what learning opportunity is affected. Respect for family members does not require treating every division of labour as unchangeable.

The common misconception that gender roles are purely biological confuses bodies with social rules. Biology does not decide who chairs a school club, who can use a computer or whose answer the teacher hears first. A rule about these activities is learned and can be questioned. Avoid replacing the misconception with another absolute claim, such as "all differences are caused by schools". Households, media, peers, workplaces and public institutions also shape expectations. Individuals can accept, negotiate or resist them.

A teacher can reinforce roles through praise as well as through restrictions. Praising a girl only for neatness and a boy only for initiative sends a message about what each should value. Try specific feedback tied to work: "Your diagram explains the connection" or "You checked the evidence before deciding." Such feedback makes the basis of achievement visible. It also avoids using a successful pupil as proof that an entire group has a fixed trait.

Boys can also be constrained by gender stereotypes. A boy discouraged from seeking help may hide confusion; a boy mocked for caregiving may avoid a useful responsibility. Recognising these effects does not erase the disadvantages faced by girls and women. It helps a teacher examine each restriction precisely. Support should follow the barrier, rather than a competition over which group deserves concern.

Classroom discussion needs care. Do not ask a child to represent "what girls think" or "what boys can do". Ask about the rule in the fictional case and what evidence would justify it. If a learner repeats a stereotype, examine the claim without humiliating the speaker. For example, ask whether it applies to every person and whether unequal practice could explain the observation. Maintain a clear standard against ridicule while allowing learners to revise an idea.

### Gender inequality

**Gender inequality** is an unequal distribution of opportunities, resources, voice or rewards associated with gender. A difference in the number of pupils attending a club is a starting observation. To explain it, ask who can join, when it meets, whether travel is safe, how invitations are sent and how work at home is organised. The observation does not by itself establish a single cause. It also does not show what any one pupil wants or can achieve.

An **indicator** is a defined measure used to describe a condition. Literacy, enrolment and labour-force participation measure different things. Literacy concerns a reading and writing capacity under the source's definition. Enrolment concerns registration in education, while attendance concerns actually being present. Labour-force participation concerns people counted as employed or seeking and available for work under a survey definition. It is not the same as all useful work: unpaid household care may be outside that count. State the source definition before making a comparison.

The Finance Division's *Pakistan Economic Survey 2024–25*, Education chapter, Table 10.2, reports 2023 Census literacy for people aged ten and above: males 68.00%, females 52.84%, and the table's transgender category 40.15%. These are the publication's categories, not a complete account of gender identities. They describe a 2023 measure, not literacy in every later year. The male–female difference is **15.16 percentage points**, calculated by subtracting 52.84 from 68.00 (Government of Pakistan, Finance Division, 2025).

A percentage-point difference compares two percentages directly. Calling it "15.16 percent less" would use a different calculation and be ambiguous. Give the unit with the result. This comparison establishes an aggregate gap. It cannot show why a particular child missed school or predict the ability of a particular girl. It also cannot tell us which proposed intervention will work. Those are separate questions requiring additional evidence.

The following source-based literacy values and proposed measurement questions serve different purposes. Only the literacy row supplies a published numerical comparison. The work and health rows identify what evidence a responsible comparison would need; blank numerical evidence is not a zero.

Now consider a **worked classroom investigation**, using invented numbers. A school has 20 eligible girls and 20 eligible boys. Eight girls and sixteen boys join an after-school computer club. The participation rates are 40% and 80%, respectively. The gap is 40 percentage points. These numbers show unequal participation in this club; they do not yet explain it. If the teacher counts only club members, she cannot discover what kept other eligible pupils away.

The trainee checks three possible explanations. Perhaps invitations did not reach everyone. Perhaps transport leaves before the club ends. Perhaps some pupils have work at home at that hour. She first checks the announcement and timetable, then discusses feasible options with the mentor. She does not demand private disclosures from children. Suppose an in-school trial session gives everyone practice and participation becomes more balanced. That supports the usefulness of changing timing in this setting. It does not establish that timing explains the national literacy gap.

Class and gender may interact. Two girls may face the same expectation about travel, but only one household can pay for a safe transport option. Two boys may receive the same encouragement to attend, but one must help earn income. Analyze the combination of circumstances rather than treating "girls" or "boys" as internally identical groups. This is sometimes called an **intersectional** approach: considering how forms of inequality work together. Here the practical question is whose barrier remains when one rule changes.

Equal treatment alone may leave such barriers in place. An invitation addressed equally to all is a useful step, but it does not create transport. Moving the club into the school day may help pupils who cannot stay late. It could reduce time for another subject, so the teacher must consider the timetable and consult colleagues. Every proposed change has a cost or limit. A defensible proposal states the barrier addressed, the person responsible and what evidence would show improvement.

Avoid explaining inequality as a moral failing of a whole community. A family may weigh distance, cost, safety and care obligations under real constraints. Discuss those constraints rather than assigning one motive without evidence. Equally, respectful explanation does not require silence about exclusion. A rule denying a pupil a task because of gender can be examined directly. Separate the action, the evidence and the response from assumptions about the worth of the people involved.

### Feminist perspectives

**Feminist perspectives** examine gender inequality and seek changes that increase equality and freedom from gender-based restrictions. They are analytical approaches, not a claim that every woman has the same experience or every man holds the same power. OpenStax discusses feminist theory alongside conflict and other sociological approaches to gender (Conerly et al., 2021, section 12.2). We will compare two lines of questioning: equal access within existing institutions and changes to the structures organising work and power.

A **liberal feminist** approach focuses on equal rights, removal of discriminatory rules and access to education, employment and decision-making. Ask whether a girl can apply on the same terms, receive the same materials and be judged by the same relevant criteria. In the science lesson, a liberal response would remove the boys-only equipment rule and give everyone opportunities to practise. It could also examine textbooks and expectations that narrow pupils' choices. Its strength is a clear target for reform: change an exclusionary rule or unequal provision.

A **structural feminist** approach asks how wider arrangements maintain inequality, even where a formal rule appears equal. In this unit the term is an umbrella for questions about power, economic dependence and the organisation of unpaid care; it is not a single unified theory. Socialist feminist questions pay particular attention to class and the relation between paid and unpaid work. Other feminist approaches place different emphasis on patriarchal power, meaning social arrangements that privilege men and masculine authority. These perspectives can disagree about causes and priorities.

Return to the fictional computer club. A liberal analysis asks whether girls were invited, allowed to enrol and given equal equipment time. A structural analysis asks who performs care work at the meeting hour, who controls transport money, and who has authority to alter those arrangements. The two approaches may recommend related actions for different reasons. Equal invitations can be necessary while still being insufficient when the timetable excludes those who cannot stay late.

Here is a **worked comparison** using a fictional secondary-school enrolment gap. A village school opens admissions to all eligible children, but fewer girls continue from primary school. The liberal reading identifies possible unequal information, fees, admission rules or teacher expectations. It proposes common criteria, accessible information and equal subject access. The structural reading follows the school journey into household labour, transport, safety and power over spending. It asks whether a formally equal place is usable under existing arrangements.

Neither reading is established just by the gap. Each generates questions to investigate. Check enrolment records for the relevant cohort; distinguish not enrolling from enrolling and then leaving. Examine distance and school provision, and consult families through an approved process rather than collecting sensitive stories independently. Compare explanations with the available evidence. If the information shows unequal fees, an admissions reform has direct support. If fees are equal but timing conflicts with care duties, a different response may be needed.

An integrated proposal might remove a discriminatory subject restriction, arrange safe travel with the responsible authorities and review schedules. The teacher can rotate classroom roles immediately, but cannot promise district transport alone. Describe the difference between what a school can do now and what needs institutional cooperation. Evaluate success through access and learning, rather than only counting how many pupils heard an awareness talk.

Feminism is sometimes misrepresented as hostility towards men or a demand that all families live identically. The central question here is whether gender-based rules restrict people's opportunities and voice. Boys and men can participate in examining and changing those rules. Women can also enforce restrictive expectations. Analysis concerns patterns and power, not an assumption that every member of one gender acts in one way.

Another misconception is that a woman in a leadership position proves inequality has ended. Her achievement is relevant evidence of opportunity in that case. It does not tell us the conditions faced by those who could not enter the profession. Compare the path from application to appointment and advancement. Ask who was eligible, who applied, who was selected and what support was available. A single success and a wider pattern of inequality can both be real.

Finally, theory should improve a teacher's judgement. A theory that leads only to a label has not yet done its work. Connect it to a question, a source of evidence and a plausible response. Consider an alternative explanation and say what remains uncertain. This approach lets you challenge restrictive practices while avoiding unsupported claims about children, families or entire communities.

## Activity: Two lenses, one school decision

**Work in groups of four for about 25 minutes.** Use the fictional club case. Two members prepare a liberal feminist account; two prepare a structural feminist account. First explain roles and stereotypes from memory. Then each pair writes a question its assigned approach would ask, a possible mechanism and a piece of evidence needed to test it.

Exchange explanations. The receiving pair must paraphrase the other pair's reasoning accurately before criticising it. Together agree on one change within the teacher's control and one requiring wider cooperation. State whose participation could still be limited after your first change. Present a joint recommendation in two minutes and invite another group to identify an unsupported assumption.

You are comparing explanations, not acting out stereotyped male or female roles. No learner has to speak about household duties, gender identity or discrimination they have experienced. Keep the analysis on the supplied fictional case.

## Check your understanding

1. Define a gender role and a gender stereotype. Give a different classroom example of each. *(Remember)*
2. Explain why a literacy gap does not measure the ability of an individual pupil. *(Understand)*
3. A club is open to everyone but meets after the last shared bus leaves. Apply the idea of a structural barrier to this rule. *(Apply)*
4. Using the published literacy percentages above, calculate the male–female gap and state its unit, population and data year. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Gender roles are learned expectations; stereotypes treat a group assumption as an individual fact. Inequality is investigated through defined indicators and specific mechanisms, not inferred ability. Liberal feminist questions examine equal rights and access, while structural feminist questions examine the organisation of work, resources and power. Combining careful evidence with both sets of questions can improve a school response without pretending that one adjustment resolves every barrier.

## Try this at your practicum school

Ask your mentor to help you review one lesson plan for access to demanding tasks, equipment and reporting roles. Analyze the planned allocation before observing children. With permission, compare the plan to one ordinary lesson, using only anonymous task counts. Discuss whether a rotation would improve access and how you would assess learning afterwards. Do not record pupil identities, ask about private household duties or infer gender identity from appearance. If observation is not approved, revise the fictional science lesson instead.

## Summative task

Write a 400–500-word recommendation about the fictional club with eight of twenty eligible girls and sixteen of twenty eligible boys attending. Analyze at least two possible mechanisms, including one involving the organisation of time or resources. Compare what liberal and structural feminist approaches would ask. Propose an initial adjustment, a follow-up measure and one remaining limitation. Explain why club participation cannot be used as a national estimate or as evidence of innate ability.

**Mini-rubric, 12 points:** distinctions and use of indicators (0–3: absent, confused, mostly accurate, accurate with proper limits); comparison of perspectives (0–4: absent, labels only, one explanation, both explained, both connected to evidence and a meaningful difference); response linked to a mechanism (0–3: absent, generic, feasible but partly linked, feasible and clearly linked); limits and follow-up (0–2: absent, one vague point, specific limit and suitable measure). The comparison criterion requires **Analyze**: two definitions without application can earn at most one of its four points.
