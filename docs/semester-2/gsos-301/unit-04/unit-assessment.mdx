## Unit summary

This unit examined two structures that shape every society: **stratification** (how groups are ranked unequally) and **gender** (how learned expectations produce unequal opportunities).

- Topic 4.1 distinguished class, caste and status as different bases of ranking, traced the direction and time comparison in social mobility, and explained how structural barriers and unequal resources produce inequality rather than natural inferiority.
- Topic 4.2 separated biological sex from socially learned gender roles and stereotypes, used defined indicators to interpret inequality evidence carefully, and compared liberal feminist (equal access and rights) with structural feminist (organisation of work, power and unpaid care) responses.

Together these give you a framework for analysing how class, inherited ranking and gender interact to shape pupils' opportunities, and for proposing feasible school responses while acknowledging what lies beyond a teacher's immediate control.

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

Choose the single best answer for each question.

1. *(Remember)* In this unit, which term describes a comparatively closed system of inherited group ranking assigned at birth?
   - A) Class
   - B) Status
   - C) Caste
   - D) Prestige

2. *(Remember)* Census 2023 literacy for people aged ten and above, as reproduced in the Pakistan Economic Survey 2024-25 Table 10.2, reports the male literacy rate as:
   - A) 52.84%
   - B) 68.00%
   - C) 40.15%
   - D) 76.00%

3. *(Understand)* A father is a daily-wage labourer; his daughter completes teacher training and obtains a secure post. Classifying this as upward intergenerational mobility combines:
   - A) A change of direction and a comparison within one year
   - B) Movement to a more advantaged position and a comparison between generations
   - C) A geographical move and a change of employer
   - D) Horizontal movement and a comparison within one person's life

4. *(Understand)* Gender, as used in this unit, refers to:
   - A) Biological characteristics only
   - B) Socially learned meanings and expectations associated with being a girl, boy, woman or man
   - C) The same thing as sex
   - D) Fixed personality traits determined at birth

5. *(Apply)* A school rule requires every pupil to print online homework at home. Three pupils have no device. The rule is unequal because:
   - A) The homework is too difficult
   - B) The rule ignores an access barrier that affects participation
   - C) All pupils dislike printing
   - D) The teacher dislikes technology

6. *(Apply)* A family in rural Sindh expects their daughter to manage all household chores after school while her brother studies uninterrupted. A sociological reading focuses first on:
   - A) The daughter's lack of ability
   - B) How learned gender roles allocate time and affect learning opportunities
   - C) That boys are naturally better at academic work
   - D) The mother's personal dislike of the daughter

7. *(Apply)* A liberal feminist analysis of the after-school club with unequal participation would first ask:
   - A) Who performs unpaid care work at the meeting hour
   - B) Whether girls were invited, allowed to enrol and given equal equipment time
   - C) The whole organisation of transport money and authority in the village
   - D) Whether the club should be abolished entirely

8. *(Apply)* Which of the following describes a structural barrier?
   - A) A teacher who dislikes one pupil
   - B) An application process whose travel cost and documentation requirements are difficult for some candidates
   - C) A pupil who forgot the deadline
   - D) A head teacher's personal decision on one case

9. *(Apply)* A woman becomes head of a government girls' school. A careful sociological reading treats this as:
   - A) Proof that gender inequality has ended everywhere
   - B) Evidence of opportunity in that single case, without by itself establishing the conditions faced by all women
   - C) Irrelevant to classroom teaching
   - D) Evidence that men no longer hold any power

10. *(Apply)* A person with high social esteem but modest wealth illustrates that:
    - A) Esteem and wealth must always match
    - B) Status (social respect) and class (economic position) are different dimensions that can diverge
    - C) Wealth is the only real form of social rank
    - D) Respect is always earned through money

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

Answer each question in the space indicated.

1. *(Remember)* Define class and caste in one sentence each, naming the key difference between them.

2. *(Remember)* Name the three indicators of gender inequality used in the unit's evidence table and state what each measures.

3. *(Understand)* Explain in two sentences why a published literacy gap does not measure the ability of an individual pupil.

4. *(Apply)* A teacher rotates laboratory tasks so every pupil handles equipment, records observations and reports results. Explain how this addresses a structural barrier in two sentences.

5. *(Apply)* Distinguish equality from equity using the example of an online homework requirement in two or three sentences.

6. *(Apply)* Classify the following fictional case: a shopkeeper loses her business after a flood and moves to a smaller rented stall with lower income. State the direction and time comparison, and name the evidence your classification depends on.

7. *(Analyze)* In a fictional club, sixteen of twenty boys and four of twenty girls attend. The club meets after the last shared bus leaves. Analyse this situation using both a liberal feminist question and a structural feminist question.

8. *(Apply)* Define a stereotype and give one original classroom example that was not used in the topic files.

9. *(Analyze)* Explain why structural mobility cannot be explained only by individual ambition. Give one fictional example.

10. *(Apply)* A school publishes clear selection criteria and checks every decision against them. Explain in two sentences how this makes one route for favouritism harder, and state one limitation.

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

Answer each question in a short paragraph (150-200 words unless stated otherwise).

1. *(Analyse)* A scholarship is advertised online. Applicants must print forms, travel twice to a district office and show examination results. A rural pupil with strong results cannot meet the travel cost; a well-connected family helps their child complete the form. Analyse how class, administrative rules and networks interact in this case. Distinguish two plausible explanations for the unequal outcome and recommend one school action and one district action.

2. *(Analyse/Evaluate)* A teacher says, "Girls in my class simply don't like science; that's why they don't volunteer for experiments." Evaluate this claim using the concepts of gender roles, stereotypes and task allocation from Topic 4.2. What evidence would you gather before accepting or rejecting the claim?

3. *(Analyse)* A village school opens admissions to all eligible children, but fewer girls continue from primary to middle school. Analyse this situation using both a liberal feminist and a structural feminist reading. Compare what each approach asks and what each would recommend.

4. *(Analyse/Create)* Describe a realistic Pakistani classroom situation in which both class and gender shape access to a learning opportunity. Analyse the interaction using at least one stratification concept and one gender concept from this unit. Propose one feasible adjustment the teacher can make and one action requiring wider cooperation.

5. *(Evaluate)* "Social inequality is natural and inevitable, so schools can do little about it." Evaluate this claim using evidence from both topics of this unit. Give one specific example of a rule or resource distribution that was changed and explain what that shows about the claim.

## Answers and marking guidance

This section is for self-study. Try every question above before you look back at the keys below.

### MCQ answer key

1. **C** - Caste. Class concerns economic position; status concerns social esteem; prestige overlaps with status. Caste is the inherited, comparatively closed form named in the unit.
2. **B** - 60.00%. A is the female rate; C is the transgender category rate; D is not a figure from this table.
3. **B** - Upward (more advantaged) intergenerational (between generations). A mixes dimensions wrongly; C confuses geographical with social movement; D mislabels the time comparison.
4. **B** - Socially learned meanings and expectations. A and C confuse sex with gender; D describes a biological view the unit rejects.
5. **B** - The rule ignores an access barrier. The other options blame difficulty, preference or the teacher's attitude rather than unequal access to a resource.
6. **B** - Learned roles allocate time and affect opportunities. A and C are ability-based explanations the unit challenges; D personalises what is a structural pattern.
7. **B** - Liberal feminism asks about equal invitations, access and provision. A and C are structural questions; D is not a feminist analytical response.
8. **B** - A structural barrier is built into a recurring arrangement. A and D are individual acts; C is a one-time omission, not a structural obstacle.
9. **B** - One case shows opportunity in that case; it does not establish conditions for all women. A is the misconception the unit names; C and D are unsupported extremes.
10. **B** - Status and class can diverge. A is contradicted by the example; C and D collapse the distinct dimensions the unit keeps separate.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

1. **Model:** Class concerns a person's economic position, including income, wealth and work; caste refers to a comparatively closed system of inherited group ranking assigned at birth. **Marks (2):** 1 - defines class in economic terms; 1 - defines caste as inherited and closed.

2. **Model:** Education (literacy at age ten and above, Census 2023), work (labour-force participation by sex), health (access to needed care by sex or gender). **Marks (2):** 1 - names all three indicators; 1 - correctly matches at least two to what they measure.

3. **Model:** A literacy gap is an aggregate measure across a population under a specific definition. It does not show why any individual missed school or predict any individual's ability; those are separate questions requiring additional evidence. **Marks (2):** 1 - names the aggregate nature of the measure; 1 - explains why it does not transfer to an individual.

4. **Model:** The original routine may allocate equipment handling by gender, limiting practice. Rotation ties support to the task and the individual rather than to a stereotype, so access to the skill is less affected by an irrelevant characteristic. **Marks (2):** 1 - identifies the barrier (unequal access to practice); 1 - explains how rotation addresses it.

5. **Model:** Equality gives everyone the same provision (the same online task). Equity responds to different barriers so people have a fair opportunity (providing a paper version that tests the same outcome). **Marks (2):** 1 - defines equality; 1 - defines equity with the example.

6. **Model:** Direction: downward, from a larger established business to a smaller stall with lower income. Time comparison: intragenerational, following one person's life. The classification depends on evidence of the change in assets, income and security, not on character. **Marks (3):** 1 - correct direction; 1 - correct time comparison; 1 - names the evidence required.

7. **Model:** Liberal: were girls invited, allowed to enrol and given equal equipment time? Structural: who performs unpaid care or earns at the meeting hour, who controls transport, and who has authority to alter the timetable. **Marks (3):** 1 - liberal question about access; 1 - structural question about time and resources; 1 - shows the approaches ask different things.

8. **Model:** A stereotype is an overgeneralised belief about a group used as though it described every member. Example: "Boys in this class are naturally noisier," applied to every boy regardless of actual behaviour. **Marks (2):** 1 - correct definition; 1 - original classroom example.

9. **Model:** Structural mobility comes from wider changes in the economy or institutions, not individual effort alone. Example: when a new secondary school opens, many households gain secure posts at once; the change cannot be explained only by a sudden rise in ambition. **Marks (2):** 1 - defines structural mobility; 1 - gives a fictional example.

10. **Model:** Published, checked criteria make it harder to select by personal favour, because decisions can be compared against a stated standard. Limitation: the rule does not by itself remove unequal preparation for the test or unequal access to the information needed to apply. **Marks (2):** 1 - explains how criteria reduce favouritism; 1 - states a remaining limitation.
