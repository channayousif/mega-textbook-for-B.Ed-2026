# Topic 4.1 - How societies rank people

## A real classroom situation

At a fictional government school near Dadu, two pupils qualify for a district science visit. One family can pay for travel immediately; the other needs the fee waived. A visitor calls the first pupil's family "more respectable" because it owns land. The teacher knows that both pupils produced strong science work. She must separate their learning from the resources and social standing attached to their families.

## Explanation

### Class, caste, and social hierarchy

**Social stratification** means a lasting pattern of ranking groups with unequal access to valued resources. **Social hierarchy** is an arrangement of positions regarded as higher or lower. A difference becomes part of stratification when it repeatedly affects access, authority or respect. Different favourite foods do not by themselves place one group above another. A rule that lets one group eat first and excludes another from the table creates a ranking. OpenStax distinguishes class systems, which allow movement, from caste systems, which restrict it through inherited membership (Conerly, Holmes, & Tamang, 2021, section 9.1).

**Class** concerns a person's economic position, including income, wealth and the work through which resources are gained. Income is a flow, such as wages received each month. Wealth is a stock of assets, such as land, savings or a house, after allowing for debts. A household with little monthly cash but valuable land differs from one that rents its home and depends on a daily wage. Do not decide class from one visible object. A pupil may carry an expensive phone that was borrowed or received as a gift.

**Caste** refers to a comparatively closed system of inherited group ranking. Membership is assigned at birth, and social rules may restrict marriage or work across group boundaries. **Status**, used here as social esteem, concerns the respect people receive. This meaning builds on Unit 3's broader use of status as a social position. Respect, money and power can overlap without being identical. A retired teacher may be widely respected while having less wealth than a trader. A wealthy person may have influence but receive little trust.

Hammond and Cheney (n.d., chapter 1.9) connect stratification with differences in wealth, power and prestige. Prestige means social respect or esteem. Their examples are mainly from the United States. We use the distinction as a question to ask about a setting, rather than carrying US class categories unchanged into Pakistan. A useful question is: which resource, relationship or rule gives someone an advantage here?

Consider this **worked example**, a fictional Sindh village meeting about a school repair. A large landowner offers money and expects to decide which room is repaired first. A retired woman teacher explains which classroom leaks most, and families listen because they trust her judgement. A craft worker's suggestion receives little attention after someone refers to the worker's inherited occupational group. First identify the evidence: the money offered, the trust expressed, and the dismissive reference. Next name the mechanism: economic resources, esteem earned through service, and inherited group ranking. Finally state the limit: the vignette does not prove that all village decisions follow this pattern.

The three dimensions should not become three labels fixed to each person. The landowner's wealth does not tell us whether families respect the repair proposal. The teacher's esteem does not prove she controls the budget. The craft worker's treatment tells us something about exclusion in this meeting, not about the worker's ability. Analyze the rule or action that produces the inequality. Do not repeat the ranking as if it were a judgement you accept.

A **biradari** is a kinship or community network. It may offer support, information and mutual help. It is not automatically the same as a caste. Ask what actually happens: are opportunities shared only through relatives, are marriage boundaries enforced, or is inherited occupation treated as a basis for exclusion? Similar-looking group names may have different meanings across communities. Neither a surname nor a family occupation gives a teacher permission to assign a child to a rank. In classroom discussion, use invented groups such as Group P and Group Q instead of naming a pupil's community.

A common misconception is that higher social rank proves greater natural ability or human worth. It does neither. If a committee hears one person's proposal first because that person supplies funds, the observed advantage concerns control of resources. It is not a test of intelligence. The distinction matters for teaching: judging an essay by its argument is different from expecting a stronger essay because its writer has a high-status surname. Apply the same academic criteria to all learners and examine unequal access to preparation separately.

### Social mobility

**Social mobility** is movement in social position. Describe both the direction of movement and the time comparison. **Upward mobility** is movement to a more advantaged position; **downward mobility** is movement to a less advantaged one. **Horizontal mobility** is a change of position with broadly similar rank or resources. **Intergenerational mobility** compares generations; **intragenerational mobility** follows one person's life. These are different dimensions, so a single example may fit two labels (Conerly et al., 2021, section 9.2).

Here is a **worked classification**. In a fictional family in Sanghar, a father relies on irregular daily labour. His son completes teacher education, qualifies through a recruitment process and obtains a secure salaried post. We compare their occupational security and access to resources, not their human worth. On the information supplied, the son's position is more secure, so the direction is upward. We compare father and son, so the time comparison is intergenerational. "Upward intergenerational mobility" combines these two conclusions.

Now change the evidence. If the father later moves from daily labour to a secure salaried job, compare the father's own earlier and later positions. That is upward intragenerational mobility. If a teacher changes from one government school to another on the same pay scale with similar responsibilities, the move may be horizontal. A change of village is geographical movement; it is not necessarily a rise or fall in social rank. If the transfer removes access to housing or creates a large commuting cost, gather that information before assuming the two positions are equivalent.

A shopkeeper who loses a business after a flood may experience downward mobility. Calling that change a failure of character would replace explanation with blame. Ask what happened to assets, debt, security and work. These questions do not deny personal choices. They make sure the account includes the circumstances in which choices were made. Equally, a better job title is not enough evidence of upward movement if pay, security and decision-making power all decline.

The labourer's son worked hard. The fictional case also includes a nearby secondary school, a fee waiver, a mentor who explained applications, reliable transport and an available teaching post. These are opportunities that made effort usable. Without them, another equally committed learner might leave school. The misconception that mobility is equally available to everyone confuses an open rule with equal access to that rule. An application may be open to all while its travel cost, documentation or preparation requirements remain difficult for some applicants.

It helps to draw a short chain: access to school, completion, access to training, qualification, application, selection and secure employment. At each link, identify a possible barrier and a possible support. A scholarship addresses fees, but not necessarily distance. A bus route addresses travel, but not a discriminatory selection process. This analysis gives more precise recommendations than saying that education "solves poverty". Education can widen possibilities without guaranteeing every graduate a secure job.

An individual success story establishes that movement occurred in that case. It does not establish how common movement is. To estimate how widely mobility is available, we would need comparable information from many people, including those who tried and could not complete the path. Looking only at those who obtained jobs hides the missing learners. In a school discussion, ask which people the story leaves out and which step might have excluded them.

You may also hear of **structural mobility**, movement associated with wider changes in the economy or institutions. For example, a fictional expansion of secondary schools creates many teaching posts in a district. Several households may gain secure income at once. The pattern cannot be explained only by a sudden increase in individual ambition. Conversely, the closure of a large employer may remove opportunities from many families together. The analytical question is whether changes affected one person's path or altered the available positions for a whole group.

### Inequality in society

**Social inequality** means unequal access to valued resources, rights, opportunities or rewards. A **structural barrier** is an obstacle built into recurring arrangements, such as a costly application process or the absence of a nearby school. It may operate even when no individual expresses hostility. Conflict analysis asks who controls resources and rules, who gains, and whose choices become narrower. Other perspectives ask different questions. A functionalist account may ask what work a role performs and why society rewards it; that is an explanation to examine, not proof that every existing reward is fair (Conerly et al., 2021, section 9.4).

Work through a **fictional school comparison**. School A is an urban private school with a science room, a library period and daily subject teaching. School B is a rural government school with one teacher covering several classes and no safe room for storing equipment. Both set the same science examination. First identify unequal inputs: time with a subject teacher, access to texts and practical experience. Next propose a mechanism: these inputs change opportunities to practise what the examination measures. Then limit the inference: the case does not tell us every pupil's attainment or establish that all private schools outperform all government schools.

Saying that a low score proves a child lacks ability ignores these differences. Saying that resources alone determine every score also goes beyond the evidence. A careful account asks about prior learning, teaching, attendance, language and assessment design. It relates these factors to the actual task. If the paper asks pupils to interpret a laboratory observation, unequal access to practical work is directly relevant. If it asks a question unrelated to laboratory work, identify a more fitting explanation.

Distinguish **equality**, giving people the same provision or rule, from **equity**, responding to different barriers so people have a fair opportunity. Giving every pupil the same online homework is equal treatment in one sense. Providing an equivalent printed task to pupils without a device addresses an access difference. The printed version should test the same learning outcome. Equity is not an excuse to assume that particular groups cannot handle demanding work. Keep the intellectual goal high while making access to the task possible.

Here is a complete evidence-to-response chain. In a fictional class of 30, 12 pupils report through a voluntary anonymous slip that they cannot use a phone for homework. The teacher first checks that the slip asked about access at the needed time, rather than ownership somewhere in the household. She provides the same reading on paper. After two weeks she compares task completion and checks whether pupils understood the reading. Improved completion would support the usefulness of this adjustment in this class. It would not prove that every difference in attainment came from phone access.

These small invented counts are a practice dataset, not a national estimate. Published indicators need a named source, population, date and definition. For instance, literacy among people aged ten and above is not the same measure as current attendance among primary-age children. A household may include an adult who cannot read and a child who attends daily. Neither fact cancels the other. Topic 4.2 shows how to read a published literacy comparison without making claims about an individual child.

The misconception that inequality is natural or inevitable prevents examination of the arrangements maintaining it. If a school replaces an unaffordable materials requirement with shared materials, one source of disadvantage changes. If a selection committee publishes clear criteria and checks decisions against them, one route for favouritism becomes harder. Neither measure removes all inequality. Both demonstrate that rules and resource distribution can be examined and changed.

A teacher's responsibility has limits as well as possibilities. You can rotate access to materials, assess work against clear criteria and avoid humiliating labels. You may need a head teacher or district office to address staffing, buildings or transport. Identify the level responsible for each action. Do not promise a family a resource you cannot provide. Record the barrier in general terms and discuss a feasible referral with your mentor.

## Activity: Follow the opportunity chain

**Work in groups of four for about 25 minutes.** Use the fictional labourer's-son case above. Assign a reader, a chain mapper, a sceptic and a reporter. Without reopening the explanation, each member first defines class, mobility and a structural barrier in their own words. Partners correct definitions by giving an example, then consult the text if needed.

Draw the path from secondary school to secure employment. Put one opportunity and one possible obstacle beside each step. The sceptic must ask, "What evidence would show that this obstacle actually matters?" Select two supports: one a school could arrange and one needing wider action. Exchange maps with another group. Their reporter should identify one claim that your story supports and one generalisation it cannot support. Revise your map in response.

Do not use real family income, caste labels or classmates' histories. If someone volunteers a personal story, thank them and return to the invented case. The task is to examine a process, not to decide which families deserve success.

## Check your understanding

1. Define class and caste in one sentence each. State one difference in how movement is allowed. *(Remember)*
2. Explain how a person can have high social esteem and modest wealth, using an invented Sindh example. *(Understand)*
3. A teacher moves to another district at the same grade and with similar benefits. Classify the move and state the evidence you would need before changing your classification. *(Apply)*
4. A school requires paid printing for every assignment. Explain one mechanism through which that rule could reduce participation and propose an equivalent alternative. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Class, inherited group boundaries and social esteem are different bases of ranking. Mobility must be described by both direction and time comparison, using evidence of a changed position. Individual effort matters within opportunities shaped by schools, households and wider institutions. An account of inequality should identify a mechanism, consider other explanations and connect a response to the barrier it addresses.

## Try this at your practicum school

With your mentor's permission, examine one routine requirement, such as bringing materials or completing homework. Use the requirement itself and ordinary classroom observation; do not collect personal income or community identity. Note its purpose, one possible access barrier and an equivalent way to demonstrate the same learning. Ask your mentor whether the proposed alternative is workable. Record what you observed separately from what you inferred, and remove all names from your practicum note. If permission is unavailable, analyze the fictional printing requirement instead.

## Summative task

Write 400–500 words about this fictional case. A district scholarship is advertised online. Applicants must print forms, travel twice to an office and show examination results. A rural pupil with strong results cannot meet the travel cost; a well-connected family receives help completing the form. Analyze how class, networks and administrative rules interact. Distinguish two plausible explanations for the unequal outcome, recommend one school action and one district action, and state a limitation of your evidence. Do not assume that either family has acted dishonestly.

**Mini-rubric, 12 points:** conceptual distinctions (0–3: absent, confused, mostly accurate, accurate with relevant distinctions); mechanism analysis (0–4: absent, labels only, one supported link, two supported links, linked account considering an alternative); feasible responses (0–3: absent, generic, one linked response, both actions linked to barriers); evidence limits (0–2: absent, vague, specific). The mechanism criterion requires **Analyze**: listing disadvantages without showing how a rule changes access cannot earn more than one of its four points.
