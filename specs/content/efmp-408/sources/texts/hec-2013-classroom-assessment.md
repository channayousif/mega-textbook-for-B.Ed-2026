## Evidence notes

1. Unit 1, Week 1 (pp. 31-34): Classroom assessment is defined as a continuous, collaborative process of gathering, analysing and interpreting evidence about student learning. It is broader than testing and includes informal observations, classroom dialogue, questioning, pupil work samples and teacher-made tests.
2. Unit 1, Week 1 (pp. 31-35): The guide contrasts assessment with evaluation and testing. A test is a specific instrument or systematic procedure for measuring a sample of behaviour (typically yielding a numerical score). Measurement is the quantitative description of student performance (assigning numbers according to rules). Assessment encompasses all methods used to collect information on learning. Evaluation is the qualitative judgment of value, worth or adequacy based on that information, often leading to decisions such as grading, certification or curricular revision.
3. Unit 1, Week 1 (pp. 31-36): Purpose and importance of assessment in Pakistani education: assessment serves to inform instruction, identify learning needs, provide feedback to learners, guide curricular decisions, and support accountability. Effective assessment is embedded in the teaching-learning process rather than standing apart as an isolated terminal event.
4. Unit 1, Week 2 (pp. 37-46): Formative versus summative assessment: Formative assessment (assessment for learning) occurs during the instructional process. Its primary purpose is to identify gaps and guide teacher adaptations and student self-correction while learning is still underway. Its stakes are low, and it emphasises constructive, actionable feedback rather than marks. Summative assessment (assessment of learning) occurs at the end of a unit, term or course. Its purpose is to evaluate and report cumulative mastery, assign grades, or certify achievement, carrying higher stakes for the student.
5. Unit 1, Week 2 & Unit 2 (pp. 47-55): Diagnostic and placement assessment: Diagnostic assessment identifies specific learning difficulties, persistent errors or misconceptions that impede progress, informing remedial support. Placement assessment determines the prerequisite entry knowledge, skills or track suitable for a learner prior to beginning instruction.

6. Unit 3, Week 9 (pp. 68-73): Performance-based assessment. The guide gives three definitions: performance assessment requires students to demonstrate knowledge and skills, including the process by which they solve problems (Project Appleseed); it includes exhibitions, investigations, demonstrations, written or oral responses, journals and portfolios (New York State Education Department); and it is a set of strategies for the application of knowledge, skills and work habits through meaningful tasks (Hibbard). A performance task carries subject content and skills, learning objectives, a list of assessment tasks and a rubric. Designing a short task requires a title and duration, subject content and skills, grade level and grouping, a description and instructions, the learning objectives assessed, the assessment activities, and a rubric no longer than one page.
7. Unit 3, Week 9 (pp. 70-72): Rubrics. A rubric has rows used at different points during a task, and each row assesses a pupil skill. Groups study which row is used at which point, which skill each row assesses, and which rows are used daily, once or more than once. The same task is graded by another group using the rubric, so that raters see how a public criterion produces a judgement.
8. Unit 3, Week 10 (pp. 73-78): Portfolios. A portfolio is a purposeful collection of a student's work that tells a story of effort, progress or achievement, and it is both an assessment tool and a teaching tool. Types named are project, growth, achievement, competence, celebration and working folders. Characteristics decided in advance: purpose and audience; the documents and artefacts; the structure; who selects the entries; evaluation criteria for entries and the completed portfolio; student responsibility for reflection and self-evaluation; and a schedule for pupil-teacher conferences. On assessing portfolios: portfolios may be more useful for teaching and learning than for assessment, they suit formative assessment but are difficult to use for summative decisions because scoring is unreliable, and standardising a portfolio improves reliability but loses the individual story.
9. Unit 1, Week 2 (pp. 37-41) and Unit 2, Week 5 (pp. 57-59): Feedback and recording assessment data. Feedback is information about a student's progress toward a goal (Wiggins, 2012) and information provided by an agent (teacher, parent, peer, book, self, experience) about aspects of performance or understanding (Hattie and Timperley, 2007). The guide reports Butler's finding that written comments had a greater influence on learning than grades alone or grades and comments. Its guidelines for feedback that facilitates learning: related to the lesson objectives; stimulates the student to think; enables a constructive action; focused on the main points; restricted in number; pupils get time in class to respond; timely. On recording: a participation record documents who contributes to discussion, anecdotal notes preserve observations, and pupils can keep some records of their own learning.
