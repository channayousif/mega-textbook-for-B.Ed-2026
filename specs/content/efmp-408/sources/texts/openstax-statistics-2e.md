## Evidence notes

1. Section 2.5, Measures of the Center of the Data: the two most widely used measures of the centre
   are the mean (average) and the median. The mean is calculated by adding all values and dividing by
   the number of values; the sample mean is written x bar and the population mean mu. The median is
   found by ordering the data and taking the value that splits the data into two equal parts, and
   its location is given by (n + 1) / 2. The text states that the median is generally a better
   measure of the centre when there are extreme values or outliers, because it is not affected by
   the precise numerical values of the outliers.
2. Section 2.6, Skewness and the Mean, Median, and Mode: the mode is the value that occurs most
   often. In a symmetrical distribution the mean, median and mode coincide. In a left-skewed
   distribution the mean is less than the median and both are less than the mode; in a
   right-skewed distribution the mean is greater than the median and the mode is the smallest of
   the three. The text notes that the mean reflects skewing more than the median does.
3. Section 2.7, Measures of the Spread of the Data: the standard deviation is a number that
   measures how far data values are from their mean, and it provides a numerical measure of the
   overall amount of variation in a data set. It is always positive or zero; it is small when the
   data are concentrated close to the mean and larger when the data are more spread out. The
   section works the example of two supermarkets with the same mean wait of five minutes but
   standard deviations of two and four minutes, concluding that the second has more variation and
   that a particular customer's wait is less predictable there. It also gives the rule of thumb
   that a value more than two standard deviations from the mean is far from the average.
