## Evidence notes

1. Unit 1 (pp. 13-17): Testing is an essential tool in educational measurement. A test is defined as an organised set of tasks or questions administered under standardised conditions to elicit a sample of student performance. Testing provides quantifiable data about student knowledge, skills and cognitive processes.
2. Unit 1 & Unit 2 (pp. 18-24): The relationship between measurement, testing and evaluation: Measurement provides the numerical foundation (e.g., getting 38 out of 50 on a test). Appraisal and assessment integrate multiple forms of evidence (quantitative and qualitative). Evaluation synthesises this evidence against established educational standards to make judgements about adequacy and progress.
3. Unit 1, Week 2 & Unit 4 (pp. 20, 34-36): Common misconceptions in assessment: Assessment and evaluation are not interchangeable synonyms. Assessment is not merely designed to put students into rank order; its primary classroom role is diagnostic and instructional. Standard-setting can and should be applied in classroom examinations to foster criterion-referenced clarity.
4. Unit 4 (pp. 34-38): Norm-referenced versus criterion-referenced interpretation: In norm-referenced interpretation, a student's score is compared to the performance of a peer group or norm sample (e.g., percentiles, class ranks, grading on a curve). In criterion-referenced interpretation, performance is evaluated against predefined learning standards or specific behavioural criteria, describing what the student can or cannot do regardless of peer performance. Diagnostic assessment frequently relies on criterion-referenced analysis to pinpoint specific unmet competencies.
5. Unit 3 (pp. 14, 42-44): A table of specifications (test blueprint) ensures content validity by systematically mapping curriculum topics against cognitive levels (such as Bloom's Taxonomy) and question formats. It specifies the number of items, marks distribution, and percentage weightage before items are drafted, preventing test setters from over-sampling simple factual recall.
6. Unit 3 (pp. 14-15, 43-44): Subjective questions (essay and short-answer): Essays provide valid measures of complex synthesis, logical organisation, and higher-order reasoning that objective items cannot assess. However, they limit content sampling and introduce severe scoring challenges (marker subjectivity, halo effects, fatigue). Developing model answers and analytic scoring guides is essential to minimise scoring variance.
7. Unit 3 (pp. 14, 42-45): Objective items (MCQs, true/false, matching): Multiple-choice questions consist of a clear stem, a keyed option, and plausible distractors based on common learner misconceptions. Objective items allow broad curriculum sampling and quick, objective scoring, but true/false items suffer from high guessing probability (50%), while matching items require homogeneous lists.
8. Unit 3 (pp. 43-47): Guidelines for writing good test items and avoiding common errors: Test items must be free from grammatical clues, ambiguous phrasing, negative stems, and non-functional words. Distractors must be plausible, mutually exclusive, and uniform in length. Checklists (such as the AKU-EB and Sukkur IBA review checklist) should be used during test review to verify instructions, question sequence, and alignment with the table of specifications.
