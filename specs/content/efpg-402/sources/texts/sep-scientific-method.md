# Bound source evidence: sep-scientific-method

Source: Hepburn, B., & Andersen, H. (2015/2026). Scientific method. In E. N. Zalta & U. Nodelman (Eds.), *The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*. First published 13 November 2015; substantive revision 8 August 2026.
URL: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-method/

Consulted: 2026-11-27, read in full from the URL above (HTTP 200).

## Excerpts

1. Opening paragraph: "Science is an enormously successful human enterprise." Among the activities "often identified as characteristic of science are systematic observation and experimentation, inductive and deductive reasoning, the construction of models, and the formation and testing of hypotheses and theories." Such characteristics "have been looked to as a way of demarcating scientific activity from non-science".
2. Section 1: "Scientific method should be distinguished from the aims and products of science, such as knowledge, predictions, or control. Methods are the means by which those goals are achieved." The same paragraph names "values such as objectivity, reproducibility, simplicity, or past successes" behind accounts of method, and adds that "Beliefs about the role of science in society will affect the place one gives to values in scientific method."
3. Section 3.3 (Popper): "regardless of the amount of confirming evidence, we can never be certain that a hypothesis is true"; Popper used "corroboration" for how well a hypothesis "has survived previous testing".
4. Section 6.1: "the legend of a single, universal scientific method has been particularly strong" in science education. "Often, 'the scientific method' is presented in textbooks and educational web pages as a fixed four or five step procedure" running from observation, to a hypothesis, to experiments, to analysis and a conclusion. Studies show this idea is part of "both students' and teachers' conception of science", and it has been argued that "science education need to focus more on teaching about the nature of science".
5. Section 6.1 also reports Dewey's (1910) five steps of inquiry, beginning with "a felt difficulty" and ending with "further observation and experiment leading to its acceptance or rejection".
6. Section 7 (Hoyningen-Huene): in the current view, what is left to characterise science "is only its fallibility"; earlier it had been "recognized that empirical knowledge was fallible".
