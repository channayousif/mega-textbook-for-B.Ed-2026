# Bound excerpt - rahman-2020-zakat

Rahman, A., &amp; Siradjuddin, S. (2020). Islamic economic concept in poverty alleviation. *Journal of Asian Multicultural Research for Economy and Management Study*, 1(2), 67-74. https://doi.org/10.47616/jamrems.v1i2.68

OpenAlex work W3115899813, verified 2026-10-31. Diamond open access under CC BY-SA 4.0. The article analyzes the Islamic economic philosophy regarding resource distribution, ethical earning, and institutional poverty eradication. Unit 5 cites it for sub-topics U5-01, U5-02, and U5-03.

## U5-01

From Section "The Concept of Ownership and Wealth in Islam" (pp. 68-69):

"In the Islamic perspective, absolute ownership of everything in heaven and earth belongs to Allah alone. Humans act merely as trustees (khalifah) who are granted relative rights to manage and utilize wealth according to divine guidance. Consequently, economic activity is bound by moral obligations: earning must occur through lawful (halal) means, free from fraud, coercion, exploitation, and monopolistic hoarding. What is prohibited (haram) includes not only physically harmful items but transactions that extract unearned gain at the expense of others."

## U5-02

From Section "Zakat as an Instrument of Poverty Alleviation" (pp. 70-71):

"Islam establishes Zakat as an obligatory socioeconomic mechanism rather than arbitrary philanthropy. By levying a fixed 2.5% rate on surplus assets exceeding the nisab threshold, Zakat prevents the continuous concentration of wealth among a privileged minority, fulfilling the Quranic injunction in Surah Al-Hashr (59:7) that wealth should not circulate solely among the rich. Furthermore, Zakat directly addresses poverty by transferring liquid capital to the eight specified asnaf (recipients), enhancing their basic purchasing power and enabling economic self-reliance."

## U5-03

From Section "The Prohibition of Riba and Economic Justice" (pp. 71-72):

"The total prohibition of riba (interest or usury) in Islamic economics aims at eliminating structural inequality and unearned exploitation. In a debt-based system with interest, the capital provider shifts the entire risk of failure onto the entrepreneur or borrower while guaranteeing risk-free gains for themselves. Islam replaces this exploitative arrangement with partnership and risk-sharing (such as mudarabah and musharakah), where capital must participate in productive risk and returns are linked to real economic effort and value creation."
