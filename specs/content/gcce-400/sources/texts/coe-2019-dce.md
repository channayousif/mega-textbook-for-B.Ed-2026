## Evidence notes

- Page 11 defines a digital citizen as someone who, through a broad range of competences, can "actively, positively and responsibly engage in both on- and offline communities". Topic 6.1 quotes this phrase (under 15 words) with the page.
- Pages 12-13 set out ten digital domains in three areas: being online (access and inclusion; learning and creativity; media and information literacy), well-being online (ethics and empathy; health and well-being; e-Presence and communications) and rights online (active participation; rights and responsibilities; privacy and security; consumer awareness).
- Page 13 describes ethics and empathy as online ethical behaviour and interaction with others, built on recognising and understanding the feelings and perspectives of others. Topic 6.2 uses this for empathy and ethical interaction.
- Page 13 describes access and inclusion as overcoming different forms of digital exclusion and the skills needed to participate in open digital spaces. Topic 6.3 uses this for the access divide.
- Page 13 describes active participation as the competences citizens need to participate actively and positively in democratic cultures. Topic 6.1 uses this for digital civic engagement.
- Page 13 describes rights and responsibilities as rights enjoyed in the physical world that also apply online, with responsibilities such as ethics and empathy. Topic 6.2 uses this for responsibility.
- Page 18 names reliable information sources as essential for positive active participation and warns that without them digital citizenship can morph into extremism. Topic 6.2 uses this for misinformation and discernment.

Support limits: the handbook concerns children and young people in digital citizenship education, and it is a European framework. The unit uses it for general definitions and principles only, not for Pakistani law or statistics. It does not define the digital divide as access, usage, socioeconomic and geographic; that framing comes from the guide and the bound Pakistani studies.
