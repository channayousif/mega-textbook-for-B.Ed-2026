## Course Description

The guide describes reflection as a fundamental component of professional growth and pedagogical
effectiveness, and positions critical thinking as the means by which teachers examine their own
instructional practice, evaluate classroom experience, and make evidence-based decisions. The
course is explicitly aimed at **prospective secondary school teachers** and is built on three
pillars in the guide's own framing: reflective models, experiential learning, and the use of
reflective journals to sustain lifelong professional learning.

Two consequences for authoring, both load-bearing:

1. **Units 1 to 3 are a logic course.** Statements, claims, arguments, premises and conclusions,
   deductive and inductive patterns, validity and strength. This is the most technical content in
   the programme so far, and HSC-register prose must carry it without either dumbing it down or
   sliding into symbolic notation the guide never asks for.
2. **Units 4 to 6 are a practice course**, and they must land on the *secondary* classroom. The
   guide says so twice. This matters for source selection - see the Pendrey caution below.

## Course review plan

Seeds an eventual `docs/semester-2/efmp-304/course-review.mdx` (Spec 008 FR-016; contract:
`specs/008-rich-unit-pedagogy/contracts/end-of-course-review.md`). Not parsed by any gate.

- **Course summary points** (the through-lines the review should recap):
  - Critical thinking is a set of **standards** applied to reasoning, not a personality trait or
    a general scepticism (Units 1 to 3).
  - An argument is a specific object with premises and a conclusion, and most of what looks like
    argument is not one (Units 2, 3).
  - Deductive and inductive arguments fail in different ways, so they are judged by different
    tests - validity and strength (Unit 3).
  - Reflection is a **method** with a cycle and a literature, not thinking hard about your day
    (Units 4, 5).
  - A reflective journal is the instrument that makes reflection survive a school year, and it is
    assessed on this course (Unit 6).
  - The two halves are one course: the logic of Units 1 to 3 is what stops the reflection of
    Units 4 to 6 becoming self-justification (all units).
- **Practice-question mix**: `### MCQs` ~15 (all six units, Remember to Apply); `### RRQs` ~10
  (Understand to Analyze, >= 1 per unit); `### ERQs` ~5 (Analyze to Evaluate/Create, each
  integrating two or more units).
- **Practicum project ideas** (3-6 briefs a trainee carries into placement):
  - *Argument audit* - collect three claims made in a staffroom, a textbook and a policy
    document; identify premises and conclusion in each, or show that there is no argument; bring
    back the analysis.
  - *Standards check* - take one of your own lesson plans and evaluate its stated rationale
    against the critical-thinking standards; bring back the annotated plan.
  - *Reflective cycle trial* - run one full cycle on a single lesson using one named model;
    bring back the record and a note on what the model made visible that ordinary review did not.
  - *Four-lens exercise* - examine one teaching assumption of your own through Brookfield's four
    lenses; bring back what each lens showed and where they disagreed.
  - *Reflective journal* - keep the course journal for the full term against the Unit 6 prompts;
    this is the guide's assessed reflection file, not an optional extra.

## Unit 1: Understanding Critical Thinking

Weeks 1-3. Unit Spec (G1) for `docs/semester-2/efmp-304/unit-01/`.

- **CLO/SLO refs**: `SLO:EFMP-304-1-1` (what critical thinking is, its standards and barriers),
  `SLO:EFMP-304-1-2` (the basic units of reasoning; the teacher as critical thinker) - traces to
  guide CLOs 1 and 2.
- **Key terms**: Critical thinking, Critical-thinking standard, Barrier to critical thinking,
  Statement, Claim, Issue, Argument.
- **Topics** (from guide 1.1-1.8): thinking as a skill and the functions of thought; what
  critical thinking is; the recognised standards applied to reasoning; the benefits of critical
  thinking in the classroom, the workplace and life; the barriers that stop it; the basic units
  it operates on - statements, claims, issues and arguments; the characteristics of a critical
  thinker; and teachers specifically as critical thinkers.
- **Worked-example / activity concepts**: take one staffroom claim ("this class cannot do word
  problems") and run it through the standards - is it clear, accurate, precise, relevant, deep,

- **Assessment blueprint**: formative 5-8 items (Remember/Understand/Apply) on the standards and
  on telling a statement from a claim from an argument; summative includes one Analyze-or-higher
  item asking the learner to diagnose which standard a piece of staffroom reasoning breaches and
  justify the diagnosis. Weighting: the course-specific 30/70 above, not the Art. III.7 default.

**Common misconceptions**: "critical thinking means criticising"; "critical thinking is just

**Worked-examples plan** (one Pakistan-grounded example per sub-topic group): thinking as a skill

them as accusations will stop listening.

## Unit 2: Recognizing and Analyzing Arguments

- **Key terms**: Argument, Explanation, Premise, Conclusion, Rhetorical triangle, Issue question,

- **Topics** (from guide 2.1-2.5): the distinction between an argument and an explanation and the

- **Worked-example / activity concepts**: one school circular analysed twice - first for what it
  asserts, then for whether any of it is actually argued - ending with the finding that most
  official prose explains rather than argues, and that this is not a criticism of it.
- **Assessment blueprint**: formative 5-8 items (Remember/Understand/Apply) on premise and
  conclusion identification; summative includes one Analyze-or-higher item giving a passage and
  asking the learner to decide whether it argues, explains or merely asserts, with reasons.
  Weighting: the course-specific 30/70.
