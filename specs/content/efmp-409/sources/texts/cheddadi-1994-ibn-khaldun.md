## Evidence notes

1. Gradual instruction (*at-tadrij*): In Chapter 6 of the *Muqaddimah*, Ibn Khaldun insists that teaching must be gradual and staged. The teacher introduces the general outline and simple principles first, repeats the subject with greater depth and detail, and only in a third cycle covers fine disputes and complex exceptions. Overloading young minds with advanced subtleties causes intellectual exhaustion and discouragement. Supports U3-12 (Ibn Khaldun's educational views).
2. Development of a practical faculty (*malaka*): True mastery of any discipline or craft is the acquisition of an enduring, deeply rooted habit or faculty (*malaka*), acquired through continuous practical exercise, application, and repeated demonstration rather than mere rote memorization of definitions. Supports U3-12 and U3-18.
3. Condemnation of harshness and corporal punishment: Ibn Khaldun severely warns teachers against severity, harshness, and physical punishment. Cruelty crushes a child's spirit, destroys intrinsic motivation, breeds deception and dishonesty, and turns pupils into cowards who act rightly only out of fear. The teacher must lead with gentleness, patience, and good example. Supports U3-12 and U3-18.
4. Social and empirical basis of education: Ibn Khaldun analyzes education within the sociological framework of civilization (*umran*). Education transmits cultural knowledge and technical crafts needed for societal prosperity. His empirical, observational approach provides points of comparison with modern active learning while remaining grounded in Islamic ethical foundations. Supports U3-12, U3-17, and U3-18.
