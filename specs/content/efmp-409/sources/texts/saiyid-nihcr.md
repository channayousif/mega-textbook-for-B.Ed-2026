## Evidence notes

1. Page 61: the three reports were written by commissions appointed by the Government of Pakistan to examine the educational system, and each was appointed at an important juncture of political transition. Supports the point that commissions are appointed at moments of political change (U5-04).
2. Page 61: the National Education Commission, also known as the Sharif Commission, was appointed in December 1958, soon after General Ayub Khan's military coup. Supports the date and political context of the Sharif Commission (U5-02, U5-05).
3. Page 62: the Commission recommended increasing pre-university duration from ten to twelve years, and three years of study at the degree level instead of two. Supports the structural recommendations (U5-05).
4. Page 62: only 50 percent of children go to primary school, and the drop-out rate is as high as 75 percent in certain areas; the Report recommended that education be made compulsory for the first five years of primary school, in ten years' time. Supports the enrolment and compulsory-education evidence (U5-05).
5. Page 62: in secondary school there should be a core of basic subjects like science, mathematics and the national language, and after the eighth class students should have a choice of vocational schools or general education. Supports the curriculum recommendation (U5-05).
6. Page 62: the recommendation on university tutorials was unrealistic, because tutorials are prevalent only in the two most elitist universities of England and could not be regarded as feasible in a Third World country with limited resources. Supports the critical point used in the unit (U5-05).
7. Page 69: while the Nur Khan report has the breadth of vision the others lack, none of the three discusses training manpower to meet the needs of the economy in any depth; only general statements are made about the need for vocational training. Supports the criticism that the reports lacked a detailed plan (U5-04, U5-06).
8. Page 69: the persistence of the British legacy of very general and non-technical education is indicative of the criticism that none of these reports worked out in detail an educational plan to provide education according to the needs of the country. Supports the analysis of why reports falter (U5-04).
9. Notes, items 1, 8 and 15: the reports evaluated are the Report of the Commission on National Education (Karachi, 1959), the Report of the Commission on Student Problems and Welfare (Karachi, 1966) and the Proposals for a New Education Policy (Islamabad, July 1969). Supports the identification of the three reports (U5-04).
