## Chapter 1 - What Is an Information System? (verified)

The chapter defines an information system, citing Laudon and Laudon, as "a set of interrelated
components that collect, process, store, and distribute information" to support decision making
and control in an organization. It states: "Information systems can be viewed as having five
major components: hardware, software, data, people, and processes", with hardware, software and
data together forming the technology category, and networking communication noted as a suggested
additional component. On people and process it warns it is easy to fixate on technology and
overlook the humans, and defines process verbatim: "A process is a series of steps undertaken to
achieve a desired outcome or goal." The chapter states that people and processes "separate the
idea of information systems from more technical fields, such as computer science."

Used for: the technology-people-process view of using technology well (U1-01).

## Chapter 11 - Globalization and the Digital Divide (verified)

The chapter defines the digital divide as the separation the internet itself creates "between
those who have access to this global network and those who do not", and states "The digital
divide can occur between countries, regions, or even neighborhoods." It identifies the divide's
dimensions: economic ("some people can afford to have a computer and Internet access while
others cannot"), usability ("technology remains so complicated that many people couldn't use a
computer even if they got one for free", naming those with low literacy and seniors), and
empowerment ("Very few users truly understand the power that digital technologies can give
them"). It lists disadvantaged groups per the ACM Code of Ethics: "low-income households, senior
citizens, single-parent children, the undereducated, minorities, and residents of rural areas."
It notes that solutions "have had mixed success" because "just providing Internet access and/or
computing devices is not enough", and links the divide to inequality: "Lack of computer education
works to the disadvantage of children with lower socioeconomic status."

Used for: the digital divide as a first-order issue, its dimensions and its inequality effects
(U1-07); ICT running across fields and global access (U1-08, U1-03 context).

## Chapter 2 - Hardware (verified)

The chapter defines hardware as "the physical parts of computing devices - those that you can
actually touch", and states that "All personal computers consist of the same basic components"
- a CPU, memory, circuit board, storage, and input/output devices. "The core of a computer is
the Central Processing Unit, or CPU", described as the "brains" of the device. "The motherboard
is the main circuit board on the computer", and "the term bus refers to the electrical
connections between different computer components". On memory: RAM "can transfer data much
faster than the hard disk" and "is 'volatile'" - data is lost when power is off. "A hard disk
is considered non-volatile storage"; "The SSD performs the same function as a hard disk, namely
long-term storage", using flash memory with no moving parts, faster and more durable though
more expensive. Keyboard and mouse "are still the primary input devices"; "The most obvious
output device is a display or monitor", plus speakers and printers. "A digital device processes
electronic signals into discrete values"; "Each one or zero is referred to as a bit"; "A group
of eight bits is known as a byte."

Used for: device families and the memory hierarchy (U2-01..U2-05).

## Chapter 3 - Software (verified)

The chapter defines software as "the set of instructions that tells the hardware what to do",
and states: "Software can be broadly divided into two categories: operating systems and
application software." Operating systems "manage the hardware and create the interface between
the hardware and the user", with three key functions: "managing the hardware resources of the
computer", "providing the user-interface components", and "providing a platform for software
developers to write applications." Linux is "open source, meaning individual developers are
allowed to make modifications to the programming code." Utilities "include programs that allow
you to fix or modify your computer in some way." Application software "performs specific tasks
such as word processing, accounting, database management, video games, or browsing the web"; a
killer application is "one that becomes so essential that large numbers of people will buy a
device just to run that application", and for the PC that was the spreadsheet. Productivity
software programs "allow office employees to complete their daily work efficiently", covering
word processing, spreadsheets and presentations.

Used for: system versus application software and productivity tools (U2-06, U2-07).

## Chapter 5 - Networking and Communication (verified)

The chapter describes the internet as "an interconnected network of networks" and defines a
protocol as "the set of rules that govern how communications take place on a network." "A LAN
is a local network, usually operating in the same building or on the same campus." Wi-Fi
"takes an Internet signal and converts it into radio waves." A packet is "The fundamental unit
of data transmitted over the Internet"; a switch "connects multiple hosts together and
forwards packets based on their destination within the local network"; a router is "A device
that receives and analyzes packets and then routes them towards their destination", and
"Routers are used to connect one network to another network." Each device is "assigned a
unique identifying number called an IP (Internet Protocol) address"; "DNS acts as the directory
of websites on the Internet"; a domain name is "a human-friendly name, convenient for
remembering a website."

Used for: communication hardware and software (U2-08).
