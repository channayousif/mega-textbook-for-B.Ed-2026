## Evidence notes

1. Section 1.1: an entrepreneur is someone "who identifies and acts on an idea or problem" that no one else has identified or acted on; this, not ownership, distinguishes an entrepreneur from a small business owner, who starts a business with an existing model. Some scholars treat the two as overlapping.
2. Section 1.1: an entrepreneurial venture is any new business, organisation, project or operation that carries a level of risk in acting on an opportunity not previously established; it may be for-profit or social.
3. Section 1.1: the difference between risk and calculated risk is due diligence, the research needed to make informed decisions that minimise risk; some founders keep their jobs while developing a venture. Entrepreneurs are described as problem solvers, and entrepreneurial ventures as adding economic development.
4. Section 1.3: the entrepreneurial mindset is "an awareness and focus on identifying an opportunity through solving a problem"; the entrepreneurial spirit is passionate, purposeful, positive, bold, curious and persistent; the mindset includes creativity, problem-solving skills, a propensity to innovation and open-mindedness; "anyone can become an entrepreneur" because anyone can change their perspective; many entrepreneurs mitigate risks before launch.
5. Section 4.2: creativity is the ability to develop something original, especially an idea; innovation is a "change that adds value" to an existing product or service and is the application of creativity; invention is a truly novel product, service or process; creativity, innovation and inventiveness can be developed.
6. Section 4.1: design thinking moves through empathy, defining the problem, ideating, prototyping and testing; statement starters such as "How might we ...?" open up ideas.
7. Section 2.1: the entrepreneurial journey runs from inspiration to launch, and difficulties are opportunities to learn.
8. Section 11.1 & 11.4: A business plan is a formal roadmap describing what a venture intends to do and how it plans to do it. It serves two core audiences: internal founders and managers (guiding decisions, timelines and milestone tracking) and external stakeholders (banks, investors, partners). It is a living document rather than a fixed contract; it must adapt as assumptions are tested. Standard components include the executive summary, company description, products/services, market analysis, marketing and sales plan, operations plan, management team and organization, financial plan, and appendices.
9. Section 11.2 & 11.4: The executive summary is a concise synopsis (1-2 pages) of the entire business plan. It must capture the essence of the venture: the problem, the proposed solution, the target market, competitive advantages, the management team, and financial highlights (funding request, break-even timeline). Although positioned at the very front of the plan, it is always drafted last after all other sections are fully developed.
10. Chapter 7 & Section 11.3: A vision statement articulates the long-term aspirational destination of the venture (what success looks like in 5-10 years). A mission statement expresses the organization's present purpose, answering who the venture is, what it does, whom it serves, and what unique value it provides today. Together, they prevent mission drift and guide operational focus.
11. Chapter 8 & Section 11.4: The marketing plan translates market research into actionable strategies covering target customer segments, positioning, pricing, distribution (place), and promotional tactics (the marketing mix). It defines how the venture will attract and retain buyers.
12. Chapter 11, 12, 13: The operational plan describes the day-to-day facilities, workflows, inventory, equipment, suppliers, and quality assurance processes required to produce and deliver goods or services. The organizational structure defines roles, responsibilities, reporting hierarchies, and governance, showing how tasks are allocated and who is accountable as the business scales from a solo founder to larger teams.

## Evidence notes for Unit 6 (sections read online 2026-11-24)

8. Section 8.1 and 8.2: the entrepreneurial marketing mix encompasses the 4 Ps (Product, Price, Place, Promotion); market positioning is establishing a distinct place in the target customer's mind relative to competitors; a brand represents the emotional and perceptual promise of value made to customers.
9. Section 8.3 and 8.4: promotion consists of communications designed to inform, persuade, and remind target customers; small businesses leverage bootstrap promotion techniques including personal selling, word of mouth, referral incentives, and targeted local notices; digital marketing employs social media, messaging platforms, and content marketing to build customer relationships cost-effectively.
10. Section 3.1: ethics refers to moral standards and principles that distinguish right from wrong in human conduct; ethical issues in entrepreneurship include truth in advertising, fair treatment of employees, consumer safety, and honoring contractual commitments under financial pressure.
11. Section 13.1: legal business structures include sole proprietorships, partnerships, and corporations/limited companies; liability ranges from unlimited personal liability in proprietorships and partnerships to limited liability in incorporated companies.
12. Section 13.2: intellectual property protects intangible creations of the human intellect; distinct categories include trademarks (brand identifiers), copyrights (original literary and artistic expressions), patents (inventions and functional technical processes), and designs; bare ideas carry no intellectual property protection until fixed in a tangible medium or registered.

Use in GENT-401 Unit 6: notes 8-9 support Topic 6.1 (branding, positioning, promotional mix, digital marketing); notes 10-12 support Topic 6.2 (business structures, intellectual property categories, ethics in entrepreneurship).

## Evidence notes for Unit 3 (chapters 5, 8 and 11, read online 2026-11-24)

8. Section 5.2, "Researching Potential Business Opportunities": an idea becomes an opportunity only where significant market demand, significant market structure and size, and significant margins and resources intersect. Primary research gathers new data through surveys and questionnaires, focus groups and in-depth interviews, secret shoppers, direct customer observation and competitor shopping; secondary research uses existing published data and is fast but often lacks specificity. Screening questions cover whether others would value the product, whether the market is definable, whether the timing is right, what the competitive advantage is, and the timeline to first sale.
9. Section 8.2, "Market Research, Market Opportunity Recognition, and Target Market": market research is "the collection and analysis of data related to a business's target market", and its purpose is "to gain an understanding of customer needs and wants". A target market is the specific consumer group a business decides to serve, described through demographics, psychographics and behaviour. Market opportunity recognition identifies "an unmet need within a target demographic", and validation is "the act of verifying that a specific product is needed in a target market". The section sets out six steps for primary research: define the research goal, determine the research design, choose a sample, collect data, analyse data, and results and next steps. The STP framework follows: segmenting a population into homogeneous groups, targeting groups by "their ability and willingness to buy", and positioning a statement of how customers should perceive the offering. On competitors: the more you know about your competition, the easier it is to determine and differentiate your offerings.
10. Section 11.1, "Avoiding the 'Field of Dreams' Approach": the field-of-dreams approach is build first and assume demand later; among 101 failed startups, 42 percent cited no market need. The alternative is customer discovery before launch. A business model is "outlining an approach but changing that approach" when assumptions and educated guesses were wrong. Ash Maurya's three common models are the direct, multisided and marketplace models. A value proposition is "a summary describing the benefits (value) customers can expect"; Clayton Christensen's jobs-to-be-done idea defines a job as what an individual wants to accomplish in a given circumstance, with social, emotional and functional dimensions.
11. Section 11.2, "Designing the Business Model": a business model "describes the rationale of how an organization creates, delivers and captures value" (Osterwalder and Pigneur). At minimum it covers revenue streams, a value proposition and customer segments. The Business Model Canvas has nine blocks: key partners, key activities, key resources, value propositions, customer relationships, channels, customer segments, cost structure and revenue streams. The Value Proposition Canvas sets customer pains and gains against pain relievers, gain creators and products. Both canvases are "designed for constant iterations"; a customer empathy map profiles what a customer sees, says, does, hears and thinks, plus pains and gains.

Use in GENT-401 Unit 3: notes 8-9 support Topic 3.1 (understanding customers and markets, data-collection methods, competitor analysis); notes 10-11 support Topic 3.2 (types of business models, value proposition, revenue streams). The market-research cycle flowchart and the four-method table are the author's teaching devices built on these notes, not the book's own diagrams.

## Evidence notes for Unit 7 (chapters 7, 10, 12, 13, 14 and 15, read online 2026-11-24)

12. Section 10.5: the business lifecycle runs from starting a business through growth, maturity, decline, and death or rebirth. In the startup stage "the owner usually wears many hats", processes are informal, and the major concern is to survive by at least breaking even. In the growth stage sales, profits and staff rise, cash coming in and going out both increase, and the owner must watch costs and customer payments to avoid cash strain; systems and formal processes must be put in place, and the owner must decide whether to continue, sell or merge. Growth brings the stress of "managing cash, delegating tasks to others, and ensuring the right people and systems are in place".
13. Section 10.5: growth strategies include product improvement, market penetration, new distribution channels, product line extension, adding new markets, born-global expansion, and the riskier integrations of licensing, franchising, joint ventures, mergers and acquisitions, and strategic alliances. Adding new markets is "a riskier path to growth". Table 10.6 lists the pros and cons of each strategy.
14. Section 12.2: no one works alone, and the entrepreneur selects the team. A cross-functional team needs complementary skills, compatibility and collaboration. Building a team is a skill: identify the tasks, identify the skills they need, then search for people with those skills, with a backup for each role. Advisors (accountant, attorney, industry expert) inform and recommend; the owner decides.
15. Section 13.7: enterprise risk management is a multistage process of risk identification, risk assessment and risk abatement. Risks come from natural causes, economic causes and human causes. Businesses use a risk matrix to assess the probability and impact of risk and to decide whether to undertake an activity. Risk appetite covers the existing risk profile, risk capacity, risk tolerance and risk attitude. Not all risks can be insured against, for example a bad economy or a wrong market decision.
16. Section 7.3: a pitch is "a formal but brief presentation" made to potential investors; "to pitch is to ask for something". Six key elements are the brand identity and tagline, the problem-solution narrative, key features and value proposition, product-market fit, competitive analysis, and financial projections. A pitch deck is a slide presentation giving a quick overview of the product and the ask; keep a core deck to no more than ten slides and practise a two-minute version. Different audiences (friends and family, competition judges, early investors, employees, trade groups, grant agencies) need different lengths and different asks. Investors want to know about the team, the product, the value proposition and the likely return, and they notice omitted competitors.
17. Section 7.3: an elevator pitch is an abbreviated, memorised talk covering problem-solution, value proposition, product-market fit and team. It should be written down, edited, free of buzzwords, and practised aloud, because it is given in person. Feedback should focus on the presentation rather than the idea.
18. Section 7.4: ideas are shared with many people to be developed, so protecting every idea is less important than protecting tested versions and know-how. Feedback and critique from investors should be treated as valuable experience.
19. Section 14.1: a venture's resources are grouped as human, economic, financial, and physical or technological, and a venture must decide which it owns, which it can access, and which it must acquire.
20. Section 15.2: escalation of commitment describes staying with a plan after the signs say it is not working, and a pivot is the decision to change it. Overconfidence and optimism are the two biases most linked to a founder's failure to recognise the need to change or end a venture.

Use in GENT-401 Unit 7: notes 12-14 support Topic 7.1 (leadership and team building, scaling strategies); note 15 supports Topic 7.1 (managing risks); notes 16-18 support Topic 7.2 (pitching, investor presentations, communication skills); notes 19-20 support Topic 7.2 (the resources a venture describes in a pitch, and the bias against changing course). The seven-part pitch structure and the risk-matrix quadrant reading are the author's teaching devices built on these notes, not the book's own procedures.
