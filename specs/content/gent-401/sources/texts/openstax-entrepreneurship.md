## Evidence notes

1. Section 1.1: an entrepreneur is someone "who identifies and acts on an idea or problem" that no one else has identified or acted on; this, not ownership, distinguishes an entrepreneur from a small business owner, who starts a business with an existing model. Some scholars treat the two as overlapping.
2. Section 1.1: an entrepreneurial venture is any new business, organisation, project or operation that carries a level of risk in acting on an opportunity not previously established; it may be for-profit or social.
3. Section 1.1: the difference between risk and calculated risk is due diligence, the research needed to make informed decisions that minimise risk; some founders keep their jobs while developing a venture. Entrepreneurs are described as problem solvers, and entrepreneurial ventures as adding economic development.
4. Section 1.3: the entrepreneurial mindset is "an awareness and focus on identifying an opportunity through solving a problem"; the entrepreneurial spirit is passionate, purposeful, positive, bold, curious and persistent; the mindset includes creativity, problem-solving skills, a propensity to innovation and open-mindedness; "anyone can become an entrepreneur" because anyone can change their perspective; many entrepreneurs mitigate risks before launch.
5. Section 4.2: creativity is the ability to develop something original, especially an idea; innovation is a "change that adds value" to an existing product or service and is the application of creativity; invention is a truly novel product, service or process; creativity, innovation and inventiveness can be developed.
6. Section 4.1: design thinking moves through empathy, defining the problem, ideating, prototyping and testing; statement starters such as "How might we ...?" open up ideas.
7. Section 2.1: the entrepreneurial journey runs from inspiration to launch, and difficulties are opportunities to learn.

8. Section 10.5: the business lifecycle runs from starting a business through growth, maturity, decline, and death or rebirth. In the startup stage "the owner usually wears many hats", processes are informal, and the major concern is to survive by at least breaking even. In the growth stage sales, profits and staff rise, cash coming in and going out both increase, and the owner must watch costs and customer payments to avoid cash strain; systems and formal processes must be put in place, and the owner must decide whether to continue, sell or merge. Growth brings the stress of "managing cash, delegating tasks to others, and ensuring the right people and systems are in place".
9. Section 10.5: growth strategies include product improvement, market penetration, new distribution channels, product line extension, adding new markets, born-global expansion, and the riskier integrations of licensing, franchising, joint ventures, mergers and acquisitions, and strategic alliances. Adding new markets is "a riskier path to growth". Table 10.6 lists the pros and cons of each strategy.
10. Section 12.2: no one works alone, and the entrepreneur selects the team. A cross-functional team needs complementary skills, compatibility and collaboration. Building a team is a skill: identify the tasks, identify the skills they need, then search for people with those skills, with a backup for each role. Advisors (accountant, attorney, industry expert) inform and recommend; the owner decides.
11. Section 13.7: enterprise risk management is a multistage process of risk identification, risk assessment and risk abatement. Risks come from natural causes, economic causes and human causes. Businesses use a risk matrix to assess the probability and impact of risk and to decide whether to undertake an activity. Risk appetite covers the existing risk profile, risk capacity, risk tolerance and risk attitude. Not all risks can be insured against, for example a bad economy or a wrong market decision.
12. Section 7.3: a pitch is "a formal but brief presentation" made to potential investors; "to pitch is to ask for something". Six key elements are the brand identity and tagline, the problem-solution narrative, key features and value proposition, product-market fit, competitive analysis, and financial projections. A pitch deck is a slide presentation giving a quick overview of the product and the ask; keep a core deck to no more than ten slides and practise a two-minute version. Different audiences (friends and family, competition judges, early investors, employees, trade groups, grant agencies) need different lengths and different asks. Investors want to know about the team, the product, the value proposition and the likely return, and they notice omitted competitors.
13. Section 7.3: an elevator pitch is an abbreviated, memorised talk covering problem-solution, value proposition, product-market fit and team. It should be written down, edited, free of buzzwords, and practised aloud, because it is given in person. Feedback should focus on the presentation rather than the idea.
14. Section 7.4: ideas are shared with many people to be developed, so protecting every idea is less important than protecting tested versions and know-how. Feedback and critique from investors should be treated as valuable experience.
15. Section 14.1: a venture's resources are grouped as human, economic, financial, and physical or technological, and a venture must decide which it owns, which it can access, and which it must acquire.
16. Section 15.2: escalation of commitment describes staying with a plan after the signs say it is not working, and a pivot is the decision to change it. Overconfidence and optimism are the two biases most linked to a founder's failure to recognise the need to change or end a venture.
