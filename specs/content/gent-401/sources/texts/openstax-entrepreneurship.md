## Evidence notes

1. Section 1.1: an entrepreneur is someone "who identifies and acts on an idea or problem" that no one else has identified or acted on; this, not ownership, distinguishes an entrepreneur from a small business owner, who starts a business with an existing model. Some scholars treat the two as overlapping.
2. Section 1.1: an entrepreneurial venture is any new business, organisation, project or operation that carries a level of risk in acting on an opportunity not previously established; it may be for-profit or social.
3. Section 1.1: the difference between risk and calculated risk is due diligence, the research needed to make informed decisions that minimise risk; some founders keep their jobs while developing a venture. Entrepreneurs are described as problem solvers, and entrepreneurial ventures as adding economic development.
4. Section 1.3: the entrepreneurial mindset is "an awareness and focus on identifying an opportunity through solving a problem"; the entrepreneurial spirit is passionate, purposeful, positive, bold, curious and persistent; the mindset includes creativity, problem-solving skills, a propensity to innovation and open-mindedness; "anyone can become an entrepreneur" because anyone can change their perspective; many entrepreneurs mitigate risks before launch.
5. Section 4.2: creativity is the ability to develop something original, especially an idea; innovation is a "change that adds value" to an existing product or service and is the application of creativity; invention is a truly novel product, service or process; creativity, innovation and inventiveness can be developed.
6. Section 4.1: design thinking moves through empathy, defining the problem, ideating, prototyping and testing; statement starters such as "How might we ...?" open up ideas.
7. Section 2.1: the entrepreneurial journey runs from inspiration to launch, and difficulties are opportunities to learn.

Use in GENT-401 Unit 1: notes 1-4 support Topic 1.1 (meaning, economic role, characteristics) and the risk and problem-solving parts of Topic 1.2; notes 5-7 support creativity and innovation in Topic 1.2. The five-step problem-to-innovation flowchart and the three-question test are the author's teaching devices built on these notes, not the book's own procedures.

## Evidence notes for Unit 3 (chapters 5, 8 and 11, read online 2026-11-24)

8. Section 5.2, "Researching Potential Business Opportunities": an idea becomes an opportunity only where significant market demand, significant market structure and size, and significant margins and resources intersect. Primary research gathers new data through surveys and questionnaires, focus groups and in-depth interviews, secret shoppers, direct customer observation and competitor shopping; secondary research uses existing published data and is fast but often lacks specificity. Screening questions cover whether others would value the product, whether the market is definable, whether the timing is right, what the competitive advantage is, and the timeline to first sale.
9. Section 8.2, "Market Research, Market Opportunity Recognition, and Target Market": market research is "the collection and analysis of data related to a business's target market", and its purpose is "to gain an understanding of customer needs and wants". A target market is the specific consumer group a business decides to serve, described through demographics, psychographics and behaviour. Market opportunity recognition identifies "an unmet need within a target demographic", and validation is "the act of verifying that a specific product is needed in a target market". The section sets out six steps for primary research: define the research goal, determine the research design, choose a sample, collect data, analyse data, and results and next steps. The STP framework follows: segmenting a population into homogeneous groups, targeting groups by "their ability and willingness to buy", and positioning a statement of how customers should perceive the offering. On competitors: the more you know about your competition, the easier it is to determine and differentiate your offerings.
10. Section 11.1, "Avoiding the 'Field of Dreams' Approach": the field-of-dreams approach is build first and assume demand later; among 101 failed startups, 42 percent cited no market need. The alternative is customer discovery before launch. A business model is "outlining an approach but changing that approach" when assumptions and educated guesses were wrong. Ash Maurya's three common models are the direct, multisided and marketplace models. A value proposition is "a summary describing the benefits (value) customers can expect"; Clayton Christensen's jobs-to-be-done idea defines a job as what an individual wants to accomplish in a given circumstance, with social, emotional and functional dimensions.
11. Section 11.2, "Designing the Business Model": a business model "describes the rationale of how an organization creates, delivers and captures value" (Osterwalder and Pigneur). At minimum it covers revenue streams, a value proposition and customer segments. The Business Model Canvas has nine blocks: key partners, key activities, key resources, value propositions, customer relationships, channels, customer segments, cost structure and revenue streams. The Value Proposition Canvas sets customer pains and gains against pain relievers, gain creators and products. Both canvases are "designed for constant iterations"; a customer empathy map profiles what a customer sees, says, does, hears and thinks, plus pains and gains.

Use in GENT-401 Unit 3: notes 8-9 support Topic 3.1 (understanding customers and markets, data-collection methods, competitor analysis); notes 10-11 support Topic 3.2 (types of business models, value proposition, revenue streams). The market-research cycle flowchart and the four-method table are the author's teaching devices built on these notes, not the book's own diagrams.

## Evidence notes for GENT-401 Unit 5 (chapter 9)

Consulted 2026-11-24, chapter 9 sections read online at https://openstax.org/books/entrepreneurship/pages/ (9-1-overview-of-entrepreneurial-finance-and-accounting-strategies, 9-2-special-funding-strategies, 9-3-accounting-basics-for-entrepreneurs, 9-4-developing-startup-financial-statements-and-projections).

8. Section 9.1: financing is the process of raising money for an intended purpose, and accounting is the system business owners use to summarise, manage and communicate financial operations. Seed-stage funding comes from personal savings, small investments from friends and family, and angel investors; early-stage funding from angel investors or funds, venture capitalists or private equity; mature growth from self-sustaining income, private equity sale or an initial public offering. An angel investor is a wealthy private individual seeking investment options; a venture capitalist specialises in funding early-stage companies. Debt financing is borrowing funds from another party; equity financing is financing provided in exchange for part ownership. Table 9.1: debt means the lender has no ownership but requires early and regular cash outflow; equity gives the lender an ownership stake but no immediate cash outflow.
9. Section 9.2: bootstrapping is the process of self-funding a company, based on the adage about pulling ourselves up by our bootstraps. Its advantages are no ownership given up, creative low-cost solutions and full founder control; its disadvantages are slow to start and personal sacrifice. Crowdfunding is collecting small sums of money from a large number of people, whose contributors are called backers; models include reward, donation, equity and debt campaigns, and Kickstarter uses an all-or-nothing model. The section warns that many projects deliver rewards late and that abuse of crowdfunding may lead to legal action.
10. Section 9.4: the balance sheet summarises the accounting equation (assets = liabilities + equity); the income statement provides the results of operations (revenue minus expenses equals net income); the statement of cash flows explains the sources and uses of cash, because the income statement records revenue when work is completed, not when cash arrives. Contribution margin is selling price minus variable costs; break-even is the level of operations that results in exactly enough revenue to cover costs, calculated as fixed costs divided by contribution margin (the pizzeria example: Rs-equivalent $2,000 / $8 = 250 pizzas a month). Burn rate is the rate at which cash outflow exceeds cash inflow.

Use in GENT-401 Unit 5: notes 8-10 support the funding sources and bootstrapping in Topic 5.2 and the budgets, break-even formula and three statements in Topic 5.1. The tuition centre numbers are the author's, not the book's.
