## Evidence notes for Unit 3 (paraphrased; short phrases quoted)

1. Chapter 5, p. 34: "the word market is defined as a collection of buyers (or potential buyers) of a particular product or service". The purpose of market research is "to gather information about a product and its demand". Knowing the size and make-up of a market indicates how lucrative a product might be.
2. Chapter 5, pp. 34-35: the objectives of a research project are to determine an accurate estimate of costs and produce a reliable estimate of revenues. Research should identify who will buy, how many will buy, and how long demand will last.
3. Chapter 5, p. 35: market research is a four-stage procedure: define a question or opportunity, decide the tools, collect the information, analyse it. Two types of data fill the second stage: "Primary Data consists of obtaining information firsthand, at its source, for a specific purpose" and "Secondary Data involves information that has already been collected". Primary methods named are questionnaires, personal interviews, focus groups, and mail and telephone surveys; secondary sources include government data, books, magazines and internet searches.
4. Chapter 5, pp. 37-38: questionnaires should be "simple, clear, concise, and purpose-driven", and should avoid biased wording, unfamiliar terms, poorly phrased or personal questions, and too many questions. The bad example "Would you be interested in a new restaurant if it opened in this area?" is biased, because most people say yes and the answer proves nothing; better questions ask how often people eat out and what makes them return.
5. Chapter 5, p. 39: "since markets have a tendency to change rapidly, different research sources and methods should be used to confirm results - a process called triangulation". Combine at least one primary and one secondary approach before establishing a conclusion. The point behind research is to reduce risk.
6. Chapter 6, pp. 40-41: external research steps include defining what you have and what you wish to do, evaluating the idea and the industry, knowing your customers (who will use the product, where they are, why and how they will use it, what benefits they derive, whether they can afford it), and researching the competition (whether a business already provides a similar product, and whether another can produce it cheaper or better).
7. Chapter 6, pp. 43-44: a SWOT analysis sets strengths and weaknesses (internal) against opportunities and threats (external) in an easy-to-read chart, so the entrepreneur can see what to address.
8. Chapter 9, p. 53: "a business model refers to a workable business strategy or the way an organization does business", based on a sales technique, a pricing strategy, the wants and needs of a target market, or any method that capitalises on what the business does best and what customers want most. Choosing a model is "mostly through research followed by trial and error".
9. Chapter 9, pp. 53-57: the model choices as pairs of opposites. Sales volume versus profit margins (Trujillo: "Percentages aren't put into a bank ... only money is put into a bank", p. 54); high prices versus low prices; market share versus profitability (Pick 'n Pay's chairman put profitability over market share, p. 55); less choice versus more choice (a travel agency cut twelve international tours to three and they filled up); competition versus cooperation (cooperative networking, p. 55); selling versus sales and service (Dow Chemical sells the benefit of its chemicals rather than the chemicals, pp. 56-57).
10. Chapter 9, p. 58: "Re-working a business plan is not a sign of weakness if the current plan is proving futile", and businesses that try to be all things to all people tend to be "confused, unfocused". Persistence versus stubbornness: know what and why you are changing before taking a different course.
11. Chapter 27, p. 144: "Price is the only component of the marketing mix ... that produces revenue. All others represent costs." Pricing can be changed quickly, and a price that is too high or too low can both turn buyers away.

## Evidence notes for Unit 4

1. Business Planning (pp. 53-56): A business plan is a practical working tool rather than an academic dissertation. Scott cautions entrepreneurs against unnecessary complexity, overly dense prose, or unrealistic projections. The plan must provide reliable analysis and clear explanations of the venture's market opportunity, resources, and operating model.
2. Executive Summary (pp. 57-60): The executive summary serves as an "at-a-glance" gateway to the business plan. Because potential investors, lenders, and partners read it first to decide whether to read further, it must be concise (one to two pages) and written strictly after the operational, market, and financial sections are completed and verified. It must summarize the business concept, market size, management capabilities, and financial needs.
3. Marketing Strategies (pp. 61-68): Marketing within the plan must focus on defined target customers, clear pricing justifications, practical distribution methods, and effective communication channels, avoiding generic assertions of market dominance.
4. Operational Workflows and Controls (pp. 69-74): Operations planning requires mapping the step-by-step path from raw material or inventory procurement through assembly, packaging, and final delivery to the buyer. Documented workflows and quality checks prevent bottlenecks, minimize waste, and ensure dependable customer service.
5. Organization and Management (pp. 75-76): Scott highlights that unassigned responsibilities lead directly to business errors. A clearly delineated organizational structure assigns distinct roles, reporting lines, and accountability, ensuring that critical administrative, operational, and customer-facing functions are not overlooked as the venture expands.

Use in GENT-401 Unit 4: notes 1-2 support Topic 4.1 (business plan components, executive summary); notes 3-5 support Topic 4.2 (marketing plan, operational plan, organizational structure).

## Evidence notes for GENT-401 Unit 5 (paraphrased; short phrases quoted)

1. Chapter 1, "Lack of Money": many practitioners believe that a better-than-average amount of start-up capital has little to do with success, and that starting with little money teaches frugality and efficiency. The chapter lists businesses that began with very small sums.
2. Chapter 12, "Sources of Finance": raising money is meant to be difficult, and the trick is getting it from the right sources in the right sequence as the business grows. Banks are cautious, seek proven cash revenues or collateral, and want firm facts and figures. Family and friends can help if the founder is trustworthy and has a viable plan, but the request must be planned with care. Trade credit lets a supplier wait up to 30 days for payment. Grants provide money that does not have to be paid back, usually in small sums, with forms to fill in. Soft loans do not require security or collateral, have a long payback schedule and extendable due dates, but every penny is repaid with interest. Venture capitalists finance businesses and, as part owners, expect to share in the success while expecting yearly growth rates of up to 40 percent or higher; many are not interested in small businesses and look for high-flying ideas with big profit expectations that pay back quickly. Crowdfunding obtains funds by publicly asking for small donations and can be as fickle as a lottery without an established network.
3. Chapter 12, "Advice from the Pros": after working out how much finance the business needs, double the figure; little expenses add up and can break a business; get to know a banker before needing money; and lending institutions are more receptive to entrepreneurs who hold production contracts (orders for a product).
4. Chapter 13, "The Importance of Numbers": paying attention to how money enters and exits a business keeps it solvent in five ways - it measures success, points out weaknesses, indicates areas that need improvement, helps anticipate a crisis, and shows that profits in good months are enough to cover unprofitable slow months. The chapter notes that too many small business owners avoid accurate bookkeeping.
5. Chapter 13, "Break-Even Point": a business breaks even when the money collected from sales equals the money spent on costs. Fixed costs remain constant no matter how much is produced (rent, monthly salaries, interest, depreciation); variable costs increase when production increases (materials, hourly labour, utilities, transport, commissions). The break-even point in units is total fixed expenses divided by the selling price per unit minus the variable costs per unit (the flowerpot example: $750 / ($5 - $2) = 250 units).
6. Chapter 13, "Balance Sheets", "Monthly Net Profit" and "Profits": a balance sheet shows assets and liabilities in two columns that must balance. Monthly net profit is total sales for one month minus labour, material and equipment costs, taxes and other expenditures. Gross profit is the total of all sales before expenses are subtracted; operating profit deducts the expenses directly related to producing a product; net profit is the amount left after all expenses.

## Evidence notes for Unit 6 (chapters 4 and 7, read online 2026-11-24)

1. Chapter 4 (Marketing and Business Development): marketing starts with deep understanding of customer segments and their unmet needs; competitive advantage requires clear positioning that separates a venture from existing market choices.
2. Chapter 4: branding represents the core promise and ongoing reputation of the enterprise; small businesses build brand equity through consistent quality, transparent communication, and customer referrals rather than expensive commercial advertising.
3. Chapter 4: bootstrap promotion techniques for startups include personal demonstrations, direct relationships with community leaders, referral programs, and targeted local notices.
4. Chapter 7 (Legal, Governance and Regulatory Frameworks): formal business registration provides credibility with institutional clients and limits personal exposure; contracts, intellectual property protection, and regulatory compliance are essential for sustainable growth.
5. Chapter 7: ethical business practices, truth in advertising, and honoring commercial agreements build long-term enterprise value and customer loyalty.
