## Evidence notes (original paraphrase)

Yazar and Batur compare the Qur'an with the Muʿallaqāt, the best-known collection of pre-Islamic odes. They find a substantial overlap in vocabulary, rhetorical frames and images of ruins, animals and nature, which reflects the shared language of the period. They argue that the two differ in purpose and method: pre-Islamic poetry mostly describes the world as it is, grounded in desert life, tribal values or personal feeling, while the Qur'an places the same images inside a wider moral and theological framework, as signs that call for reflection.

Their worked contrast on ruins supports the unit's table in topic 1.1: abandoned campsites (aṭlāl) in the odes evoke longing and the passing of time, while ruined towns in the Qur'an are traces meant to prompt reflection, moral warning and historical awareness. They also show inherited words reinterpreted in the Qur'an. The unit uses the article for the relation "shared language, new purpose" (U1-02, U1-03), not for any claim about specific religious terms such as ṣalāh, which rests on general knowledge.
