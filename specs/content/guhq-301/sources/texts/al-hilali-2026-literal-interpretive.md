## Evidence notes (original paraphrase)

The author compares English translations of the Qur'an and places them on a spectrum between two approaches. A literal approach renders each Arabic word by a corresponding English word and keeps the Arabic word order, even when the English becomes awkward. An interpretive approach rephrases the source text into idiomatic English and may expand implicit meanings. Between the two lie intermediate positions: a conservative literal translation, a moderate literal translation with commentary, an interpretive translation with explanatory notes, and a functional-equivalence translation that prioritises natural English.

Using Eugene Nida's terms, formal equivalence reproduces the form and content of the source as closely as possible, while dynamic equivalence aims at a natural equivalent that produces the same response in the target reader. The author concludes that it is improbable to find a fully literal or fully interpretive translation; every translation sits somewhere on the spectrum. A literal approach normally preserves lexical fidelity to the original, while an interpretive approach is more successful at rendering the spiritual effect and functional meaning for the English reader.

The unit uses the article for the literal-to-interpretive spectrum (U4-02, U4-04) and for the point that no single translation gives the whole meaning. It does not use the article for any claim about a specific translator's accuracy, and it does not quote any translation from the article.
