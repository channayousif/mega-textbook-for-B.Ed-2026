# Bound excerpt - purvis-2019-pillars

Purvis, B., Mao, Y., & Robinson, D. (2019). Three pillars of sustainability: in search of conceptual origins. *Sustainability Science, 14*(3), 681-695. CC BY 4.0. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11625-018-0627-5

Full text read from the publisher page on 2026-11-07.

## Passages used by Unit 1

"The 1972 UN Conference on the Human-Environment in Stockholm marked the first global summit to consider human impacts on the environment, and the first major attempt to reconcile economic development with environmental integrity which were commonly regarded as incompatible" (U1-02)

"The first prominent occurrence of the phrase 'sustainable development' in published literature appeared in 1980" (U1-01). The same passage quotes the World Conservation Strategy: "must take account of social and ecological factors, as well as economic ones". The successor, Caring for the Earth (1991), calls for development that is "both people-centered ... and conservation-based" (U1-01, fig-U1-2).

"the Brundtland commission is widely credited with popularising the concept of 'sustainable development' by introducing it into international policy discourse" (U1-01, U1-03)

"the 'Rio Process', initiated at the 1992 Earth Summit in Rio, where the world's political leaders pledged their support to the principle of sustainable development" (U1-01, U1-04)

"we argue that 'sustainable development' arose here from a twin critique of the previously popular notion of 'economic development', from both a 'quality of life' or social perspective, and an ecological perspective" (U1-01)

"there is no single point of origin of this three-pillar conception" and "Nowhere have we found a theoretically rigorous description of the three pillars." (U1-05)

Barbier articulates development as "an interaction among three systems: the biological (and other resource) system, the economic system, and the social system", the objective being to "maximise the goals across all these systems through an adaptive process of trade-offs" (U1-05)

"Some works consider additional pillars such as institutional ... cultural ... and technical" (U1-05). Figure 1 of the article shows the alternatives: literal pillars and concentric circles.
