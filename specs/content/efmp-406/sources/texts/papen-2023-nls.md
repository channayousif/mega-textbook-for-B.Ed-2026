## Section 2, "The dominant discourse: literacy as skills", pp. 69-70 (used by Unit 1, U1-01, U1-02)

"Literacy is generally assumed to be a set of generic cognitive skills."

"The 'skills view' (Papen 2005), which Street (2003) called 'the autonomous model of literacy', is pervasive, shaping educational policy and public opinion"

"This view turns literacy into a 'simplified construct' (Prinsloo and Krause 2019, p. 158) that serves as the basis for developing and implementing approaches to teaching and assessing literacy that can be scaled up, for example, across all the schools in a country."

"Another effect of the autonomous model is that it imposes 'Western conceptions of literacy on to other cultures' (Street 2003, p. 2). As literacy in this view is seen as a 'property' of the individual (Prinsloo and Krause 2019, p. 159), it becomes connected to personhood ... and linked to class, race and ethnicity-related prejudices and negative stereotypes."

## Section 3, "A different view: the no longer 'New' Literacy Studies", pp. 70-71 (used by Unit 1, U1-01, U1-02)

"In the 1980s, a new orientation within research on literacy emerged. Called New Literacy Studies (NLS), it offered an alternative view of literacy that challenged the orthodox idea (then and now) of literacy as a set of generic cognitive abilities."

"the NLS put forward an understanding of literacy as social and cultural practice: embedded in, shaped by and shaping values, beliefs and power relations."

"Literacy practices are understood here to mean shared cultural ways of using and valuing literacy in different contexts. Driving this view, which Street (2003) aptly termed an 'ideological model' of literacy, is a strong commitment to inclusion and social justice"

## Section 4, "Multiliteracies and multimodality", p. 71 (used by Unit 1, U1-02, U1-03)

"The term multiliteracies was introduced in the 1990s to capture the changing nature of reading and writing practices in contemporary societies as changing economic and political circumstances, migration and the spread of digital technologies require us to rethink the meaning of literacy."

"However, there is also a risk of new illiteracies emerging as the perceived threshold of literacy continues to evolve."

"The 'multi' dimension of multiliteracies encompasses two elements: the use and recognition of multiple languages ... and the central role of multiple modes (including visual and gestural) in communication and learning (Cope and Kalantzis 2009)."

## Section 5, "Real literacies and home-school literacy links", pp. 71-72 (used by Unit 1, U1-02, U1-04)

"Children from mainstream middle-class backgrounds tend to grow up surrounded by literacy practices such as bedtime stories that match some of the practices and expectations common in schools, giving them an advantage over children from other backgrounds."

"The real literacies approach (Street 2012; Street et al. 2006) was developed in the context of adult literacy teaching in the Global South."

"It works on the assumption that for literacy curricula to be meaningful they must be closely linked to the learners' own everyday uses of literacy. Literacy teaching should focus on 'real' or authentic texts from the learners' environment: for example, signboards, bureaucratic forms, religious texts and other documents."

## Literacy as social practice and the critique of the autonomous model (used by U3-01)

From pages 68-71:
"The New Literacy Studies (NLS) emerged in the 1980s as a critical alternative to traditional, cognitive conceptualizations of literacy. Traditional views, which Brian Street termed the 'autonomous model', treat literacy as an independent, neutral cognitive technology - a set of universal decoding and encoding skills that exist separately from social context. In contrast, NLS proposes an 'ideological model', arguing that literacy is always socially situated, culturally shaped, and inextricably bound to power relations. Rather than viewing literacy as a single, static ability that a person either possesses or lacks, NLS approaches literacy as multiple, dynamic 'literacy practices' that vary across different cultural, institutional, and historical contexts."

## Literacy events and literacy practices (used by U3-01)

From pages 71-73:
"Central to the social practice view is the conceptual distinction formulated by David Barton and Mary Hamilton between 'literacy events' and 'literacy practices'. A literacy event is any observable occasion in which a written text is central to the nature of the participants' interactions and their interpretive processes. In contrast, literacy practices are the broader cultural models, values, attitudes, and social relations that give meaning to those events. Practices are not directly observable; they encompass folk models of what reading and writing are for, who has the authority to interpret a text, and how literacy connects to social status, religious duty, economic survival, or institutional compliance."

## Dominant versus vernacular literacies, identity, and power (used by U3-02)

From pages 74-77:
"In any society, not all literacies carry equal weight or prestige. NLS distinguishes between 'dominant literacies' - those codified, regulated, and demanded by formal institutions such as schools, courts, banks, and state bureaucracies - and 'vernacular literacies', which originate in everyday community life, home domains, informal work, and grassroots networks. Dominant literacies are associated with social power, formal credentials, and economic mobility. When educational institutions fail to recognize learners' vernacular literacies and home language varieties, they communicate that the pupils' linguistic identities and cultural funds of knowledge are deficient. Effective and inclusive pedagogy requires understanding that literacy practices shape learner identity and that schooling must build bridges between community practices and formal academic expectations."
