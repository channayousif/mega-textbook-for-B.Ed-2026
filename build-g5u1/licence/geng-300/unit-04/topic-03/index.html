Topic 4.3 - Adapting communication for your context

A real classroom situation​

Miss Laila teaches Class 8 in a government school in Jacobabad. She writes a note to parents about an upcoming exam. Her first version reads: "Examinations shall commence on 10 October. Pupils are expected to be seated by 8.30 a.m. Non-compliance will result in denial of entry." The parents, many of whom have limited formal schooling, do not respond. Her mentor suggests rewriting it in plainer, warmer language. This topic is about that rewriting: how to take a message and reshape it for a different reader without losing its meaning.

Explanation​

Topics 4.1 and 4.2 gave you two sets of tools: the professional writing formats, and the cultural knowledge to understand how communication varies across contexts. This topic puts them together. Adapting communication is the skill of taking a message and reshaping its register, tone and content so it works for a new audience or a new purpose.

Register is the level of formality a situation demands. Tone is the writer's attitude toward the subject and the audience. A single fact ("The exam is on 10 October") can be expressed in many registers and tones, and the choice depends entirely on who will read it and what relationship the writer wants with them.

Analysing the audience and purpose​

The figure above maps the adaptation process. The first step is always to analyse the two situations:

  • Original audience and purpose: Who was this written for, and what should they think, feel or do after reading it?
  • New audience and purpose: Who will read it now, and what do they need?

For each audience, ask four questions:

  1. What do they already know? (A district education officer knows what an exam timetable is; a parent with limited schooling may not.)
  2. What is their relationship to the writer? (Familiar or formal? Equal or hierarchical?)
  3. What is their likely communication style? (Higher-context or lower-context? More direct or more face-saving?)
  4. What do they need from this message? (The facts only, or also reassurance, motivation or an explanation of why it matters?)

Miss Laila's first version assumed a formal, low-context reader who would respond to authority ("shall", "non-compliance", "denial of entry"). Her actual audience was parents who needed a clear, warm, practical message. The mismatch is a register and tone problem, not a grammar problem.

Shifting register and tone​

The figure above compares three common register and tone combinations you will move between as a teacher. The same core message changes shape in each:

  • Formal-official (to a district officer): "I am writing to inform you that the Class 8 examinations will commence on 10 October. Pupils are required to be seated by 8.30 a.m."
  • Formal-professional (to a colleague): "Just to let you know, the Class 8 exams start on 10 October. Pupils need to be seated by 8.30."
  • Informal-warm (to parents with limited schooling): "Dear parents, please note that the Class 8 exam will begin on 10 October. Kindly make sure your child reaches school by 8.30 a.m. so they can sit the exam on time. Thank you for your support."

Notice what changes and what stays the same. The facts (the date, the time) stay the same in all three. What changes is the level of formality (shall/kindly/please), the distance between writer and reader (I am writing to inform you / just to let you know / dear parents), and the attitude (neutral-authoritative / collegial / warm-inviting).

Worked example before you adapt one. Miss Laila's first parent note ("Examinations shall commence… Non-compliance will result in denial of entry") is rewritten for her actual audience as: "Dear parents, the Class 8 exam will begin on 10 October. Please send your child to school by 8.30 a.m. on that day. If a child arrives late, they may not be allowed to sit the exam. Your support helps your child do their best. Thank you." The facts are identical. The register is plainer, the tone is warmer, and the reason (your child's success) is made explicit for an audience that needs it.

Revising content, not just words​

Adapting communication is sometimes more than swapping formal words for plain ones. It can also mean adding, removing or reordering information:

  • Add what the new reader needs: a reason, a definition, a reassurance.
  • Remove what the new reader does not need: jargon, official phrasing, assumptions.
  • Reorder so the most important information comes first. A busy parent needs the date and time before the background.

Hall (1976) and Brown and Levinson (1987) both remind us that what counts as "enough" information varies by context. A low-context reader wants the point first and the reason after. A higher-context reader may expect the relationship and context to be established before the point. Adapting a message means judging which pattern your new reader expects.

Common misconception. "One communication style works everywhere." It does not. A message that sounds respectful and professional to a district officer can sound cold and distant to a parent, and a message that sounds warm and plain to a parent can sound unprofessional to a supervisor. The skill is not to pick one "correct" style and use it always; the skill is to read the situation and adapt.

Activity: Adapt a message for three audiences​

Work in pairs. Take the following message, written by a headteacher to a colleague, and rewrite it for the two other audiences described. For each rewrite, explain in two or three sentences what you changed and why. Allow 25 minutes. Then compare your rewrites with another pair.

Original (to a colleague): "The mid-term exam schedule has been moved forward by two days because of the sudden holiday. Papers need to be ready by Friday. Can you handle the Class 7 English paper?"

Audiences to adapt for:

  1. A formal letter to the district education officer explaining the change.
  2. A note to parents informing them of the new exam dates.

Check your understanding​

  1. Explain the difference between register and tone, and give one example of each. (Remember)
  2. A teacher writes to a parent: "Your child's academic performance is unsatisfactory and requires immediate remedial intervention." Rewrite this sentence for a parent with limited schooling and explain the changes you made. (Apply)
  3. A colleague says, "You should always write professionally, no matter who the reader is." Using this topic's account, explain what that advice overlooks. (Understand)
  4. Analyse the two versions of Miss Laila's parent note (the original and the rewrite in the worked example). Identify two specific changes and explain how each one serves the new audience. (Analyze)

Summary​

Adapting communication means analysing the audience and purpose, then reshaping the register, tone and content of a message so it works for a new reader. The facts usually stay the same; what changes is the level of formality, the distance between writer and reader, the attitude, and sometimes the amount and order of information. The frameworks from Topic 4.2 (Hall's high-/low-context spectrum and Brown and Levinson's face framework) help you judge what a given audience expects and needs. One style does not work everywhere; the skill is to read the situation and adapt.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can analyse a described audience using the four questions this topic sets out.
  • I can rewrite a message for a different register and tone without changing its facts.
  • I can explain why a given message does or does not fit its intended reader.
  • I can add, remove and reorder information to suit a new audience.
  • I can reject the claim that one communication style is right for every situation.

Try this at your practicum school​

Find one written communication the school sends to parents or the community (a notice, a letter or a message). Analyse it using this topic's four audience questions. Then rewrite it for a specific parent audience you know (for example, parents with limited formal schooling, or parents who work long hours and read messages quickly). Write half a page on what you changed and why.

Do not name the school, the staff or the families in your write-up.

Summative task​

Write 400 to 450 words on the following.

A teacher writes the following message to a group of parents, many of whom are not comfortable reading English: "Guardians are hereby notified that the annual parent-teacher meeting is scheduled for 5 November at 10:00 hours. Attendance is compulsory. Those failing to attend will be sent a show-cause notice." Analyse why this message may fail for the intended audience, rewrite it for that audience, and explain how your rewrite applies at least two principles from this topic.

Further reading​

  • Hall, E. T. (1976). Beyond Culture. Anchor Press. Chapter 7 discusses how the same message is read differently in high- and low-context settings.
  • Brown, P., & Levinson, S. C. (1987). Politeness: Some Universals in Language Usage. Cambridge University Press. The discussion of positive and negative face (Chapter 1) is the basis for choosing face-saving strategies for a given reader.
