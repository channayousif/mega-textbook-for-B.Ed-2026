Unit 1 teacher notes

Sequencing across the four-week, twelve-hour block​

The unit is built to be taught in order, and the dependency is real: Topic 1.2's definition of classroom management draws on the three theories from Topic 1.1; Topic 1.3's observation protocol assumes that definition; and Topic 1.4's design work applies everything that came before.

A workable division for the twelve hours is three hours per topic, with the six hours of classroom observation the guide asks for in Week 2 folded into Topic 1.3 as a practicum task. If the block is compressed, compress Topic 1.4 rather than Topic 1.1. The three theories are the foundation everything else rests on, and a trainee who has not grasped them will treat the rest of the course as a set of tips rather than a system.

Topic 1.1 is the load-bearing topic of the unit. If only one topic is taught well, it should be that one, because the management/discipline distinction in Topic 1.2 and the observation protocol in Topic 1.3 both assume the trainee can move between the three theoretical lenses.

The misconceptions that recur, and how to handle them​

Four come up almost every cohort. Each is named inside the topic that corrects it, but they are worth anticipating.

"Behaviourism was disproved and constructivism replaced it." This is the most persistent one, and it matters because a trainee who believes it will skip the hard work of building routines. The strongest counter is not an argument but the Mr Karim vignette in Topic 1.1: the same teacher, the same content, three different lessons, each drawing on a different theory, none of them wrong. Let the class reach the conclusion that the three describe different parts of one process rather than asserting it.

"Classroom management means keeping children quiet." Trainees who have been schooled in tightly-controlled classrooms hold this firmly. The two-column observation activity in Topic 1.3 is designed for this moment: when a trainee records a silent copying lesson and then asks "what was the intellectual demand?", the gap between order and learning becomes visible.

"Discipline and management are the same thing." This one resurfaces whenever a trainee describes a well-run classroom as one where "the teacher is strict". The sorting activity in Topic 1.2 is the place to correct it. The failure mode is that the group settles each item by majority and moves on; the value is entirely in the disagreement, so intervene if that happens.

"I cannot design my classroom because the benches are fixed and I have no budget." Topic 1.4 is built to answer this. Have the class complete the audit table for a real local classroom they know. The list of things they can change this week is almost always longer than they expected, and the exercise does more to build a design mindset than any amount of theory.

Running the activities safely​

Two of the activities ask trainees to work with real classrooms. Two cautions.

The observation task assumes access to a live classroom, and not every trainee has one. The topic provides a described classroom as an alternative, but a trainee who can observe a real lesson will learn more. Coordinate with the practicum placement early in the block, and have a recorded lesson ready as a second alternative. A trainee watching a recording should still use the two-column protocol; the discipline of separating observation from inference matters more than the source of the evidence.

The audit task in Topic 1.4 can surface frustration. Trainees who teach in the most under-resourced schools may find the gap between the design ideals in the text and the reality of their placement demoralising rather than empowering. Name this explicitly: the point of the audit is not to complain about what cannot change, but to find the one thing that can, and to do it this week.

The activity that most often goes wrong​

The Topic 1.1 "one lesson, three ways" activity produces a common failure: the group produces three lesson versions that are identical in practice but labelled with different theory names. This defeats the purpose. The three versions should differ in what the teacher does first, what the pupils do, and what success looks like. If a group's versions are the same, ask them to return to the vignette and identify one thing Mr Karim did on Monday that he did not do on Friday, and one thing he did on Friday that he did not do on Monday. That usually unlocks the distinction.

Practical work​

Observation task (Week 2). The Topic 1.3 practicum task is the unit's most reliable piece of evidence that a trainee can read a classroom. Collect the two-column records and, if the cohort is willing, read a selection of left-column evidence aloud without naming the school or the teacher. Trainees often discount the protocol when they do it; they accept it when they hear three peers describe the same feature of the same room differently.

Theory journal (Weeks 1 to 4). Ask trainees to keep a brief weekly note: one classroom moment they observed or experienced that week, the theory it is best read through, and why. Collect it once, at the end of Week 4. The purpose is to make the habit of reading classroom moments through a theoretical lens, not to test the journal's content.

Resource audit (Week 4). The Topic 1.4 audit is the transfer task that matters most for the rest of the course, because Units 2 to 4 all assume a trainee who can look at a classroom and ask "what is working here, and what is working against the learning?" Set it early enough in the week that trainees can carry it out in their practicum school before the block ends.

Marking the unit assessment​

The bank is on the unit assessment page with model answers and analytic rubrics. Two notes.

The RRQ mark schemes are point-by-point and total more than a typical examination would carry; select from them rather than setting all ten.

For the ERQs, the rubric caps a response that does not reach "Adequate" on the analysis criterion at 10 out of 20, whatever its other merits. This is deliberate. A fluent, well organised answer that never analyses anything is the most common strong-looking weak response at this level, and the cap is what stops it scoring like a good one.
