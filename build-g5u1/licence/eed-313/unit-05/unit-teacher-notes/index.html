Unit 5 teacher notes

Sequencing note​

Unit 5 is the lightest unit in the course by design. The work here is synthesis and application of material already covered in Units 1 to 4, not new content. A useful session order is to teach the topics in numerical order across the single week the guide allows (Week 16), using the first session for the synthesis (Topic 5.1), the second for the peer-critique protocol (Topics 5.2 and 5.3), and the third for the closing synthesis (Topic 5.4) and the presentation of revised plans.

Managing the activities​

Topic 5.1 - Build your philosophy (15 minutes, individual). This is a quiet, individual activity. The key teaching move is the pair share: ask trainees to identify one place where their philosophy draws on a specific idea from one of the four units. If trainees cannot name the connection, their philosophy is still at the level of opinion; push them to cite the evidence.

Topic 5.2 - Peer critique in pairs (20 minutes). The trap is that trainees give vague praise or vague criticism. The protocol is the tool that prevents this: remind trainees to name a specific criterion, cite a specific passage, and suggest a specific revision. Circulate and listen for feedback that is specific; if you hear "I like it," ask, "Which criterion does that refer to?"

Topic 5.3 - Present and revise (30 minutes, groups of three). This is the most important activity in the unit. The key teaching move is the revision step: ask trainees to name the feedback they are responding to, show the change, and explain the reasoning. If trainees revise without naming the feedback, they are editing, not responding to critique.

Topic 5.4 - Examine your beliefs (10 minutes, individual). This is a closing reflection. The key teaching move is the pair share: ask trainees to identify one belief that changed for both of them. The common changes are usually about the management/discipline distinction, the purpose of routines, and the meaning of care.

The culminating assessment​

The summative task for Unit 5 is the classroom management plan, the course's culminating artefact. The guide's sample assignment asks for a plan that covers all four layers: philosophy and theory, curriculum decisions, routines, and care and accountability.

What to look for in a strong plan:

  • The philosophy is stated in one sentence and connected to at least two of the three theories.
  • The curriculum decision is explained as a management act, not just a content choice.
  • The routine set is designed for a named constraint, not generically.
  • The care section describes consequences that are restorative, not punitive.
  • Every choice is justified by reference to the described pupils or setting.

What to look for in a weak plan:

  • The philosophy is stated but not connected to a theory.
  • The four layers are listed but not integrated.
  • The routine set is generic ("I will use routines to manage the classroom").
  • The care section describes punishment without naming it as such.
  • The justifications are generic ("this is good practice").

A note on the figures​

The unit carries four figures: one concept-map figure (fig-U5-1), one flowchart figure (fig-U5-3), and two tables (fig-U5-2, fig-U5-4). The tables are the ones trainees most often try to read as prose. Before you project any of them, ask trainees to read the column headers first and say what the table is comparing.
