Topic 1.1 - What makes teaching a profession

A real classroom situation​

A parent in a village in interior Sindh comes to the government primary school to enrol a child. She asks the new teacher a fair question: "You studied for two years to do this. The shopkeeper next door started selling on the day he opened. Why does teaching need all that training?"

It is a good question, and the honest answer is not "because the rules say so". The answer is that teaching is a profession, and a profession is a particular kind of work with particular demands. This topic sets out what that word means and why it applies to teaching.

Explanation​

What a profession is​

Not every job is a profession. In everyday speech people sometimes call any respectable, salaried job "a profession", but the word has a narrower meaning. A profession is an occupation built on a body of specialised knowledge, entered only after an extended period of training, governed by a shared code of conduct, and held accountable to a wider community of practitioners - not only to an employer.

A professional is a member of such an occupation who is expected to use judgement, keep learning, and act in the interest of the people they serve even when no one is watching. Carr (2000) argues that this last part - acting for the good of others as a matter of principle, not just following instructions - is central to what "professional" means in teaching.

Common misconception - "a profession just means a respectable, well-paid job." Pay and status are not the test. Some well-paid jobs are not professions, and some modestly paid ones are. The test is the four features below.

Features that distinguish a profession from an occupation​

Four features are usually used to tell a profession apart from an ordinary occupation:

  1. Specialised knowledge - a body of knowledge specific to the work that outsiders do not have.
  2. Extended training and a qualification - you cannot enter simply by being hired; you must complete a recognised course of study and be certified.
  3. A code of conduct - shared standards of ethical behaviour that members agree to follow.
  4. Accountability to a professional community - a body of peers, an association, or a regulator that can judge your practice, not only your employer.

Worked example. Take the parent's three jobs and check each against the four features. The shopkeeper needs no qualification to start, follows no shared written code, and answers only to the owner - no feature is clearly met, so this is an occupation. The doctor studied for years, belongs to a council that can suspend a licence, follows a medical code of ethics, and uses trained judgement about each patient - all four features are met. The teacher is in the same position as the doctor: subject and pedagogical study, a certified B.Ed route, codes of ethical conduct, and answerability to professional standards bodies as well as to a head teacher. Teaching meets all four features.

Some jobs sit in between and are worth arguing about - a pharmacist's assistant, a paramedic, a draughtsman. When you are unsure, name the feature that is only partly met; that is usually where the disagreement is.

Professionalism in teaching versus professionalisation of teaching​

Two similar-sounding ideas are easy to confuse.

  • Professionalism in teaching is about how one teacher conducts themselves day to day - their skill, their ethics, their judgement, their willingness to keep improving.
  • Professionalisation of teaching is a slower, collective process: the way teaching as a field has built its own training routes, licensing, standards, and associations, in the way that medicine and law did earlier.

The two are linked but separate. A teacher can show strong personal professionalism even in a system where professionalisation is still developing. Demirkasımoğlu (2010), reviewing how different writers define teacher professionalism, notes that some accounts stress the individual teacher's expertise and autonomy while others stress the collective structures - standards, bodies, accountability. Both senses are in normal use; keep them apart when you write and talk about the profession. Pakistan's move toward the National Professional Standards for Teachers (Unit 4) is part of the professionalisation process.

Comparative perspective: teaching and other professions​

Set against medicine, law, or engineering, teaching is sometimes called a "semi-profession": its training route was historically shorter, its practitioners have had less collective control over entry to the field, and much of the work is done in public institutions under close administrative supervision. Demirkasımoğlu (2010) shows there is no single agreed definition of teacher professionalism - some writers emphasise autonomy and expertise, others accountability and service.

The comparison is not a ranking. It is useful because it points to the parts of professional status that teaching is still building: a longer and more rigorous training route, stronger professional bodies, and more room for teachers to exercise informed judgement. Treat "semi-profession" as a description of work in progress, not an insult.

Activity: Profession or occupation?​

Work in pairs. About 20 minutes.

  1. Pick three jobs you know well - for example a government-school teacher, a shopkeeper, and a doctor at a Rural Health Centre.
  2. Draw a table with the three jobs as columns and the four features of a profession as rows.
  3. In each cell write yes, no, or partly, with one reason.
  4. Decide: which job is clearly a profession? Which is clearly not? Which one do you and your partner disagree about - and what evidence would settle it?
  5. Join another pair, compare tables, and agree on one job that is genuinely hard to classify and the feature that makes it hard. Bring that to the whole class.

Check your understanding​

  1. Define a profession in one sentence, and name its four distinguishing features. (Remember)
  2. Give one example of a well-paid job that is not a profession, and one modestly paid job that is. Explain each choice using the four features. (Understand)
  3. A colleague says, "Teaching became a profession the day the government issued standards for it." Use the distinction between professionalism and professionalisation to explain what is right and what is wrong in that statement. (Apply)
  4. Teaching is sometimes called a "semi-profession". Name two things teaching is still building toward fuller professional status, and say why each one matters. (Apply)

Summary​

A profession is defined by specialised knowledge, extended training and certification, a shared code of conduct, and accountability to a professional community - not by pay or prestige. Teaching meets all four features. Professionalism is how an individual teacher acts; professionalisation is how the field organises itself; the two develop at different speeds. Compared with older professions, teaching is still strengthening its training route, its professional bodies, and the room it gives teachers to use judgement.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can define a profession and name its four distinguishing features.
  • I can use the four features to judge whether a described job is a profession, an occupation, or a hard-to-classify case.
  • I can explain the difference between professionalism in teaching and professionalisation of teaching, with an example of each.
  • I can explain why teaching is sometimes called a "semi-profession" without treating that as an insult.

Try this at your practicum school​

Ask your cooperating teacher which of the four features of a profession they feel most strongly in their daily work, and which they feel least. Note their answer with one concrete example they give, and bring it to the next session.

Summative task​

Choose any job that is not teaching and that you know well. In about 250–300 words, judge it against the four features of a profession. For each feature say whether the job shows it clearly, partly, or not at all, and give a specific reason. End with a one-sentence verdict: profession, occupation, or borderline - and why.
