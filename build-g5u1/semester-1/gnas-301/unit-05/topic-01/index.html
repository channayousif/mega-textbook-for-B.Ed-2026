Topic 5.1 - Toxic impacts of atmospheric agents

A real classroom situation​

In a Karachi apartment block, the July load-shedding arrives at midnight and a family starts the generator on the landing, door closed against thieves. By 3 a.m. the grandfather has a headache and nausea; by morning he is in the emergency unit with carbon monoxide poisoning. The doctor's first question is not "what factory is near you?" It is "where was the generator?" The poison came from inside the house.

This topic follows that question: how the agents in air, mostly invisible, travel from a source to a target organ and do their harm.

Explanation​

Toxic impacts of atmospheric agents​

A toxic agent is a substance that harms a living organism when it reaches a sensitive site in enough amount. For atmospheric agents the journey has a fixed shape: emission, inhalation, entry into the blood, and arrival at a target organ, and each agent differs in where it ends up and what it does there.

  • Carbon monoxide (CO) is the quiet killer of the worked example: colourless, odourless, and it binds the blood's oxygen seats hundreds of times more tightly than oxygen itself, so the body suffocates with clean-looking lungs. The WHO names it among the pollutants of concern in its global air quality guidelines (WHO, 2021).
  • Sulfur dioxide and nitrogen dioxide dissolve in the airways' lining and inflame them; asthma worsens on high days.
  • Ozone, at ground level, is a lung irritant cooked by sunlight from other pollutants, worst on hot afternoons.
  • Particulate matter (PM2.5) carries the burden the WHO counts: fine particles cause cardiovascular and respiratory disease and cancers, part of the 4.2 million premature deaths a year the WHO attributes to outdoor air pollution (WHO, 2024).
  • Lead attacks the developing brain; no safe level exists.

Two ideas organise the whole table. Dose matters: the harm depends on how much reaches the target, which depends on concentration and time; the family on the landing had a high concentration for hours. The route matters: the same substance can be a different poison by a different door, and toxic agents are not only atmospheric. Sindh's groundwater carries arsenic, a toxic agent that reaches people by the water route, and a study of twenty-four districts along the Indus found levels demanding health-risk assessment (Alibhatti et al., 2017). Air is this topic's door; Unit 6 opens the others.

A worked example, completed. Trace the grandfather's CO. Emission: the generator's incomplete combustion on a closed landing. Inhalation: high concentration, hours of it. Blood: CO takes the oxygen seats. Target: the oxygen-hungry brain. Effect: headache, nausea, collapse. Protection, at the receptor: never indoors; at the source: a vented generator. The chain you learned for pollution in Unit 2 is the same chain, walked inside the body.

Common misconception. "If you cannot smell it, it is not poisoning you." CO is the standing refutation, but the rule is general: the sensory system did not evolve to detect industrial chemistry. Detection is an instrument's job, which is why CO alarms exist and noses do not.

Activity: Chain the agent​

Work in pairs. Your teacher assigns each pair one agent from the table. In ten minutes, draw the agent's chain, emission to effect, on one sheet, marking the one link where protection is cheapest. Then each pair presents in sixty seconds and the class votes on which protection saves the most lives per rupee. Allow 25 minutes in total.

Check your understanding​

  1. Name the six atmospheric agents with one target organ of each. (Remember)
  2. Why does carbon monoxide harm without damaging the lungs at all? (Understand)
  3. The family ran the generator for hours in a closed room. Explain the dose idea using concentration and time. (Apply)
  4. Arsenic reached Sindh's families by a different route than the table's agents. Which idea, dose or route, does its case underline? (Analyze)

Summary​

Atmospheric agents travel a fixed chain, emission, inhalation, blood, target organ, effect, and differ in their destination and their damage: CO takes the blood's oxygen seats, sulfur and nitrogen dioxide inflame airways, ozone irritates on hot afternoons, PM2.5 causes cardiovascular and respiratory disease and cancers, and lead attacks the developing brain with no safe level. Dose, concentration times time, and the route of entry decide the harm, and toxic agents arrive by water as well as air, as Sindh's arsenic shows. What cannot be smelled can still poison.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can name the six agents and one effect of each.
  • I can draw the emission-to-effect chain for any agent in the table.
  • I can explain dose as concentration times time with a real example.
  • I can explain why odour is not a poison detector.

Try this at your practicum school​

Count the combustion sources within one hundred metres of the school gate: generators, kitchens, traffic, burning waste. For each, name the agent it emits most and the protection that applies. Bring back the list and mark the one source the school could actually influence.

Summative task​

Write 150 to 200 words on the following.

A landlord tells tenants that generators on the stairwell are "fine, everyone does it". Using the chain, the CO mechanism and the dose idea, write the reply, ending with the two protections you would demand, one at the source and one at the receptor.
