Topic 6.3 - Pollutants in the body: essentiality, absorption, accumulation

A real classroom situation​

On a school trip to Keenjhar Lake, the guide points to the fishing families and says what the pupils' textbook says not: "The big fish are the worst to eat, if the water is dirty." A pupil asks why. The guide draws in the dust: small plants, small fish, big fish, family. "What the water carries a little of," he says, "the big fish carries a lot of, and the family eats the big fish."

He is drawing bio-magnification, and this topic is the explanation behind his dust diagram.

Explanation​

Biological properties of organic and inorganic pollutants; essentiality and toxicity​

Pollutants behave in the body according to their chemistry. Organic pollutants, built on carbon, pesticides, solvents, dioxins, tend to dissolve in fat, which makes them slow to leave and prone to the accumulation this topic builds to. Inorganic pollutants, the metals and minerals, lead, mercury, arsenic, cadmium, do not break down at all; the body can store them in bone and tissue, and some bind the body's own working parts, which is how they do harm.

Essentiality and toxicity sit on one ladder, not two shelves. Essential substances, iron, zinc, copper, even fluoride in small amounts, are required for life; the same substances at higher dose poison, and the ladder's position differs by substance. Iron cures anaemia at one dose and hospitalises a child at another. Arsenic, famously non-essential, is toxic at every rung. The WHO's chemical-safety work treats the essential and the non-essential with the same question, how much reaches whom (WHO, n.d.).

Routes of absorption​

Unit 5 named the three doors: the lung (fastest, the air pollutants' door), the gut (food and water, arsenic's door), and the skin (the sprayer's door). The route changes the dose that reaches the blood, and the same substance can be a different exposure by a different door: a farm worker takes in the same pesticide by skin, breath and hand-to-mouth, and the route mix decides how much rides Unit 5's journey.

Bioaccumulation and bio-magnification​

Two words, two scales. Bioaccumulation is within one organism: a substance that enters faster than it leaves builds up over time in the organism's fat or bone. Bio-magnification is up the food chain: each predator eats many prey and inherits what they accumulated, so the concentration rises step by step toward the top, the big fish, the fish-eating bird, the fishing family.

A worked example. The Keenjhar dust diagram, explained. If the water carries a trace of a fat-loving pollutant, the plankton hold a little; each small fish eats many plankton and holds more; each big fish eats many small fish and holds a lot; the family eats the big fish and receives the whole chain's collections. The guide's warning is the food-chain arithmetic, and it is why the top predators, birds of prey, big fish, and people, are the monitoring stations of environmental poisoning, the story the Minamata bay's mercury taught the world.

Common misconception. "If the water is only slightly polluted, the fish are only slightly polluted." The chain is a multiplier, not a mirror: each step concentrates, so a trace in the water can be a dose in the fish. This is why testing the water is not enough, and why the fishing family's big fish, not the lake sample, is the exposure that matters.

Activity: Build the ladder​

Work in groups of four. Each member takes one rung of the Keenjhar ladder, plankton, small fish, large fish, family, and receives a card showing how many of the rung below they eat and what each carries. Multiply up the ladder, write your rung's total on the board, and the class reads the magnification factor from water to family. Then answer: which single control would cut the family's dose most? Allow 25 minutes.

Check your understanding​

  1. Distinguish organic from inorganic pollutant behaviour in the body. (Understand)
  2. Iron is essential and toxic. Explain the ladder idea using it. (Apply)
  3. Why does the route of absorption change the dose that reaches the blood? (Analyze)
  4. Distinguish bioaccumulation from bio-magnification with the lake's own rungs. (Understand)

Summary​

Organic pollutants dissolve in fat and linger; inorganic metals never break down and bind the body's working parts. Essentiality and toxicity sit on one ladder, iron essential at one dose and poisonous at another, arsenic toxic at every rung. The three routes of absorption, lung, gut and skin, each deliver a different dose to the blood. Bioaccumulation builds within one organism and bio-magnification up the chain, so a trace in the water becomes a dose in the top predator, the big fish and the family that eats it. The chain is a multiplier, not a mirror.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can state the body-behaviour difference of organic and inorganic pollutants.
  • I can place iron, fluoride and arsenic correctly on the essentiality ladder.
  • I can name the three absorption doors and rank them for a given exposure.
  • I can build and explain a magnification ladder with real numbers.

Try this at your practicum school​

Find out what fish, if any, appears in the school's or the neighbourhood's meals, and from where. Write three sentences: the fish's place in its own food chain, whether it is a top predator, and the one question a family should ask about the water it came from. Do not name the school.

Summative task​

Write 170 to 200 words on the following.

The district fisheries office asks for a one-page explanation of why it tests big fish, not only water. Using the ladder, the multiplier idea and one real pollutant, write the page, ending with the advice you would give the fishing families of Keenjhar.
