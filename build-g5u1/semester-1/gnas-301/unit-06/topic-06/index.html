Topic 6.6 - Climate change: mitigation and adaptation

A real classroom situation​

Two photographs hang side by side in the corridor of a Hyderabad college. The first: Karachi in June 2015, the week the morgues ran out of space, more than a thousand lives lost to the heatwave. The second: the same province in August 2022, a third of the country under water. A first-year student asks the question the whole course has been building toward: "What do we actually do about this?"

The answer is two words, and this topic is them.

Explanation​

Climate change: causes, effects, mitigation strategies and adaptation​

The causes are Topic 6.4's enhancement: fossil-fuel energy, deforestation, industry and agriculture thickening the greenhouse layer. The effects arrive as the extremes the thickened layer feeds, and the WHO reports that 3.6 billion people already live in places highly susceptible to them, with about 250,000 additional deaths a year projected between 2030 and 2050 from undernutrition, malaria, diarrhoea and heat stress alone (WHO, 2023). Pakistan's own decade holds both photographs: the heatwave and the floods, and the review literature describes the country as suffering the disastrous effects of a crisis it did least to cause, examining the floods' environmental, social and economic implications and the policies that answer them (Adnan et al., 2024).

The two responses answer different questions. Mitigation reduces the causes: cutting emissions, moving to renewable energy, planting and protecting forests, efficient transport and industry. It works on the shared layer, for everyone, over decades. Adaptation reduces the harm of what already arrives: heat-action plans and cool shelters, flood-proof schools and early-warning systems, drought-resistant crops, water storage. It works locally, now, for the exposed. The two are partners, not alternatives: mitigation without adaptation abandons the exposed present; adaptation without mitigation surrenders the future.

A worked example. Read the two photographs through the responses. The heatwave's answer is adaptation's textbook: Karachi's heat-action plan, drinking points, cool rooms, checked-on neighbours, the measures that cut the 2015 toll in the calmer years since. The floods' answer is both: adaptation in early warning, elevated schools, re-built wells; mitigation in the national and global emissions policy that decides how many such floods the next decade holds. Pakistan's own climate policy carries both, and its plantation drives and renewable programme sit on the mitigation side while its flood plans sit on the other.

The course, whole. Every tool returns here. The chain from Unit 2 tells the receptor-end school what to do on a heat day. The hierarchy from Unit 3 orders the adaptations: remove the exposure, relocate the assembly; guard, shade and water points; procedures, the heat drill; protect, the water bottle. The governance stack from Unit 5 names who owns the mitigation, the Paris pledges, the national policy, the provincial plan. And the teacher's own step, Unit 1's fifth, influence others, multiplies through a classroom. The student's question, what do we do, has a two-word answer and a whole-course method behind it.

Common misconception. "Mitigation and adaptation are alternatives; choose one." They answer different questions, the future's causes and the present's harms, and every serious plan carries both. The second misconception is quieter: "It is too late for anything." The honest reading is the opposite of paralysis: every fraction of a degree avoided is harm prevented, and every adaptation installed is harm softened, which is why the WHO describes cutting emissions as able to result in very large gains for health (WHO, 2023).

Activity: Sort the responses​

Work in groups of three. Your teacher gives each group sixteen cards, real climate responses from tree plantation to heat-action plans to solar panels to flood warnings. Sort them into mitigation and adaptation, then flag the four you would present to the district government first, with one sentence of reasoning each. Allow 25 minutes, then each group presents its four.

Check your understanding​

  1. Define mitigation and adaptation, one sentence each. (Remember)
  2. Why are the two responses partners rather than alternatives? (Analyze)
  3. Place the heatwave's and the floods' answers on the two sides, and justify any response that sits on both. (Analyze)
  4. Apply Unit 3's hierarchy to a school's heat-day plan, one control per level. (Apply)

Summary​

Climate change's causes are the enhanced greenhouse layer; its effects arrive as extremes, heatwaves and floods, with 3.6 billion people highly susceptible and a quarter-million additional deaths a year projected. Mitigation cuts the causes for the shared future, renewable energy, forests, efficient industry; adaptation softens the harms of the arriving present, heat plans, flood-proofing, early warning. Pakistan's two photographs answer to both sides, and the course's tools, the chain, the hierarchy, the governance stack, the teacher's multiplication, are the method. The student's question has a two-word answer: both, now.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can define mitigation and adaptation and give two examples of each.
  • I can explain why both are needed, future and present.
  • I can place the heatwave's and the floods' answers on the right sides.
  • I can carry the course's tools into one school-scale climate response.

Try this at your practicum school​

Write the school's one-page climate page: two hazards the school already faces, one adaptation the school could install this term, one mitigation the school community could join this year, and the two names, who leads each. This is the course's last practicum task; make it the one you would actually hand over.

Summative task​

Write 170 to 200 words on the following.

The principal asks: "Heat and floods, which should we prepare for?" Write the answer this course supports: what the two responses are, why the school prepares for both, the one adaptation and the one mitigation you would start this term, and the line that says who checks. End with the sentence you want the staffroom to remember.
