Topic 1.4 - Shaping teaching, assessment and reflection

A real classroom situation​

Miss Farah plans a Class 8 lesson on the water cycle. She has forty minutes and fifty-two pupils.

Her first plan is the one she was taught: define evaporation, condensation, precipitation and collection; draw the diagram on the board; pupils copy it; set the textbook questions for homework.

She teaches it. The copying is immaculate. The homework, next morning, is not: a third of the class have reproduced the diagram and answered nothing, and several have labelled the arrows in the wrong direction while copying them correctly from their own notebooks.

Nobody misbehaved. Nobody was absent. By every visible measure the lesson went well, and almost nothing was learnt.

The question this topic asks is what Miss Farah should change - and how she could know whether the change worked.

Explanation​

Planning: choosing method, sequence and pace​

Planning is where most psychological ideas do their quiet work, and where Miss Farah's lesson went wrong before she opened her mouth.

Start from what they know. The water cycle is not new to pupils who have watched a puddle dry and rain fall. A plan that begins with four definitions throws away everything they could have attached the lesson to.

Respect the limits of attention. Four new terms in the first five minutes is more than working memory will hold while also copying a diagram. Copying and understanding compete for the same limited capacity, which is why the notebooks can be perfect and the understanding absent (Seifert & Sutton, 2009).

Show the move before you ask for it. A worked example, done aloud with the reasoning visible, costs five minutes and saves the exercise. Miss Farah asked pupils to explain the cycle without ever modelling an explanation.

Decide the one thing. With forty minutes and fifty-two pupils, a plan that tries to cover everything covers nothing. What must they be able to do by the bell that they could not do at the start?

Classroom management as a psychological task, not only a disciplinary one​

Management is usually taught as a set of rules and consequences. That is half of it, and the less useful half.

Most of what looks like misbehaviour is a pupil doing something rational given how the lesson feels from their seat. A pupil who cannot start the work and does not want that noticed will find something else to do. A pupil who finished six minutes ago will too. In Topic 1.3 these were Sana and Kiran; from the front they look like the same discipline problem and they are not.

So the first management question is not "what is the consequence?" but "what is this behaviour getting them, and what would make it unnecessary?" A task pitched where pupils can start it removes more disruption than any rule.

The second is that a classroom is a psychological environment. Whether it is safe to be wrong out loud decides whether anyone will answer a question honestly. A teacher who greets a wrong answer with visible impatience will, within a fortnight, have a class that only volunteers when certain - and will have lost the main instrument for finding out what they know.

Assessment and feedback informed by psychology​

Assessment in most classrooms means marking, and marking means a number at the end. Psychology suggests two corrections.

Assessment is for finding out, and it is most useful while you can still act on it. A test at the end of the chapter tells you what to write in the register. Three questions in the middle of the lesson tell you whether to carry on. Miss Farah discovered the failure the next morning, which was one day too late and fifty-two pupils too wide.

Feedback works when it tells the pupil what to do next. A mark tells a pupil where they stand and nothing else; worse, once a mark is attached, most pupils stop reading anything else on the page. Feedback that says which step went wrong, and what to try, is the part that changes performance (Vosniadou, 2001).

There is also the matter of what assessment teaches pupils to value. If the test rewards reproducing a diagram, pupils will become excellent at reproducing diagrams. Miss Farah's class did exactly what her assessment asked of them.

The reflective teacher: using evidence to improve one's own practice​

The four steps in the figure above are a cycle, and the fourth is the one that gets skipped.

Reflection here does not mean feeling bad about a lesson. It means treating your own classroom as the place where claims get tested - the loop from Topic 1.2, run on yourself. What did I expect? What happened? Which explanation fits, and what would separate it from the rival explanation?

Miss Farah's second attempt is a small piece of research. She starts from a puddle in the school yard, models one explanation aloud, cuts the four terms to two, and asks three ungraded questions with ten minutes left. If more pupils can explain the cycle in their own words than last time, she has learnt something about her own teaching. If not, she has learnt something too.

This is also a professional obligation rather than a personal virtue: Continuous Professional Development is one of Pakistan's National Professional Standards for Teachers, and it asks for exactly this habit of examining and improving one's own practice (Khizar et al., 2019).

The misconception to clear now. Reflection is widely taken to mean writing a diary entry after a difficult lesson - a mood, recorded. On that reading it is honest but useless, because nothing about it could come out unexpectedly. Useful reflection has a prediction in it. "I think they copied without understanding because the terms came before the example" can be wrong, and checking it is what makes the next lesson better.

Activity: Rebuild Miss Farah's lesson​

In groups of four, about 30 minutes.

You have forty minutes, fifty-two pupils, a blackboard, and no projector.

  1. Decide the one thing pupils must be able to do by the bell. Write it as something they do, not something they know.
  2. Sketch the lesson in four moves, and mark where the worked example goes and why it goes there.
  3. Write the three questions you would ask with ten minutes left, and say what answer would tell you to slow down.
  4. Name one thing you have deliberately left out, and defend leaving it out.

Swap plans with another group. Their job is to find the place where a pupil like Sana, from Topic 1.3, would fall out of your lesson.

Check your understanding​

  1. Name the four steps of the plan-teach-assess-reflect cycle. (Remember)
  2. Explain why a class can produce perfect notebooks and still not understand the lesson. (Understand)
  3. Why does the text say a mark can get in the way of feedback? (Understand)
  4. A teacher says "my class is badly behaved in the last ten minutes." Give two explanations drawn from this topic and say what you would observe to choose between them. (Apply)
  5. Rewrite this reflection so that it could turn out to be wrong: "The lesson did not go well. The pupils were not interested. I will try to make it more interesting next time." (Apply)

Summary​

Psychological ideas shape a teacher's work long before any pupil misbehaves. Planning decides whether a lesson has anything to attach to, whether it exceeds the limits of attention, and whether the move is modelled before it is demanded. Management is better read as a question about what a behaviour is getting a pupil than as a question about consequences, and the emotional climate of a room decides whether a teacher can find out what pupils know. Assessment is most useful while there is still time to act on it, and feedback works when it names the next step rather than the position. Reflection completes the cycle, and it is only useful when it contains something that could be shown to be wrong.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can name the four steps of the cycle and say which is most often skipped.
  • I can explain why copying and understanding compete, in terms of attention.
  • I can explain the difference between a mark and feedback that changes performance.
  • I can turn a vague reflection into one that makes a checkable prediction.

Try this at your practicum school​

Ask to see a lesson plan, then watch that lesson.

Afterwards, write down the one thing the teacher wanted pupils to be able to do, and how they found out whether it had happened. If they found out after the lesson rather than during it, work out where in the forty minutes a three-question check could have gone, and what it would have cost them.

Summative task​

Write 350-400 words analysing the Miss Farah episode at the top of this topic.

Your answer must do three things. First, explain what went wrong using at least two ideas from this unit, drawn from more than one topic. Second, weigh the ideas against each other - which best explains the wrong-direction arrows in particular, and what makes you prefer it? Third, set out her second attempt, including the evidence she would gather and the result that would tell her she was still wrong.

Range across the unitDraws on ideas from at least two topics and connects them rather than listing them.
