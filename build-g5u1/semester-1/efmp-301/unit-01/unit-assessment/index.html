Introduction to Educational Psychology - assessment and review

Unit summary​

Educational psychology studies mind and behaviour in educational settings, asking its questions from the teacher's side of the desk. Its scope is the learner, the learning process and the learning situation; a teacher who reaches for only one of the three will misread most classrooms. It is a scientific study because its claims are framed so evidence could count against them, and gathered through observation, experiment and case study.

Psychology and education feed each other. Psychology supplies tested theories, methods and a precise language for describing learners. Classrooms supply the conditions, the problems and the corrections that laboratories cannot. Because the field is applied, a finding is judged by whether it survives real conditions, and acting on one carries an ethical weight, since pupils cannot opt out.

Learners in a single room differ in readiness and prior knowledge, in motivation and interest, and in language and home support. Each difference looks similar from the front of the class, each is routinely collapsed into the word "ability", and each calls for a different response.

Those ideas reach a teacher's work through planning, management, assessment and reflection. Planning decides whether a lesson has anything to attach to and whether it exceeds attention. Management is better read as a question about what a behaviour gets a pupil. Assessment is most useful while there is still time to act on it, and feedback works when it names the next step. Reflection closes the cycle, and earns its place only when it makes a prediction that could turn out to be wrong.

Summative assessment​

Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)​

  1. Educational psychology is best described as the study of: (Remember) a) mental illness in school-age children b) mind and behaviour in educational settings c) the history of school systems d) methods of school administration

  2. Which of these is not one of the three parts of the scope of educational psychology? (Remember) a) the learner b) the learning process c) the learning situation d) the examination board

  3. "Educational psychology is just common sense." The strongest objection to this claim is that: (Understand) a) common sense is usually wrong b) common sense offers contradictory answers and cannot be checked c) psychology uses technical terms d) teachers have no time for common sense

  4. A teacher teaches the same topic to two similar sections, changing only the order of the examples, and compares the test results. This is closest to: (Remember) a) a case study b) an experiment c) systematic observation d) a survey

  5. The main thing a classroom gives psychology that a laboratory cannot is: (Understand) a) more participants b) faster results c) real conditions in which a finding may fail d) better equipment

  6. Calling educational psychology an applied field means chiefly that: (Understand) a) it is easier than pure psychology b) it is judged partly by whether its findings can be acted on c) it does not use experiments d) it applies only to primary schools

  7. A pupil copies the board perfectly and cannot do the exercise alone. The most likely explanation offered in this unit is: (Apply) a) the pupil is lazy b) the pupil lacks the prior knowledge the lesson assumes c) the pupil dislikes the teacher d) the pupil has poor handwriting

  8. A pupil who finishes early and then disturbs others is best understood as: (Apply) a) badly behaved b) under-motivated by that particular task c) unable to learn d) in need of a stricter rule

  9. According to this unit, feedback changes performance mainly when it: (Understand) a) is given as a mark out of ten b) is given in front of the class c) tells the pupil what to do next d) is written in red

  10. Which reflection is most useful by this unit's standard? (Apply) a) "The lesson went badly and I felt discouraged." b) "The pupils were not interested today." c) "They copied without understanding, I think because the terms came before the example." d) "I will try harder to make the next lesson interesting."

Restricted-response questions (RRQs)​

Answer each in two to four sentences.

  1. Define educational psychology and name the three parts of its scope. (Remember)
  2. Give one question a general psychologist and one question an educational psychologist might each ask about memory. (Understand)
  3. Name the three ways of gathering evidence described in Topic 1.1 and state one limit of each. (Understand)
  4. Explain what psychology contributes to education, under three headings. (Understand)
  5. Explain what education contributes back to psychology, with one example. (Understand)
  6. A trainer reports that testing helps pupils remember. Write the two questions you would ask before changing your own teaching. (Apply)
  7. Explain why a pupil who has failed repeatedly may stop trying, and why "lazy" is a poor description of this. (Understand)
  8. A pupil speaks confidently in Sindhi and answers poorly on an English written test. Give two explanations and say what would tell them apart. (Apply)
  9. Explain why copying a diagram and understanding it can compete with each other. (Understand)
  10. Rewrite this so it could be shown to be wrong: "My class is weak in mathematics." (Apply)

Extended-response questions (ERQs)​

Answer each in 300-400 words.

  1. "Educational psychology is common sense dressed up in technical language." Set out the strongest case for this claim, then answer it, using the idea of scientific study from Topic 1.1. (Analyze)
  2. Three teachers hear the same research finding. One ignores it, one adopts it wholesale, and one changes a single thing and checks. Analyse what each has understood and misunderstood about the relationship between psychology and education. (Analyze)
  3. Three pupils in one class have all stopped participating, for three different reasons. Explain how a teacher could tell the reasons apart, and why acting on the wrong one may make matters worse. (Analyze)
  4. A lesson produces immaculate notebooks and almost no understanding. Analyse what went wrong using ideas from at least two topics of this unit, and set out a redesigned lesson together with the evidence you would gather to judge it. (Evaluate)
  5. "A teacher's own classroom is the place where claims about learning get tested." Evaluate this statement. Consider what a single teacher can and cannot establish, and what would make their evidence worth more than an anecdote. (Evaluate)

Answers and marking guidance​

MCQ answer key​

  1. b - the definition used throughout the unit; the field is defined by its setting and its question, not by clinical work.
  2. d - the examination board belongs to the system around a school, not to the three-part scope.
  3. b - the objection is not that common sense is wrong but that it is unfalsifiable, since some proverb predicts every outcome.
  4. b - one variable changed on purpose, with a comparison; that is an experiment however small.
  5. c - laboratories can supply participants and equipment; only real classrooms supply the conditions under which a finding may fail.
  6. b - applied means aimed at improving something, so usefulness is part of how it is judged.
  7. b - copying is possible without the prior knowledge the lesson assumes; a and c are verdicts rather than explanations.
  8. b - motivation is task-specific; the same behaviour from a failing pupil would have the opposite cause.
  9. c - a mark reports position; feedback that names the next step is what alters performance.
  10. c - it contains a prediction that could turn out to be wrong; the others record a mood or an intention.

RRQ model answers and mark schemes​

Award marks for the substance below, not for matching wording.

  1. The study of mind and behaviour in educational settings (1), plus the learner, the learning process and the learning situation (1 for all three).
  2. One question about how memory works in general (1); one asked from the teacher's side, about what to do differently as a result (1).
  3. Observation, experiment, case study (1). One fair limit each, for example an unusual day, a result that may not hold elsewhere, a single case (1).
  4. Tested theories; methods for finding things out; a precise language for describing learners (2, one mark for two headings, two for all three with examples).
  5. Real conditions, problems laboratories do not pose, and corrections when findings fail (1), with a concrete example (1).
  6. Any two of: what exactly does the claim state, under what conditions, do those conditions hold in my class, what would count as it failing (1 each).
  7. Repeated failure makes trying costly, because failing visibly is worse than not trying (1); "lazy" describes the behaviour as a trait rather than as a response to a situation (1).
  8. Two plausible explanations, for example a language cost versus an understanding gap (1); a way to separate them, such as allowing the answer in another form or language (1).
  9. Both draw on the same limited attention, so effort spent reproducing the diagram is not available for making sense of it (2).
  10. Any rewriting that names a specific, checkable claim, for example "most of my class cannot convert an improper fraction, which I will check with five items on Monday" (2).
