# Topic 5.3 - Presenting and improving the plan

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U5-3" src="/img/figures/eed-313/unit-05/fig-U5-3.svg" alt="Flowchart of the presenting-and-improving cycle: present the plan, peers give structured feedback using the protocol, the author identifies the most useful feedback, revises the plan, and presents the revision." width={780} height={470} />

The guide's worked example asks each student teacher to present the classroom management plan they
have built across Units 1 to 4. Peers give structured feedback using the protocol from Topic 5.2.
The author identifies the most useful feedback, revises the plan, and presents the revision. This
topic is about that cycle and about why the revision step is the point.

## Explanation

The guide treats presenting and improving as a cycle, not a one-off event. The figure above maps
the cycle: present, receive feedback, identify the most useful feedback, revise, present the
revision. The management question is: how do you give feedback that the author can actually use,
and how do you receive feedback without defending or dismissing it?

<Figure id="fig-U5-7" src="/img/figures/eed-313/unit-05/fig-U5-7.svg" alt="Table comparing strong feedback against weak feedback: strong feedback names a specific criterion and cites a passage, weak feedback is vague; strong feedback suggests a revision, weak feedback only praises or criticises." width={780} height={470} />

### Presenting the plan

The figure above maps the cycle. The guide treats the presentation as a structured event, not a
reading-aloud. A strong presentation does three things:

1. **States the philosophy in one sentence.** The audience needs to know what you believe before
   they can evaluate what you have planned.
2. **Names the constraint the plan is designed for.** A plan for a class of twenty is not the same
   as a plan for a class of sixty; the constraint is part of the plan.
3. **Walks through the four layers.** Theory, curriculum, routines, care - each gets a moment, and
   the audience uses the protocol to evaluate each.

### Giving and receiving structured feedback

The figure above maps the cycle. The guide treats feedback as a skill, not an opinion. Strong
feedback follows the protocol: it names a specific criterion (clarity, consistency or coverage),
cites a specific passage from the plan, and suggests a specific revision. Weak feedback is vague
("I liked it") or prescriptive ("You should do it my way").

The guide treats receiving feedback as a skill too. The author's job is to listen, to ask
clarifying questions, and to decide which feedback to act on. The author does not defend the plan
and does not accept every suggestion; the author decides.

### Revising the plan

The figure above maps the cycle. The guide treats revision as the point of the exercise, because
a plan that is presented but not revised is a plan that has not been tested. A strong revision
does three things:

1. **Names the feedback it is responding to.** "My reviewer noted that my philosophy and my
   curriculum decision were inconsistent. I have revised the curriculum decision to match the
   philosophy."
2. **Shows the change.** The revision is visible: the before and after are both present.
3. **Explains the reasoning.** The author explains why the revision strengthens the plan.

## Activity: Present and revise

Work in groups of three. Each person presents their plan (5 minutes). Peers give structured
feedback using the protocol (5 minutes). The author identifies the most useful feedback and
revises one section of the plan (10 minutes). Then the author presents the revision (5 minutes).
Allow 30 minutes total.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the three things a strong presentation of a plan does. *(Remember)*
2. A peer says, "Your plan is good, but I would do it differently." Using Topic 5.3, explain why
   that feedback is not yet useful. *(Analyze)*
3. State the three things a strong revision does. *(Remember)*
4. A reviewer identifies an inconsistency between your philosophy and your routine set. Using the
   protocol, explain how you would revise. *(Apply)*
5. Evaluate the claim that "peer feedback is just opinion." Use the protocol from Topics 5.2 and
   5.3 in your answer. *(Evaluate)*

## Summary

Presenting and improving a plan is a cycle: present, receive feedback, identify the most useful
feedback, revise, present the revision. Strong feedback follows the protocol; strong revision
names the feedback it responds to, shows the change, and explains the reasoning.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can present a classroom management plan in a structured way.
- [ ] I can give structured feedback using the protocol.
- [ ] I can receive feedback without defending or dismissing it.
- [ ] I can revise a plan in light of specific feedback.
- [ ] I can explain why a revision strengthens the plan.

## Try this at your practicum school

Present your plan to a colleague and ask for structured feedback using the protocol. Revise one
section in light of the feedback, and write half a page on what changed and why.

Do not name the colleague in your write-up.

## Summative task

Write 300 to 350 words on the following.

> A trainee presents a classroom management plan. A peer says, "I do not agree with your
> philosophy, so I cannot use it." Using Topics 5.2 and 5.3, explain what that response overlooks
> and propose how the peer could give useful feedback despite disagreeing with the philosophy.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong response does |
|---|---|
| Grasp of the protocol (Understand) | Names the criteria and explains how they apply regardless of agreement |
| Analysis of the response | Explains that feedback evaluates the plan, not the philosophy |
| **Proposal for useful feedback (Apply)** | **Proposes a specific, protocol-based feedback move the peer could make** |
| Register and clarity | Writes for a colleague, defines any technical term used, stays within the word count |
