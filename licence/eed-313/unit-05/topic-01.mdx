# Topic 5.1 - One philosophy from four units

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U5-1" src="/img/figures/eed-313/unit-05/fig-U5-1.svg" alt="Concept map connecting the four units: Unit 1 theories and definition at the centre, with branches to Unit 2 curriculum and differentiation, Unit 3 routines and time, Unit 4 community and care, all converging on 'my classroom management philosophy'." width={780} height={470} />

At the start of this course, you wrote a personal philosophy of teaching. Now, at the end, you have
more to say. You know that management is centred on learning, that three theories explain how
learning happens, that curriculum decisions are management acts, that routines buy time, and that
care and accountability build the relationships a classroom runs on. This topic is about pulling
those pieces into one philosophy you can use.

## Explanation

The guide treats a philosophy not as a statement you write once but as a synthesis you build from
the material of the course. The figure above maps the synthesis: each unit contributes a layer,
and the layers connect.

<Figure id="fig-U5-5" src="/img/figures/eed-313/unit-05/fig-U5-5.svg" alt="Table showing what each unit contributes to a classroom management philosophy: Unit 1 contributes the definition and theories, Unit 2 contributes curriculum decisions and differentiation, Unit 3 contributes routines and time management, Unit 4 contributes community and care." width={780} height={470} />

### The four layers

- **Unit 1 gave you the foundation.** Management is the proactive work of creating and maintaining
  the conditions in which student learning happens. Three theories explain how learning happens, and
  a fluent teacher moves between them as the learning target demands.
- **Unit 2 gave you the plan.** Curriculum decisions are management acts: what to teach, in what
  order, at what pace, for whom. Differentiation adjusts the route without lowering the target.
- **Unit 3 gave you the machinery.** Routines buy instructional time. The anchor routine, the
  independent-work routine, and subject-specific routines carry a plan through a day.
- **Unit 4 gave you the relationships.** Community, care and accountability build the culture a
  classroom runs on. A caring classroom has consequences, but they are restorative.

The figure above shows how these layers connect. The centre is your philosophy: what you believe
learning is, and what that belief implies you should do first, second, third in a classroom. The
four layers are the evidence for that philosophy, drawn from the course.

### Building the synthesis

The guide's worked example asks you to build the synthesis in three moves:

1. **State your belief.** What do you believe learning is? Unit 1's three theories are your
   options; you do not have to choose one, but you have to say which you draw on most and why.
2. **State the implication.** What does that belief imply you should do in a classroom? Unit 2's
   curriculum decisions, Unit 3's routines and Unit 4's relationships are your evidence.
3. **State the test.** How would you know your philosophy is working? The observable features of a
   well-managed classroom (Unit 1) are your answer: practised routines, high intellectual demand,
   teacher withitness, an environment that supports learning.

## Activity: Build your philosophy

Work alone for 15 minutes. Write a one-paragraph philosophy of classroom management that does all
three of the following: states what you believe learning is, states what that belief implies you
should do in a classroom, and states how you would know it is working. Then share with a partner
and identify one place where your philosophy draws on a specific idea from one of the four units.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the four layers that a classroom management philosophy draws on, and state what each
   contributes. *(Remember)*
2. A trainee says, "I believe learning is the teacher transferring knowledge." Using the four-layer
   model, explain what that belief implies for curriculum, routines and relationships. *(Analyze)*
3. State the three moves for building a philosophy from the course material. *(Remember)*
4. A friend's philosophy says what they believe but not how they would know it is working. Using
   Topic 5.1, explain what is missing and why it matters. *(Analyze)*
5. For the objective in the Activity, state one way your philosophy has changed since the start of
   the course, and explain why. *(Apply)*

## Summary

A classroom management philosophy is a synthesis of the four units: the definition and theories of
Unit 1, the curriculum and differentiation of Unit 2, the routines of Unit 3, and the community and
care of Unit 4. Building the synthesis is a three-move process: state your belief, state the
implication, state the test.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can name the four layers that a classroom management philosophy draws on.
- [ ] I can state my own philosophy of classroom management in one paragraph.
- [ ] I can explain how my philosophy connects to a specific idea from each of the four units.
- [ ] I can state how I would know my philosophy is working in a real classroom.

## Try this at your practicum school

Write your philosophy in one paragraph. Then observe one classroom and note one moment where the
teacher's practice is consistent with your philosophy and one moment where it is not. Write half a
page on what the comparison tells you.

Do not name the school or the teacher in your write-up.

## Summative task

Write 300 to 350 words on the following.

> A trainee says, "I do not have a philosophy of classroom management; I just manage." Using
> Topic 5.1's account of what a philosophy is and the four layers it draws on, explain what that
> claim overlooks and argue which single layer would most strengthen the described trainee's
> practice.
