# Topic 2.3 - Differentiation, multigrade and overcrowding

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U2-3" src="/img/figures/eed-313/unit-02/fig-U2-3.svg" alt="Diagram showing one curriculum objective differentiated three ways for a mixed-ability class: a core task for all, a scaffolded version for pupils who need support, and an extended version for pupils who are ready." width={780} height={470} />

Mr Hassan teaches Class 4 mathematics in a government school in Sukkur. In his class of
fifteen, five pupils can multiply confidently, six are still building the skill, and four
are not yet secure with addition. One worksheet for all three groups is not differentiation;
it is a recipe for boredom at the top and confusion at the bottom. This topic is about what
genuine differentiation looks like, and about the realities - multigrade rooms and classes
of sixty - that make it both harder and more necessary.

## Explanation

Differentiation is the teacher's response to the fact that pupils in the same room are not
at the same point. The guide treats it as a management act because undifferentiated
instruction produces the disruptions that come from pupils who are either lost or bored.

### Differentiation of instruction by need, interest and level

<Glossary term="Differentiation">Differentiation</Glossary> means adjusting the content,
the process, or the product of a lesson so that every pupil is working at the edge of
their current ability. It does **not** mean making some children do easier work. That is
the single most common misconception about differentiation, and it matters because easier
work produces lower expectations, and lower expectations produce less learning.

The figure above shows one objective differentiated three ways. The core task is the same
for all: every pupil works on two-digit addition. The **scaffolded** version gives the
pupils who need support a number line and a worked example. The **extended** version asks
the pupils who are ready to apply the skill to a word problem with extra information. The
learning target is the same; the route and the degree of structure differ.

Evertson and Emmer (2009) describe three axes of differentiation:
- **By need** - adjusting the scaffolding a pupil requires to reach the same target.
- **By interest** - offering a choice of context or material that engages the pupil.
- **By level** - adjusting the complexity of the task while keeping the learning target
  constant.

A well-differentiated lesson manages the room because every pupil has a task they can
actually do, and a task they can actually do is the best defence against disruption.

**Common misconception.** "Differentiation means making some children do easier work." It
does not. It means adjusting the support, the context, or the complexity so that every
pupil reaches the same target. Easier work lowers the target; differentiation keeps the
target and changes the route.

### Managing multigrade classrooms

<Glossary term="Multigrade classroom">A multigrade classroom</Glossary> is one in which a
single teacher is responsible for pupils of two or more grade levels at the same time, in
the same room. It is common in rural and low-enrolment schools across Pakistan, and it
requires management tactics that a single-grade classroom does not.

<Figure id="fig-U2-6" src="/img/figures/eed-313/unit-02/fig-U2-6.svg" alt="Table comparing multigrade and overcrowded classroom tactics with the constraint each addresses: peer tutoring, activity corners, whole-class anchors, and structured routines." width={780} height={470} />

The figure above maps the tactics this topic describes. The core management idea is the
**anchor activity**: a task that one grade can do independently while the teacher works
with another grade. A good anchor activity is self-checking, connected to the current
topic, and familiar enough that pupils do not need the teacher to start it. Without an
anchor, a multigrade teacher spends the lesson managing transitions between grades rather
than teaching.

**Peer tutoring** is the second tactic: a pupil who has mastered an explanation teaches it
to a peer. The management weight is that peer tutoring must be structured - the tutor needs
a clear role, the tutee needs a clear task, and the teacher needs to check that the
explanation is correct. Unstructured peer tutoring becomes one pupil doing the work while
the other watches.

Vincent (The Multigrade Classroom: Book 3) stresses that the multigrade teacher's most
important management investment is a small set of practised routines: how to signal a grade
change, how to start an anchor activity, how to end a peer-tutoring pair. The routines do
not eliminate the complexity of two grades, but they make it manageable.

### Managing overcrowded classrooms

<Glossary term="Overcrowded classroom">An overcrowded classroom</Glossary> is one in which
the number of pupils far exceeds the room's designed capacity - a reality in many urban
Pakistani government schools, where classes of sixty or more are common. The management
problem is not just space; it is the impossibility of individual attention at that scale.

The figure above maps the tactics. **Whole-class anchors** - a question, a problem, a
shared text - give the teacher a single point of focus for the whole room, which is easier
to manage than sixty individual tasks. **Structured routines** - how to hand in work, how
to ask for help, how to move between activities - prevent the chaos that comes from sixty
pupils improvising the same transition.

**Activity corners** are the third tactic: designated areas of the room where a group works
on a self-checking task while the teacher works with another group. In an overcrowded room,
corners are harder to set up, but the principle is the same as in a multigrade classroom:
the teacher cannot be in two places at once, so part of the room must be able to run
itself for stretches of time.

**Common misconception.** "An overcrowded class cannot be managed, only survived." It
cannot be managed the way a class of twenty can, but the tactics above - whole-class
anchors, structured routines, activity corners - make a class of sixty workable. The
survival mindset is the real management failure, because it stops the teacher from
investing in the routines that make the room run.

## Activity: Differentiate one objective

Work in groups of three. Take the following objective:

> Class 2 Urdu: pupils will be able to write a sentence using a given word.

Now plan this objective for a mixed-ability class in which some pupils can write
independently, some need a sentence frame, and some are still forming letters. Produce
three versions of the task that share the same target. Allow 20 minutes. Then present and
identify one moment where a pupil might slip from "scaffolded" to "easier", and explain
how your plan prevents it.

## Summary

Differentiation adjusts the support, context, or complexity of a task so that every pupil
reaches the same target - it does not lower the target. Multigrade and overcrowded
classrooms require specific management tactics: anchor activities, peer tutoring, structured
routines, and activity corners. In each case the principle is the same: the teacher
cannot be everywhere, so parts of the room must be able to run on practised routines.

Identify one classroom that is either multigrade or overcrowded. Over two lessons, note the
management tactics the teacher uses to handle the constraint, and write half a page on which
tactic makes the most difference and why.

Do not name the school or the teacher in your write-up.
