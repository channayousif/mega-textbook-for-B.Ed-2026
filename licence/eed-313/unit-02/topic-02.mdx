# Topic 2.2 - Plan, motivate, teach, assess

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U2-2" src="/img/figures/eed-313/unit-02/fig-U2-2.svg" alt="Flowchart of the four-stage cycle: plan the lesson, motivate the learners, teach the content, assess understanding, with the management decision named at each stage and a return arrow from assess back to plan." width={780} height={470} />

Miss Saira teaches Class 5 science in a government school in Quetta. Her lessons follow the
same four moves every time: she decides what the pupils will learn and what they will do,
she opens with a question that makes them want to find out, she teaches the content through
a mix of explanation and activity, and she checks before the bell whether they have got it.
Her pupils know what to expect, and the room runs on that predictability. This topic names
the four moves and the management decision each one depends on.

## Explanation

The guide describes a four-stage cycle that carries a teaching plan from intention to
evidence: **plan, motivate, teach, assess**. The cycle is not a list of tips. It is a
sequence in which each stage creates the condition for the next, and the management decision
at each stage is the thing that determines whether the lesson stays on track or drifts.

### Planning the curriculum

<Glossary term="Lesson planning">Planning</Glossary> is the stage in which the teacher
decides the learning target, the activities, the materials, and the time each part of the
lesson will take. The management decision here is **clarity of target**: a teacher who can
state in one sentence what the pupils will be able to do by the end of the lesson has a
compass for every later decision; a teacher who cannot will spend the lesson improvising,
and improvisation is the enemy of a well-managed classroom.

Marzano, Marzano and Pickering (2003) found that the clarity of the learning target was one
of the strongest predictors of pupil learning, because it lets the teacher cut anything that
does not serve the target and keep the lesson focused. A focused lesson is easier to manage
than a wandering one.

The plan also decides the **routines** the lesson will rely on: how pupils will get
materials, what they will do when they finish early, how transitions between activities will
be handled. These are management decisions, and Topic 1.3's observation protocol is the tool
for checking whether the routines you planned are the routines that actually happen.

### Motivating learners within the curriculum

<Glossary term="Motivation">Motivation</Glossary> is the stage in which the teacher
creates the want to learn before the teaching begins. The management decision here is
**relevance**: pupils who can see why the lesson matters to them are easier to teach than
pupils who cannot.

<Figure id="fig-U2-5" src="/img/figures/eed-313/unit-02/fig-U2-5.svg" alt="Table comparing motivation strategies against what each costs in time and materials: a hook question, a real-world problem, a competition, and a pupil choice." width={780} height={470} />

The figure above maps four common motivation strategies against what each one costs in time
and materials. A well-chosen hook question costs almost nothing and takes two minutes. A
hands-on investigation costs materials and time but produces deeper engagement. A
competition is fast but can produce winners and losers, which is a management cost of its
own. A pupil choice is motivating but needs clear boundaries or it becomes noise.

There is no universally best strategy. The management question is: given this class, this
content, and the time I have, which strategy gives me the most engagement for the least
management cost? Wong and Wong (1998) argue that the first minutes of a lesson determine
its management tone for the whole period, so the motivation stage is where the teacher
earns or loses the room.

### Teaching the curriculum

Teaching is the stage in which the teacher delivers the content, and the management
decision here is **pacing and participation**. A teacher who talks for thirty minutes
manages attention by force of personality; a teacher who mixes explanation with activity
manages it by design.

The guide's preferred pattern is short explanation followed by pupil activity, repeated in
cycles: tell them what they are going to learn, show them how, let them try, check, move on.
Each cycle is a small management unit with a beginning, a middle, and an end, and pupils
learn to move with the rhythm.

Evertson and Emmer (2009) stress the transition points between activities as the moments
most likely to produce disruption. A teacher who signals the transition clearly, gives a
time bound, and checks that every pupil has started the new task is managing the room; a
teacher who moves on while half the class is still finishing the last thing is not.

### Assessing the curriculum

<Glossary term="Assessment">Assessment</Glossary> is the stage in which the teacher checks
whether the learning target has been met. The management decision here is **evidence**: a
teacher who ends the lesson without checking does not know whether to move on or reteach,
and a teacher who does not know is managing on hope.

Assessment does not have to be a test. A show of hands, an exit ticket, a pair share, a
question to a pupil who looked uncertain - each is a quick check that tells the teacher what
to do next. Marzano, Marzano and Pickering (2003) treat feedback as one of the most
powerful management tools available, because it tells the teacher whether the plan worked
and what to adjust.

The figure at the start of this topic shows a return arrow from "assess" back to "plan".
That arrow is the point: assessment is not the end of the cycle. It is the information the
teacher uses to plan the next lesson, and the management habit of closing that loop is what
separates a teacher who improves from a teacher who repeats.

## Activity: Trace a lesson through the cycle

Work in pairs. Take a lesson you have observed during practicum (or one from your own
schooling). Trace it through the four stages: what was the plan, how did the teacher
motivate, how did the teacher teach, and how did the teacher assess? For each stage, name
the management decision the teacher made. Allow 20 minutes. Then compare with another pair
and identify one stage where a different management decision would have changed the lesson.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the four stages of the plan-motivate-teach-assess cycle and the management decision
   at each stage. *(Remember)*
2. A teacher says, "I don't need a hook question; I just start teaching." Using this
   topic's account, explain what that approach costs in management terms. *(Analyze)*
3. Explain why the transition between activities is the moment most likely to produce
   disruption. *(Understand)*
4. A teacher ends every lesson with the same question: "Did you all understand?" Assess
   whether this counts as assessment in the sense this topic uses the term. *(Analyze)*
5. Give two examples from your own schooling of a motivation strategy and state what it
   cost in time or materials. *(Apply)*

## Summary

The plan-motivate-teach-assess cycle carries a teaching plan from intention to evidence.
The management decisions are: a clear target at the planning stage, relevance at the
motivation stage, pacing and participation at the teaching stage, and evidence at the
assessment stage. The return arrow from assess to plan closes the loop and is what makes
the cycle a habit rather than a formula.

## Try this at your practicum school

Over three lessons taught by the same teacher, keep a tally of the time spent on each of the
four stages. At the end, calculate the proportion and write half a page on which stage
dominates and whether that dominance fitted the content.

Do not name the school or the teacher in your write-up.

## Summative task

Write 350 to 400 words on the following.

> Describe a lesson you have observed (in practicum or your own schooling). Trace it

> stage you judge to have been the weakest, propose a specific improvement, and justify it
> by reference to the pupils or the content.
