# Unit 2 assessment

## Unit summary

Curriculum decisions are management acts. What a teacher chooses to teach, in what order, at what pace, and for whom shapes the classroom before the lesson begins: a well-chosen, well-sequenced, well-paced plan reduces the friction that produces disruption, while a plan that ignores the pupils in the room creates the conditions a teacher then has to manage reactively.

A teaching plan that is consistent with the teacher's own philosophy of learning produces a coherent, manageable classroom. The plan-motivate-teach-assess cycle then carries that plan from intention to evidence: the teacher sets a clear target, creates the want to learn, delivers the content through paced cycles of explanation and activity, and checks whether the target was met - then uses that information to plan the next lesson.

When one-size-fits-all is impossible, the teacher differentiates. Genuine differentiation adjusts the support, the context, or the complexity of a task so that every pupil reaches the same target; it does not lower the target. Multigrade and overcrowded classrooms impose specific constraints, and the tactics that work within those constraints - anchor activities, peer tutoring, structured routines, activity corners, whole-class anchors - all share one principle: the teacher cannot be everywhere, so parts of the room must be able to run on practised routines.

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. In this unit's account, "curriculum" refers to the teacher's answer to four questions. Which of the following is one of them? *(Remember)*
   a) How will I mark the homework?
   b) At what pace will I teach?
   c) Which textbook will I use?
   d) How many rules will I set?

2. A teacher sequences a lesson so that the foundation is taught before the hard idea that depends on it. This decision is a management act because it: *(Understand)*
   a) ensures the lesson is easier than the pupils expect
   b) reduces the disruption that comes from pupils losing the thread
   c) guarantees every pupil will understand the content
   d) removes the need for assessment at the end of the lesson

3. Wong and Wong (1998) argue that a well-managed classroom has a plan that is: *(Remember)*
   a) elaborate and detailed
   b) approved by the headteacher
   c) consistent and the teacher's own
   d) different every day to maintain interest

4. A teacher opens every lesson with a question that makes the pupils want to find out. In the four-stage cycle, this is the: *(Understand)*
   a) planning stage
   b) motivation stage
   c) teaching stage
   d) assessment stage

5. A teacher ends a lesson by asking three pupils to explain what they learned. This is an assessment move because it: *(Apply)*
   a) gives the teacher evidence about whether the target was met
   b) keeps the pupils quiet until the bell
   c) replaces the need for planning the next lesson
   d) counts those three pupils as the only ones who matter

6. Differentiation by "level" means: *(Remember)*
   a) giving struggling pupils easier work and strong pupils harder work
   b) adjusting the complexity of the task while keeping the learning target constant
   c) teaching the strong pupils first and the struggling pupils later
   d) grouping pupils by test score for the whole year

7. An anchor activity in a multigrade classroom is one that: *(Understand)*
   a) the teacher leads for the whole room at the same time
   b) one grade can do independently while the teacher works with another grade
   c) requires the teacher to explain it again for each grade
   d) only the strongest pupils in the room can complete

8. A teacher in a class of sixty decides to use a shared text as the single point of focus for the whole room. This is an example of: *(Apply)*
   a) an anchor activity for a multigrade room
   b) whole-class anchoring for an overcrowded room
   c) differentiation by interest
   d) the motivation stage of the cycle

9. Which statement best captures the relationship between a teacher's philosophy and their management, as Topic 2.1 presents it? *(Understand)*
   a) Philosophy matters only for experienced teachers
   b) A plan that contradicts the teacher's philosophy produces friction the teacher must then manage
   c) Philosophy is a private belief and has no effect on the classroom
   d) The textbook determines the philosophy, not the teacher

10. A teacher who says "an overcrowded class cannot be managed, only survived" is overlooking the argument that: *(Apply)*
    a) overcrowded classes should be split regardless of the cost
    b) survival is the only realistic goal in a large class
    c) specific tactics such as structured routines and whole-class anchors make a large class workable
    d) management is only possible with fewer than thirty pupils

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

1. Name the four curriculum decisions this unit identifies as management acts, and state the management purpose of any one of them. *(Remember)*

2. A teacher says, "I just follow the textbook, so I don't make curriculum decisions." Using Topic 2.1, explain what that claim overlooks. *(Understand)*

3. Explain why a fast pace can be a management failure even when the content is correct. *(Understand)*

4. Name the four stages of the plan-motivate-teach-assess cycle and the management decision at each stage. *(Remember)*

5. A teacher says, "I don't need a hook question; I just start teaching." Using Topic 2.2, explain what that approach costs in management terms. *(Analyze)*

6. Explain the difference between genuine differentiation and simply making some pupils do easier work, and give one example of each. *(Understand)*

7. A teacher gives an extension worksheet to the strong pupils and a simpler worksheet on a different topic to the struggling pupils. Assess whether this counts as differentiation in the sense Topic 2.3 uses the term. *(Analyze)*

8. Explain why an anchor activity is the core management tactic in a multigrade classroom, and describe one feature a good anchor activity must have. *(Understand)*

9. A teacher in a class of sixty says, "I cannot differentiate; there are too many children." Using one idea from Topic 2.3, propose a workable first step and explain why it is realistic at that scale. *(Apply)*

10. Analyze why a teacher who relies on an inconsistent plan from one lesson to the next is likely to spend more time managing disruption than a teacher whose plan is consistent. Refer to the philosophy-plan relationship Topic 2.1 describes. *(Analyze)*

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. A teacher believes that "learning is the pupil's active construction of meaning, not the teacher transferring knowledge." Analyze how that belief would shape the planning, motivation, teaching and assessment stages of the four-stage cycle, and argue which stage would change most if the teacher switched to a transmission belief. *(Analyze)*

2. Take the Class 4 mathematics objective "pupils will be able to add two-digit numbers with carrying." Plan this objective three ways: for a well-resourced single-grade class, for a multigrade class (Classes 2 to 4 in one room), and for a class of sixty. For each version, state one curriculum decision that changed because of the room and explain why. *(Analyze)*

3. A teacher in a multigrade classroom (Classes 3 and 4 in one room) says, "I just teach two lessons at once." Using Topic 2.3, evaluate that claim and propose a management plan that includes one anchor activity and one structured routine. Justify each by reference to the constraint a multigrade room imposes. *(Evaluate)*

4. Evaluate the claim that "differentiation is impossible in an overcrowded classroom." Use the differentiation framework from Topic 2.3, the tactics for overcrowded classrooms, and at least one concrete example of a task that could be differentiated for a class of sixty without lowering the target. *(Evaluate)*

5. **Integrative.** You are placed in a government primary school. A teacher in Class 5 (sixty pupils, single-grade, fixed benches) currently teaches the same task to every pupil and manages disruption mainly by reprimand.

   Design a plan for one lesson that does all three of the following: differentiates one objective by need, interest or level without lowering the target; includes one tactic that makes the overcrowded room workable; and explains how the plan shifts the teacher's balance from reactive discipline toward proactive management. Justify each choice by reference to the constraint the room imposes. *(Create)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### MCQ answer key

1. **b** - pacing ("at what pace will I teach?") is one of the four curriculum decisions; the other three are what to teach, in what order, and for whom.
2. **b** - sequencing that builds foundation before hard idea keeps pupils on thread and reduces the disruption that follows confusion.
3. **c** - Wong and Wong's test is that the plan is consistent and the teacher's own, not that it is elaborate or approved.
4. **b** - an opening question that creates the want to learn is the motivation stage.
5. **a** - asking pupils to explain gives the teacher evidence about whether the learning target was met.
6. **b** - differentiation by level adjusts complexity while keeping the target constant; (a) describes the misconception of lowering the target.
7. **b** - an anchor activity is a task one grade can do independently while the teacher works with another grade.
8. **b** - a shared text as a single point of focus for a whole class of sixty is whole-class anchoring for an overcrowded room.
9. **b** - Topic 2.1 argues that a plan contradicting the teacher's philosophy produces friction the teacher must then manage.
10. **c** - Topic 2.3 argues that specific tactics make a large class workable; the survival mindset is the real management failure.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

**3. (4 marks)** Explains that a fast pace leaves struggling pupils behind (1); links this to the management cost: lost pupils produce disruption (1); notes that a fast pace can also bore strong pupils (1); the explanation is grounded in Topic 2.1's account of pacing (1).

**4. (4 marks)** Names all four stages: plan, motivate, teach, assess (1); names the management decision at each: clear target, relevance, pacing and participation, evidence (1 each up to 3).

**5. (5 marks)** Identifies the missing stage as motivation (1); explains that the motivation stage creates the want to learn and sets the management tone for the lesson (1); argues that without it the teacher starts the lesson having to earn attention the pupils have not yet chosen to give (1); links this to Wong and Wong's finding about the first minutes (1); the reasoning is coherent (1).

**6. (4 marks)** Genuine differentiation adjusts support, context or complexity while keeping the target constant (1); making work easier lowers the target (1); the example of genuine differentiation keeps the target and changes the route (1); the example of easier work lowers the target (1).

**7. (5 marks)** States that this is not differentiation in Topic 2.3's sense (1); explains that the struggling pupils are given a different topic, which means a lower target, not a different route to the same target (1); contrasts this with what genuine differentiation would look like (same target, different support or complexity) (1); the reasoning draws on the differentiation framework (1); the reasoning is coherent (1).

**8. **9. (5 marks)** Proposes a realistic first step (e.g. whole-class anchor, structured routine, or one differentiated version of a single task) (1); explains why the step is realistic at scale (e.g. a whole-class anchor needs no individual attention) (1); links the step to Topic 2.3's argument that specific tactics make a large class workable (1); the proposal is concrete, not generic (1); the reasoning is coherent (1).

(5 marks)** Explains that a consistent plan lets pupils learn to rely on the pattern, which reduces the management demand of each transition (1); links this to Topic 2.1's argument that a plan contradicting the teacher's philosophy produces friction (1); argues that an inconsistent plan forces the teacher to re-establish expectations each lesson, which costs time and attention (1); the reasoning draws on the philosophy-plan relationship (1); the reasoning is coherent (1).

### ERQ rubrics

Each ERQ is marked out of 20 on the four criteria below. A response that does not reach "Adequate" on the analysis criterion cannot be awarded more than 10 overall, whatever its other merits.

**ERQ 1 - A constructivist belief across the four-stage cycle**

| Criterion | Limited (1-2) | Adequate (3) | Strong (4-5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grasp of the constructivist belief | Names the belief vaguely | Identifies active construction as the core belief | Shows what the belief implies for what the teacher does first and what the pupils do |
| Analysis of the four stages | Lists the four stages without linking them to the belief | Links at least two stages to the constructivist belief | Shows how the belief produces a coherent pattern across all four stages |
| **Argument about the stage that changes most (Analyze)** | **Asserts a stage without reasons** | **Argues for one stage with a reason tied to the belief** | **Argues for one stage and explains why the shift to transmission belief reshapes that stage more than the others** |
| Use of evidence | Generic | A concrete classroom example | The example is specific and genuinely illustrates the pattern |

**ERQ 2 - One objective planned three ways**

| Criterion | Limited (1-2) | Adequate (3) | Strong (4-5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coverage of the three settings | Fewer than three settings, or one is generic | All three settings addressed | The three settings are distinct and the constraint of each is visible |
| Quality of the curriculum decisions | Decisions are vague or unrelated to the room | At least one decision per setting is tied to the room | The decisions are specific and the reason for each is the constraint, not the mathematics |
| **Justification by constraint (Analyze)** | **Asserts a change without linking it to the room** | **Links each change to a feature of the room** | **Explains why the same objective demands a different decision because of the room** |
| Use of the objective | The objective is ignored or replaced | The objective is present in all three versions | The objective stays constant and the route changes, illustrating differentiation |

**ERQ 3 - A management plan for a multigrade room**

| Criterion | Limited (1-2) | Adequate (3) | Strong (4-5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grasp of the multigrade constraint | Describes the room but not the management problem | States that the teacher cannot work with both grades at once | Explains why the constraint is about independent work, not just large numbers |
| Quality of the proposed tactics | Tactics are vague or generic | An anchor activity and a routine are named | Both are specific, workable, and described in enough detail to recognise in a real room |
| **Justification by constraint (Evaluate)** | **Asserts the tactics work** | **Links each tactic to the multigrade constraint** | **Evaluates the "two lessons at once" claim and shows why the proposed plan is a better use of the teacher's time** |
| Realism | Ignores the constraints | Acknowledges them | The plan survives the constraints named: one teacher, two grades, one room |

**ERQ 4 - "Differentiation is impossible in an overcrowded classroom"**

| Criterion | Limited (1-2) | Adequate (3) | Strong (4-5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grasp of the differentiation framework | Names differentiation but not its axes | Names the three axes and the target-constant principle | Uses the framework to show what differentiation requires and what it does not require |
| Use of overcrowded-classroom tactics | Names a tactic without linking it to differentiation | Links at least one tactic to a differentiated task | Shows how a tactic (e.g. whole-class anchor, activity corners) makes differentiation possible at scale |
| **Evaluative reasoning (Evaluate)** | **Asserts a verdict** | **Argues a verdict with reasons** | **Takes the claim at its strongest, then shows what it leaves out using the framework and a concrete example** |
| Quality of example | Generic | A concrete task that could be differentiated | The example keeps the target constant and changes the route, and is realistic for sixty pupils |

| Criterion | Limited (1-2) | Adequate (3) | Strong (4-5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quality of the differentiation | Named but not applied | Applied to a specific task | The task keeps the target constant and the route changes by need, interest or level |
| Use of an overcrowded-classroom tactic | A tactic is named but not explained | A tactic is described and linked to the room | The tactic is realistic for sixty pupils on fixed benches |
