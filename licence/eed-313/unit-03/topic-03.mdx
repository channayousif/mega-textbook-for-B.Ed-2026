# Topic 3.3 - Subject-specific routines

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U3-3" src="/img/figures/eed-313/unit-03/fig-U3-3.svg" alt="Table showing one routine per subject with what it protects: mathematics has a materials routine and a checking routine, science has a safety routine and a cleanup routine, literacy has a reading routine and a response routine." width={780} height={470} />

Miss Nasreen teaches Class 5 in a government school in Lahore. On Monday morning she teaches
mathematics, then science, then Urdu literacy, each in a different part of the room. The pupils
know what to expect in each session: how to set out their mathematics materials, how to handle
science equipment, how to come to the reading corner. The routines are different for each subject,
and that is the point. This topic is about why subject-specific routines matter and what they
protect.

## Explanation

Topic 3.1 described routines as time-recovery tools. This topic adds a layer: different subjects
make different management demands, and a routine that works for one may not work for another. The
guide treats subject-specific routines as protections - each one guards against the particular
friction that subject creates.

### Routines for teaching mathematics

<Figure id="fig-U3-7" src="/img/figures/eed-313/unit-03/fig-U3-7.svg" alt="Diagram of a literacy-block layout: a reading corner with cushions, a shared-text area on the mat, a writing area with clipboards, and a response area, with arrows showing the flow between them." width={780} height={470} />

The figure above maps the routines this topic describes. Mathematics teaching carries two management
risks: materials (pencils, rulers, textbooks, exercise books) that take time to distribute and
collect, and checking (is my answer right?) that can produce a queue of pupils waiting for the
teacher while everyone else sits idle.

The **materials routine** governs how mathematics materials are distributed, used and packed away.
A well-designed materials routine uses a materials monitor, a predictable sequence (textbook first,
then exercise book, then pencil), and a time bound. Without it, the first five minutes of a
mathematics lesson are spent handing out books.

The **checking routine** governs how pupils get feedback without queuing. Self-checking (answers
on the board), peer-checking (swap books with a partner), and traffic-light cards (green = I am
confident, yellow = I am unsure, red = I need help) are all checking routines. Marzano, Marzano
and Pickering (2003) treat feedback as one of the most powerful management tools available, and a
checking routine is the mechanism that makes feedback happen at scale.

### Routines for teaching science

Science teaching carries two different risks: safety and cleanup. The **safety routine** governs
how equipment is handled, what pupils do before and after an investigation, and what happens when
something goes wrong. The **cleanup routine** governs how materials are returned, how the room is
restored, and how the transition out of science is managed. Evertson and Emmer (2009) stress that
the cleanup routine is the one most teachers of practical subjects neglect, and that a science
lesson without a cleanup routine ends with the teacher tidying up while the next lesson waits.

### Routines for teaching literacy

The figure above shows a literacy-block layout: a reading corner, a shared-text area, a writing
area and a response area, with the flow between them. The layout is the structure; the routines
are what move the pupils through it.

The **reading routine** governs how pupils come to the reading corner, how they select a text,
how they read (silently, in pairs, aloud to a partner), and how they record their reading. The
**response routine** governs how pupils respond to a text: a written response, a pair share, a
drawing, a discussion. Wong and Wong (1998) argue that the literacy block is the part of the day
where routines matter most, because reading and writing require sustained concentration and the
routines protect that concentration from interruption.

**Common misconception.** "Group work and collaborative learning are the same thing." They are
not. Group work is pupils sitting together; collaborative learning is pupils working together toward
a shared goal with individual accountability. A routine for group work manages seating and noise;
a routine for collaborative learning manages roles, task structure and closure. Topic 3.4 takes
this further.

## Activity: One routine, three subjects

Work in pairs. Take the routine "how pupils get help when they are stuck." Now redesign it three
ways: for a mathematics lesson, for a science investigation, and for a literacy session. For each,
decide: how the pupil signals, how the teacher responds, and what the pupil does while waiting.
Allow 25 minutes. Then present and identify one feature that changed because of the subject, not
because of the pupils.

## Check your understanding

1. Name the two management risks that a mathematics routine set typically addresses, and give one
   routine for each. *(Remember)*
2. A teacher says, "I do not need a cleanup routine; the pupils can just tidy up at the end." Using
   this topic's account, explain what that approach costs. *(Analyze)*
3. Describe the difference between a reading routine and a response routine in a literacy block.
   *(Understand)*
4. A science lesson ends with the teacher spending ten minutes cleaning up while the next class
   waits. Propose a routine that addresses the problem and explain why it is realistic in a
   low-resource classroom. *(Apply)*
5. Assess whether a checking routine designed for mathematics can be used unchanged in a literacy
   lesson. Refer to the kind of feedback each subject requires. *(Analyze)*

## Summary

Subject-specific routines protect against the particular friction each subject creates: materials
and checking in mathematics, safety and cleanup in science, reading and response in literacy. The
routines are different because the subjects are different, but the principle is the same: a
practised routine removes the need for the teacher to manage every moment.

## Try this at your practicum school

Over one week, observe one teacher across two different subjects. Note the routines the teacher
uses in each, and write half a page on which routines are the same across subjects and which are
different, and why.

Do not name the school or the teacher in your write-up.
