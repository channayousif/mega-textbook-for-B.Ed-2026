# Unit 3 assessment

## Unit summary

Routines are repeated ways of doing things that pupils learn to do automatically; structures hold
them; procedures guide specific tasks; rules set boundaries. Together they are the management
system that buys instructional time, especially at transition points where classrooms most often
leak it.

Multigrade and mixed-need classrooms require routines that are deliberately redesigned for the
constraint. The anchor routine and the independent-work routine are the two workhorses of a
multigrade room; routines for special needs provide the structure that allows a pupil to work
without constant teacher attention. Subject-specific routines protect against the friction each
subject creates: materials and checking in mathematics, safety and cleanup in science, reading and
response in literacy. Routines for co-operation and collaborative learning build the social skills
a well-managed classroom runs on, and the difference between the two - individual accountability -
is what makes group work into learning.

## Summative assessment

### Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)

1. In this unit's account, a "routine" is best described as: *(Remember)*
   a) a statement of what is allowed in the classroom
   b) a repeated way of doing something that pupils learn to do without being told each time
   c) a step-by-step sequence for a specific task
   d) the framework that holds classroom procedures

2. A teacher has pupils line up, then walk quietly to the canteen. This is an example of a:
   *(Understand)*
   a) rule
   b) routine
   c) procedure
   d) structure

3. Wong and Wong (1998) argue that the first weeks of term should be spent teaching: *(Remember)*
   a) rules, then procedures, then routines
   b) routines, then procedures, then rules
   c) the curriculum, then routines, then rules
   d) consequences, then routines, then procedures

4. A teacher signals the end of an activity, gives a time bound, and prompts the start of the next
   task. This is a management move that addresses: *(Understand)*
   a) the transition between activities
   b) the set-up of a collaborative task
   c) the independent-work routine
   d) the checking routine

5. In a multigrade classroom, the anchor routine is the one that: *(Remember)*
   a) the teacher uses to begin the lesson
   b) one grade follows while the teacher works with the other grade
   c) governs how pupils line up for the canteen
   d) the teacher uses to end the lesson

6. A pupil finishes every task early and then disrupts the class. The most appropriate routine
   response is: *(Apply)*
   a) a rule that forbids finishing early
   b) a procedure for what to do when finished
   c) a structure that keeps the pupil seated
   d) a routine that removes the early-finishing pupil from the room

7. Which pair correctly matches a subject with the management risk its routine set addresses?
   *(Understand)*
   a) Mathematics - safety and cleanup
   b) Science - materials and checking
   c) Literacy - reading and response
   d) Mathematics - reading and response

8. A teacher says, "I put my pupils in groups every lesson, so I use collaborative learning." This
   claim overlooks the importance of: *(Apply)*
   a) individual accountability within each group
   b) the number of pupils in each group
   c) the subject being taught
   d) the time of day

9. The three phases of a collaborative task are: *(Remember)*
   a) plan, teach, assess
   b) set up, run, close
   c) explain, practise, test
   d) open, work, hand in

10. Marzano, Marzano and Pickering (2003) found that the effectiveness of group work depends
    most on: *(Apply)*
    a) the structure chosen
    b) the size of the group
    c) the routines that carry the structure
    d) the subject being taught

### Restricted-response questions (RRQs)

1. State the difference between a routine and a rule, and give one example of each. *(Remember)*

2. A teacher says, "I told my class the routine, so now they know it." Using Topic 3.1, explain
   what that claim overlooks. *(Understand)*

3. Explain why the transition between activities is the moment most likely to leak instructional
   time. *(Understand)*

4. Name the two workhorse routines of a multigrade classroom and describe one feature each must
   have. *(Remember)*

5. A multigrade teacher gives the non-taught grade a worksheet and then interrupts the lesson
   repeatedly to manage behaviour. Using Topic 3.2, explain what routine is missing and why it
   matters. *(Analyze)*

6. Describe the difference between a reading routine and a response routine in a literacy block.
   *(Understand)*

7. A teacher always uses think-pair-share for group work. Assess whether this counts as
   collaborative learning in the sense Topic 3.4 uses the term. *(Analyze)*

8. Explain the difference between co-operation and collaborative learning, and give one example of
   each. *(Understand)*

9. A teacher in a class of sixty says, "I cannot do group work; it is too noisy." Using one idea
   from Topic 3.4, propose a workable first step and explain why it is realistic at that scale.
   *(Apply)*

10. Analyze why a teacher who relies on rules but neglects routines is likely to spend more time
    managing behaviour than a teacher who invests in routines. Refer to the time-recovery argument
    Topic 3.1 describes. *(Analyze)*

### Extended-response questions (ERQs)

1. A teacher believes that "routines make a classroom rigid." Analyze what that belief overlooks,
   using Topic 3.1's account of how routines recover instructional time, and argue which single
   routine would most improve the management of a described lesson. *(Analyze)*

2. Take the Class 4 classroom described in Topic 3.4 (groups of four, reader/writer/reporter/
   timekeeper). Analyze that room against the three phases of a collaborative task, and propose
   one routine for the phase you judge to be the weakest. Justify the choice by reference to the
   evidence. *(Analyze)*

3. A teacher in a multigrade classroom (Classes 3 and 4 in one room) says, "I just teach two
   lessons at once." Using Topic 3.2, evaluate that claim and propose a routine set that includes
   one anchor routine and one independent-work routine. Justify each by reference to the
   constraint a multigrade room imposes. *(Evaluate)*

4. Evaluate the claim that "group work is the same as collaborative learning." Use the distinction
   from Topic 3.4, the three-phase model, and at least one concrete example of a task that is
   group work but not collaborative learning. *(Evaluate)*

5. **Integrative.** You are placed in a government primary school. A teacher in a multigrade
   classroom (Classes 2 and 3, forty pupils, one room) currently manages mainly by rules and
   spends the day reacting to disruption.

   Design a routine set for one day that does all three of the following: includes at least one
   anchor routine for the multigrade constraint, includes at least one subject-specific routine,
   and explains how the routine set shifts the teacher's balance from reactive rule-enforcement
   toward proactive management. Justify each choice by reference to the constraint the room
   imposes. *(Create)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### MCQ answer key

1. **b** - a routine is a repeated way of doing something that pupils learn to do without being
   told each time.
2. **c** - lining up and walking to the canteen is a step-by-step sequence for a specific task, which
   is a procedure.
3. **b** - Wong and Wong's order is routines first, then procedures, then rules.
4. **a** - a signal, a time bound and a prompt are the three parts of a transition routine.
5. **b** - the anchor routine is the task one grade follows while the teacher works with the other
   grade.
6. **b** - a procedure for what to do when finished addresses the early-finishing problem.
7. **c** - literacy routines address reading and response; (a), (b) and (d) each pair a subject with
   a risk it does not carry.
8. **a** - collaborative learning requires individual accountability, not just seating pupils in
   groups.
9. **b** - the three phases are set up, run and close.
10. **c** - Marzano et al. found that the routines that carry the structure matter more than the
    structure itself.

### RRQ model answers and mark schemes

**1. (4 marks)** A routine is a repeated way of doing something pupils learn automatically (1); a rule
is a statement of what is allowed (1); the routine example is genuinely automatic (1); the rule
example is genuinely a statement of expectation (1).

**2. (4 marks)** Explains that a routine must be practised, not just announced (1); distinguishes
between knowing a routine and being able to do it without prompting (1); links this to Wong and
Wong's finding that the first weeks are spent teaching routines (1); the reasoning is coherent (1).

**3. (4 marks)** Explains that a transition involves ending one activity and starting another (1);
names the three parts: a signal, a time bound, a prompt (1); links the absence of any part to lost
time and disruption (1); the explanation is grounded in Topic 3.1 (1).

**4. (4 marks)** Names the anchor routine and the independent-work routine (1); describes one
feature of the anchor routine (self-checking, connected, familiar) (1); describes one feature of
the independent-work routine (how to get materials, ask for help, what to do when finished) (1);
the explanation is coherent (1).

**5. (5 marks)** Identifies the missing routine as the anchor routine or the independent-work
routine (1); explains that a worksheet without a routine is just a task sheet, not a management
system (1); links the missing routine to the repeated interruptions (1); argues that the teacher's
time is being spent on management rather than teaching (1); the reasoning is coherent (1).

**6. (4 marks)** The reading routine governs how pupils select, read and record a text (1); the
response routine governs how they respond to a text (1); the two routines protect different parts
of the literacy block (1); the explanation is coherent (1).

**7. (5 marks)** States that think-pair-share is a co-operative structure, not necessarily a
collaborative one (1); explains the difference: collaborative learning requires individual
accountability (1); assesses that think-pair-share can become collaborative if roles and
accountability are added (1); the reasoning draws on Topic 3.4 (1); the reasoning is coherent (1).

**8. (4 marks)** Co-operation means pupils work in the same space toward a shared task (1);
collaborative learning adds individual accountability (1); the co-operative example has pupils
helping each other (1); the collaborative example has distinct, necessary roles (1).

**9. (5 marks)** Proposes a realistic first step (e.g. think-pair-share, which is quiet and works
at scale) (1); explains why the step is realistic (think-pair-share needs no group furniture and
produces little noise) (1); links the step to Topic 3.4's argument that structure must match the
constraint (1); the proposal is concrete, not generic (1); the reasoning is coherent (1).

**10. (5 marks)** Explains that rules set boundaries but do not remove the need to manage every
moment (1); links this to the time-recovery argument: a rule without a routine produces repeated
enforcement (1); argues that a teacher with practised routines spends less time on behaviour and
more on teaching (1); the reasoning draws on Topic 3.1 (1); the reasoning is coherent (1).

### ERQ rubrics

Each ERQ is marked out of 20 on the four criteria below.

**ERQ 1 - "Routines make a classroom rigid"**

| Criterion | Limited (1-2) | Adequate (3) | Strong (4-5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grasp of the routine/rule distinction | Names the distinction vaguely | Distinguishes routines from rules | Shows what routines make possible that rules alone do not |
| Analysis of the rigidity claim | Asserts the claim is wrong | Explains what the claim overlooks | Takes the claim at its strongest, then shows what it leaves out using the time-recovery argument |
| **Argument for the single routine (Analyze)** | **Asserts a routine without reasons** | **Argues for one routine with a reason** | **Argues for one routine and explains why it would recover more time than the alternatives** |
| Use of the described lesson | Generic classroom | Refers to the description | The evidence is drawn from specific details in the description |

**ERQ 2 - Analysing the Class 4 room**

| Criterion | Limited (1-2) | Adequate (3) | Strong (4-5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coverage of the three phases | Fewer than two phases analysed | Two phases analysed with evidence | All three phases analysed with evidence from the described room |
| Quality of the proposed routine | Vague or unrelated to the phase | Specific and phase-appropriate | Specific, and the choice of phase is justified |
| **Justification by evidence (Analyze)** | **Asserts a phase is weak** | **Links the weakness to a feature of the lesson** | **Links the weakness to a feature and explains why the current routine does not address it** |
| Use of the described room | Generic classroom | Refers to the description | The evidence is drawn from specific details in the description |

**ERQ 3 - A routine set for a multigrade room**

| Criterion | Limited (1-2) | Adequate (3) | Strong (4-5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grasp of the multigrade constraint | Describes the room but not the routine problem | States that the teacher cannot work with both grades at once | Explains why the constraint is about independent work, not just large numbers |
| Quality of the proposed routines | Routines are vague or generic | An anchor routine and an independent-work routine are named | Both are specific, workable, and described in enough detail to recognise in a real room |
| **Justification by constraint (Evaluate)** | **Asserts the routines work** | **Links each routine to the multigrade constraint** | **Evaluates the "two lessons at once" claim and shows why the proposed set is a better use of the teacher's time** |
| Realism | Ignores the constraints | Acknowledges them | The plan survives the constraints named: one teacher, two grades, one room |

**ERQ 4 - "Group work is the same as collaborative learning"**

| Criterion | Limited (1-2) | Adequate (3) | Strong (4-5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grasp of the distinction | Conflates the two | Distinguishes co-operation from collaborative learning | Shows what each is for and what each cannot do |
| Use of the three-phase model | Names the phases without linking them to the distinction | Links at least one phase to the distinction | Shows how the set-up and closure routines are what make individual accountability real |
| **Evaluative reasoning (Evaluate)** | **Asserts a verdict** | **Argues a verdict with reasons** | **Takes the claim at its strongest, then shows what it leaves out using the distinction and a concrete example** |
| Quality of example | Generic | A task that is group work but not collaborative | The example genuinely illustrates the difference, not just one side of it |

**ERQ 5 - Integrative: a routine set for a multigrade room**

| Criterion | Limited (1-2) | Adequate (3) | Strong (4-5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quality of the anchor routine | Named but not applied | Applied to a specific part of the day | The routine is specific, self-checking and connected to the current topic |
| Use of a subject-specific routine | A routine is named but not linked to a subject | A routine is described and linked to a subject | The routine is realistic for the described constraints |
| **Shift from reactive to proactive (Create)** | **Asserts the plan is proactive** | **Shows at least one proactive move** | **Explains how the routine set reduces the need for rule-enforcement by building the conditions in which the wanted behaviour is more likely** |
| Justification and realism | Ignores the constraints | Acknowledges them | The plan survives the constraints named: two grades, forty pupils, one room |
