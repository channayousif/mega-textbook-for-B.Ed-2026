# Unit 3 teacher notes

## Sequencing note

The four topics follow the logic of a single week. Topic 3.1 asks what routines are and why they
buy time; Topic 3.2 asks how to adapt them when the room makes one-size-fits-all impossible;
Topic 3.3 asks what subject-specific routines look like; Topic 3.4 asks how to design routines that
build co-operation and collaborative learning.

A useful session order is to teach the topics in numerical order across the three weeks the guide
allows (Weeks 9 to 11), using the first session of the unit to time a real transition and the last
to rehearse the integrative assessment. The unit is designed so that a tutor who needs to compress
it can merge the activity time of Topics 3.1 and 3.2 into a single routine-design workshop without
losing the conceptual spine.

## Common misconceptions to probe

These are the misconceptions the unit names explicitly. Surface them early and return to them;
trainees often assent to the correct version in a discussion and then revert when they plan.

1. **"Routines make a classroom rigid."** This is the most persistent one. Probe it by asking:
   "If the routine removes the need to negotiate every transition, what does the teacher gain?"
   Trainees who equate flexibility with absence of structure will find this counter-intuitive. The
   figure in Topic 3.1 (routine, structure, procedure, rule distinguished) is designed to make the
   distinction visible; use it as a poster or handout.

2. **"Routines are for young children only."** Probe it by asking: "What routine do you use every
   morning before you come to university?" The content of the routine changes with age; the
   principle does not.

3. **"Group work and collaborative learning are the same thing."** This one belongs to Topic 3.4.
   Probe it by asking: "If four pupils sit together and one does all the work, is that
   collaboration?" The answer is no, and the reason is the absence of individual accountability.

## Managing the activities

**Topic 3.1 - Time one transition (practicum-based).** The key teaching move is the arithmetic:
ask trainees to bring their timed data and calculate the minutes recovered across a week. The
number is the argument. If trainees cannot produce data, use the worked example in the figure
(a week's recovered minutes, transition by transition) and ask them to verify the calculation.

**Topic 3.2 - Design an anchor routine (25 minutes).** The trap is that trainees produce a task
description rather than a routine. The difference: a task description says what pupils do; a
routine says how they start, what they do when stuck, how they check, and what they do when
finished. The debrief question - "identify one moment where a pupil might drift from working to
just busy" - is designed to catch this.

**Topic 3.3 - One routine, three subjects (25 minutes).** Pairs work well here because the task is
a structured redesign, not a design from scratch. The key teaching move is the debrief: "What
changed because of the subject, not because of the pupils?" If trainees answer "the difficulty",
push them back: the routine protects against a management risk, not a cognitive one.

**Topic 3.4 - Design a collaborative routine (25 minutes).** This is the most cognitively
demanding activity. The trap is that trainees produce roles without accountability. The debrief
question - "identify one moment where a group might slip from collaborative to one person doing
the work" - is designed to catch this. If trainees cannot answer it, they have assigned roles but
not made them necessary.

## Practical work and practicum links

Each topic carries a "Try this at your practicum school" task. The unit works best when the tutor
sets these up explicitly before trainees leave for practicum and debriefs them when trainees
return.

- **Topic 3.1 practicum task** (timing a transition) produces a simple data set. Use it to open
  Topic 3.2: ask trainees which transition lost the most time, and use their examples to motivate
  the anchor routine.

- **Topic 3.2 practicum task** (observe a multigrade room) produces the evidence trainees need
  for ERQ 3. If the practicum school has no multigrade room, ask trainees to observe a single-grade
  room and note the routines that would stop working if a second grade were added.

- **Topic 3.3 practicum task** (observe one teacher across two subjects) produces a comparison.
  Use it to make the point that subject-specific routines are not arbitrary: they protect against
  the friction each subject creates.

- **Topic 3.4 practicum task** (tally the time across the three phases) produces a simple data
  set. Use it to make the point that most group work spends too long on set-up and too little on
  closure, and that the close routine is where the learning transfers to the whole class.

## A note on the figures

The unit carries eight figures: two concept-map figures that make a conceptual structure visible
(fig-U3-1, fig-U3-7), two flowchart figures that sequence a process (fig-U3-4, and the
collaboration cycle), and four tables that compare options against a criterion (fig-U3-3, fig-U3-5,
fig-U3-6, fig-U3-8). The tables are the ones trainees most often try to read as prose. Before you
project any of them, ask trainees to read the column headers first and say what the table is
comparing.

## Assessment note

The 10/10/5 bank is designed to be used in two passes: the MCQs and RRQs as formative checks at
the end of Topics 3.2 and 3.4, and the ERQs as a summative task at the end of the unit. ERQ 5 (the
integrative item) is the one that most reliably separates trainees who have understood the unit
from those who have memorised its definitions. If you use only one ERQ, use that one.
