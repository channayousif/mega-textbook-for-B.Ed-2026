# Topic 3.4 - Routines for co-operation and collaboration

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U3-4" src="/img/figures/eed-313/unit-03/fig-U3-4.svg" alt="Flowchart showing how a collaborative task is set up, run and closed: assign roles, set the task, monitor participation, close with a whole-class share, and reflect on how the group worked." width={780} height={470} />

Mr Sajid teaches Class 4 social studies in a government school in Quetta. His pupils work in
groups of four. Each group has a reader, a writer, a reporter and a timekeeper. The pupils know
what each role does, how to begin the task, how to check that everyone is contributing, and how
to close the activity with a share. This topic is about the routines that make group work into
collaborative learning, and about the difference between the two.

## Explanation

Topic 3.3 ended with a distinction: group work is pupils sitting together; collaborative learning
is pupils working together toward a shared goal with individual accountability. This topic takes
that distinction seriously and asks what routines are needed to make it real.

### Co-operation and collaborative learning distinguished

<Figure id="fig-U3-8" src="/img/figures/eed-313/unit-03/fig-U3-8.svg" alt="Table comparing co-operation structures against group size and noise cost: think-pair-share, round-robin, jigsaw, and numbered heads, each matched to a group size and a noise level." width={780} height={470} />

The figure above maps the structures this topic describes. The distinction matters because the
routines for each are different.

**Co-operation** means pupils work in the same space, toward a shared task, helping each other as
needed. The routine manages seating, noise and mutual help. Think-pair-share, round-robin and
numbered heads are co-operative structures.

**Collaborative learning** adds individual accountability: every pupil has a role, every role is
necessary, and the group cannot succeed unless every member contributes. The routine manages roles,
task structure, participation checking and closure. The jigsaw method is a collaborative
structure.

**Common misconception.** "Group work and collaborative learning are the same thing." They are
not. Group work without individual accountability produces one pupil doing the work while the
others watch. Collaborative learning requires a routine that makes every role necessary and every
contribution visible.

### The collaborative routine: set up, run, close

The figure above shows the three phases of a collaborative task. Each phase has its own routine.

The **set-up routine** assigns roles, states the task, checks that every pupil understands their
role, and sets a time bound. A strong set-up routine uses a roles card (reader, writer, reporter,
timekeeper) and a quick check: "Readers, point to your text. Writers, show your clipboard."
Without this routine, the first five minutes of group work are spent negotiating who does what.

The **run routine** governs how the group works: how to take turns, how to check that everyone is
contributing, what to do when the group is stuck. The teacher's role during the run phase is to
monitor participation, not to teach content - a group that is off task needs a management
response, not a content explanation. Evertson and Emmer (2009) stress that the run phase is where
most collaborative learning fails, because the teacher confuses "pupils are talking" with "pupils
are learning" and does not check that every role is being filled.

The **close routine** governs how the activity ends: a whole-class share, a reflection on how the
group worked, a pack-away sequence. A strong close routine includes a reflection question: "What
did your group do well? What would you change next time?" Without it, the learning stays in the
group and does not transfer to the whole class.

### Matching structure to group size and noise cost

The figure above maps four co-operative structures against the group size and noise cost each one
carries. Think-pair-share is quiet and works in any size of class; the jigsaw is louder and needs
groups of four to six; round-robin is structured and works when the task has a clear sequence.
The management question is: given this class, this content, and the noise I can afford, which
structure gives me the most collaboration for the least management cost?

Marzano, Marzano and Pickering (2003) found that the effectiveness of group work depends less on
the structure chosen and more on the routines that carry it. A think-pair-share with a practised
routine produces more learning than a jigsaw with a routine that has not been taught.

## Activity: Design a collaborative routine

Work in groups of four. Take the following task:

> Class 5 science: pupils will investigate which material conducts electricity, in groups of four.

Now design the full routine: the set-up (roles, task statement, time bound), the run (how to check
participation, what to do when stuck), and the close (share, reflection, pack-away). Allow 25
minutes. Then present your routine and identify one moment where a group might slip from
"collaborative" to "one person doing the work", and explain how your routine prevents it.

## Summary

Co-operation and collaborative learning are different, and the routines for each are different. A
collaborative task has three phases - set up, run and close - and each phase needs its own
routine. The effectiveness of group work depends less on the structure chosen and more on the
routines that carry it.

Over two lessons that use group work, keep a tally of the time spent on each phase: set up, run
and close. At the end, calculate the proportion and write half a page on which phase dominates
and whether that dominance fitted the task.

Do not name the school or the teacher in your write-up.
