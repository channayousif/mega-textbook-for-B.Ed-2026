# Topic 3.1 - What routines are, and the time they buy

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U3-1" src="/img/figures/eed-313/unit-03/fig-U3-1.svg" alt="Concept map distinguishing routine, structure, procedure and rule: a routine is a repeated way of doing something, a structure is the framework that holds routines, a procedure is a step-by-step sequence, and a rule is a statement of what is allowed." width={780} height={470} />

Miss Tahira teaches Class 3 in a government school in Hyderabad. Her lessons run on time. Her
pupils know what to do when they enter the room, how to get their books out, what to do when they
finish early, and how to hand in work. None of this is accidental. It is the result of a small set
of practised routines that Miss Tahira taught in the first week of term and reinforced until they
became automatic. This topic is about what those routines are, how they differ from rules and
procedures, and why they are one of the most powerful time-management tools a teacher has.

## Explanation

The guide treats routines not as a list of tips but as a management system that buys instructional
time. A classroom without routines spends the day negotiating every transition: how to start, how
to move between activities, how to finish. A classroom with practised routines spends that time on
learning instead.

### Routines, structures, procedures and rules distinguished

The figure above maps four terms that are often used interchangeably but mean different things.
Confusing them produces confusion about what to teach and what to enforce.

- A **routine** is a repeated way of doing something that pupils learn to do without being told
  each time. "When the bell rings, we put our books away and stand quietly" is a routine once it
  is practised.
- A **structure** is the framework that holds routines. The timetable is a structure; the seating
  arrangement is a structure; the way the room is organised for group work is a structure.
- A **procedure** is a step-by-step sequence for a specific task. How to sharpen a pencil, how to
  line up for the canteen, how to hand in homework - each is a procedure.
- A **rule** is a statement of what is allowed or expected. "We raise our hands before speaking"
  is a rule.

Wong and Wong (1998) argue that the first weeks of term are the time to teach routines, procedures
and rules - in that order, because routines make the room run, procedures make specific tasks
manageable, and rules set the boundaries. A teacher who starts with rules and never teaches the
routines behind them spends the term enforcing compliance instead of teaching.

### How routines buy instructional time

<Figure id="fig-U3-5" src="/img/figures/eed-313/unit-03/fig-U3-5.svg" alt="Table showing a week's recovered minutes, transition by transition: before a routine and after, for handing out materials, moving between activities, and settling at the start of a lesson." width={780} height={470} />

The figure above shows the arithmetic of a routine. The guide's worked example asks you to time one
transition before and after a routine is introduced, and count the minutes recovered across a week.
The numbers are striking even for a single transition.

Consider the transition between activities. Without a routine, the teacher gives instructions,
waits for the noise to settle, repeats the instructions to the pupils who were not listening,
deals with the two pupils who have started the wrong task, and finally begins. The same transition
with a practised routine takes half the time: the teacher gives a signal, pupils move, the new
activity starts. Marzano, Marzano and Pickering (2003) found that the average classroom loses
significant instructional time to poorly managed transitions, and that a small set of practised
routines recovers more time than almost any other management investment.

The key word is **practised**. A routine that has been announced but not rehearsed is just a rule.
A routine that has been practised until it is automatic is a time machine.

**Common misconception.** "Routines make a classroom rigid." They do the opposite. A routine
removes the need to negotiate every transition, which frees the teacher to be flexible about
content, pacing and response. Rigidity is what happens when there are no routines and the teacher
has to control every move by force.

### The transition as the critical moment

Evertson and Emmer (2009) identify the transition between activities as the moment most likely
to produce disruption. A well-managed transition has three parts: a clear signal that the current
activity is ending, a bounded time for the move, and a prompt that starts the next activity before
the pupils have time to drift. A poorly managed transition has none of these, and the teacher
spends the first minutes of the next activity re-establishing order instead of teaching.

## Activity: Time one transition

Over two lessons taught by the same teacher, pick one transition (moving between activities,
handing out materials, settling at the start). Time it in seconds on two occasions. Then work
with the teacher to introduce a simple routine for that transition, and time it again on the next
two occasions. Calculate the minutes recovered across a typical five-day week and write one
paragraph on where that time would otherwise have gone.

5. For a described classroom that loses time at the start of every lesson, propose one specific
   routine and explain how it would recover time. *(Apply)*

## Summary

Routines, structures, procedures and rules are distinct management tools. Routines are repeated
ways of doing things that pupils learn to do automatically; structures hold them; procedures guide
specific tasks; rules set boundaries. Practised routines recover instructional time, especially at
transition points, and are the foundation on which a well-managed classroom runs.

## Try this at your practicum school

Over one week, keep a tally of the time lost to one transition across five lessons. Then work with
the teacher to design a routine for that transition and tally the time again the following week.
Write half a page on the difference and what it tells you about the relationship between routines
and instructional time.

Do not name the school or the teacher in your write-up.
