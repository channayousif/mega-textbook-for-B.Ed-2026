# Topic 3.2 - Routines in multigrade and mixed-need classes

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U3-2" src="/img/figures/eed-313/unit-03/fig-U3-2.svg" alt="Diagram of a multigrade timetable showing which group is with the teacher when: Group A works independently while the teacher teaches Group B, then they swap, with a whole-class anchor at the start and end." width={780} height={470} />

Mr Karim teaches Classes 2 and 4 in one room in a government school in Sanghar. He has one
blackboard, forty-two pupils, and no assistant. When he works with Class 4 mathematics, Class 2
must be able to work without him. When he moves to Class 2, Class 4 must not sit idle. This topic
is about the routines that make that possible.

## Explanation

Topic 3.1 described routines as time-recovery tools. This topic asks what happens when the room
makes a one-routine-fits-all approach impossible: two grades in the same room, or pupils with
different needs in the same class. The answer is not to abandon routines but to redesign them for
the constraint.

### Routines in the multigrade classroom

<Figure id="fig-U3-6" src="/img/figures/eed-313/unit-03/fig-U3-6.svg" alt="Table matching routines to specific special needs: a routine for a pupil who needs extra time, a routine for a pupil who is easily distracted, a routine for a pupil who finishes early, and a routine for a pupil who needs help but will not ask." width={780} height={470} />

The figure above maps the routines this topic describes. The two workhorses of a multigrade
classroom are the **anchor routine** and the **independent-work routine**.

The **anchor routine** is the task one grade does while the teacher works with the other. Topic
2.3 introduced the anchor activity; this topic adds the routine that makes it work: how pupils
start the anchor without being told, what they do when they are stuck, how they check their own
work, and what they do when they finish. A good anchor routine is self-checking, connected to the
current topic, and familiar enough that the teacher is not interrupted to explain it. Without the
routine, the anchor activity is just a task sheet; with the routine, it is a management system that
runs itself.

The **independent-work routine** governs what pupils do when they are working alone: how to get
materials, how to ask for help without calling out, what to do when finished. Evertson and Emmer
(2009) stress that the independent-work routine is the one most multigrade teachers neglect,
because it is easier to keep the pupils busy than to teach them to work independently. But a busy
pupil who is not learning is just a quiet disruption, and the teacher's time is still being spent
on management rather than teaching.

Vincent (The Multigrade Classroom: Book 3) describes the multigrade teacher's routine set as a
small, practised collection: how to signal a grade change, how to start an anchor, how to end a
peer-tutoring pair, how to close the lesson so both grades stop at the same time. The routines are
few, but they must be taught and rehearsed as deliberately as in a single-grade classroom.

### Routines for special needs and situations

The figure above also maps routines matched to specific special needs. The guide treats these as
routine adaptations, not as separate management systems, because the underlying principle is the
same: the pupil who needs something different needs a routine that provides it without requiring
the teacher's constant attention.

- A pupil who needs **extra time** needs a routine for what to do when the rest of the class moves
  on: a continuation task, a self-checking extension, a way to signal "I am not finished yet"
  without disruption.
- A pupil who is **easily distracted** needs a routine for where to sit, how to use a timer, how
  to break a task into smaller steps - the routine provides the structure that the pupil cannot
  yet provide alone.
- A pupil who **finishes early** needs a routine for what to do next: a reading corner, a peer-tutoring
  role, a self-checking extension. Without this routine, the early finisher becomes the source of
  disruption.
- A pupil who **needs help but will not ask** needs a routine for how to signal for assistance: a
  coloured card on the desk, a place in the question queue, a check-in point with the teacher at
  a predictable moment.

**Common misconception.** "Routines are for young children only." They are for every age. The
content of the routine changes - older pupils do not need a routine for how to line up - but the
principle does not. A well-designed routine frees the teacher to teach, and that is as valuable in
a lower-secondary classroom as in a primary one.

## Activity: Design an anchor routine

Work in groups of three. Take the following scenario:

> Class 2 and Class 4 share one room. The teacher is working with Class 4 on two-digit addition.
> Class 2 is working independently on a reading task.

Now design the anchor routine: how Class 2 starts the task, what they do when they are stuck, how
they check their work, and what they do when they finish. Allow 25 minutes. Then present your
routine and identify one moment where a pupil might drift from "working" to "just busy", and
explain how your routine prevents it.

## Summary

Multigrade and mixed-need classrooms require routines that are deliberately redesigned for the
constraint. The anchor routine and the independent-work routine are the two workhorses of a
multigrade room; routines for special needs provide the structure that allows a pupil to work
without constant teacher attention. In each case the principle is the same: the routine removes
the need for the teacher to manage every moment.

## Try this at your practicum school

Identify one multigrade classroom. Over two lessons, note the routines the teacher uses when one
group is working independently, and write half a page on which routine makes the most difference
to the teacher's ability to teach the other group.

Do not name the school or the teacher in your write-up.
