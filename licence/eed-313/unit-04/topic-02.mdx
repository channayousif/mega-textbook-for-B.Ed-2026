# Topic 4.2 - Bringing the community in

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U4-2" src="/img/figures/eed-313/unit-04/fig-U4-2.svg" alt="Flowchart showing how a community member is invited into a lesson: identify a need, find a community member, make first contact, plan the visit together, host the visit, follow up with thanks and feedback." width={780} height={470} />

Mr Hassan teaches Class 5 in a government school in Sukkur. A local nurse visits his class every
month to talk about hygiene. A parent who is a carpenter helps with the science fair. The imam
comes during the Islamiat unit. None of this is accidental. Each visit follows a routine: the
teacher plans with the visitor beforehand, the pupils prepare questions, and the visit ends with
a thank-you and a follow-up activity. This topic is about the routines that make community
involvement work, and about how those routines differ in a multigrade classroom.

## Explanation

Topic 4.1 distinguished participation from involvement. This topic asks how to move from one to
the other: what routines and structures turn a visitor into a genuine contributor, and what changes
when the room holds two grades.

### Inviting a community member into a lesson

<Figure id="fig-U4-6" src="/img/figures/eed-313/unit-04/fig-U4-6.svg" alt="Table matching community roles to what each can realistically contribute: a health worker with hygiene lessons, a carpenter with design and measurement, a parent with career talk, a farmer with a crops lesson." width={780} height={470} />

The figure above maps the process. The guide treats community involvement as a routine with six
phases, not a one-off event.

1. **Identify a need.** The starting point is the curriculum, not the visitor. A teacher who asks,
   "Who could help with this topic?" before "Who do I know?" is more likely to produce a visit
   that serves the learning.
2. **Find a community member.** The figure above maps community roles to what each can realistically
   contribute. A health worker can teach hygiene; a carpenter can support design and measurement;
   a farmer can talk about crops. The match between the person and the topic is what makes the visit
   work.
3. **Make first contact.** The invitation is specific: what the class is learning, what the visitor
   would do, how long it would take, what the pupils will have prepared. A vague invitation
   produces a vague visit.
4. **Plan the visit together.** The teacher and visitor plan the session: what the visitor will do,
   what the pupils will do, what materials are needed. Evertson and Emmer (2009) stress that a
   visitor who is left to "just talk" is a visitor who will talk for too long and lose the class.
5. **Host the visit.** The teacher introduces the visitor, sets the expectations, and stays in the
   room. A teacher who leaves the room during a visitor's session is not hosting; the pupils will
   read the absence as a signal that the visit does not matter.
6. **Follow up.** A thank-you note, a follow-up activity, a reflection in the next lesson. Without
   follow-up, the visit is an event; with follow-up, it becomes part of the learning.

### Community involvement in multigrade classrooms

The figure above maps the community roles. Community involvement in a multigrade classroom is both
harder and more valuable. It is harder because the teacher is already managing two grades; adding
a visitor adds complexity. It is more valuable because a community member can work with one grade
while the teacher works with the other, effectively doubling the adult presence in the room.

Vincent (The Multigrade Classroom: Book 3) describes the community member as a potential anchor:
a parent or local professional who can run a session with one grade while the teacher teaches the
other. The management demand is the same as for any anchor: the community member needs a clear
role, a prepared task, and a practised routine for starting and finishing.

**Common misconception.** "An ethic of care means never correcting a child." It does not. An ethic
of care means correcting a child in a way that preserves dignity and builds accountability. Topic
4.3 takes this further.

## Activity: Plan a community visit

Work in groups of three. Take the following scenario:

> Class 4 science: pupils will learn about the water cycle. You want to invite a community member
> to contribute to the lesson.

Now plan the full process: the need, the community member you would invite, the first-contact
message, the plan for the visit, how you would host it, and the follow-up. Allow 25 minutes.
Then present your plan and identify one moment where the visit could become a performance rather
than a learning experience, and explain how your plan prevents it.

## Summary

Community involvement is a routine with six phases: identify a need, find a community member, make
first contact, plan the visit, host it, and follow up. In a multigrade classroom, a community
member can serve as an anchor, working with one grade while the teacher teaches the other. The
match between the person and the topic is what makes the visit work.
