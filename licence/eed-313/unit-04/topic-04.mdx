# Topic 4.4 - Accountability, and when things break down

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U4-4" src="/img/figures/eed-313/unit-04/fig-U4-4.svg" alt="Flowchart showing a behaviour breakdown handled through a caring, accountable response: stop the behaviour calmly, check for harm, hear both sides, agree a repair, follow up, and restore the relationship." width={780} height={470} />

Mr Tariq teaches Class 5 in a government school in Hyderabad. During group work, Bilal hits
Hamid. The class stops. Mr Tariq does not shout. He stops the behaviour, checks that Hamid is all
right, hears both boys, and asks Bilal, "What will you do to make this right?" Bilal apologises
and helps Hamid with the task. The lesson continues. This topic is about what makes that response
work, and about what happens when the unexpected disrupts even the best-managed classroom.

## Explanation

Topic 4.3 described an ethic of care. This topic asks what happens when that ethic is tested: when
behaviour breaks down, when the unexpected happens, when a teacher is alone in a room with a problem
and no script. The management question is: how do you respond in a way that is both caring and
accountable?

### Building responsible action and personal accountability

<Figure id="fig-U4-8" src="/img/figures/eed-313/unit-04/fig-U4-8.svg" alt="Diagram showing an unexpected-event decision aid for a teacher alone in a classroom: a flow chart with three branches - safety first, then learning, then relationship." width={780} height={470} />

The figure above maps the decision aid this topic describes. The guide treats personal accountability
not as a trait but as a skill that a caring classroom builds through routine.

**Responsible action** is the habit of acting with awareness of the consequences for others. A pupil
who has learned to act responsibly does not need to be told to help a classmate; the routine of the
classroom has made helping normal. Henley (2009) argues that responsible action is built through
three moves: giving pupils real responsibility (not just tasks), making the consequences of
action visible, and reflecting on what happened afterwards.

**Personal accountability** is the willingness to own the consequences of your actions. A pupil who
has learned accountability does not blame others, make excuses, or wait to be caught; they say, "I
did that, and I will make it right." The guide treats accountability as the internalised version of
discipline: discipline is external (the teacher enforces), accountability is internal (the pupil
owns).

The figure above shows how a caring classroom builds accountability: through routines that make
expectations clear, through consequences that are restorative rather than punitive, and through
reflection that connects the action to its impact on others.

### What happens when behaviour breaks down

The figure above maps the response. The guide treats a behaviour breakdown as a moment that tests
the classroom's culture, not just the teacher's authority. A punitive response stops the behaviour
but damages the relationship; a caring, accountable response stops the behaviour and rebuilds it.

The flowchart in the figure shows the six steps:

1. **Stop the behaviour calmly.** A teacher who shouts stops the behaviour but models the loss of
   control. A teacher who stops calmly models the self-regulation they are teaching.
2. **Check for harm.** The first question is not "who started it" but "is anyone hurt." This sends
   the message that safety matters more than blame.
3. **Hear both sides.** Each pupil gets to tell their version without interruption. This sends the
   message that every voice matters, even when the behaviour was wrong.
4. **Agree a repair.** The pupil who caused the harm helps decide how to make it right. This is the
   accountability step: the pupil owns the consequence.
5. **Follow up.** The teacher checks later that the repair happened and the relationship is
   restored. Without follow-up, the repair is a transaction; with follow-up, it is a learning.
6. **Restore the relationship.** The teacher makes sure the two pupils are not left in conflict.
   A classroom where unresolved conflict lingers is a classroom where learning suffers.

### Dealing with unexpected events

The figure above maps the decision aid. The unexpected is, by definition, not covered by a routine:
a pupil who becomes ill, a visitor who arrives unannounced, a power cut, a fight in the corridor.
The guide treats the unexpected as a moment when the teacher's management system is most visible,
because there is no script to follow.

The decision aid has three branches, in priority order:

1. **Safety first.** Is anyone in danger? If yes, act immediately and calmly. The management
   principle is: a safe classroom is the precondition for everything else.
2. **Learning second.** Can the lesson continue? If yes, adapt and continue. If not, switch to a
   backup activity. The management principle is: a disrupted lesson is not a failed lesson if the
   teacher adapts.
3. **Relationship third.** What does this incident tell you about the classroom's culture? A pupil
   who becomes ill and is cared for by classmates is a classroom where care is real. A power cut
   that produces panic is a classroom where routines are not yet automatic.

**Common misconception.** "An ethic of care means never correcting a child." It does not. An ethic
of care means correcting a child in a way that preserves dignity and builds accountability. The
figure above shows the difference: a punitive response says "you are bad"; a caring, accountable
response says "what you did was not acceptable, and you can make it right."

## Activity: Evaluate a response

Work in groups of three. Read the following two responses to the same incident. Then evaluate each
using the six-step model, and write a verdict on which is more effective and why.

> **Incident:** During a group task, Ali refuses to do his share of the work and pushes the task
> away. The group stops working.

> **Response A:** The teacher says, "Ali, go and stand outside the classroom until you are ready to
> behave." Ali leaves. The group continues.

> **Response B:** The teacher stops the class briefly, asks Ali to step aside, checks that the
> other group members are all right, hears Ali's side, and asks, "What will you do to make this
> right?" Ali rejoins the group and contributes.

Allow 20 minutes. Then present your evaluation and identify one moment where the two responses
diverge, and explain what message each sends to the class.

## Summary

A caring classroom builds personal accountability through routines that make expectations clear,
consequences that are restorative, and reflection that connects action to impact. When behaviour
breaks down, a caring, accountable response follows six steps: stop calmly, check for harm, hear
both sides, agree a repair, follow up, and restore the relationship. When the unexpected happens,
the priority is safety, then learning, then relationship.

## Try this at your practicum school

Over one week, note one behaviour incident and trace how the teacher responded. Using the six-step
model, write half a page on which steps were present and which were missing, and what the cost was
for the classroom's culture.

Do not name the school or the teacher in your write-up.
