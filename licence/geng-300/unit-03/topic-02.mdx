# Topic 3.2 - Inclusive and confident speaking

## A real classroom situation

<Figure id="fig-U3-2" src="/img/figures/geng-300/unit-03/fig-U3-2.svg" alt="Diagram showing an anxiety-confidence curve with anxiety on the horizontal axis and performance on the vertical axis, rising to a peak at moderate anxiety then falling; labelled zones for too little, optimal and too much anxiety, with strategies listed in each zone." width={780} height={470} />

Miss Hina is asked to present her practicum report to forty teachers at a training workshop in
Sukkur. Her hands shake, her voice goes quiet, and she reads every slide word for word. After the
workshop a colleague says, "You knew the material; you just needed to believe you could say it."
This topic is about why speaking in front of others feels difficult, and what you can do about it.
It is also about making sure the language you use includes everyone in the room.

## Explanation

Effective communication is not only about structure and clarity. It is also about who is included
by your language and how you carry yourself when you speak in front of others. This topic covers
two skills that matter in every classroom and every staffroom: using inclusive language and
managing the demands of public speaking.

### Inclusive language and cross-cultural communication

<Glossary term="Stereotype">Stereotype</Glossary> is a fixed, oversimplified belief about a group
of people. In communication, stereotypes show up in the assumptions we make: that a headteacher must
be a man, that a science teacher is unlikely to be a woman, that a pupil from a rural background
cannot speak English well. <Glossary term="Inclusive language">Inclusive language</Glossary> is
language that avoids these assumptions and addresses everyone on equal terms (Verma & Raman).

**Gender-neutral language.** English has several habits that exclude or assume. Using "he" to mean
"he or she", calling a group of mixed pupils "you boys", or using job titles like "headmaster" when
you mean any head of school. Gender-neutral alternatives use "he or she" or rephrase in the plural
("pupils ... they"), use "headteacher" or "principal" instead of "headmaster", and use "chairperson"
instead of "chairman". The figure referenced in Topic 3.1 maps these alternatives alongside the six
Cs: courtesy and consideration both demand that your language does not leave people out.

**Avoiding stereotypes.** Stereotypes are not only about gender. They are also about ethnicity,
disability, region and class. A sentence like "Even the slow learners understood it" assumes that
some pupils are defined by their speed. A more inclusive version says, "All pupils understood it"
or "Pupils at every level understood it". The principle is the same: describe people by what they
did, not by the group you have placed them in.

**Cross-cultural communication.** <Glossary term="Cross-cultural communication">Cross-cultural
communication</Glossary> is communication between people from different cultural backgrounds, and
it is the norm in Pakistani schools, where pupils and teachers may speak different first languages
and come from different regions. Verma and Raman note that effective cross-cultural communicators
do three things: they avoid assuming that their own way of speaking is the only correct one, they
listen for meaning rather than judging accent or word choice, and they adapt their register to the
listener. In a school in Karachi, a teacher might switch between Urdu and English to include a
pupil who is still building confidence in English. That choice is not a failure of English; it is a
success of communication.

### Public speaking: confidence and delivery

<Glossary term="Public speaking">Public speaking</Glossary> is speaking to a group with a
purpose: to inform, persuade or explain. The guide lists it as a core skill for teachers, and the
workshop vignette above shows why it matters. The figure above maps the relationship between
anxiety and performance.

**The anxiety-confidence curve.** A small amount of nervousness before speaking is normal and even
useful: it sharpens your focus and gives your voice energy. As anxiety rises past a certain point,
performance falls. You forget your points, your voice tightens, and you rush. The goal is not to
eliminate anxiety but to keep it in the zone where it helps rather than harms you.

**Overcoming stage fright.** <Glossary term="Stage fright">Stage fright</Glossary> is the
intense fear of speaking in front of others. Strategies that help include: preparing thoroughly
(knowing your material reduces the fear of forgetting), practising out loud (rehearsal builds
familiarity), starting with a deep breath (this slows your heart rate and steadies your voice), and
making eye contact with one friendly face rather than scanning the whole room. Verma and Raman
stress that stage fright shrinks with repeated, small exposures: speak to a pair, then a small
group, then a larger one.

**Voice modulation.** <Glossary term="Voice modulation">Voice modulation</Glossary> is the
deliberate variation of your volume, pace and pitch to hold attention and convey meaning. A flat,
monotone voice loses an audience even when the content is good. modulate your voice by slowing down
for important points, pausing instead of filling silence with "um", and raising your volume slightly
to signal a new idea. In a large hall, projecting your voice (speaking from the diaphragm, not the
throat) carries further without shouting.

**Body language.** Body language includes your posture, gestures, eye contact and facial
expression. Standing with your shoulders back and your weight even signals confidence. Gesturing
naturally emphasises points. Eye contact builds trust with your audience. Avoid crossing your arms,
turning your back to write on the board while speaking, or gripping the podium. Your body speaks
before your words do.

**Common misconception.** "Public speaking is a natural talent, not a skill." It is not. Like
classroom management or lesson planning, it is a set of techniques that improve with practice.
Teachers who speak confidently in front of a staffroom have almost always practised, failed and
adjusted. The anxiety-confidence curve shows that the goal is management, not magic.

## Activity: Inclusive-language audit and mini-speak

This activity has two parts. Work in groups of four.

**Part 1 (15 minutes).** Take a short text (a textbook paragraph, a school notice, or a job
advertisement). Identify three phrases that assume, exclude or stereotype. Rewrite each phrase so
it is inclusive. Use the figure referenced below as a guide.

**Part 2 (15 minutes).** Each group member delivers a one-minute talk to the group on a topic they
know well (a favourite lesson, a hobby, a place in Sindh). After each talk, the group gives one
piece of positive feedback on voice or body language and one suggestion. The goal is practise, not
perfection.

## Check your understanding

1. Explain the difference between a stereotype and an assumption, and give one example of each.
   *(Understand)*
2. A notice says, "The chairman will address the staff at 10 a.m." Rewrite this sentence using
   gender-neutral language and explain the change. *(Apply)*
3. A friend says, "I cannot speak in front of people because I am just not a confident person."
   Using the anxiety-confidence curve, explain what this claim overlooks. *(Analyze)*
4. A teacher says, "Even the weakest pupils understood the lesson." Identify the stereotype in
   this sentence and rewrite it so it is inclusive. *(Apply)*
5. Name three strategies for managing stage fright and explain how each one helps. *(Remember)*

## Summary

Inclusive language avoids stereotypes and addresses everyone on equal terms, through gender-neutral
pronouns, careful job titles and descriptions that focus on what people do rather than the group
they belong to. Cross-cultural communication requires the listener to suspend judgement and adapt
to the audience. Public speaking anxiety follows an anxiety-confidence curve: a little nervousness
helps, too much harms, and strategies such as preparation, rehearsal, deep breathing and eye
contact keep you in the helpful zone. Voice modulation and body language are skills that improve
with practice, not talents you are born with.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can identify stereotypes and assumptions in a described text and rewrite them inclusively.
- [ ] I can explain the anxiety-confidence curve and what it implies for managing stage fright.
- [ ] I can name at least three strategies for managing public speaking anxiety.
- [ ] I can use voice modulation and body language deliberately when I speak to a group.

## Try this at your practicum school

Observe one school assembly or staff meeting. Note the speaker's voice (volume, pace, pauses),
body language (posture, gestures, eye contact) and language (any stereotypes or inclusive
choices). Write half a page on one strength and one area for improvement, with a specific suggestion
drawn from this topic.

Do not name the school or the speaker in your write-up.

## Summative task

Write 350 to 400 words on the following.

> A colleague in your practicum school says, "I have to give a short talk at the morning assembly
> tomorrow and I am terrified. I will forget everything and everyone will laugh." Using this
> topic's account of the anxiety-confidence curve and the strategies for managing stage fright, write
> a response that (a) explains why some nervousness is normal and even useful, (b) proposes three
> specific strategies your colleague can use before and during the talk, and (c) justifies each
> strategy by reference to the curve or to voice and body language.

**Mini-rubric**

| Criterion | What a strong response does |
|---|---|

| Proposal of three strategies (Apply) | Three specific, practicable strategies drawn from the topic |
