# A09. The sociological foundations of education

## What the test asks

CRQs on this objective ask you to **explain** one of the three sociological perspectives on education,
**compare** two of them, or **apply** them to a described school or policy. A typical stem: "How would a
functionalist and a conflict theorist each explain why children from poor families in rural Sindh leave
school earlier?" The course guide also asks how social structure and culture influence schooling and how
schooling, in turn, influences society (HEC, 2012, Foundations of Education, Unit 3).

Marks go to the correct perspective name, its key concepts (socialisation, social placement,
reproduction, hidden curriculum, labelling), a named thinker where relevant, and an application to a
Pakistani school. Candidates lose marks by describing the perspectives in the abstract without an
example, by presenting one perspective as the truth, and by confusing conflict theory with ordinary
classroom conflict.

Your degree has no topic that teaches this; everything the test asks is below.

## Explanation

### Why sociology matters to a teacher

**Sociology** studies how societies are organised: their institutions, groups, roles, norms and
inequalities. The **sociology of education** asks how schooling fits into that organisation. Its
starting point is that a school is not an island. Pupils arrive carrying their family's language,
income, beliefs and expectations, and they leave to take places in the economy and society. The course
guide asks you to see how society's culture, structure, history and economics influence schooling, and
how education in turn strengthens or changes that culture and structure (HEC, 2012, Unit 3).

**Social structure** means the stable patterns that organise a society: family, kinship, class,
gender roles, religion, language and ethnicity, and the division between rural and urban life. In
Pakistan these include the importance of the extended family and of kinship networks, differences
between rural and urban areas, and the many languages pupils bring from home. **Culture** is the shared
way of life of a group: its values, norms, language and customs. Both enter the classroom every day.

Sociologists use three main perspectives to explain the relationship between school and society. Each
sees something the others miss.

### The functionalist perspective

**Functionalism** sees society as a system of connected parts, like the organs of a body, each
performing a function that keeps the whole stable. Education is one of those parts. The French
sociologist Emile Durkheim described schools as agencies of socialisation that pass on a society's
shared values and prepare children for adult roles (Durkheim, 1956; OpenStax, 2021).

Functionalists distinguish **manifest functions**, the intended and visible purposes of schooling, from
**latent functions**, unintended outcomes (OpenStax, 2021). Manifest functions include:

- **Socialisation**: pupils learn society's norms, such as punctuality, respect for rules and working
  with others.
- **Transmission of culture**: national language, history, religion and shared values.
- **Social control**: schools teach obedience to legitimate authority.
- **Social placement**: schools sort pupils by achievement and prepare them for different occupations.

Latent functions include friendships and social networks, and the keeping of children in a safe place
while parents work. Talcott Parsons argued that the school class moves the child from the family, where
he is valued for who he is, to a wider society in which people are judged by what they achieve, and that
schools select people for roles on merit (Parsons, 1959).

**For a Pakistani school**: the morning assembly, the national anthem, shared textbooks and the
examination system are functionalist institutions. They build common identity and sort pupils for
further study and work.

**Criticism**: functionalism assumes that selection is fair and that shared values are everyone's
values. It has little to say about why some groups consistently do worse.

### The conflict perspective

**Conflict theory** sees society as made up of groups with unequal power competing for scarce resources.
Education, in this view, tends to **reproduce** inequality: it passes advantage from privileged parents
to their children while appearing to reward merit.

Key ideas:

- **Correspondence**: Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis argued that the way schools are organised,
  with obedience, fixed timetables and rewards that come from outside the work, mirrors the discipline of
  the workplace, preparing working-class children for subordinate jobs (Bowles & Gintis, 1976).
- **Cultural capital**: Pierre Bourdieu argued that children of educated, powerful families arrive with
  the language, tastes and knowledge that schools value and reward, so their success looks like merit
  but is partly inheritance (Bourdieu & Passeron, 1977; OpenStax, 2021).
- **Hidden curriculum**: the unwritten lessons of schooling, such as who speaks, whose knowledge counts
  and how to accept authority (Jackson, 1968; OpenStax, 2021).
- **Tracking**: sorting pupils into higher and lower streams can fix their futures early (OpenStax,
  2021).

**For a Pakistani school**: a conflict theorist would ask why children in some schools learn in a
language they speak at home while others must learn in one they do not, why facilities differ so much
between schools serving rich and poor families, and whose history and culture the textbook presents.

**Criticism**: conflict theory can underplay the real social mobility that education does bring to many
individuals, and the agency of teachers and pupils to resist.

### The interactionist perspective

**Symbolic interactionism** looks at the small scale: the daily, face-to-face interactions in which
people create meanings. Instead of asking what schooling does for society as a whole, it asks what goes
on between a teacher and a pupil, and how the meanings they create shape the pupil's identity.

Key ideas:

- **Labelling**: when a teacher labels a pupil "weak", "naughty" or "bright", the label shapes how the
  teacher treats the pupil and how the pupil sees himself (OpenStax, 2021).
- **The self-fulfilling prophecy**: an expectation, once acted on, can bring about the very result it
  predicted. Rosenthal and Jacobson's well-known study reported that pupils whom teachers were told
  (falsely) to expect intellectual gains showed greater gains than others (Rosenthal & Jacobson, 1968).
  The study has been debated, but the idea that expectations matter is widely accepted.
- **Classroom interaction**: who is asked questions, who is praised, who sits at the back, and how
  mistakes are treated all send messages about who belongs.

**For a Pakistani school**: a teacher who calls on the same bright pupils at the front, and routinely
seats pupils from poorer homes at the back, may be creating the very gap she later explains as ability.

**Criticism**: interactionism can neglect the wider structures, such as poverty and policy, that set the
limits within which classroom interaction happens.

### Comparing the three perspectives

| | Functionalist | Conflict | Interactionist |
|---|---|---|---|
| Level | Society as a whole (macro) | Society as a whole (macro) | Classroom interaction (micro) |
| View of school | Integrates society, sorts by merit | Reproduces inequality | Creates identities through interaction |
| Key concepts | Socialisation, social placement, manifest and latent functions | Reproduction, cultural capital, hidden curriculum | Labelling, self-fulfilling prophecy |
| Key thinkers | Durkheim, Parsons | Bowles and Gintis, Bourdieu | Rosenthal and Jacobson |
| Question for a teacher | What does my school give society? | Who gains and who loses from how my school works? | What are my expectations teaching each child? |

### Education changes society too

The course guide stresses that the relationship runs both ways: society shapes schooling, and
education in turn strengthens or reshapes society's culture and structure (HEC, 2012, Unit 3). A school
can reproduce existing patterns, or it can open new roles and ways of thinking to children whose
families never had them. For the teacher, the practical lesson from all three
perspectives is the same: notice how pupils' social backgrounds enter the classroom, hold high
expectations for every pupil, and teach in ways that widen rather than narrow their chances.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Compare** the functionalist and conflict perspectives on the role of education in society.
Use one example from Pakistani schooling to show how each perspective would interpret it.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) In a Class 4 in Jacobabad, the teacher calls on the same six pupils at the front and has told
the class that the pupils at the back "will never pass". **Analyse** this situation from the
interactionist perspective and **suggest** three changes the teacher should make.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Functionalist perspective | 4 | Society as an integrated system; school socialises, transmits culture and sorts by merit; manifest and latent functions; Durkheim or Parsons. |
| Conflict perspective | 4 | Society as groups with unequal power; school reproduces inequality; cultural capital, hidden curriculum or correspondence; Bourdieu or Bowles and Gintis. |
| Comparison | 3 | At least two explicit contrasts (for example consensus versus conflict; merit versus reproduction). |
| Pakistani example interpreted both ways | 4 | One example (for example the examination system or medium of instruction) read from each perspective. |

### CRQ 2

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Interactionist concepts | 4 | Labelling and the self-fulfilling prophecy correctly defined. |
| Analysis of the case | 5 | Shows how the teacher's questioning, seating and words create expectations that pupils may internalise and live down to; notes the effect on the back-row pupils' identity and effort. |
| Three changes | 6 | Two marks each for practical changes, such as distributing questions with random selection and wait time, rotating seating, replacing labels with specific feedback, and setting high expectations for all. |
