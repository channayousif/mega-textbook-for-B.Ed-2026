# A10. The psychological foundations of education

## What the test asks

The course guide names four psychological perspectives: **behaviourist**, **constructivist**, **social
cognitivist** and **humanist**, and asks how each shapes instruction, the learning process and
assessment (HEC, 2012, Foundations of Education, Unit 4). Its performance task is to construct an
assessment procedure that represents one perspective. So expect CRQs that ask you to **explain** a
perspective, **compare** two, **apply** one to a lesson, or **design** an assessment in its spirit.

Marks go to the right perspective with its key thinkers and concepts, and above all to the
**implications**: what a teacher who holds this view actually does when teaching and assessing.
Candidates lose marks by summarising the theories without implications, and by leaving out humanism,
which many candidates know only as Maslow's pyramid.

- [EFMP-301 Topic 3.1 - Learning as behaviour: Pavlov and Skinner](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-03/topic-01) teaches the behaviourist perspective: classical and operant conditioning, reinforcement and punishment.
- [EFMP-301 Topic 3.2 - The cognitive turn: Piaget and Bruner](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-03/topic-02) teaches schemas, assimilation and accommodation, and Bruner's discovery learning and spiral curriculum.
- [EFMP-301 Topic 3.3 - Learning from others: Bandura's social learning theory](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-03/topic-03) teaches observational learning, its four steps and vicarious reinforcement.
- [EFMP-301 Topic 3.4 - Constructivist approaches and the classroom](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-03/topic-04) teaches constructivism, the zone of proximal development, scaffolding, and choosing among the four theory families.
- [EFMP-301 Topic 6.1 - Why learners try: theories of motivation](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-06/topic-01) teaches Maslow's hierarchy of needs and self-determination theory.

Those topics teach the behaviourist and constructivist perspectives, and the observational core of the
social cognitive one. This page adds what they leave out: the wider social cognitive ideas of
self-efficacy and reciprocal determinism, the humanist perspective as a whole, and a side-by-side view
of how each perspective shapes instruction and assessment.

## Explanation

### Social cognitive theory: beyond imitation

Topic 3.3 teaches Bandura's observational learning. Bandura later widened this into **social cognitive
theory**, which adds two ideas the licence expects (Bandura, 1986).

**Reciprocal determinism.** Behaviour, personal factors (beliefs, expectations, knowledge) and the
environment all influence one another. A pupil's belief that she is bad at mathematics affects how she
behaves in the lesson; her behaviour affects how the teacher responds; the teacher's response feeds
back into her belief. No single factor is the cause.

**Self-efficacy.** A person's belief in his or her ability to succeed at a particular task (Bandura,
1997). Self-efficacy affects which tasks pupils attempt, how hard they try and how long they persist.
Bandura identified four sources: **mastery experiences** (succeeding at the task), **vicarious
experience** (seeing someone similar succeed), **verbal persuasion** (credible encouragement) and
**physiological and emotional states** (calm versus anxiety). The strongest is mastery.

**Implication for teaching**: break tasks into steps pupils can succeed at, show peer models who are
similar to the learners, give specific, believable encouragement, and teach pupils to set goals and
monitor their own progress. **Implication for assessment**: frequent, low-stakes checks that let pupils
see their own progress, and self-assessment against clear criteria.

### The humanist perspective

**Humanism** in psychology arose in the mid-twentieth century as a reaction against both behaviourism,
which treated learners as shaped by rewards, and psychoanalysis. It sees each person as a whole being
with feelings, choices and a natural drive to grow. Its two best-known figures are Abraham Maslow and
Carl Rogers.

**Maslow** proposed that human needs form a hierarchy, from physiological needs and safety through love
and belonging and esteem, to **self-actualisation**, the realisation of one's full potential (Maslow,
1943). Topic 6.1 teaches the hierarchy. The humanist implication for schools is that a hungry, frightened
or friendless child is not ready to learn, so meeting basic and emotional needs is part of teaching, not
a distraction from it.

**Carl Rogers** carried his client-centred approach from counselling into education. In *Freedom to
Learn* he argued that the most significant learning is self-initiated, involves the whole person,
feelings as well as intellect, and is best evaluated by the learner (Rogers, 1969). The teacher's role
becomes that of a **facilitator** of learning rather than a transmitter of knowledge. Rogers described
three attitudes that make a facilitator effective:

- **Realness or genuineness**: being honest and open, not hiding behind the role of "teacher".
- **Prizing, acceptance and trust**: valuing each pupil as a person, including his or her mistakes and
  feelings.
- **Empathic understanding**: seeing the classroom from the pupil's point of view.

Main features of a humanist classroom:

- **Learner-centred**: pupils have real choices about what and how to learn.
- **Whole-person**: emotional and social growth matter as much as marks.
- **Safe climate**: mistakes are part of learning and never humiliated.
- **Intrinsic motivation**: curiosity and personal meaning, not rewards and punishments.
- **Self-evaluation**: pupils judge their own progress.

**Implication for instruction**: offer choice (a choice of topics or ways to show learning), connect
lessons to pupils' lives and feelings, use discussion and cooperative work, and build relationships.
**Implication for assessment**: self-assessment, reflective journals, portfolios and teacher-pupil
conferences, with feedback rather than ranking.

**Criticism**: humanism is hard to measure, can be difficult to reconcile with a fixed syllabus and
examination, and its concepts (such as self-actualisation) are broad.

### How each perspective shapes instruction and assessment

The guide's last topic in this unit asks for "instruction, learning process, and assessment strategies
in light of the psychological perspective" (HEC, 2012, Unit 4). This table brings the four perspectives
together.

| Perspective | Learning is... | Instruction | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Behaviourist | A change in observable behaviour through reinforcement | Clear objectives, small steps, practice, immediate feedback and reinforcement | Objective tests of observable performance; mastery checks; criterion-referenced scoring |
| Constructivist | Building understanding from experience and prior knowledge | Inquiry, problem-solving, discussion, scaffolding within the zone of proximal development | Performance tasks, explanations, concept maps, formative questioning about reasoning |
| Social cognitive | Learning by observation, shaped by beliefs such as self-efficacy | Modelling, worked examples, peer models, goal-setting and self-monitoring | Progress tracking, self-monitoring charts, demonstrations after modelling |
| Humanist | Personal growth of the whole person, self-directed | Choice, relevance to pupils' lives, safe climate, facilitation | Self-assessment, portfolios, reflective journals, conferences |

### Constructing an assessment procedure: an example

The guide's performance task asks you to construct an assessment procedure in the spirit of one
perspective (HEC, 2012, Unit 4). Here is a humanist example for a Class 5 Urdu writing unit in a
Karachi school:

1. **Pupils choose** the topic of their own story from three options.
2. **Criteria are shared** in simple words at the start: a clear beginning, middle and end; describing
   feelings; neat handwriting.
3. **Drafts go into a portfolio**, so progress is visible over the unit.
4. **Self-assessment**: after each draft, pupils write two sentences, "What I did well" and "What I will
   improve".
5. **Conference**: the teacher meets each pupil for three minutes, listens first, then gives one
   specific suggestion.
6. **Final reflection**: pupils choose their best piece and explain why.

The same unit assessed in a behaviourist way would use a checklist of correct spellings and sentence
forms, scored after each practice exercise, with reinforcement for accuracy. Neither is wrong; each
serves a different view of what learning is.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Explain** the humanist perspective on education with reference to Maslow and Rogers.
**Describe** how a humanist teacher would plan instruction and assessment for a Class 6 science unit.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) **Compare** the behaviourist and social cognitive perspectives on learning, and **design** a
short assessment procedure for a Class 3 mathematics skill that reflects the social cognitive perspective.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Humanist perspective | 3 | Whole person, natural drive to grow, choice, reaction against behaviourism. |
| Maslow | 3 | Hierarchy of needs to self-actualisation, with the implication that basic and emotional needs must be met. |
| Rogers | 3 | Significant, self-initiated learning; teacher as facilitator; genuineness, acceptance and empathy. |
| Instruction for the unit | 3 | Choice, relevance, safe climate, discussion or cooperative work in a Class 6 science context. |
| Assessment for the unit | 3 | Self-assessment, portfolio, journals or conferences, with feedback rather than ranking. |

### CRQ 2

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Behaviourist perspective | 3 | Observable behaviour shaped by reinforcement and consequences. |
| Social cognitive perspective | 3 | Observational learning plus personal factors: self-efficacy, reciprocal determinism, self-regulation. |
| Comparison | 3 | At least two explicit contrasts (for example, internal beliefs matter versus only behaviour; learning without direct reinforcement). |
| Assessment design | 6 | A procedure that includes modelling before assessment, pupil goal-setting or self-monitoring, and visible progress tracking, suited to Class 3. |
