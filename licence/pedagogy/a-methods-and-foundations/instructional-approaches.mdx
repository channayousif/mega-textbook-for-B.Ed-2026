# A01. Instructional approaches and strategies

## What the test asks

A constructed-response question (CRQ) on this objective usually gives you a topic, a class and a
constraint, and asks you to **choose**, **justify** or **compare** instructional strategies. Typical
stems: "Explain the difference between an approach and a strategy, with an example"; "Compare
inquiry-based and project-based learning for a Class 6 science topic"; "Justify the use of a
lecture followed by discussion for this lesson".

A strong answer earns marks for three things: the right term used precisely, a match between the strategy and the
lesson's purpose, and a realistic Pakistani classroom (large class, one board, forty minutes).
Candidates lose marks by listing methods without saying what each is for, by treating "strategy" and
"method" as loose synonyms, and by calling any group activity "inquiry".

## Where your degree teaches this

- [EFMP-301 Topic 10.1 - Teaching methods and strategies](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-10/topic-01) teaches the six main methods (lecture, demonstration, discussion, question-answer, group work, case study) with what each is good for, and the three-part choosing frame: purpose, constraints, method.
- [EFMP-301 Topic 10.2 - Instructional design and teacher effectiveness](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-10/topic-02) teaches backward design (outcome, evidence, activity) and how to write an observable objective, which is how a strategy is chosen in a lesson plan.

Read both first. This page teaches only what those topics leave out: the vocabulary of approach,
strategy, method and technique; the three members of the inquiry family that the course guide asks
you to tell apart; and the internal structure of a good lecture and a good demonstration.

## Explanation

### Approach, strategy, method, technique: four levels, not four synonyms

A useful way to keep the terms apart comes from Anthony (1963), who separated three levels in
language teaching: an **approach** (a set of beliefs about the nature of learning), a **method** (an
overall plan built on that approach) and a **technique** (a specific classroom action). Teacher
educators commonly add **strategy** between approach and method, for the broad plan a teacher adopts
to reach an objective. The levels nest, from most general to most specific:

- **Approach**: the belief about learning. "Children build understanding from experience" is a
  constructivist approach; "skills are learned through modelling and practice" is a behavioural one.
- **Strategy**: the broad plan chosen to reach a lesson's objective within that belief, such as
  direct instruction, inquiry, or cooperative learning.
- **Method**: the organised procedure used inside the strategy, such as lecture, demonstration or
  discussion.
- **Technique**: the single classroom move, such as think-pair-share, a two-minute recap, or waiting
  three seconds after a question.

The course guide makes the same distinction in its own words: direct and indirect instruction are
"comprehensive methods" shaped by theories of learning, while lecture, demonstration and discussion
are "teaching strategies" used within them (HEC, 2012, Unit 4). Terms vary between books, so in an
answer define the term you use and stay consistent. What matters is the nesting: a technique serves a
method, a method serves a strategy, and a strategy expresses an approach.

### The inquiry family: inquiry, problem-based and project-based learning

The course guide asks directly whether "inquiry-based, problem-solving, and project-based learning"
are the same or different (HEC, 2012, Unit 3). They share a root, indirect instruction, in which pupils
build understanding by working on a question rather than receiving an answer. They differ in what
drives the work and what comes out of it (Savery, 2006).

**Inquiry-based learning** starts from a question and asks pupils to gather evidence to answer it.
The teacher may pose the question or help pupils pose their own. The product is an explanation backed
by evidence. Example: a Class 5 teacher in Sukkur asks, "Why does the water in the clay pot stay
cooler than the water in the steel jug?" Pupils predict, test with two containers, record, and
explain.

**Problem-based learning** starts from an ill-structured, realistic problem that has more than one
reasonable solution. Pupils identify what they need to learn, research it, and propose a solution
they must defend (Savery, 2006). The problem comes first and the knowledge is learned because the
problem needs it. Example: "Our school's handpump water tastes salty in summer. What should the
school do?" Pupils need knowledge of groundwater, filtration and cost before they can answer.

**Project-based learning** is organised around producing something over an extended period: a
model, a report, a campaign, a display. The product is the organiser, and learning happens in making
it. Example: a Class 7 group spends three weeks producing a map and poster of the safe drinking-water
sources in their mohalla for the school assembly.

So the short distinction for an exam answer is: inquiry is driven by a **question**, problem-based
learning by a **problem to solve**, and project-based learning by a **product to make**. All three
need careful scaffolding; none means leaving pupils alone. A common misreading is that any group
activity is inquiry. If the teacher tells the pupils what they will find and they simply confirm it,
the activity is a demonstration done in groups, not inquiry.

### Structuring a lecture and a demonstration

Your degree names lecture and demonstration and says what each is for. The course guide's Unit 4
also asks for their **structure** and for the idea of an **active lecture** (HEC, 2012, Week 8).

A **lecture** is justified when pupils need information that is not easily available to them and
that needs interpretation (HEC, 2012, Unit 4 overview). A well-structured lecture has four parts:

1. **Opening**: state the question the lecture will answer and connect it to what pupils already
   know.
2. **Body in short chunks**: two or three main points, each with an example, in segments short
   enough for attention to hold.
3. **Active breaks**: after each chunk, pupils do something with the idea. They summarise it to a
   partner, answer one question in writing, or label a diagram. This is what turns a lecture into an
   **active lecture**. The course guide models this itself by telling the instructor to pause after
   ten minutes and ask two or three student teachers to summarise (HEC, 2012, Week 5).
4. **Closing summary**: the teacher or a pupil restates the main points and the opening question is
   answered.

A **demonstration** shows a process or skill so that pupils can see it before they try it. Its
structure is:

1. **Prepare**: rehearse, check the materials, and make sure every pupil can see. In a class of
   sixty, this may mean demonstrating twice or moving pupils to stand around the table.
2. **Explain the purpose**: tell pupils what to watch for.
3. **Show in steps**: perform each step slowly, naming it as you go, and pause to ask what pupils
   predict will happen next.
4. **Check and let pupils try**: ask pupils to explain the steps back, then let some or all of them
   perform it under guidance.
5. **Summarise**: link what was seen to the concept or rule it illustrates.

### Putting it together in one lesson

A Class 6 science teacher in Larkana teaching "states of matter" might choose a mainly direct
strategy for the definitions (a ten-minute active lecture with one partner recap), a demonstration of
ice melting and water boiling to show the change of state, and a short inquiry question to close:
"Where does the water go when a wet floor dries?" Each method is chosen for a purpose, and together
they serve one objective. That is what the test means by selecting instructional approaches and
strategies: not naming them, but matching them.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Distinguish** between an instructional approach, a strategy, a method and a technique.
Illustrate each level with one example drawn from a single lesson you could teach to Class 5 in a
government school in Sindh.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) A Class 7 teacher wants her pupils to learn about local water safety. **Compare**
inquiry-based, problem-based and project-based learning as ways of teaching this topic, and
**justify** which one you would choose for a class of 55 pupils with one science period a day.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Defines approach | 3 | A belief or theory about learning (for example constructivist or behavioural), not a procedure. |
| Defines strategy | 3 | A broad plan to reach an objective within an approach (for example direct instruction, inquiry, cooperative learning). |
| Defines method | 3 | An organised procedure within the strategy (lecture, demonstration, discussion). |
| Defines technique | 3 | A single specific classroom move (think-pair-share, wait time, a two-minute recap). |
| Coherent single-lesson example | 3 | All four levels illustrated from one lesson, showing how each serves the level above it. |

Deduct up to 2 marks if the candidate uses the terms interchangeably after defining them. Accept
reasonable variation in terminology where the candidate defines the terms and keeps the nesting clear.

### CRQ 2

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Inquiry-based learning described | 3 | Driven by a question; pupils gather evidence to answer it; product is an evidence-based explanation. |
| Problem-based learning described | 3 | Driven by an ill-structured realistic problem; pupils identify what they need to learn; product is a defended solution. |
| Project-based learning described | 3 | Organised around making a product over an extended time. |
| Comparison | 3 | At least two explicit points of similarity or difference (what drives the work, the product, time needed, teacher role). |
| Justified choice for the stated context | 3 | Choice fits 55 pupils and one period a day, and names how the teacher will manage groups and scaffold. |

A full-mark answer reaches a clear choice and defends it against at least one of the alternatives.
