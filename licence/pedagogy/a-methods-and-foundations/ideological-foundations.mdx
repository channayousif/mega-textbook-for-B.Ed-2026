# A07. The ideological foundations of education

## What the test asks

A CRQ on this objective usually asks you to **explain** the Islamic perspective on education, **discuss**
the Islamic concept of peace and its meaning for schools, **explain** the obligations of an Islamic state
and its teachers toward religious minorities, or **describe** the role and expectations of a teacher in
the light of the Quran and Sunnah. The course guide's essential questions are exactly these: how the
Islamic perspective has influenced education, the teacher and the learner in Pakistan, and what the
ideological foundations say about the state's obligations to its minority members (HEC, 2012,
Foundations of Education, Unit 1).

Marks go to answers that cite specific sources accurately (a verse by surah and number, a
constitutional article by number) and then draw a clear implication for the classroom. Candidates lose
marks by writing general praise without sources, by misquoting or inventing references, and by ignoring
the minority question, which is part of the syllabus, not an extra.

Your degree has no topic that teaches this; everything the test asks is below.

## Explanation

### What "ideological foundation" means

An **ideology** is a connected set of beliefs and values about what is true, what is good and how
society should be ordered. Every education system rests on one, stated or not, because an ideology
answers the first question of education: what kind of person, and what kind of society, are we trying
to produce? The answer shapes the aims of education, the content of the curriculum, the methods
teachers use and the kind of teacher a society wants.

In Pakistan the ideological foundation is stated in the Constitution. Article 2 declares Islam the
state religion. Article 2A makes the Objectives Resolution (1949), reproduced in the Annex, a
substantive part of the Constitution. Article 31 asks the state to enable Muslims to order their lives
in accordance with the fundamental principles of Islam and, among other steps, to make the teaching of
the Holy Quran and Islamiat compulsory (Article 31(2)(a)). The National Education Policy 2009 describes
itself as "embedded within the Islamic ethos" enshrined in the Constitution (Government of Pakistan,
2009). So the aims of schooling in Pakistan are meant to be read in the light of Islamic teaching.

### Education and knowledge in the Quran and Sunnah

The value Islam places on knowledge is visible from the first revelation. The opening verses of Surah
Al-Alaq (96:1-5) command the Prophet to "read" in the name of his Lord, and speak of the Lord who
taught by the pen and taught humankind what it did not know. Other verses carry the same theme:

- The Prophet is taught to pray, "My Lord, increase me in knowledge" (20:114).
- The Quran asks whether those who know and those who do not know can be equal (39:9).
- Allah raises in rank those who believe and those who have been given knowledge (58:11).

The Sunnah reinforces this. The Prophet said that the best of people are those who learn the Quran
and teach it (Sahih al-Bukhari 5027), which places teaching itself among the most honoured activities.

Read together, these sources point to a set of **aims** for education:

1. **Faith and God-consciousness**: knowledge leads to recognition of the Creator (tawhid).
2. **Character (tarbiyah and tazkiyah)**: education purifies and trains character, not only the mind.
   The Quran describes the Prophet's mission as reciting Allah's verses, purifying people and teaching
   them the Book and wisdom (62:2).
3. **The whole person**: intellect, body, emotions and spirit are developed together.
4. **Stewardship**: the human being is described as a vicegerent (khalifah) on earth (2:30), so
   knowledge carries responsibility for using the world well, which includes the sciences.
5. **Service of society**: the educated person serves the community with justice.

Two implications follow for the classroom. Knowledge in this view is not divided into "religious"
and "worldly" compartments of unequal value: studying the natural world is also a way of reflecting on
creation. And education that fills the mind while neglecting character has missed half its aim.

### The Islamic concept of peace

The course guide gives a week to the Islamic concept of peace (HEC, 2012, Unit 1). The word Islam
shares its Arabic root (s-l-m) with salam, peace, and Muslims greet one another with a prayer for peace.
Several Quranic principles define what peace requires:

- **Sanctity of life**: taking one innocent life is described as like killing all humanity, and saving
  one as like saving all humanity (5:32).
- **Justice even toward opponents**: believers are told not to let hatred of a people lead them to
  injustice (5:8).
- **Diversity as a sign**: humanity was made into nations and tribes so that people may know one another
  (49:13), not so that they despise one another.
- **Freedom of belief**: there is no compulsion in religion (2:256).

For a school, peace is not only the absence of fighting. It means pupils learn to settle disagreements
by talk, to treat classmates of other sects, languages and religions with respect, and to see justice as
a duty. A teacher who allows mockery of a pupil's sect, ethnicity or faith is failing this aim.

### Other religions in an Islamic state: the rights of minorities

The guide's third week asks how other religions interact with Islam in an Islamic state, and its
essential question asks about the state's obligations to minority members (HEC, 2012, Unit 1). Both the
Islamic sources and the Constitution give clear answers.

**In the Islamic sources.** Besides "no compulsion in religion" (2:256), the Quran does not forbid
Muslims from dealing kindly and justly with those who have not fought them or driven them from their
homes (60:8). Surah Al-Kafirun ends, "For you is your religion, and for me is mine" (109:6). The Prophet
warned that whoever kills a person who has a treaty of protection with the Muslims will not smell the
fragrance of Paradise (Sahih al-Bukhari 3166).

**In the Constitution of Pakistan.**

- The **Objectives Resolution** (Annex, made substantive by Article 2A) requires adequate provision for
  minorities to profess and practise their religions freely and to develop their cultures, and to
  safeguard their legitimate interests.
- **Article 20** gives every citizen the right to profess, practise and propagate his religion, and
  every religious denomination the right to establish and manage its religious institutions.
- **Article 22(1)** says no person attending an educational institution shall be required to receive
  religious instruction, take part in a religious ceremony or attend worship of a religion other than
  his own.
- **Article 22(3)(b)** says no citizen shall be denied admission to an educational institution receiving
  public funds on the ground only of race, religion, caste or place of birth.
- **Article 36** requires the state to safeguard the legitimate rights and interests of minorities.

The founder's vision matches these provisions. Addressing the Constituent Assembly on 11 August 1947,
Quaid-e-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah told the citizens of the new state, "You are free; you are free to go
to your temples" and to any other place of worship (National Assembly of Pakistan).

**In the classroom**, this means a Hindu pupil in a Tharparkar school or a Christian pupil in a Karachi
school has the same right to admission, respect, attention and fair marking as any other pupil, cannot
be compelled to take religious instruction in a faith other than his or her own, and should never be
singled out or shamed for belief.

### The role and expectations of the teacher

The guide asks for the role and expectations of a teacher in the light of the Quran, the Hadith and
other religions (HEC, 2012, Unit 1). The Islamic tradition treats the Prophet as the model teacher, and
the Quran describes him as an excellent example to follow (33:21). From the sources above, the teacher
is expected to be:

- **Knowledgeable and still learning**, praying "increase me in knowledge" (20:114).
- **A role model of character**, since pupils learn more from what a teacher does than what he says.
- **Gentle and patient**: the Quran notes that had the Prophet been harsh and hard-hearted, people would
  have left him (3:159). This is a direct argument against humiliation and harsh punishment.
- **Wise in method**: the Quran advises inviting people with wisdom and good instruction (16:125),
  which fits teaching that explains and persuades rather than forces.
- **Just to every pupil**, whatever the pupil's family, sect or religion (5:8).
- **A builder of peace** who models respectful disagreement.

Other religious and ethical traditions in Pakistan share these expectations of honesty, care and
fairness, which gives a teacher common ground with every family in the community.

### A classroom illustration

Miss Farzana teaches Class 6 in a government school in Mirpurkhas, where a quarter of her pupils are
Hindu. During a lesson on the Prophet's character she draws the lesson of kindness to neighbours of
every faith from the sources, asks her Hindu pupils to share a value of kindness from their own
families, and stops a pupil who mocks a classmate's name. When the Islamiat period comes, her
non-Muslim pupils follow the alternative arrangement the school provides rather than being required to
sit the Islamic instruction. Her practice expresses the ideological foundation as the Constitution
states it: Islamic in its values, and protective of every pupil's faith and dignity.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Explain** three aims of education derived from the Quran and Sunnah, supporting each with
a specific reference, and **describe** how each aim should shape a teacher's work in a Pakistani
elementary classroom.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) A parent complains that a Christian pupil in a Sindh government school was made to attend
Islamiat lessons and was mocked by classmates. **Discuss** the obligations of the state and the teacher
in this situation with reference to Islamic teaching and the Constitution of Pakistan, and **suggest**
what the teacher should do.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Three aims explained | 6 | Two marks each for aims such as faith and God-consciousness, character building (tarbiyah, tazkiyah), development of the whole person, stewardship (khalifah), service to society. |
| Accurate supporting references | 3 | One mark each for a correct reference (for example 96:1-5, 20:114, 39:9, 58:11, 62:2, 2:30, Bukhari 5027). Invented or misattributed references earn no mark. |
| Classroom implications | 6 | Two marks each for a specific, practical implication (for example modelling honesty, integrating values into science lessons, caring for pupils' character as well as marks). |

### CRQ 2

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Islamic teaching | 4 | For example no compulsion in religion (2:256), kind and just dealing with non-Muslims (60:8), justice even toward those one dislikes (5:8), Bukhari 3166 on protected persons. |
| Constitutional provisions | 5 | Article 22(1) (no compulsory religious instruction in another faith), Article 20, Article 36, Article 22(3)(b), and the Objectives Resolution. Deduct for wrong article numbers. |
| Teacher's action | 4 | Arrange the alternative to Islamiat per school policy, stop and address the mockery, restore the pupil's dignity, involve the head and parents. |
| Coherence | 2 | Shows that Islamic teaching and the Constitution point the same way. |
