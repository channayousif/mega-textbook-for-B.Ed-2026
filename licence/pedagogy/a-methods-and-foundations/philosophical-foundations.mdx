# A08. The philosophical foundations of education

## What the test asks

This objective carries real weight on the paper. The 2024 sample paper included a CRQ on Dewey's active
learning principle and another asking candidates to write a teaching philosophy statement. Expect to be
asked to **explain** a philosophy and its implications for aims, curriculum, method and the teacher,
to **compare** two philosophies, to **apply** a philosophy to a described classroom, or to **write**
and **justify** your own philosophy of teaching.

The course guide's own performance task asks student teachers to compare two philosophies and their
influence on instruction, the school environment, subject matter and the teacher's role (HEC, 2012,
Foundations of Education, Unit 2). Use exactly those four headings in an answer. Candidates lose marks by
describing a philosopher's life instead of the philosophy's implications, by confusing realism with
pragmatism, and by writing a personal philosophy that names no philosophy at all.

Your degree has no topic that teaches this; everything the test asks is below.

## Explanation

### What philosophy of education does

Philosophy asks basic questions about reality, knowledge and value. **Philosophy of education** applies
those questions to teaching and learning: what is worth knowing, how do we know it, and what kind of
person should education produce (Siegel, Phillips & Callan, 2018). The course guide names three
branches you must be able to use (HEC, 2012, Unit 2):

- **Metaphysics**, including **ontology**: what is real? Is reality made of ideas, of matter, or of
  changing experience?
- **Epistemology**: what is knowledge, and how do we come to know? By reason, by the senses, by testing
  in experience?
- **Axiology**: what is valuable? It includes **ethics** (what is good) and **aesthetics** (what is
  beautiful).

Each school of thought answers these questions differently, and each set of answers leads to a
different picture of the school. That is the function of philosophy of education: it gives a teacher
reasons for choices about aims, content and method, instead of habit alone. The guide makes the point
that knowledge is never free of philosophical underpinnings (HEC, 2012, Unit 2).

### Idealism

**Idealism** holds that ultimate reality is made of ideas or spirit rather than matter. Its classical
source is Plato, for whom the things we see are imperfect copies of perfect, unchanging Forms that can
be grasped only by reason. Truth and value are therefore permanent and universal.

- **Aim**: develop the mind and character; lead the pupil toward truth, goodness and beauty.
- **Curriculum**: subjects that carry the great ideas of a culture: literature, history, philosophy,
  religion and mathematics.
- **Method**: dialogue and questioning in the manner of Socrates, lecture, discussion of great texts, and
  learning from worthy models.
- **Teacher**: a moral and intellectual exemplar whom pupils imitate.

In a Pakistani classroom, idealism is visible in the importance given to moral education, to the
memorisation and study of respected texts, and to the teacher as a person of character.

### Realism

**Realism** holds that the world exists independently of our minds and that we can know it through the
senses and reason. Its classical source is Aristotle, who studied the natural world by observing and
classifying.

- **Aim**: understand the world as it really is and learn to think rationally about it.
- **Curriculum**: organised subjects, especially the sciences and mathematics, taught in logical order.
- **Method**: observation, demonstration, experiment and systematic, well-sequenced instruction.
- **Teacher**: an expert in the subject who presents accurate, organised knowledge.

A Class 6 science lesson in which pupils examine real leaves, classify them and learn their parts in a
clear sequence is realist in spirit.

### Pragmatism and Dewey's active learning principle

**Pragmatism** holds that reality is experience, which is always changing, and that an idea is true if
it works when tested in experience. Values too are tested by their consequences. The American
philosophers Charles Peirce and William James developed it, and John Dewey applied it to schools.

Dewey rejected the idea of the pupil as a passive receiver. In "My Pedagogic Creed" he wrote that
education "is a process of living and not a preparation for future living" (Dewey, 1897). The school
should be a small community in which children learn by working on real problems together, and
democracy should be practised in it, not only taught (Dewey, 1916). In *Experience and Education* he
added a warning: not every experience educates. An experience is educative when it connects with what
came before and leads to further growth (Dewey, 1938).

This is the **active learning principle** that the licence tests: pupils learn by doing and by thinking
about what they did. Learning starts from a real problem that matters to the learner, moves through
investigation and trial, and ends in reflection on the result.

- **Aim**: growth, problem-solving ability and preparation for democratic living.
- **Curriculum**: integrated and built on pupils' interests and real problems, not divided into rigid
  subjects.
- **Method**: learning by doing, inquiry and projects. Dewey's colleague William Kilpatrick popularised
  the **project method** (Kilpatrick, 1918).
- **Teacher**: a guide who arranges experiences and asks questions, not a lecturer.

Example: instead of copying notes on measurement, Class 5 pupils in a Hyderabad school plan a small
vegetable bed in the school ground, measure it, calculate the seeds needed and record growth. They
learn arithmetic, science and cooperation through one real task.

### Critical theories of education

**Critical theories** hold that education is never neutral: what schools teach, and to whom, reflects
the power of some groups over others. The most influential voice is the Brazilian educator Paulo Freire.
In *Pedagogy of the Oppressed* he criticised **banking education**, in which the teacher "deposits"
facts into passive pupils, and proposed **problem-posing education**, in which teacher and pupils
examine real problems of their own lives through dialogue (Freire, 1970). The aim is critical
consciousness: learners come to see the social conditions that shape their lives and how they could be
changed.

- **Aim**: critical awareness, empowerment and social justice.
- **Curriculum**: issues from learners' own lives; it also asks whose knowledge the textbook presents
  and whose it leaves out.
- **Method**: dialogue, questioning and problem-posing.
- **Teacher**: a co-learner and questioner who shares authority with pupils.

Example: a Class 7 teacher in a rural Sindh school uses the question "Why do many girls in our village
leave school after Class 5?" as the starting point for reading, data collection and discussion.

### Writing a teaching philosophy statement

A **teaching philosophy statement** is a short, reasoned account of what you believe about teaching and
learning and how those beliefs show in your classroom. A strong statement has four parts:

1. **Your belief about learning**, named and linked to a philosophy or theory ("Like Dewey, I believe
   children learn best by working on real problems").
2. **Your aims**: what you want pupils to become.
3. **Your practice**: two or three concrete things you do because of the belief (for example, starting
   units with a question, using group projects, displaying pupils' work).
4. **Evidence and growth**: how you know it works and what you are still improving.

Most practising teachers are **eclectic**: they combine philosophies. You might value an idealist
emphasis on character, a realist respect for accurate subject knowledge and a pragmatist commitment to
active learning. Eclecticism is acceptable in an answer if you explain which element comes from where
and why you combine them.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Explain** Dewey's principle of active learning. **Describe** a Class 5 lesson that applies
it, and **compare** it with how the same topic would be taught by a teacher guided by idealism.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) **Write** a teaching philosophy statement of about 200 words for an elementary teacher in
Sindh. Your statement must name at least one educational philosophy and **justify** how it shapes your
aims and your classroom practice.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Dewey's principle explained | 5 | Learning by doing and reflecting on real experience; education as living, not preparation; educative experience connects to past and leads to growth; school as a democratic community. |
| Lesson applying it | 5 | A realistic Class 5 lesson built on a real problem, with pupil activity, investigation and reflection, and the teacher as guide. |
| Comparison with idealism | 5 | Idealist version: aims at mind and character, content from respected texts or ideas, method of lecture or Socratic dialogue, teacher as model. At least two explicit contrasts. |

### CRQ 2

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Belief about learning, linked to a named philosophy | 4 | Accurate use of idealism, realism, pragmatism, critical theory or a justified eclectic mix. |
| Aims | 3 | Clear statement of what pupils should become. |
| Practice | 5 | At least two concrete classroom practices clearly derived from the belief. |
| Context and reflection | 3 | Fits a Sindh elementary classroom and mentions evidence or growth. |

Deduct up to 2 marks if the statement is well over or under the word limit.
