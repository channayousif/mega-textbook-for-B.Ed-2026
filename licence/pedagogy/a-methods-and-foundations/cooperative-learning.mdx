# A03. Cooperative learning strategy

## What the test asks

CRQs on cooperative learning ask you to **define** it and **distinguish** it from ordinary group work,
**explain** its essential elements, **describe** a named model step by step, **evaluate** its
limitations, or **design** a cooperative lesson for a large Pakistani class. A case study may show
group work going wrong (one pupil does everything, others chat) and ask you to **diagnose** the problem
and **fix** it.

The mark-winning move is to show that cooperative learning is a structured strategy, not simply
"putting pupils in groups". Name the elements, show where each one appears in your lesson, and say how
the teacher will know that every individual learned. Candidates lose marks by describing seating
arrangements only, by naming models without steps, and by ignoring assessment.

Your degree mentions group work in one paragraph of [EFMP-301 Topic 10.1 - Teaching methods and strategies](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-10/topic-01), which notes that group work needs a task that
genuinely needs the group, roles and monitoring. Everything else the test asks is taught below.

## Explanation

### What cooperative learning is

**Cooperative learning** is the instructional use of small groups so that pupils work together to
maximise their own and each other's learning (Johnson & Johnson, 2009). The key word is "each other's".
In cooperative learning a pupil succeeds only when the group's members also succeed, so helping a
classmate understand is part of the task.

This is different from ordinary group work, where pupils sit together but one can do the whole task
while others watch. It is also different from **collaborative learning** in the looser sense, where the
teacher sets a shared task and leaves the group to organise itself. Cooperative learning is more
structured: the teacher designs roles, accountability and rewards on purpose.

### Why use it: the rationale

The course guide grounds cooperative learning in "another tenet of contemporary learning theories: the
opportunity to learn can be enhanced by two or more students learning together" (HEC, 2012, Week 7).
Three reasons support this in practice:

- **Cognitive**: explaining an idea to a peer forces the explainer to organise it, and the listener
  hears it in language close to their own. This fits social constructivism, in which understanding is
  built through interaction with a more knowledgeable other.
- **Social and motivational**: pupils learn to listen, take turns, disagree politely and depend on
  each other, which the course guide's Unit 5 treats as the basis of a classroom that supports learning.
- **Practical in large classes**: in a class of sixty, one teacher cannot hear every pupil, but
  fifteen groups of four can all be talking about the lesson at once.

Johnson and Johnson's (2009) review of a large body of studies reports that cooperative arrangements
generally produce higher achievement, more positive relationships and better psychological health than
competitive or individualistic ones, when the essential elements are in place.

### The five essential elements

Johnson and Johnson (2009) identify five elements that turn a group into a cooperative group. Use
these as the backbone of any exam answer.

1. **Positive interdependence**: pupils believe they "sink or swim together". The teacher builds it
   through a single group product, shared materials (one worksheet per group), or roles that each hold
   part of the task.
2. **Individual accountability**: each pupil's own learning is checked, for example by an individual
   quiz or by calling on any member at random to explain the group's answer. This prevents free riding.
3. **Promotive interaction**: pupils help, explain to, encourage and challenge each other, usually face
   to face. Seating matters: four desks turned together, not a row of four.
4. **Social skills**: listening, taking turns, giving reasons, resolving disagreement. These are taught
   and practised, not assumed.
5. **Group processing**: at the end, groups reflect briefly on how well they worked together and what
   they will do better next time.

### Three models you should be able to describe

<Figure id="fig-A03-1" src="/img/figures/licence/a-methods-and-foundations/fig-A03-1.svg" alt="A table comparing three cooperative learning models, Think-Pair-Share, Jigsaw and STAD, by how each runs, what it is best for, and how individual accountability is built in." width={960} height={382} />

**Think-Pair-Share** (associated with Frank Lyman, 1981). The teacher asks a question; each pupil
thinks alone for a set time; pupils discuss their answers with a partner; then some pairs share with the
class. It takes two or three minutes, needs no materials, and gives every pupil a rehearsed answer. The
course guide lists it among formative assessment strategies (HEC, 2012, Handout 9). It is the simplest
way to start cooperative work in a crowded room.

**Jigsaw** (developed by Elliot Aronson and colleagues; Aronson et al., 1978). The lesson material is
cut into parts, for example four sections of a chapter on the provinces of Pakistan.

1. Pupils sit in **home groups** of four; each member receives one section.
2. Pupils with the same section meet in **expert groups** to study it and agree on the key points.
3. Experts return to their home groups and teach their section to the others.
4. Every pupil takes an individual quiz on all four sections.

Jigsaw builds strong interdependence, because no one can learn the whole without the others.

**Student Teams-Achievement Divisions, STAD** (developed by Robert Slavin; Slavin, 1995).

1. The teacher presents the lesson to the whole class.
2. Pupils study the material in mixed-ability teams of four or five, making sure every member is ready.
3. Each pupil takes a quiz **alone**.
4. Each pupil earns **improvement points** by comparing the quiz score with his or her own past average.
5. Team scores are the sum of members' improvement points, and teams that meet a standard are
   recognised.

STAD suits content with clear right answers, such as spelling, arithmetic facts or map work.

### The incentive structure

The course guide lists the **incentive structure** of cooperative learning as a topic (HEC, 2012,
Week 7). Johnson and Johnson (2009) describe three ways of structuring goals in a classroom:

- **Competitive**: I succeed only if others fail (ranking, position holders).
- **Individualistic**: my success is unrelated to others'.
- **Cooperative**: I succeed only if my group members also succeed.

STAD's improvement points show how a cooperative incentive can be fair: a weaker pupil who improves
from 4 to 7 contributes as much to the team as a strong pupil who improves from 7 to 10. Everyone has
a reason to help, and everyone can contribute.

### Limitations and how to manage them

The course guide also asks for the **limitations** of cooperative learning (HEC, 2012, Week 7). The
main ones, and a remedy for each:

- **Free riders**: some pupils let others work. Remedy: individual accountability, random reporter.
- **Domination**: an able or loud pupil takes over. Remedy: assigned roles (reader, recorder,
  checker, reporter) rotated each lesson.
- **Noise and time**: groups in a crowded room are loud and setting up takes time. Remedy: short,
  practised routines, a quiet signal, and simple structures such as pairs.
- **Weaker pupils dependent on stronger ones**: Remedy: individual tasks and quizzes, and improvement
  scoring.
- **Unfair grading**: giving everyone the group mark can reward those who did nothing. Remedy: combine
  individual and group assessment.
- **Unprepared teachers and pupils**: social skills need teaching. Remedy: start with pairs and build up.

### Checklists as assessment devices

The course guide asks student teachers to build a **checklist** for assessing individual and group
performance during cooperative work (HEC, 2012, Week 7). A checklist lists observable behaviours that
the teacher, or pupils themselves, tick while watching a group. Example items for a Class 6 group:

- Every member spoke at least once.
- Members listened without interrupting.
- The group gave reasons for its answer.
- The recorder wrote the group's agreed answer.
- Each member could explain the answer when asked.

Checklists make the social skills visible, give feedback for group processing, and support individual
accountability.

### A Sindh classroom example

A Class 5 science teacher in Mirpurkhas with 56 pupils uses Jigsaw for a lesson on four kinds of
pollution. Home groups of four receive four short cards (air, water, land, noise). Expert groups meet
at the four corners of the room for eight minutes, then return and teach. The teacher circulates with a
checklist, then gives a five-question individual quiz and asks one random member of each group to
explain one type of pollution. Each element is present: shared goal, individual quiz, face-to-face
teaching, turn-taking, and a two-minute reflection at the end.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Distinguish** cooperative learning from ordinary group work. **Explain** the five
essential elements of cooperative learning identified by Johnson and Johnson, showing how a teacher
would build each element into a lesson.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) **Describe** the steps of the Jigsaw model and **evaluate** its suitability for a Class 6
social studies lesson in a school with 60 pupils per class. Identify two limitations and how you would
manage them.
