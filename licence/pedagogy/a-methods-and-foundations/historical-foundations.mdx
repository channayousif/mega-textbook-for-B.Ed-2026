# A11. The historical foundations of education

## What the test asks

History questions on the licence paper are not date-recall quizzes. They ask you to **compare**,
**analyse** and **evaluate**, which is what the course guide asks too: compare the education system of
the Mughal period with today's, critically analyse Sir Syed's work, and discuss the place of education
in the 18th Amendment and what provinces must do to provide quality education (HEC, 2012, Foundations
of Education, Unit 5). A typical CRQ: "Compare the aims and methods of Deoband and Aligarh" or
"Evaluate the effect of the 18th Amendment on elementary education in Sindh."

Marks go to accurate facts (a correct date or article number earns trust; a wrong one loses it), to a
clear comparison on named points (aims, curriculum, method, funding, outcome), and to a lesson drawn for
the present. Candidates lose marks by writing narrative without analysis, by confusing the movements,
and by inventing policy details.

Your degree has no topic that teaches this; everything the test asks is below.

## Explanation

### Education before British rule

Under Muslim rule in the subcontinent, education was organised largely outside the state, supported by
rulers, nobles and religious endowments (waqf). It had two main levels.

- **The maktab**, often attached to a mosque, taught young children to read the Quran, basic literacy
  (in Persian, the language of administration, and local languages) and simple arithmetic, together
  with manners and religious duties.
- **The madrasa** provided higher learning: Arabic grammar, logic, philosophy, jurisprudence (fiqh),
  Quranic commentary (tafsir) and hadith, and in many places mathematics, astronomy and medicine.

In the eighteenth century, scholars of the Farangi Mahal family in Lucknow developed a course of study
associated with Mulla Nizamuddin, the **Dars-e-Nizami**, which gave weight to the rational sciences such
as logic and philosophy as well as the religious ones and became the most widely followed madrasa
curriculum in the region (Robinson, 2001).

**Methods and grooming.** Teaching was personal: a student read texts with a teacher (ustad), often in a
circle, and progressed text by text at his own pace rather than by year groups. Memorisation, oral
explanation and disputation were central. The relationship between teacher and student was close and
respectful, and education aimed at character and conduct (adab) as much as knowledge. The course guide
asks you to analyse exactly these "methods of teaching, the ways of learning, and the ways of grooming
the learners" from the Mughal period to the end of the colonial era (HEC, 2012, Unit 5).

**The colonial turn.** In his Minute of 2 February 1835, Thomas Babington Macaulay argued that British
government funds should support education in English and Western learning rather than in Arabic, Persian
and Sanskrit (Macaulay, 1835). Over the following decades English became the language of government
employment, and the old system lost state support and prestige. After 1857, Muslims in particular faced
a crisis: how to preserve their religious tradition and also gain a place in the new order. Three
institutions answered in three different ways.

### Darul Uloom Deoband (1866)

The seminary at **Deoband**, in the United Provinces, was founded in 1866 (some historians date its
opening to 1867) by scholars including Maulana Muhammad Qasim Nanautvi and Maulana Rashid Ahmad Gangohi.

- **Aim**: preserve Islamic learning and train religious scholars (ulama) who could guide Muslims in
  their faith and practice without depending on the colonial state.
- **Curriculum**: a version of the Dars-e-Nizami with strong emphasis on hadith and the religious
  sciences.
- **Organisation**: Metcalf (1978) shows that Deoband borrowed a modern institutional form: a fixed
  course of study, classes, examinations, a paid staff and a central administration, rather than the
  older pattern of individual teachers.
- **Funding**: popular donations from the Muslim public rather than government grants or princely
  patronage (Metcalf, 1978).

**Assessment**: Deoband kept religious scholarship alive and independent and produced a network of
madrasas across the region, including in present-day Pakistan. Critics argue that it gave little place
to modern science and English.

### Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College, Aligarh (1875-77)

Sir Syed Ahmad Khan concluded that Muslims had to acquire modern Western knowledge and English to
recover their position. He founded the **Scientific Society** in 1864 to translate Western works into
Indian languages, and in 1870 began publishing the journal *Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq* to promote social reform
(Aligarh Muslim University). His school at Aligarh opened in 1875, and in January 1877 he founded the
**Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental (MAO) College**, patterned after the Oxford and Cambridge colleges he had
visited in England. By 1920 it had become **Aligarh Muslim University** (Aligarh Muslim University).

- **Aim**: combine Western science and English with Islamic education, producing Muslims fit for
  public service, the professions and leadership.
- **Curriculum**: English, modern sciences, literature and history, with religious instruction.
- **Method and life**: a residential college in which discipline, sport and community life trained
  character.

**Assessment**: Aligarh produced a new Muslim educated class, and its graduates were prominent in the
public life that led toward Pakistan. Critics at the time objected to its Western orientation and to
some of Sir Syed's religious views.

### Nadwatul Ulama (1894; Lucknow seminary 1898)

The **Nadwatul Ulama** movement sought a middle path. According to the institution's own account, Syed
Muhammad Ali Mungeri proposed it in 1892 and its first public gathering was held in Kanpur in April 1894
(Darul Uloom Nadwatul Ulama). Its seminary, **Darul Uloom Nadwatul Ulama**, began in Lucknow in 1898.
Allama Shibli Nomani was closely associated with its academic development.

- **Aim**: in the institution's words, "to reform the system and curriculum of madarsas" and renew them
  according to the times (Darul Uloom Nadwatul Ulama).
- **Curriculum**: the traditional religious sciences in a revised syllabus that made room for subjects
  suited to the times.

**Assessment**: Nadwa produced scholars who could speak to both the traditional and the modern world,
though it remained smaller than either Deoband or Aligarh.

| | Deoband | Aligarh (MAO) | Nadwatul Ulama |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aim | Preserve religious learning; train ulama | Modern and Western education for Muslims | Reform madrasa education; bridge old and new |
| Curriculum | Dars-e-Nizami, hadith emphasis | English, sciences, with religious instruction | Religious sciences in a syllabus renewed for the times |
| Model | Organised madrasa, public donations | Residential college on Oxford and Cambridge lines | Reformed seminary |

### Education in Pakistan: the policies

Pakistan's National Education Policy 2009 describes itself as one of "a series of education policies
dating back to the very inception of the country in 1947" (Government of Pakistan, 2009). The official
list maintained by the Pakistan Institute of Education includes the documents below.

- **1947, Pakistan Educational Conference (Karachi).** Quaid-e-Azam's message said the future of the
  state would greatly depend on the type of education given to its children. The conference called for
  a national system resting on free and compulsory primary education, and recommended universal primary
  education within 20 years (Bengali, 1999).
- **1959, Report of the Commission on National Education.** Noted that fewer than half of primary-age
  children were enrolled and recommended eight years of compulsory education (Bengali, 1999).
- **1970, The New Education Policy.** Stressed education for economic development and aimed at free,
  universal enrolment to Class V by 1980; it was never implemented because of the war with India and the
  separation of East Pakistan (Bengali, 1999).
- **1972-80, The Education Policy.** Declared education free and universal up to Class X in government
  and privately managed schools, and planned a mass adult literacy programme (Bengali, 1999).
- **1979, National Education Policy and Implementation Programme.** Emphasised building on indigenous
  institutions, including 5,000 mosque schools for boys and 5,000 mohalla schools for girls, and set
  targets for universal Class I enrolment (Bengali, 1999).
- **1992, National Education Policy** and **1998-2010, National Education Policy.** Both acknowledged
  high drop-out and low literacy, turned toward non-formal education, and the 1998 policy involved
  foreign donor agencies on a large scale (Bengali, 1999).
- **2009, National Education Policy.** Among its commitments: universal and free primary education by
  2015 and up to Class 10 by 2025; a Bachelor's degree with a B.Ed as the requirement for teaching at
  the elementary level, with the older PTC and CT certificates phased out; and professional development
  for all teachers on a three-year cycle (Government of Pakistan, 2009).

**Elementary, secondary and tertiary education.** A recurring theme across these documents is the gap
between targets and results: the 1998 policy itself admitted "an unprecedented history of producing
excellent documents of plans and policies which could not be implemented" (quoted in Bengali, 1999).
For tertiary education, the **Higher Education Commission** was established by the Higher Education
Commission Ordinance, 2002, to fund, regulate and assure the quality of universities; the course guide
asks you to consider its role (HEC, 2012, Unit 5).

### The 18th Amendment (2010) and after

The Constitution (Eighteenth Amendment) Act, 2010 changed the constitutional position of education in
two ways.

1. It inserted **Article 25A**: "The State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children
   of the age of five to sixteen years in such manner as may be determined by law." Education became a
   fundamental right.
2. It abolished the **Concurrent Legislative List**, on which education had appeared, so that
   curriculum, planning, policy and standards became mainly provincial responsibilities.

Provinces then passed their own laws. In Sindh, the **Sindh Right of Children to Free and Compulsory
Education Act, 2013** gives effect to Article 25A and, among other provisions, requires private schools
to give free education to at least 10 percent of their admissions from underprivileged children.

The course guide asks what issues the amendment raised (HEC, 2012, Unit 5). The main ones are
provincial capacity to write curricula and set standards, keeping some national coherence across
provinces, financing the right to education for every child aged five to sixteen, and enforcing the
provincial acts.

### Lessons for today

History shows several things a teacher can use in an answer: the tension between preserving tradition
and adopting modern knowledge has been present since 1857; separate streams (madrasa, English-medium,
Urdu-medium public schools) have deep historical roots; and policy targets have repeatedly been missed
because of weak implementation. Each historical movement also succeeded where it built strong
institutions and teachers, which is a reminder that reform ultimately happens in classrooms.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Compare** Darul Uloom Deoband and the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College, Aligarh, in terms
of their aims, curriculum, organisation and contribution to Muslim society. **Evaluate** which approach
offers more for education in Pakistan today.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) **Explain** how the 18th Amendment (2010) changed the constitutional position of education in
Pakistan, and **discuss** two challenges Sindh faces in implementing the right to free and compulsory
education.
