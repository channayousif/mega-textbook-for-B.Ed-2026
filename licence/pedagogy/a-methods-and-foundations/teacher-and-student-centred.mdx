# A04. Teacher and student-centred instructional approaches

## What the test asks

CRQs on this objective ask you to **compare** teacher-centred and student-centred instruction,
**explain** the learning theory behind each, **plan** a short lesson using a direct or indirect
template, or **argue** whether the two are opposed or complementary. That last question is the
central one in the course guide, which asks student teachers to "take a position as to whether these
are oppositional or complementary methods" (HEC, 2012, Unit 3).

Examiners reward answers that link each approach to its theory (behavioural or constructivist), to
the kind of learning it serves (lower-order or higher-order), and to concrete lesson steps. Candidates
lose marks by describing teacher-centred teaching only as "bad" or "old", by treating student-centred
as "no teacher", and by giving no lesson steps.

## Where your degree teaches this

- [EFMP-301 Topic 3.1 - Learning as behaviour: Pavlov and Skinner](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-03/topic-01) teaches behavioural learning theory, classical and operant conditioning, and reinforcement, which is the theory behind direct instruction.
- [EFMP-301 Topic 3.4 - Constructivist approaches and the classroom](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-03/topic-04) teaches constructivism, the zone of proximal development and scaffolding, the theory behind indirect, student-centred instruction.
- [EFMP-302 Topic 1.2 - From an industrial model to inquiry-based teaching](/semester-1/efmp-302/unit-01/topic-02) contrasts a syllabus-centred, delivery-focused picture of teaching with one centred on the learner and on evidence of pupils' thinking.

Read those for the theories. This page teaches what they leave out: the distinction between lower-
and higher-order learning, the two lesson templates the course guide asks for, the different roles of
teacher and pupil, and how to decide between the approaches.

## Explanation

### Lower-order and higher-order learning

The course guide begins this unit with a distinction it attributes to Benjamin Bloom (HEC, 2012,
Week 5). **Lower-order thinking** means recalling facts or applying rules and procedures that have
been learned to mastery, such as spelling, times tables or the steps of long division. **Higher-order
thinking** means transforming information: combining facts to explain, generalise, hypothesise, solve
a new problem or reach an interpretation. Higher-order work has an element of uncertainty, because
pupils may reach different, defensible conclusions.

The two are not separate worlds. The guide notes that most school learning, especially from about
Class 4, involves both along a continuum: lessons start with the knowledge pupils need and move toward
using it (HEC, 2012, Week 5). This continuum is the key to the whole topic.

### Teacher-centred: direct instruction

**Teacher-centred instruction**, in its structured form called **direct instruction**, is designed for
lower-order goals: knowledge and skills that have a clear right form. It derives from behavioural
learning theory. The teacher controls the content, sequence and pace, models the skill, and gives
feedback until pupils perform it without error (HEC, 2012, Handout 4).

**Template for a direct instruction lesson** (HEC, 2012, Handout 4):

1. **Introduction and review**: gain attention, recall prior knowledge, and tell pupils what they
   will be able to do and why it matters.
2. **Development**: the teacher models the skill with clear explanations and many examples, and checks
   understanding with questions.
3. **Guided practice**: pupils practise under close supervision; the teacher corrects errors at once.
4. **Closure**: after enough error-free practice, the teacher reviews the objective and sets
   independent practice.
5. **Evaluation**: progress is checked during and at the end of the lesson, and the lesson is retaught
   to those who have not mastered it.

Rosenshine's (2012) summary of research on effective instruction supports the same pattern: begin with
a short review, present new material in small steps with practice after each step, ask many questions
to check understanding, provide models and worked examples, guide practice until pupils reach a high
success rate, and then move to independent practice with regular review.

The guide notes that direct instruction is most effective for basic skills in reading, mathematics,
spelling and handwriting, and records the criticism that when it is fully scripted it leaves pupils
no room for exploration or discussion (HEC, 2012, Handout 4).

### Student-centred: indirect instruction

**Student-centred instruction**, called **indirect instruction** in the guide, is designed for
higher-order goals: understanding the physical, social and psychological world. It derives from
constructivist learning theory. Inquiry-based, problem-based and project-based learning are its main
forms (HEC, 2012, Unit 3 overview; see A01 for the differences). The teacher sets up a question or
problem, provides materials and scaffolding, and guides pupils as they build and test explanations.

**Template for an indirect instruction lesson.** A widely used template is the 5E model (Bybee et al.,
2006):

1. **Engage**: a puzzling question, object or event that raises curiosity and surfaces what pupils
   already believe.
2. **Explore**: pupils investigate in pairs or groups, collecting observations or information.
3. **Explain**: pupils present their explanations; the teacher then introduces the formal concept and
   terms, connected to what pupils found.
4. **Elaborate**: pupils apply the concept to a new situation.
5. **Evaluate**: pupils and teacher check understanding, often by asking pupils to explain or apply
   the idea in their own words.

Notice that the teacher is active throughout: choosing the question, structuring the exploration,
teaching the concept at the Explain stage, and assessing. Student-centred does not mean teacher-absent.

### Different roles for teacher and pupils

| | Teacher-centred (direct) | Student-centred (indirect) |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Knowledge and skills, lower-order | Understanding, higher-order |
| Theory | Behavioural | Constructivist |
| Teacher's role | Presenter, model, corrector | Designer, guide, questioner |
| Pupil's role | Listens, practises, reproduces | Investigates, discusses, explains |
| Typical talk | Recitation: teacher asks, one pupil answers | Discussion: pupils talk to each other |
| Evidence of success | Accurate, fluent performance | Reasoned explanation, transfer to new cases |

### Opposed or complementary?

The course guide's own position is that "both methods belong in schools", because knowing and
understanding are different but related, and each is a legitimate goal for all children (HEC, 2012,
Unit 3). An effective teacher chooses by the lesson's purpose and the pupils' needs, and often combines
the two.

A Class 4 mathematics example from a school in Nawabshah shows the combination. On Monday the
teacher uses direct instruction to teach the procedure for measuring length in centimetres: she models
it on the board, pupils practise measuring their pencils with her correcting, then measure three
objects alone. On Wednesday she uses indirect instruction: groups investigate "Whose handspan is the
best unit for measuring the classroom?", measure the room with their hands, find that the numbers
disagree, and are led to explain why the world needs a standard unit. The procedure made the inquiry
possible, and the inquiry gave the procedure meaning.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Compare** teacher-centred (direct) and student-centred (indirect) instruction in terms of
their goals, underlying learning theory, the role of the teacher and the role of the pupils. Take and
**justify** a position on whether they are opposed or complementary.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) Using the steps of a direct instruction lesson, **plan** a 35-minute lesson to teach Class 3
pupils to write the Urdu or English letters of their name correctly. Then **explain** what one part of
the lesson would look like if you redesigned it as indirect instruction.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Goals | 2 | Direct serves knowledge and skills (lower-order); indirect serves understanding (higher-order). |
| Theory | 2 | Direct from behavioural theory; indirect from constructivist theory. |
| Teacher's role | 2 | Presenter, model and corrector versus designer, guide and questioner. |
| Pupils' role | 2 | Practise and reproduce versus investigate, discuss and explain. |
| Position | 4 | A clear position (complementary is the guide's view, but a well-argued alternative is accepted) with at least two reasons. |
| Example | 3 | A classroom example showing the two used in sequence or combination. |

### CRQ 2

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction and review | 2 | Attention, prior knowledge, stated objective. |
| Modelling | 3 | Teacher demonstrates letter formation with explanation and examples, checks understanding. |
| Guided practice | 3 | Pupils practise with immediate correction. |
| Closure, independent practice and evaluation | 3 | Review, independent task, check of each pupil, reteaching plan. |
| Indirect redesign | 4 | One part reworked with an inquiry element (for example pupils compare letter shapes and discover a rule), with the teacher's guiding role stated. |

Timings should be realistic for 35 minutes; deduct 1 mark if they are absent or impossible.
