# A02. Effective questioning strategy

## What the test asks

Questioning is one of the most frequently used teaching techniques, and CRQs on it are practical. You
may be asked to **explain** the purposes of questions, **classify** given questions as open or closed
or by cognitive level, **explain** wait time and why it matters, or **design** a short sequence of
questions for a lesson and **justify** it. A case study may describe a teacher whose questions reach
only four pupils at the front and ask you to **suggest** improvements.

Marks go to precise terms (open, closed, lower-order, higher-order, wait time, probing, redirecting),
to examples written as real questions a teacher would say, and to a Pakistani classroom context.
Candidates lose marks by defining terms without examples, by writing "ask more open questions" as if
closed questions had no use, and by forgetting that questions must reach the whole class, not only
volunteers.

Your degree touches questioning in one paragraph of [EFMP-301 Topic 10.1 - Teaching methods and strategies](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-10/topic-01), which names question-answer as a method
and mentions the pause after a question. Everything the test asks beyond that is taught below.

## Explanation

### Why teachers ask questions

Questions are among the most common events in a primary classroom. The course guide's Handout 7
reports observers counting as many as 84 questions in one hour, mostly asked by teachers (HEC, 2012,
Handout 7). With that many questions, the quality of each one matters. Teachers ask questions for
different purposes, and one question can serve more than one:

- **To check prior knowledge** before new teaching, so the lesson starts where pupils are.
- **To check understanding** during the lesson, so the teacher can re-teach before errors settle.
- **To develop thinking**, by asking pupils to explain, compare, predict or judge.
- **To involve and motivate**, by drawing pupils into the lesson.
- **To manage the class**, such as "Has everyone brought the homework?" These are procedural
  questions, not learning questions (HEC, 2012, Handout 7).
- **To review and summarise** at the end of a lesson.

Questioning is also a core formative assessment technique. Black and Wiliam (1998) advise teachers
to ask thoughtful, reflective questions rather than only simple factual ones and then to give pupils
enough time to respond (HEC, 2012, Handout 9).

### Open and closed questions

A **closed question** has one expected answer, usually short: "What is the capital of Sindh?" "What
is 7 times 8?" Closed questions are quick, easy to mark, and useful for checking facts and
vocabulary.

An **open question** has more than one acceptable answer and invites a longer response: "Why do you
think people settled along the Indus?" "What would happen to our village if the canal dried up?" Open
questions develop reasoning and language, and they show the teacher how pupils think.

Neither is better in general. A lesson often begins with closed questions that make sure everyone has
the facts and then moves to open questions that use those facts. The course guide's recitation
example shows that even factual questions differ: "what" questions call for recall, while a chain of
"why" questions pushes pupils toward explanation (HEC, 2012, Week 8).

### Cognitive levels of questions

Questions can also be classified by the thinking they demand. Using the revised Bloom's taxonomy
(Krathwohl, 2002), which page A05 teaches in full:

| Level | What the pupil does | Example question (Class 6, water cycle) |
|---|---|---|
| Remember | Recall a fact | What is evaporation? |
| Understand | Explain in own words | Why do clouds form? |
| Apply | Use the idea in a new case | Why do clothes dry faster on a windy day? |
| Analyse | Break down, compare | How is rain in Karachi different from snow in Murree? |
| Evaluate | Judge with reasons | Is building more dams the best way to save water? Why? |
| Create | Produce something new | Design a way to collect rainwater at our school. |

The first three are often called **lower-order** questions and the last three **higher-order**.
Cotton's (1988) review of the research reports that about 60 percent of questions in classroom
recitations are lower-order, 20 percent higher-order and 20 percent procedural. The same review adds a
caution that matters for elementary teachers: with young primary-level children, lower-order questions
were found to be more effective, while above the primary grades a combination of both kinds works
better than either alone, and raising the share of higher-order questions well above 20 percent
produces better learning gains. The practical rule is to plan a deliberate mix suited to the class, and
to write the higher-order questions into the lesson plan in advance, because they are hard to invent
on the spot.

### Wait time

**Wait time** is the pause between the teacher's question and the next speaker. Mary Budd Rowe's
research found that teachers typically wait one second or less before answering the question
themselves, repeating it, or calling on someone else. When teachers extended the wait to three
seconds or more, pupils gave longer answers, more pupils answered, fewer failed to respond, and more
pupils offered reasons and asked their own questions (Rowe, 1986). Rowe also described a second pause,
after a pupil answers and before the teacher responds, which gives the pupil and classmates time to add
or correct. Cotton (1988) refines the finding: for lower-order questions a wait of about three seconds
works best, while for higher-order questions pupils seem to engage more the longer the teacher is
willing to wait.

In practice: ask the question, then count silently to at least three, looking round the room. For a
harder question, say "Think first, no hands yet" and give ten seconds, or use think-pair-share so that
every pupil rehearses an answer with a partner before anyone speaks.

### Handling answers: probing, prompting and redirecting

What a teacher does after the first answer often matters more than the question itself.

- **Probing** asks the pupil to go further: "Why do you say that?" "Can you give an example?" "How do
  you know?" The course guide's list of discussion questions includes this kind: questions asking for
  more evidence and for clarification (HEC, 2012, Handout 7).
- **Prompting** gives a hint when a pupil is stuck, instead of moving on: "Think about what happens to
  a puddle on a hot afternoon."
- **Redirecting** passes the question or the answer to another pupil: "Sana, do you agree with Ali?
  Why?" This spreads participation and makes pupils listen to each other.
- **Rephrasing** simplifies a question that no one could answer, rather than repeating it louder.
- **Responding to wrong answers** without humiliation: acknowledge the part that is right, then probe
  or prompt. A pupil who is shamed for a wrong answer learns not to answer.

### Reaching the whole class

In a class of fifty, asking volunteers means the same few pupils answer every day. Techniques that
widen participation include asking every pupil to write an answer (on a slate or in a notebook) before
a few are read out, and presenting several possible answers for the class to vote on (HEC, 2012,
Handout 9). Selecting who answers at random, as the course guide itself does when it draws student
teachers' names from an envelope (HEC, 2012, Unit 4), keeps everyone thinking; name the pupil after the
pause, not before, or the rest of the class stops thinking.

### Recitation and discussion need different questions

The course guide draws a sharp line between two kinds of classroom talk (HEC, 2012, Handout 7).
In a **recitation**, the teacher asks, one pupil answers briefly, the teacher evaluates and asks the
next question. It is fast, like a quiz, and each question has one right answer. Recitation is useful
for reviewing facts before a discussion. In a **discussion**, pupils are the main speakers, answers are
longer, the pace is slower, pupils respond to each other, and questions are open. The guide lists
question types that keep a discussion going: questions asking for **more evidence** ("What does the
story say that supports that?"), for **clarification** ("Can you say that another way?"),
**hypothetical** questions ("What might have happened if...?"), **cause-and-effect** questions, and
**summary and synthesis** questions ("What is the most important idea from today?"). A discussion
turns back into a recitation when the teacher asks only questions with one short answer.

### A worked example from a Sindh classroom

A Class 5 Urdu teacher in Hyderabad has read a story about a boy who returns a lost purse. Her plan
starts with three quick closed questions to check the plot (who found the purse, where, what was in
it), asked to different rows. She then asks, "Why do you think Ahmed returned it when no one saw
him?", waits five seconds, tells pupils to discuss with a partner for one minute, and draws two names.
When one pupil says "because he is good", she probes: "What in the story shows he is good?" She
redirects the answer to another pupil: "Do you agree? Can you find another reason?" She closes with a
synthesis question: "What is the lesson of this story for us in school?" In twelve minutes she has
used closed and open questions, lower- and higher-order levels, wait time, pair talk, probing and
redirecting.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Explain** what is meant by wait time. Describe what research has found when teachers
increase it, and **suggest** three practical ways a teacher of a class of 50 can make sure more pupils
think about and answer each question.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) For a Class 4 social studies lesson on "Our Community Helpers", **design** a sequence of
six questions that moves from lower-order to higher-order thinking. Label each question as open or
closed and name its cognitive level, and **explain** how you would respond if a pupil gave an
incomplete answer.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Definition of wait time | 3 | The pause after a teacher's question before anyone speaks; credit a mention of the second pause after a pupil answers. |
| Research findings | 5 | Typical wait is one second or less; at three seconds or more, answers are longer, more pupils respond, fewer fail to respond, more reasoning and pupil questions (Rowe); about three seconds suits lower-order questions and longer waits suit higher-order ones (Cotton). One mark per accurate finding, up to 5. |
| Three practical techniques | 6 | Two marks each for techniques such as silent count to three or more, think-pair-share, writing on slates before answering, random name selection after the pause, hand signals or voting. Each must be explained, not just named. |
| Context | 1 | Techniques are realistic for a large class. |

### CRQ 2

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Six relevant questions | 3 | All on the stated topic and phrased as a teacher would say them. |
| Progression from lower to higher order | 4 | A clear rise through recall, understanding, application, then analysis, evaluation or creation. |
| Accurate labels (open/closed and level) | 4 | Labels match the questions; at least two open questions at higher levels. |
| Response to an incomplete answer | 4 | Describes probing (asking for reasons or examples), prompting with a hint, or redirecting to another pupil, without discouraging the first pupil. |

Deduct 1 mark if every question is closed or every question is open, since the progression requires a mix.
