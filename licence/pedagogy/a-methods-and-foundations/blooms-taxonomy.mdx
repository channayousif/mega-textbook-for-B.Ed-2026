# A05. Bloom's Taxonomy of educational objectives

## What the test asks

Bloom's Taxonomy appears on the licence paper in two ways. Directly, a CRQ may ask you to **name** and
**explain** the levels, **distinguish** the original taxonomy from the revised one, or **classify**
given objectives or questions by level. Indirectly, almost every planning or assessment question
expects you to **write** objectives at stated levels, for example "write two lower-order and two
higher-order objectives for this lesson".

Marks go to the levels in the right order, to accurate action verbs, and to objectives written as
observable pupil behaviour. Candidates lose marks by mixing the old and new level names without saying
so, by using verbs such as "know" or "understand" in objectives (they cannot be observed), and by
forgetting that the taxonomy covers three domains, not only the cognitive one.

Your degree does not teach the taxonomy itself. [EFMP-301 Topic 10.2 - Instructional design and teacher effectiveness](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-10/topic-02) teaches how to write an observable objective with
a condition and a criterion; this page adds the levels that tell you what kind of thinking the
objective demands.

## Explanation

### What the taxonomy is and where it came from

A **taxonomy** is an ordered classification. In 1956 a committee of university examiners led by
Benjamin Bloom published a classification of educational objectives in the **cognitive domain**,
the domain of knowledge and thinking (Bloom et al., 1956). Their aim was to give teachers and test
makers a shared language for describing what learners should be able to do, so that objectives,
teaching and tests could be matched. The committee planned three domains: cognitive (thinking),
**affective** (feelings, attitudes and values) and **psychomotor** (physical skills).

The course guide introduces the taxonomy for exactly this practical purpose: to help student teachers
write learning objectives that require both lower- and higher-order thinking (HEC, 2012, Week 12).

### The original cognitive levels (1956)

The 1956 taxonomy has six levels, from simple to complex. Each level was meant to build on the ones
below it.

1. **Knowledge**: recall of facts, terms, rules. "Name the provinces of Pakistan."
2. **Comprehension**: grasp the meaning; explain or summarise in one's own words. "Explain why the
   Indus is important to Sindh's farmers."
3. **Application**: use a rule or method in a new situation. "Calculate the area of the classroom
   floor."
4. **Analysis**: break material into parts and see how they relate. "Compare the diets of two families
   and identify what each lacks."
5. **Synthesis**: combine parts into a new whole. "Plan a healthy weekly lunch menu for the school
   canteen."
6. **Evaluation**: judge value against criteria. "Decide which of two menus is healthier and justify
   your choice."

The course guide groups knowledge, comprehension and application as **lower-order** thinking skills
and analysis, synthesis and evaluation as **higher-order** skills (HEC, 2012, Week 12). Note that the
guide lists the higher group as "analysis, evaluation, and synthesis"; in the 1956 taxonomy itself the
order is analysis, then synthesis, then evaluation.

### The revised taxonomy (2001)

In 2001 a group led by Lorin Anderson, a former student of Bloom, and David Krathwohl, one of the
original authors, published a revision (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001; Krathwohl, 2002). It made three
main changes:

- **Nouns became verbs**, because objectives describe what learners do: remember, understand, apply,
  analyse, evaluate, create.
- **The top two levels were reordered and renamed**: synthesis became **create** and moved to the top,
  above **evaluate**.
- **A second dimension was added**: the kind of knowledge involved.

| Revised level (2001) | Replaces (1956) | Useful action verbs |
|---|---|---|
| Remember | Knowledge | list, name, recall, identify, define |
| Understand | Comprehension | explain, summarise, classify, give an example |
| Apply | Application | use, calculate, solve, carry out |
| Analyse | Analysis | compare, distinguish, organise, find the cause |
| Evaluate | Evaluation | judge, justify, critique, decide with reasons |
| Create | Synthesis | design, plan, compose, produce |

The **knowledge dimension** has four types (Krathwohl, 2002): **factual** knowledge (terms and
details), **conceptual** knowledge (categories, principles, theories), **procedural** knowledge (how to
do something, methods and skills) and **metacognitive** knowledge (awareness of one's own thinking and
learning strategies). Crossing the four knowledge types with the six process levels gives a
**taxonomy table** of 24 cells. A teacher can place each objective, activity and test item in a cell and
check that they line up: if the objective is "apply procedural knowledge" but the test only asks pupils
to remember facts, the test does not measure the objective.

If an exam question does not say which version to use, name the version you are using. Using the
revised verbs is usually clearer.

### The affective and psychomotor domains

The cognitive domain is the best known, but a complete answer mentions the other two.

The **affective domain** (Krathwohl, Bloom & Masia, 1964) describes growth in attitudes and values in
five levels: **receiving** (willing to attend), **responding** (participates actively), **valuing**
(attaches worth to something and shows it consistently), **organisation** (brings different values
together into a system) and **characterisation** by a value (the value governs the person's
behaviour). Example objective: "Pupils will volunteer to help keep the school water tank area clean"
(responding moving toward valuing).

The **psychomotor domain** covers physical skills. The committee did not complete it; several later
versions exist. Simpson's (1972) version moves from **perception** and **set** (readiness) through
**guided response**, **mechanism** (habitual performance) and **complex overt response**, to
**adaptation** and **origination**. Example: a Class 2 pupil forming Urdu letters first copies the
teacher's model with guidance, then writes fluently, then adapts the letter shapes when joining words.

### Using the taxonomy in the classroom

**Writing objectives.** An objective combines a level (the verb) with content, and ideally a condition
and a criterion. The course guide asks student teachers to write two lower-order and two higher-order
objectives for a Class 4 lesson on nutrition (HEC, 2012, Week 12). For example:

- Remember: Pupils will **list** the three main food groups.
- Understand: Pupils will **explain** in one sentence why each food group is needed.
- Analyse: Pupils will **compare** two lunch boxes and **identify** the missing food group.
- Create: Pupils will **design** a balanced meal using foods available in their local market.

"Pupils will know the food groups" or "pupils will understand nutrition" are not usable objectives,
because knowing and understanding cannot be seen. Choose a verb that names a visible action.

**Planning questions.** The same levels guide questioning (see A02). A lesson that asks only recall
questions teaches only recall.

**Designing assessment.** Test items should sample the levels named in the objectives. If every item
asks pupils to recall, pupils learn that recall is all that counts; a teacher who also writes items at
the analyse, evaluate and create levels gives pupils a reason to think, not only to memorise.

### Limits of the taxonomy

The taxonomy is a tool, not a law. The levels are not strictly cumulative in real learning: a pupil can
evaluate a simple argument before mastering every fact. Lower levels are not unimportant: without
remembered knowledge, higher-order thinking has nothing to work on. And the same task can sit at
different levels for different pupils: comparing two maps is analysis for a pupil seeing them for the
first time and mere recall for one who memorised the textbook's comparison. Use the taxonomy to plan a
range of thinking, not to rank pupils.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Explain** the six levels of the cognitive domain of Bloom's Taxonomy with a classroom
example for each, and **describe** two changes made in the 2001 revision.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) A Class 5 teacher's objectives for a lesson on the water cycle are: "Pupils will know the
water cycle" and "Pupils will understand evaporation." **Criticise** these objectives and **rewrite**
the lesson's objectives as four observable objectives, two lower-order and two higher-order, naming the
level of each.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Six levels in order | 3 | Knowledge, comprehension, application, analysis, synthesis, evaluation (or the revised names, if the candidate says so). Deduct 1 mark for wrong order. |
| Explanation and example for each level | 6 | One mark per level for an accurate meaning with a fitting classroom example. |
| Two changes in the 2001 revision | 4 | Two marks each for any two of: nouns to verbs; synthesis renamed create and moved above evaluate; knowledge dimension added (factual, conceptual, procedural, metacognitive). |
| Clarity and accuracy | 2 | Terms used precisely; no mixing of versions without comment. |

### CRQ 2

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Criticism | 4 | "Know" and "understand" are not observable, give no condition or criterion, and cannot guide assessment; both sit at low levels only. |
| Four rewritten objectives | 8 | Two marks each: observable verb, clear content about the water cycle, and a correct level label. Two must be lower-order (remember, understand, apply) and two higher-order (analyse, evaluate, create). |
| Quality | 3 | Objectives are realistic for Class 5 and at least one includes a condition or criterion. |
