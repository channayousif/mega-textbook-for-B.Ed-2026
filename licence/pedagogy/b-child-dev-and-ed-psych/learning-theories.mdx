# B09. Learning theories: behaviourism, cognitivism, constructivism and humanism

## What the test asks

The guide's learning unit covers the definition of learning, behaviourist, cognitive, social learning,
constructivist and brain-based theories, each with "a critique of its use in the classroom", then
"transfer of learning" and "factors affecting learning". Its outcomes are to compare theories, identify
one's own theory of learning, and "critique and justify which theories are best suited for students'
individual differences" (HEC, 2012, Educational Psychology, Unit 2). The licence objective names four
theories, including humanism. CRQs ask you to **compare** two theories, **apply** one to a lesson, or
**critique** a theory's classroom use.

Marks go to the view of learning each theory holds, the teacher's and learner's roles, a classroom
application and a limitation. Candidates lose marks by leaving out humanism, by describing theories with
no application, and by giving no critique when the question asks for one.

- [EFMP-301 Topic 3.2 - The cognitive turn: Piaget and Bruner](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-03/topic-02) teaches cognitivism: schemas, assimilation and accommodation, and Bruner's representation, discovery and spiral curriculum.

## Explanation

### The humanist theory of learning

Humanism sees the learner as a whole person, feelings as well as intellect, with a natural drive to grow.
Two ideas carry it into the classroom.

**Maslow: needs before learning.** Maslow (1943) proposed that basic needs (physiological, safety, love and
belonging, esteem) must be reasonably met before the need for self-actualisation can drive behaviour. In
learning terms, a hungry, frightened or rejected child is not free to learn. Your degree's Topic 6.1
teaches the hierarchy.

**Rogers: significant learning and the facilitator.** Rogers (1969) distinguished learning that involves the
intellect only, with no personal meaning, from **significant (experiential) learning**, which is self-initiated, involves the
whole person, makes a difference to the learner, and is evaluated by the learner. The teacher is a
**facilitator** who shows genuineness, acceptance ("prizing") and empathic understanding.

**In the classroom.** Choice of topics or ways to show learning; lessons linked to pupils' lives and
feelings; a climate where mistakes are safe; self-assessment and reflection. A Class 7 Urdu teacher in
Sanghar lets pupils choose to write a letter, a story or a speech on the same theme, discusses feelings in
the texts, and asks each pupil to judge their draft against shared criteria before she marks it.

**Critique.** Humanism is hard to measure, can conflict with a fixed syllabus and examinations, and its key
terms (self-actualisation) are broad. It says little about how knowledge is actually built.

### Brain-based learning and its critique

"Brain-based learning" is a family of claims that teaching should follow how the brain learns. Caine and
Caine (1991) set out principles such as: the brain seeks meaning and patterns, emotions are critical to
learning, learning involves both focused attention and peripheral perception, and learning is enhanced by
challenge and inhibited by threat. Several of these agree with cognitive psychology and give sensible
advice: connect new ideas to what pupils know, keep the climate safe, use varied and active tasks.

The critique is serious. Bruer (1997) argued that the bridge from brain science directly to classroom
practice was "a bridge too far": most useful advice comes from cognitive psychology, not from brain
imaging. Howard-Jones (2014) documents **neuromyths** that many teachers believe, such as teaching to
preferred "learning styles", "left-brained and right-brained" learners, and the claim that we use only
10 per cent of our brains. None is supported. A teacher should ask, of any "brain-based" product, what
evidence from classrooms supports it.

### Transfer of learning

**Transfer** is the use of something learned in one context in another. It is the purpose of schooling,
and it often fails: a pupil who can do sums in the notebook cannot work out change in the bazaar.

- **Positive transfer**: earlier learning helps new learning (knowing Urdu script helps with reading Arabic script).
- **Negative transfer**: earlier learning interferes (a rule from one language misapplied in another).
- **Zero transfer**: no effect.
- **Near and far transfer**: to closely similar or to quite different situations (Perkins & Salomon, 1992).
- **Low-road and high-road transfer**: low-road transfer happens automatically when a well-practised skill meets a similar situation; high-road transfer needs deliberate abstraction of a principle and a mindful search for connections (Perkins & Salomon, 1992).

**Teaching for transfer.** Perkins and Salomon (1992) name two strategies. **Hugging**: make learning
conditions resemble the situations where it will be used (practise measurement with real objects and
real prices). **Bridging**: help pupils abstract the principle and ask where else it applies ("Where
else do we share things equally?"). Varied examples, explaining why and not only how, and reflecting on
what was learned all support transfer.

### The four theories: a compact critique

| Theory | Strength in class | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Behaviourism | Clear objectives, practice, feedback, managing behaviour | Ignores understanding; rewards can undermine interest |
| Cognitivism | Explains understanding, memory and misconceptions | Can neglect emotion and social context |
| Constructivism | Active, meaningful learning built on prior knowledge | Time-consuming; weak guidance can leave novices lost |
| Humanism | Motivation, climate, the whole child | Hard to measure; tension with fixed syllabus and exams |

The degree's Topic 3.4 makes the key point: a skilled teacher chooses and combines theories to suit the
learner and the task.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Compare** the behaviourist and humanist theories of learning, and **explain** how each would
shape a Class 5 lesson on writing.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) **Explain** transfer of learning with its types, and **suggest** three strategies a
mathematics teacher can use to promote positive transfer.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Behaviourism | 3 | Learning as change in observable behaviour through reinforcement; teacher controls conditions. |
| Humanism | 3 | Whole person, needs (Maslow), significant learning and facilitator role (Rogers). |
| Comparison | 3 | At least two explicit contrasts (external reward versus intrinsic growth; teacher-directed versus learner-directed; performance versus meaning). |
| Behaviourist lesson | 3 | Clear steps, model, practice, immediate feedback and reinforcement. |
| Humanist lesson | 3 | Choice of topic or form, personal relevance, safe climate, self-assessment. |

### CRQ 2

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | 2 | Use of learning from one context in another. |
| Types | 5 | Positive, negative, zero; near and far; low-road and high-road, with brief examples. |
| Three strategies | 6 | E.g. hugging (realistic practice), bridging (abstracting the principle), varied examples, explaining why, reflection (2 marks each). |
| Mathematics context | 2 | Strategies set in believable mathematics situations. |
