# B07. Basics of educational psychology: different theories

## What the test asks

The Educational Psychology guide's Unit 1 covers the nature and scope of educational psychology, how it
differs from psychology, "conceptual approaches to psychology", and the schools of thought:
structuralism and functionalism, cognitivism, behaviourist, humanistic and constructivist theory, Gestalt
psychology and psychoanalysis, ending with "why do we study educational psychology?" Its outcomes ask
student teachers to explain each school's "implication for teaching and learning" and its "advantages and
disadvantages" (HEC, 2012, Educational Psychology, Unit 1). CRQs ask you to **explain** or **compare**
schools, **identify** the school behind a classroom practice, or **evaluate** one school's usefulness.

Marks go to the founder, the key idea and a classroom implication for each school. Candidates lose marks
by mixing up structuralism and functionalism, by reducing psychoanalysis to "Freud said everything is
unconscious", and by giving no weakness when asked to evaluate.

## Explanation

### Why schools of thought matter to a teacher

A "school" is a group of psychologists who share a view of what psychology should study and how. Each
school noticed something real about learners, and each left something out. The guide's sample session
asks student teachers to read one case through each school and see how the explanation changes (HEC,
2012, Unit 1). That is the skill: seeing a pupil through more than one lens.

### Structuralism: the parts of consciousness

Wilhelm Wundt, often called the first psychologist, set up a laboratory to study conscious experience,
mainly through **introspection**, "a process by which someone examines their own conscious experience".
His student Edward Titchener developed **structuralism**, which "focused on the contents of mental
processes rather than their function" (Spielman et al., 2020): breaking experience into basic sensations,
images and feelings.

*For teaching:* it made psychology a subject of systematic study and showed that learners' inner
experience matters. *Weakness:* introspection is subjective and cannot be checked; it tells us little
about children who cannot describe their own thinking.

### Functionalism: what the mind is for

William James and the functionalists, influenced by Darwin, asked what mental activity is **for**: how it
helps a person adapt to the environment (Spielman et al., 2020). Attention, memory and habit matter
because of what they let us do. Functionalism fed directly into education through John Dewey, who argued
that learning should come from experience and solving real problems (Dewey, 1938).

*For teaching:* learning should be useful and connected to life; habits can be built. *Weakness:* it is
broad and less precise as a method.

### Gestalt psychology: the whole and insight

Max Wertheimer, Kurt Koffka and Wolfgang Köhler argued that "how those parts relate to each other as a
whole is often what the individual responds to" (Spielman et al., 2020). The whole is more than the sum
of its parts: a tune is still recognised in a different key. Köhler's (1925) studies with chimpanzees
described **insight learning**: after a period of apparent failure, the animal suddenly saw how sticks or
boxes could be combined to reach food, a reorganisation of the whole problem rather than slow trial and
error.

*For teaching:* present the whole before the parts (the full story, then its vocabulary; the complete
map, then its regions), help pupils see patterns and relationships, and give problems that allow
insight. *Weakness:* concepts like insight are hard to measure, and the school says less about slow skill
learning.

### Psychoanalysis: the unconscious

Sigmund Freud proposed that much behaviour comes from the **unconscious mind**, "a repository of feelings
and urges of which we have no awareness" (Spielman et al., 2020). Freud also described personality as
the interplay of the id (basic urges), the ego (reality) and the superego (conscience), and stressed the
importance of early childhood experience. Erikson later reshaped this into a psychosocial theory (see
[B01](/licence/pedagogy/b-child-dev-and-ed-psych/theories-and-domains)).

*For teaching:* behaviour may have causes the pupil cannot explain; early experience and emotion shape
learning; teachers should look behind misbehaviour and offer emotional security. *Weakness:* many of its
claims are difficult to test scientifically.

### Behaviourism and cognitivism in one line each

**Behaviourism** (Watson, Pavlov, Skinner) studies observable behaviour shaped by associations and
consequences. **Cognitivism** (Piaget, Bruner, information processing) studies mental processes such as
thinking, memory and understanding. Your degree teaches both in depth (links above).

### Humanism: the whole person

Abraham Maslow and Carl Rogers reacted against both behaviourism and psychoanalysis. They saw people as
whole beings with a natural drive to grow. Maslow proposed a hierarchy of needs leading to
self-actualisation; Rogers stressed "unconditional positive regard, genuineness, and empathy" (Spielman et
al., 2020) and carried them into education as the teacher's role of **facilitator** (Rogers, 1969).

*For teaching:* a caring climate, choice and personal meaning. *Weakness:* broad concepts that are hard
to measure. The learning-theory side of humanism is on [B09](/licence/pedagogy/b-child-dev-and-ed-psych/learning-theories).

### Integrating the schools

The guide's final outcome is that teachers "consider and successfully integrate aspects of different
approaches" (HEC, 2012, Unit 1). In a Class 6 in Nawabshah, a pupil who will not attempt a geometry
problem might be helped by seeing the whole figure first (Gestalt), by a calm, accepting response to
errors (humanism), by praise for each attempt (behaviourism) and by a question that links the problem to
what she knows (cognitivism).

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Compare** structuralism and functionalism, and **explain** how functionalism influenced
education.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) **Explain** the key ideas of Gestalt psychology and psychoanalysis, and **suggest** one
classroom implication and one limitation of each.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Structuralism | 4 | Wundt and Titchener; contents or structure of consciousness; introspection. |
| Functionalism | 4 | William James; purpose or function of mental activity in adapting to the environment; Darwin's influence. |
| Comparison | 3 | At least two explicit contrasts (structure versus function; introspection of elements versus adaptive use). |
| Influence on education | 4 | Dewey and learning through experience and real problems; useful, life-connected learning; habit formation. |

### CRQ 2

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Gestalt key ideas | 4 | Wertheimer, Koffka, Köhler; the whole and relationships between parts; insight learning (Köhler's chimpanzees). |
| Psychoanalysis key ideas | 4 | Freud; the unconscious; id, ego, superego; importance of early experience. |
| Implications | 4 | E.g. whole before parts, pattern-finding (Gestalt); looking behind behaviour, emotional security (psychoanalysis). 2 marks each. |
| Limitations | 3 | E.g. insight hard to measure; psychoanalytic claims hard to test. 1.5 marks each. |
