# B02. Characteristics of developmental stages

## What the test asks

The guide works through early childhood (infant, toddler, preschool), the primary years (6-12) and
adolescence, asking at each stage what "major theorists said" and what it means for the teacher (HEC,
2012, Child Development, Units 2-4). CRQs typically ask you to **describe** the characteristics of one
stage according to two theorists, **compare** how Piaget and Erikson describe the same age, or
**analyse** a pupil's behaviour and **identify** the stage it fits.

Marks go to correct stage names and approximate ages, to characteristics in more than one domain, and to
a teaching implication. Candidates lose marks by reciting Piaget alone, by treating ages as deadlines,
and by leaving out the critiques the guide asks for, especially of adolescence as a category.

Those topics give you Piaget and the physical picture. This page adds Erikson's stages from infancy to
adolescence, the characteristics of each age band across theorists, identity formation, and the
critiques of stage theories.

## Explanation

### Erikson's stages from birth to adolescence

Erikson described each stage as a crisis between two poles. The ages below follow Spielman et al. (2020);
the guide gives slightly different ranges, which is a reminder that the ages are approximate.

- **Trust vs. mistrust (0-1).** The infant learns whether basic needs will be met. Reliable care builds trust.
- **Autonomy vs. shame and doubt (1-3).** The toddler wants to do things alone: feed, dress, choose. Harsh control produces doubt.
- **Initiative vs. guilt (3-6).** The preschooler plans games and activities. Constant criticism of their plans breeds guilt.
- **Industry vs. inferiority (7-11).** The school-age child wants to be competent, to finish work and be recognised for it. Repeated failure and comparison produce inferiority.
- **Identity vs. role confusion (12-18).** The adolescent asks "who am I?" and tries out roles, values and futures (Erikson, 1963; Spielman et al., 2020).

### Characteristics by age band

**Infancy and toddlerhood (0-3).** Rapid physical growth and gross motor milestones; sensorimotor
thinking (knowing through the senses and action); attachment to caregivers; the first "no" of autonomy.
The guide's point for later teachers is that a child arrives at school having already learned a great
deal (HEC, 2012, Unit 2).

**Preschool (3-6).** Preoperational thinking led by appearances, symbolic and pretend play, a fast-growing
vocabulary, initiative in play, and beginning self-control. Language is the main tool of thinking at
this age, which is why Vygotsky stressed adult talk.

**Primary school-age (6-12).** Steady physical growth and improving fine motor control; concrete
operational thinking; Erikson's **industry**, a drive to make and complete things (the guide's
"industriousness"); a growing, more stable self-concept; friendships built on loyalty; and peers who
increasingly matter. The guide stresses "the whole child" and the use of play in the classroom (HEC,
2012, Unit 3).

**Adolescence (12-18).** Puberty and a growth spurt at different times for different pupils; the start of
formal operational (abstract, hypothetical) thinking; identity work; a peer group that becomes a main
reference point; and the gradual growth of self-regulation (HEC, 2012, Unit 4).

### Identity formation in adolescence

James Marcia (1966) turned Erikson's identity stage into four **identity statuses**, based on whether the
young person has explored options and whether they have committed to one:

| Status | Explored? | Committed? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diffusion | No | No | "I don't know what I want and I'm not thinking about it." |
| Foreclosure | No | Yes | Accepts the family's plan for him without question. |
| Moratorium | Yes | Not yet | Actively trying out interests and asking questions. |
| Achievement | Yes | Yes | Has explored and chosen a direction of his or her own. |

The guide's vignette of Rabiah, a good student who plays football with the boys and questions what she
is taught, is a moratorium picture: exploration, not defiance (HEC, 2012, Unit 4). A teacher who
punishes every question may push a pupil towards foreclosure or diffusion; one who gives safe spaces for
questions, debate and responsibility supports identity achievement.

### Critiques of stage theories

- **Stages describe, they do not prescribe.** The guide warns that "ages and stages" say what children tend to do, "not prescriptive of what every child should do", and that many children show traits of two stages at once (HEC, 2012, Summary of essential knowledge).
- **Culture.** A listed misconception is that development is "fixed and invariant across cultures" rather than culturally situated (HEC, 2012). A teacher should therefore check a theorist's description against the children actually in the room, and read a family's expectations as part of the child's context rather than as an obstacle to "normal" development.
- **Adolescence as a category.** The guide asks in what ways adolescence might be "socially constructed". The old view of adolescence as universal "storm and stress" has been revised: conflict with parents, mood swings and risk-taking are more likely in adolescence but are not universal, and they vary across individuals and cultures (Arnett, 1999).
- **Continuity.** Many psychologists now see much development as gradual rather than as clear-cut jumps between stages; see [B05](/licence/pedagogy/b-child-dev-and-ed-psych/conceptions-of-development).

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Compare** how Piaget and Erikson describe children aged 6 to 12, and **explain** two
implications of their descriptions for a Class 4 teacher.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) A Class 8 girl has started questioning rules and trying new activities. **Analyse** her
behaviour using Erikson's identity stage and Marcia's statuses, and **evaluate** the claim that
adolescence is always a time of "storm and stress".

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Piaget | 4 | Concrete operational stage; logical thinking about real objects, conservation, reversibility, classification; limits with abstraction. |
| Erikson | 4 | Industry vs. inferiority; drive for competence and recognition; risk of inferiority from repeated failure or comparison. |
| Comparison | 3 | Cognitive versus psychosocial focus; both see the age as a time of growing competence; one similarity and one difference made explicit. |
| Two implications | 4 | E.g. concrete materials before symbols; tasks sized so every pupil can finish and be recognised; avoid public ranking (2 marks each, justified). |

### CRQ 2

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Erikson's identity stage | 3 | Identity vs. role confusion; trying roles, values and futures is the task of the stage. |
| Marcia's statuses | 4 | Names the four statuses with the explore/commit logic and places the girl, most likely in moratorium, with a reason. |
| Teacher response | 3 | Supports exploration safely: discussion, responsibility, respectful answers to questions rather than punishment. |
| Evaluation of "storm and stress" | 5 | Recognises some increase in conflict, mood change or risk-taking, but not universal; individual and cultural variation; concludes with a reasoned judgement. |
