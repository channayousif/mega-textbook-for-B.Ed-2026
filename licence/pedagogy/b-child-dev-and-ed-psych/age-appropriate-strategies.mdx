# B06. Age-appropriate teaching strategies

## What the test asks

The guide asks student teachers to learn "developmentally appropriate practices" for toddlers and
preschoolers, to use play in the primary classroom, to understand motivation and self-regulation in
adolescence, and to "design age-appropriate classroom tasks and assessments" (HEC, 2012, Child
Development, Units 2-4). A CRQ will usually ask you to **design** or **suggest** strategies for a named
age group and **justify** each from a theory, or to **evaluate** whether an activity is developmentally
appropriate.

Marks go to the match between the strategy, the age and the theory, with a concrete example. Candidates
lose marks by giving generic strategies ("use group work") that would suit any age, by naming a theory
with no link to the strategy, and by forgetting the constraints of a real Pakistani classroom.

- [EFMP-301 Topic 3.2 - The cognitive turn: Piaget and Bruner](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-03/topic-02) teaches Bruner's enactive-iconic-symbolic route, discovery learning and the spiral curriculum.
- [EFMP-301 Topic 3.4 - Constructivist approaches and the classroom](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-03/topic-04) teaches the zone of proximal development and scaffolding.

Those topics give you the general principles, mostly for the primary years. This page adds
developmentally appropriate practice and its critics, and concrete strategies for early childhood,
the primary years and adolescence, each tied to a theory.

## Explanation

### Developmentally appropriate practice

**Developmentally appropriate practice (DAP)** is an approach, set out by the US National Association
for the Education of Young Children, in which teaching decisions rest on what is known about how children
develop and learn. The guide's reading list includes Bredekamp and Copple's DAP book. The 2020 position
statement frames decisions around three core considerations: **commonality** (what is generally true of
children's development and learning), **individuality** (each child's characteristics and experiences) and
**context** (the social and cultural setting in which development and learning occur) (NAEYC, 2020).

The guide asks instructors to point out that "the concept of 'developmentally appropriate' has been
criticized", and to connect it with Bruner's scaffolding, which "may help children go beyond what we
traditionally expect" (HEC, 2012, Unit 2). Two criticisms are worth knowing. If age norms are applied
rigidly, DAP can set expectations too low. And early versions leaned on one cultural picture of childhood;
NAEYC's 2020 statement itself gives much more weight to context. The guide also asks how DAP can work in
"crowded conditions or poor facilities", which is the question a Sindh teacher must answer.

### Early childhood (about 3-6): learning through play and talk

- **Play-based activities** that combine the three domains the guide names (physical, cognitive, emotional): building with blocks, sorting seeds, shop and house role-play. *Theory:* preoperational thinking works through action and symbols in play (Piaget); play is where young children practise social rules.
- **Choice within routines.** Let children choose between two activities and do small tasks alone. *Theory:* Erikson's autonomy and initiative; criticism of every plan breeds doubt and guilt.
- **Rich talk.** Describe what children do, ask open questions, tell stories and rhymes in the home language. *Theory:* for Vygotsky, language is the main tool of thought.
- **Short, active episodes.** Attention spans are short, so alternate sitting and moving.

### The primary years (about 6-12): concrete, competent, cooperative

- **Concrete materials before symbols**, then pictures, then numerals and words. *Theory:* concrete operations (Piaget) and the enactive-iconic-symbolic route (Bruner).
- **Tasks that can be finished and recognised**: projects, charts, class displays, tasks in small steps. *Theory:* Erikson's industry versus inferiority; the guide's "industriousness".
- **Structured pair and group work** with clear roles. *Theory:* peers matter more at this age, and the zone of proximal development can be reached with a more able peer.
- **Play in the classroom**: games for practising tables or spelling, drama for stories. The guide gives a whole session to "utilizing play in the classroom" (HEC, 2012, Unit 3).
- **Private recognition of effort and progress** rather than public ranking, which feeds inferiority.

### Adolescence (about 12-16): reasoning, identity and self-regulation

- **Hypothetical and abstract tasks**: "what would happen if...", debates, designing a fair test. *Theory:* the start of formal operations (Piaget).
- **Real choices and responsibility**: choice of project topic, class roles, helping to set rules. *Theory:* Erikson's identity stage; adolescents need to try out roles and values.
- **Peer learning used deliberately**: structured discussion groups, peer review. *Theory:* the peer group becomes a main reference point.
- **Teaching self-regulation.** Zimmerman (2002) describes self-regulated learning as a cycle of **forethought** (setting goals and planning), **performance** (monitoring and using strategies) and **self-reflection** (judging results and adjusting). Teach it explicitly: pupils set a weekly goal, keep a simple progress log, and write two lines about what worked.
- **Respect in correction.** Public humiliation damages identity and relationships at this age; speak privately.

### Adapting to large, under-resourced classes

In a Class 2 of 60 in a Jacobabad government school with one blackboard, DAP still applies. Concrete
materials can be free (stones, seeds, bottle caps, sticks). Play can happen in rows (clapping games,
number chants, turn-to-your-partner). Choice can be as simple as "choose question A or B". Grouping by
bench makes pair work possible without moving furniture. The principle is the match between task and
child, not the cost of the materials.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Explain** developmentally appropriate practice and **evaluate** one criticism of it. **Describe**
how a teacher could apply it in a crowded Class 1 with few resources.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) **Suggest** three teaching strategies for Class 7 pupils and **justify** each from a
developmental theory. Include one strategy that develops self-regulation.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| DAP explained | 4 | Decisions based on knowledge of development; commonality, individuality and context. |
| Criticism evaluated | 4 | E.g. rigid use of age norms lowers expectations, or cultural narrowness; with a balanced judgement (scaffolding, context). |
| Application in a crowded Class 1 | 7 | At least three realistic practices (free concrete materials, play and chants in rows, choice, short active episodes, home-language talk), each linked to the child's development. |

### CRQ 2

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Three strategies | 6 | Specific and suited to about 12-13 year olds (2 marks each). |
| Theoretical justification | 6 | Each tied correctly to a theory, e.g. formal operations, identity, peer group, ZPD (2 marks each). |
| Self-regulation | 3 | A strategy that makes pupils set goals, monitor and reflect (e.g. Zimmerman's cycle). |
