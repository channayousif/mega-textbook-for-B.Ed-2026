# B01. Major theories and domains of child development

## What the test asks

The Child Development guide opens with "the key models, theorists, and debates in child development",
presented as "a holistic process": psychosocial models, behaviourism and socio-cultural models,
cognitive models, and the factors that affect the child (HEC, 2012, Child Development, Unit 1). A CRQ
on this objective usually asks you to **name and explain** two or three theories, **compare** them, or
**identify** the domains of development and **show** how they interact in one child.

Marks go to accurate theorist-concept pairs (Erikson with psychosocial crises, Vygotsky with the zone of
proximal development, Piaget with stages of thinking) and to a classroom implication for each.
Candidates lose marks by listing theorists with no concepts, by treating the domains as separate
boxes, and by forgetting that the question says **child development**, not learning in general.

## Where your degree teaches this

- [EFMP-301 Topic 2.1 - What development is, and the principles it follows](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-02/topic-01) teaches growth, maturation and learning, the principles of development, and heredity and environment as joint drivers.
- [EFMP-301 Topic 2.3 - Cognitive, emotional and social development](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-02/topic-03) teaches the cognitive, emotional and social domains and Piaget's four stages.
- [EFMP-301 Topic 3.1 - Learning as behaviour: Pavlov and Skinner](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-03/topic-01) teaches classical and operant conditioning, the behaviourist model.
- [EFMP-301 Topic 3.3 - Learning from others: Bandura's social learning theory](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-03/topic-03) teaches observational learning and modelling.
- [EFMP-301 Topic 3.4 - Constructivist approaches and the classroom](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-03/topic-04) teaches Vygotsky's zone of proximal development and scaffolding, the socio-cultural model.

Those topics teach the cognitive, behaviourist, social learning and socio-cultural models well. This
page adds what they leave out: the domains as one holistic framework, Erikson's psychosocial model as
a whole, Bronfenbrenner's ecological model, and a way to compare the theory families.

## Explanation

### The domains of development, read holistically

A **domain** is an area of development that can be described on its own: **physical** (body, motor
skills, health), **cognitive** (thinking, memory, language and reasoning), **social** (relationships
with family, peers and teachers) and **emotional** (feelings, self-concept and their regulation). The
guide uses four domains in its early childhood unit and asks student teachers to look at the child "as
a whole" rather than divide growth "into domains (mind and body)" (HEC, 2012, Unit 2).

The two ideas are not in conflict. The domains are a tool for **observing**; the holistic view is a rule
for **interpreting**. A Class 2 pupil in a Mirpurkhas school who has not eaten breakfast (physical) cannot
attend to a new word (cognitive), snaps at her partner (social) and cries when corrected (emotional). One
cause shows up in four domains. A teacher who reads only the cognitive domain concludes she is "weak";
a teacher who reads across domains sends her to the school's midday meal first.

### Psychosocial theory: Erikson

Erik Erikson "followed Sigmund Freud's psychoanalytic theory but altered it" (HEC, 2012, Summary of
essential knowledge). Where Freud stressed biological drives, Erikson stressed the **social** world. He
described eight life stages, each built around a **crisis** or developmental task, such as trust versus
mistrust in infancy or industry versus inferiority in the primary years (Erikson, 1963; Spielman et al.,
2020). The way the crisis is resolved affects the person's later social, psychological and cognitive
development.

Two implications are named in the guide: children need support to resolve each task (a child who has
not learned to trust adults at home may not trust the teacher either), and classrooms need
"opportunities for private thinking, interaction with materials, and social interaction". The stages
themselves, with their ages and classroom signs, are on [B02](/licence/pedagogy/b-child-dev-and-ed-psych/developmental-stages).

### The behaviourist model in development: Watson

Your degree teaches Pavlov and Skinner. The guide adds **John B. Watson**, who held that directly
observable stimuli and responses, not the mind, should be studied, and that children could be shaped in
any direction if adults controlled the associations. His "Little Albert" experiment (with Rosalie Rayner)
conditioned an infant to fear a white rat by pairing it with a loud noise; later investigation raised
serious questions about its ethics and validity (HEC, 2012, Summary of essential knowledge). For child
development, the lesson is that early fears and habits can be learned by association, and that a view of
children as wholly shaped from outside leaves out their own activity.

### The ecological model: Bronfenbrenner

Urie Bronfenbrenner (1979) argued that a child develops inside a set of nested systems, and that
development cannot be understood by studying the child alone.

- **Microsystem**: the settings the child is in directly, such as the family, the classroom, the peer group and the mosque or madrasa.
- **Mesosystem**: the links between microsystems, such as how often parents and teachers talk.
- **Exosystem**: settings the child is not in but which affect him, such as a parent's workplace or the district education office.
- **Macrosystem**: the wider culture, values, laws and economy, such as beliefs about girls' education.
- **Chronosystem**: change over time, both in the child's life (a new sibling, a move to the city) and in history. Bronfenbrenner (1986) added this dimension later.

The classroom value is diagnostic. When a Class 6 boy in Larkana stops doing homework, the microsystem
question is what changed at home or in class; the mesosystem question is whether his parents and teacher
have spoken at all; the exosystem question may be that his father's shift work now keeps the house awake
at night. A teacher can rarely change the outer systems but can nearly always strengthen the mesosystem.

### Comparing the theory families

| Family | Key theorists | Driver of development | Teacher's role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Psychosocial | Erikson | Resolving social crises across the lifespan | Support each stage's task, e.g. trust, industry, identity |
| Behaviourist and social learning | Watson, Skinner, Bandura | Associations, consequences and models | Arrange consequences; model the behaviour wanted |
| Cognitive | Piaget | The child's active reorganising of thinking | Match tasks to the stage of thinking |
| Socio-cultural | Vygotsky | Interaction with more skilled others and language | Teach in the zone of proximal development; scaffold |
| Ecological | Bronfenbrenner | Nested systems around the child | Build links between home, school and community |

No single family explains a child. The guide's point in presenting development as holistic is that a
teacher chooses the lens that fits the question and keeps the others in view.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Explain** the four domains of child development and **show**, with one example of a Class 3
pupil, how a difficulty in one domain can affect the other three.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) **Describe** Bronfenbrenner's ecological model and **compare** it with Erikson's psychosocial
theory. **Suggest** two actions a primary teacher could take based on the ecological model.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Domains defined | 4 | Physical, cognitive, social and emotional, each with a correct one-line meaning (1 mark each). |
| Holistic principle | 3 | States that domains interact and the child develops as a whole; domains are a tool for observing, not separate compartments. |
| Worked example | 6 | One believable Class 3 case in which a single cause shows in all four domains, with each link explained (up to 1.5 marks per domain). |
| Teaching implication | 2 | A response that addresses the cause across domains, not only the academic symptom. |

### CRQ 2

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Ecological model | 5 | Child at the centre; micro-, meso-, exo- and macrosystem with examples; chronosystem as change over time. |
| Erikson | 3 | Psychosocial stages, each with a crisis; resolution shapes later development. |
| Comparison | 3 | At least two explicit contrasts, e.g. stages over time versus systems around the child; inner crisis versus environmental context; both stress the social world. |
| Two actions | 4 | Two feasible actions, e.g. regular parent contact (mesosystem), learning about home circumstances before judging a pupil, each justified from the model (2 marks each). |
