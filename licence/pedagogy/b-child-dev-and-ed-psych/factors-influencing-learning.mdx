# B03. Factors influencing a child's learning

## What the test asks

The guide asks student teachers to "identify factors influencing the learning process" and spends a whole
unit on how "families, society, schools, and teachers influence child development": the nuclear and
extended family, community and culture, gender balance, the media, school, peers and teachers (HEC,
2012, Child Development, Units 3 and 6). The Educational Psychology guide adds "factors affecting
learning" to its learning unit. Expect CRQs that ask you to **classify** factors, **explain** how a
named factor works, or **analyse** a case and **propose** what the teacher can influence.

Marks go to a clear classification (within the child, home, school, wider society), to a mechanism for
each factor (how it affects learning, not only that it does), and to realistic teacher action.
Candidates lose marks by writing an unsorted list, by blaming families, and by ignoring the factors the
teacher controls.

## Explanation

### A framework: four layers of factors

It helps to sort factors from the child outwards, which follows Bronfenbrenner's (1979) nested systems
(see [B01](/licence/pedagogy/b-child-dev-and-ed-psych/theories-and-domains)).

1. **Within the child**: maturation and readiness, physical health, nutrition and sleep, sight and hearing, prior knowledge, emotional state, motivation and self-concept.
2. **The home**: family structure, parenting style, the language spoken at home, parents' expectations and involvement, and the material resources of the household.
3. **The school**: the teacher, methods, classroom climate, peers, and the physical conditions of the room.
4. **The wider society**: culture and community values, gender expectations and the media.

The first layer and part of the third are what the teacher sees daily; the others explain much of what
the teacher sees.

### Physical health and well-being

The guide gives the primary years a session on "encouraging healthy physical development" and links
health and nutrition to thinking (HEC, 2012, Units 2 and 3). The mechanism is simple: a child who is
hungry, tired, ill or cannot see the board has less attention and energy for learning. Uncorrected
problems with vision or hearing are easily misread as inattention or low ability. The teacher cannot
treat them but can notice, seat the child sensibly and refer.

### The family

**Structure.** The guide names "the nuclear and extended family". In an extended family a child may have
grandparents, uncles and cousins who tell stories, help with homework or share care, and several adults
whose expectations shape him. In a nuclear family the parents carry more of this alone. Neither is
better in itself; what matters is the time, talk and warmth available to the child.

**Parenting style.** Diana Baumrind described parenting along two lines, warmth and control, producing
styles now usually named **authoritative** (reasonable demands and consistent limits with warmth),
**authoritarian** (strict, valuing obedience), **permissive** (warm but few demands) and **uninvolved**
(indifferent) (Baumrind, 1971; Spielman et al., 2020). Authoritative parenting is associated with good
outcomes in many studies, but "the authoritative style is not necessarily preferred or appropriate in all
cultures" (Spielman et al., 2020). A teacher should not judge a family by one Western template.

**Expectations and involvement.** Parents who talk with children, read or tell stories, ask about school
and expect effort give the child both language and a reason to try. Parents with little schooling can
still do most of this, which is why teachers' advice to families should be about talk and interest, not
about teaching the syllabus at home.

### Culture, community and gender

A child's community tells him what learning is for, which subjects matter, and who is expected to do what.
The guide's session on "gender balance" asks student teachers to examine the stereotypes they bring and
whether teachers are aware of gender balance as an issue (HEC, 2012, Unit 6). In a classroom this appears
as who is asked the hard questions, who is praised for neatness and who for thinking, and who is sent to
fetch water. Unequal expectations become unequal learning over time.

### The media

The guide includes the "influence of media" in its society unit. Social learning theory explains the
mechanism: children imitate models they observe, including models on a screen, and they notice what
happens to those models (Bandura, Ross & Ross, 1961). Media can teach vocabulary, general knowledge and
positive models as well as aggression or stereotypes. What matters is content, time and whether an adult
talks with the child about what was watched.

### School, peers and teachers

The guide ends its Unit 6 with "teachers' influence on child development" and "schools, families, and
communities as partners". The teacher's expectations, methods and relationships are the factors most
under professional control. Peers matter more with age: a class where trying is admired pulls children
up, one where trying is mocked pushes them down.

**A Sindh example.** In a Class 5 in Thatta, Asif's marks fall after his family moves in with his
grandparents. Within the child: he is tired. Home: the new house is crowded, but his grandfather tells him
stories every night. School: he now sits at the back and his new friends tease pupils who answer. The
teacher cannot change the house, but can move his seat, give him a responsibility that earns respect,
build on the grandfather's stories in Urdu lessons, and meet the family.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Classify** the factors that influence a child's learning into those within the child, the
home, the school and the wider society. **Explain** how two of them, one from the home and one from the
school, affect learning.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) **Explain** Baumrind's parenting styles and **discuss** how a teacher should use this idea
when working with families in a Sindh community.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Classification | 6 | Four layers named, each with at least two correct examples (1.5 marks per layer). |
| Home factor explained | 4 | A named home factor (e.g. parenting style, language at home, expectations) with the mechanism by which it affects learning. |
| School factor explained | 4 | A named school factor (e.g. teacher expectations, peers, climate) with its mechanism. |
| Teacher's sphere of control | 1 | Notes which factors the teacher can influence directly. |

### CRQ 2

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Four styles | 6 | Authoritative, authoritarian, permissive and uninvolved, each correctly described on warmth and control (1.5 marks each). |
| Link to learning | 3 | Plausible effect of style on the child's confidence, self-regulation or motivation. |
| Cultural caution | 3 | Recognises that effective styles vary by culture and the teacher must not judge families by one template. |
| Practical use | 3 | Respectful actions: listening to families, suggesting talk and interest at home, joint goals; no blaming. |
