# B11. Learning and motivation at home, in school and in the community

## What the test asks

The guide's motivation unit asks student teachers to "identify how learning and motivation are shaped by
social contexts in homes, school, and communities", to analyse Maslow's theory for their teaching, and to
"identify success seekers and failure avoiders in their own classrooms" (HEC, 2012, Educational
Psychology, Unit 4). CRQs ask you to **explain** how one context shapes motivation, **analyse** a case of
a demotivated pupil across contexts, or **propose** a plan that links home, school and community.

Marks go to specific mechanisms in each context (not "parents should support"), to naming the motivational
pattern (for example, failure avoidance), and to realistic actions a teacher can take. Candidates lose
marks by blaming the home and by ignoring what the school itself does to motivation.

## Where your degree teaches this

- [EFMP-301 Topic 6.1 - Why learners try: theories of motivation](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-06/topic-01) teaches intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, the overjustification effect, Maslow, Herzberg and self-determination theory.
- [EFMP-301 Topic 6.2 - Achievement motivation and emotions in learning](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-06/topic-02) teaches mastery, performance and failure-avoidant goals, attributions, and anxiety, interest and curiosity.

Those topics give you the theories of motivation. This page adds what the guide asks about contexts:
how home, school and community each shape motivation, the success seeker and failure avoider
distinction, and how the three contexts can work together.

## Explanation

### Success seekers and failure avoiders

Covington's (1992) **self-worth theory** holds that pupils are driven to protect their sense of worth,
which school often ties to ability as shown by marks. This produces recognisable types.

- **Success seekers (success-oriented)** value learning, attribute results to effort and strategy, and treat failure as information. They choose moderately hard tasks.
- **Failure avoiders** fear that failure will show they lack ability. They protect themselves by not trying ("I didn't study, so it doesn't count"), making excuses, procrastinating, or choosing tasks that are too easy or impossibly hard.
- **Failure accepters** have concluded that they lack ability and have largely given up.

Your degree's Topic 6.2 describes failure-avoidant goals and attributions. The guide asks you to spot these
pupils in your own class: the Class 8 boy who says loudly he "doesn't care" before every test and hands in
a blank page is protecting himself, not showing laziness.

### The home

- **Expectations and values.** Children absorb what their family believes education is for and whether effort pays. High but realistic expectations, expressed as interest rather than threat, support motivation.
- **Involvement.** Asking about the school day, listening to reading, and providing a time and place for homework matter more than parents' own schooling.
- **Basic needs.** Food, sleep, safety and affection come first, as Maslow's hierarchy shows.
- **Style of control.** Warm, consistent guidance supports self-motivation more than harsh punishment for low marks, which can breed failure avoidance.

### The school

- **Classroom goal structure.** Ames (1992) showed that classrooms signal what success means. A **mastery** structure (progress, effort, understanding, mistakes as part of learning) fosters mastery goals. A **performance** structure (public ranking, comparison, only the top marks praised) fosters performance and failure-avoidant goals. Position lists pinned on the notice board are a performance structure.
- **Teacher expectations.** Rosenthal and Jacobson (1968) reported that pupils whom teachers were led to expect to "bloom" made greater gains, the "Pygmalion effect". The size of the effect has been debated, but the lesson for teachers is to watch how expectations show in who is asked hard questions, how long the teacher waits for an answer, and what feedback each pupil gets.
- **Relationships and peers.** A teacher who knows pupils' names and interests, and a peer culture where trying is respected, both raise effort.
- **Tasks.** Tasks that are meaningful, of moderate difficulty and offer some choice build intrinsic motivation (see Topic 6.1).

### The community

- **Role models.** Seeing people from one's own community succeed through education (a local doctor, a teacher who studied in the same school) makes effort seem worthwhile.
- **Cultural and religious values.** Respect for knowledge and for teachers can be a strong motivator when school learning is connected to it.
- **Relevance.** Learning that visibly connects to community life (measuring fields, reading notices, writing letters) motivates more than learning that seems to belong only to the examination.
- **Economic pressures.** Where children are needed for household work or earning, motivation competes with real necessity.

### Bringing the three together

Epstein (1995) describes six types of school, family and community involvement: **parenting** (helping
families support children at home), **communicating** (two-way school-home contact), **volunteering**,
**learning at home** (guidance on homework and talk), **decision making** (families in school
committees) and **collaborating with the community**.

**A Sindh example.** In a Class 5 in Tando Allahyar, attendance and effort drop in the cotton-picking
season. The teacher cannot remove the economic pressure, but she: invites a former pupil, now a nurse, to
talk to the class (community model); replaces the position list with individual progress charts (mastery
structure); sends home a one-line weekly note for parents in Sindhi (communicating); and sets short,
resource-free homework that can be done after work (learning at home).

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Distinguish** between success seekers and failure avoiders, and **suggest** three classroom
practices that reduce failure avoidance.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) **Explain** how the home, the school and the community each shape a child's motivation to
learn, and **propose** one action for each context that a primary teacher can take.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Self-worth basis | 2 | Pupils protect self-worth, often tied to ability shown by marks (Covington). |
| Success seekers | 3 | Value learning, effort attributions, failure as information, moderate challenge. |
| Failure avoiders | 4 | Fear that failure shows low ability; self-protection by not trying, excuses, procrastination, too-easy or too-hard tasks; example. |
| Three practices | 6 | E.g. mastery goal structure, private feedback on progress, rewarding effort and strategy, safe mistakes, tasks of moderate difficulty (2 marks each). |

### CRQ 2

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Home | 3 | At least two mechanisms, e.g. expectations, involvement, basic needs, style of control. |
| School | 3 | At least two mechanisms, e.g. goal structure, teacher expectations, peers, tasks. |
| Community | 3 | At least two mechanisms, e.g. role models, values, relevance, economic pressures. |
| Three actions | 6 | One feasible, justified action per context (2 marks each). |
