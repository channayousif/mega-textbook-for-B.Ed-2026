# B12. Basic needs and motivation

## What the test asks

Questions on this objective assess your ability to **explain** different types of motivation and **apply** motivational theories to classroom scenarios. You may be asked to **justify** strategies to enhance learner motivation or to **analyse** a student's lack of motivation using frameworks like Maslow's hierarchy of needs or Weiner's attribution theory.

Candidates often lose marks when they confuse intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, or when they fail to link a child's behaviour to unmet physiological or safety needs before suggesting academic interventions.

## Explanation

### Maslow's hierarchy of needs

Abraham Maslow (1943) proposed that human needs are arranged in a hierarchy. Lower-level "deficiency needs" must be met, at least partially, before higher-level "growth needs" can influence behaviour (Woolfolk, 2010). The hierarchy consists of five levels:

1. **Physiological needs**: The fundamental requirements for survival, such as food, water, sleep, and shelter.
2. **Safety needs**: The need to feel physically and psychologically secure, free from danger, fear, or chaos.
3. **Belongingness and love needs**: The desire for affection, relationships, and feeling accepted as part of a group.
4. **Esteem needs**: The need for self-respect, achievement, competence, and recognition or respect from others.
5. **Self-actualisation**: The highest level, representing the desire to fulfill one's potential and become everything one is capable of becoming.

### How unmet basic needs block learning

In the context of learning, Maslow's theory means that a student whose basic needs are unmet will struggle to focus on academic tasks (HEC, 2012, Unit 4). The drive to satisfy deficiency needs overrides the cognitive desire to learn or self-actualise.

For example, consider a Class 5 pupil in a Karachi public school who arrives without having eaten breakfast (unmet physiological need). The child may appear lethargic, irritable, or distracted. If the teacher attempts to motivate the student by promising a gold star (esteem) or praising the intrinsic value of the math problem (self-actualisation), the strategy will likely fail. The biological need for food dominates the child's attention. Similarly, a child who experiences bullying (unmet safety and belongingness needs) will expend mental energy on self-protection rather than participating in class discussions.

### Practical classroom implications

The HEC guide emphasizes that educators must consider the whole child and address basic needs to foster motivation (HEC, 2012, Unit 4). Teachers can apply Maslow's hierarchy through specific strategies:

- **Addressing physiological needs**: Allowing water bottles at desks, keeping the classroom well-ventilated, or directing students to school feeding programmes.
- **Ensuring safety**: Establishing clear routines, enforcing anti-bullying rules, and creating a predictable environment where mistakes are treated as learning opportunities, not causes for punishment.
- **Fostering belongingness**: Using inclusive language, encouraging peer collaboration, learning students' names and interests, and making every child feel welcome in the classroom community.
- **Building esteem**: Providing specific, constructive feedback, acknowledging effort rather than just natural ability, and ensuring tasks are appropriately challenging to allow for genuine success.

By creating a classroom environment that supports these foundational levels, teachers free their students to engage deeply with the curriculum and strive for self-actualisation.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) A Class 6 student in Hyderabad frequently falls asleep during the first period and avoids group work, choosing to sit alone at break time. **Analyse** this student's behaviour using Maslow's hierarchy of needs and **propose** three distinct strategies the teacher could use to support the student's motivation to learn.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Award up to 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| Identification of unmet needs | 6 | Correctly identifies unmet physiological needs (falling asleep) and belongingness/safety needs (avoiding group work, sitting alone), explaining how these lower-level needs must be addressed before the student can engage academically (3 marks per need identified and explained). |
| Application of Maslow's theory | 3 | Explains that deficiency needs override growth needs; the student cannot be motivated by achievement or learning until basic survival and social needs are secure. |
| Proposed strategies | 6 | Three feasible, specific strategies addressing the identified needs (e.g., checking if the student needs a breakfast programme or rest, creating safe low-stakes opportunities for peer interaction, building a supportive teacher-student relationship). 2 marks per justified strategy. |
