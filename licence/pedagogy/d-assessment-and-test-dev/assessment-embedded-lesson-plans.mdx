# D06. Assessment-embedded lesson plans

## What the test asks

Expect a task such as **design** the assessment part of a lesson plan for a given topic,
**differentiate** between learning objectives, learning targets and success criteria, or
**evaluate** a sample plan and **improve** its assessment component. Design questions are
marked on whether every objective has a matching piece of evidence.

Marks are usually lost by:

- writing objectives that cannot be observed ("pupils will understand...");
- adding a quiz at the end that tests something other than the objectives;
- leaving out when and how the teacher will record and use what the checks show.

## Where your degree teaches this

- [EFMP-301 Topic 10.2 - Instructional design and teacher effectiveness](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-10/topic-02)
  teaches backward design: outcome first, evidence second, activity third, with Mager's rule
  that an objective must name an observable behaviour, a condition and a criterion.

That is the logic. What the degree does not teach is the vocabulary the HEC guide uses for an
assessment-embedded plan (goals, objectives, targets, success criteria), the steps for writing
them, or how to record assessment evidence. That residue follows.

## Explanation

### Four terms that build the assessment plan

The HEC guide notes that textbooks often use "learning goals", "learning objectives" and
"learning targets" interchangeably, but it defines them as different steps (HEC, 2012, Unit 2):

- **Learning goal.** A broad statement at the level of a unit, usually taken from the
  curriculum. Example: "Pupils will be able to explain the relationships between the Sun, Earth
  and Moon and their effect on life on Earth."
- **Learning objective.** The knowledge or skill a pupil is expected to learn from one lesson.
  Objectives are either **essential** (must be achieved to reach the unit goal) or
  **supportive** (widen the picture).
- **Learning target.** The essential objectives of a lesson. Targets leave out the supporting
  objectives, so the teacher knows what must be checked.
- **Success criteria.** Statements written with action verbs that describe what a pupil must be
  able to do to show a target has been met. Because they are observable, they are what the
  assessment activities check.

The fifth element is the **formative assessment** itself: an activity during the lesson that
produces evidence against the success criteria and gives feedback to pupils and teacher.

### Steps for writing an assessment-embedded plan

The guide sets out the process in this order (HEC, 2012, Unit 2):

1. Study the unit's learning goal in the curriculum (in Pakistan, the relevant SLOs).
2. Break the goal into learning objectives.
3. Decide how many objectives fit one period, grouping similar ones together.
4. Pick out the essential objectives: these are the learning targets.
5. Turn each target into success criteria using measurable action verbs.
6. Design a formative assessment activity for each success criterion, decide when in the lesson
   it happens, and how the result will be recorded and used.

The guide also recommends "working backward": taking an existing lesson's activities and asking
what objectives, targets and criteria they actually serve. This is a good way to test your own
plans. If an activity serves no target, cut it or change it.

### What makes success criteria usable

Success criteria must be measurable, because they "form the basis for creating assessments for
the lesson as well as the unit" (HEC, 2012, Unit 2). Compare:

- Weak: "Pupils will know about day and night."
- Better: "Pupils can use a torch and a ball to show why one side of the Earth has day while the
  other has night, and explain it in two sentences."

The better version names what the pupil does, the conditions (torch and ball), and a standard
(two sentences that explain, not just describe). Sharing the criteria with pupils at the start
of the lesson lets them judge their own progress, which supports self-assessment (Clarke, 2008).

### Recording assessment evidence

An assessment plan is incomplete if nothing is recorded. The guide's second week of this unit is
about recording, and it offers practical tools a teacher with a large class can manage (HEC,
2012, Unit 2):

- a **monitoring notebook** in which the teacher jots brief observations, organised by date or by
  pupil, as in the guide's example of Ms Khan's Class 4;
- pupils' **subject journals**, where pupils record their own work and reflections;
- a **participation record** for class discussions, noting who contributed and what their
  contributions showed about understanding;
- a **class record** that documents achievement across the unit.

The point of recording is decision-making: the notebook entry "8 pupils still think the Sun
moves round the Earth" should change the next lesson.

### A worked plan: Class 4 science, "Day and night" (40 minutes)

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Unit goal | Explain the relationships between the Sun, Earth and Moon and their effects on life on Earth |
| Learning objectives | (1) State that the Earth spins on its axis once in about 24 hours. (2) Explain day and night as a result of this spin. (3) Name the direction in which the Sun appears to rise (supportive). |
| Learning targets | Objectives 1 and 2 |
| Success criteria | I can show the Earth's spin with a ball and torch. I can explain in two sentences why it is day in Karachi while it is night on the other side of the world. |
| Formative checks | Minute 10: thumbs up/down on "The Sun goes round the Earth every day" (true or false?). Minute 25: pairs demonstrate with ball and torch while the teacher circulates with a checklist. Minute 35: exit slip with the two-sentence explanation. |
| Recording | Checklist ticks for the demonstration; exit slips sorted into "met", "partly met", "not yet" |
| Use of evidence | "Not yet" pupils are grouped for a five-minute re-demonstration at the start of the next lesson |

Notice that every target has at least one check, every check matches a success criterion, and the
plan says what happens to the evidence. That is what "assessment-embedded" means: assessment is
built into the lesson rather than added after it, which is also what NPST Standard 5.1 asks for
when it describes assessment "fully integrated into teaching and learning planning" (HEC, 2012,
NPSTP Standard 5 rubric).

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

**Differentiate** between a learning goal, a learning objective, a learning target and success
criteria, using one example from a primary school subject of your choice. *(15 marks)*

### CRQ 2

**Design** the assessment component of a 40-minute Class 3 mathematics lesson on telling the time
to the half hour. Include targets, success criteria, at least two formative checks, and how you
will record and use the evidence. *(15 marks)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Learning goal defined as a broad unit-level statement, often from the curriculum | 3 |
| Learning objective defined as the knowledge or skill for one lesson; essential versus supportive | 3 |
| Learning target defined as the essential objectives of the lesson | 3 |
| Success criteria defined as observable, action-verb statements of what shows mastery | 3 |
| One consistent example that moves from goal to objective to target to criteria | 3 |

Full marks need correct definitions and one worked chain in the same subject. Definitions that
treat the four terms as synonyms earn at most 6.

### CRQ 2

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| One or two clear learning targets appropriate to Class 3 | 2 |
| Success criteria that are observable and measurable (e.g. "I can set a clock to half past 4") | 3 |
| At least two formative checks placed at specific points in the lesson | 3 |
| Each check matches a success criterion | 3 |
| A practical recording method suited to a large class | 2 |
| States how the evidence will change teaching (regrouping, re-teaching, extension) | 2 |

A full-mark answer is internally consistent: targets lead to criteria, criteria to checks, checks
to records and records to a decision. Generic answers ("ask questions", "give a test") without
alignment earn at most 7.
