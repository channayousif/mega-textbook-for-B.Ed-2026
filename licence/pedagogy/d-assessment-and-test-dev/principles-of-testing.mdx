# D13. Principles of testing

## What the test asks

CRQs ask you to **explain** principles of test construction or of testing in general, to
**apply** them to a flawed test and **suggest** improvements, or to **justify** why a particular
principle matters. The best answers state a principle, explain the reason behind it, and show
what it looks like in a real classroom test.

Marks are usually lost by:

- listing principles as single words ("validity, reliability, fairness") with no explanation;
- mixing up principles of testing with the steps of test construction (the steps are *what* you do;
  the principles are the *rules* that decide whether you did it well);
- forgetting administration, scoring and use: a well-written test can still be spoiled in the room.

## Explanation

### Why principles matter

The Test Development and Evaluation guide expects student teachers to "understand the purpose,
principles, and scope of test and evaluation" before learning the technical steps (HEC, 2012, TDE
Unit 1). A test is a tool for a decision. Principles keep the tool honest at every stage: planning,
writing, giving, scoring and using.

Miller, Linn and Gronlund (2013) summarise the foundations in five general principles of
assessment, which apply fully to tests:

1. Clearly specifying **what is to be assessed** comes first.
2. A procedure should be chosen for its **relevance** to what is to be measured.
3. Comprehensive assessment needs a **variety** of procedures.
4. Proper use requires awareness of each procedure's **limitations**.
5. Assessment is **a means to an end**, not an end in itself.

The principles below turn these into rules for the classroom test.

### Principles for planning and writing a test

**1. Begin with the purpose.** Decide what decision the results will feed: diagnosis, grading,
promotion or selection. The purpose decides the length, difficulty, item types and interpretation.
A diagnostic test needs many items on few objectives; a term test needs broad coverage.

**2. Align the test with the objectives.** Every item should measure a stated learning objective or
Student Learning Outcome, at the intended level of thinking. The Classroom Assessment guide defines a
fair test as one that represents the material taught "in its entirety" and includes nothing that was
not taught (HEC, 2012, Unit 4). This is the principle of **content validity**.

**3. Sample the content representatively.** A test can never ask everything, so the items must be a
fair sample, weighted by the importance of each topic and the teaching time given to it. The tool for
this is the table of specifications (see
[D14](/licence/pedagogy/d-assessment-and-test-dev/domains-standards-taxonomies) and
[D16](/licence/pedagogy/d-assessment-and-test-dev/test-development-and-review)).

**4. Match item type to objective.** Use selected-response items for broad factual and conceptual
coverage and constructed responses or performance tasks where pupils must explain, argue or do (see
[D07](/licence/pedagogy/d-assessment-and-test-dev/types-of-test-questions)).

**5. Keep language clear and simple.** Items should test the subject, not reading ability. The Test
Development and Evaluation guide asks teachers to "reduce unnecessary complexity in test items" and to
overcome language barriers that threaten the validity of content-based assessment (HEC, 2012, TDE).
In multilingual Sindh classrooms, where a pupil may be learning in a second or third language, this
principle decides whether a science test measures science.

**6. Be fair and free of bias.** No group should be disadvantaged by content, examples or vocabulary
irrelevant to what is measured: for example, questions that assume urban experiences a rural pupil
has not had, or that favour one gender or language group.

**7. Write clear directions.** The Classroom Assessment guide devotes a session to the
characteristics of clear test directions (HEC, 2012, Unit 4). Directions should say what to do, how
and where to record answers, how much time is allowed, how many marks each section carries, and
whether pupils should attempt every item. For young pupils, directions are also read aloud.

### Principles for administering a test

**8. Standard, comfortable conditions.** All pupils should have the same time, the same instructions
and reasonable physical conditions (light, space, quiet). Unequal conditions add error.

**9. Security and honesty.** Papers are kept secure before the test and invigilation is fair during
it. The National Education Policy 2009 calls for a plan to eliminate cheating and other unfair means
from examinations (Government of Pakistan, 2009, Section 6.4). A test that can be copied measures
copying.

### Principles for scoring

**10. Objectivity.** Scoring should depend on the pupil's response, not on who marks it or when. The
Test Development and Evaluation guide asks teachers to "score tests while minimizing probable biases"
and to prepare model answers and marking guides for short-answer and essay questions (HEC, 2012, TDE
Unit 3). Practical rules: prepare the model answer and the marking guide before marking; mark one
question across all scripts before moving to the next; hide pupils' names where possible; use a rubric
for extended answers.

### Principles for interpreting and using results

**11. Interpret with care.** Every score contains error (see
[D12](/licence/pedagogy/d-assessment-and-test-dev/tests-and-measurement)), so small differences
between pupils should not be over-read, and important decisions should use several sources of
evidence.

**12. Use results to improve learning.** Results should lead to feedback for pupils and adjustments
to teaching, not only to a mark in a register.

**13. Use results ethically.** Keep individual scores confidential, do not use results to shame
pupils, and report results to parents in a form they can understand.

**14. Practicality.** A test should be usable with the time, money and staff available. A perfect test
that takes three weeks to mark is not a practical classroom test.

Above all these sit **validity** and **reliability**: the test must measure what it claims to measure,
and do so consistently. They are the subject of
[D15](/licence/pedagogy/d-assessment-and-test-dev/validity-reliability).

### A before-and-after example

A Class 8 science teacher in Nawabshah writes a monthly test in one evening: 20 true-false items
copied from textbook sentences, all on the first chapter, with the direction "Attempt all." Applying
the principles, she revises it:

- she lists the month's four topics and gives items in proportion to teaching time (alignment and
  sampling);
- she replaces half the true-false items with multiple-choice and two short-answer items that ask
  pupils to explain (item type matched to objective);
- she rewrites long sentences in simpler words and removes an item about a city underground railway
  that her pupils have never seen (clarity and fairness);
- she writes directions stating time, marks per section and how to mark answers (clear directions);
- she prepares a marking guide for the short-answer items before the test (objectivity);
- after marking, she checks which items most pupils missed and re-teaches that topic (use of
  results).

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

**Explain** five principles that a teacher should follow when constructing a classroom test, with an
example of each. *(15 marks)*

### CRQ 2

A teacher's term test contains only recall items from two of the five chapters taught, uses difficult
English vocabulary in a Sindhi-medium class, and is marked without a marking guide. **Identify** the
principles of testing that have been violated and **suggest** how to correct each. *(15 marks)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Five distinct principles correctly named (purpose, alignment/content validity, representative sampling, item type matched to objective, clarity of language, fairness, clear directions, objectivity of scoring, practicality), 1 mark each | 5 |
| Each principle explained with the reason it matters, 1 mark each | 5 |
| Each principle illustrated with a relevant classroom example, 1 mark each | 5 |

Full marks need five different principles, each explained and exemplified. Single-word lists earn at
most 5.

### CRQ 2

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Alignment/representative sampling violated (only two of five chapters); correction via a table of specifications | 3 |
| Item type/level violated (recall only); correction with items at understanding and application | 3 |
| Clarity/fairness violated (language load in a Sindhi-medium class); correction with simpler wording or appropriate language | 3 |
| Objectivity violated (no marking guide); correction with model answers, a marking guide or rubric prepared before marking | 3 |
| Explains the overall effect on validity and reliability of the results | 3 |

A full-mark answer names each violated principle, links it to a specific flaw, and gives a practical
correction; it also explains that the scores cannot support valid conclusions about the pupils'
learning.
