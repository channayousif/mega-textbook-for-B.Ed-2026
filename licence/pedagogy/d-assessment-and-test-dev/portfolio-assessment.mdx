# D10. Portfolio assessment

## What the test asks

Expect to **define** a portfolio and **explain** what makes it different from a file of work, to
**describe** types of portfolios with their purposes, to **explain** how portfolios are assessed,
or to **plan** a portfolio for a class and subject. Evaluation questions ask you to **weigh** the
advantages and disadvantages in a Pakistani school.

Marks are usually lost by:

- describing a portfolio as "a folder of the pupil's best work", which ignores purpose, selection
  and reflection;
- listing types with no purpose for each;
- saying nothing about how the portfolio will actually be judged.

## Explanation

### What makes a collection a portfolio

The HEC guide opens its portfolio week with a definition: a portfolio is **a purposeful collection
of a student's work that tells a story of effort, progress, or achievement** (HEC, 2012, Unit 3).
Three words carry the meaning:

- **Purposeful**: the purpose and audience are decided first, and they decide what goes in.
- **Collection**: several pieces, gathered over time, not a single task.
- **Story**: the entries are selected and arranged so that they show something, such as growth
  from the first term to the third.

Paulson, Paulson and Meyer (1991) add the element that most distinguishes a portfolio from a
file: the pupil takes part in **selecting** the contents and **reflecting** on them. Without
selection and reflection, a collection is just storage.

The guide also insists that the portfolio is both an **assessment tool** and a **teaching tool**.
Building one is an excellent way for a pupil to learn to evaluate their own work (HEC, 2012,
Unit 3).

### Types of portfolios

The guide's outline lists these types (HEC, 2012, Unit 3), drawing on McMillan (2011):

| Type | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Working folder** | Holds all work in progress, from which entries are later chosen | Every draft of every Urdu composition in a term |
| **Project portfolio** | Documents the stages of one project | Plan, data, photos and report from a school garden project |
| **Growth portfolio** | Shows change over time | A pupil's first, middle and last pieces of handwriting, with reflections |
| **Achievement portfolio** | Shows the level reached, often for reporting | Selected best tests and assignments at the end of a year |
| **Competence portfolio** | Provides evidence that standards have been met | A student teacher's practicum portfolio judged against the NPST |
| **Celebration portfolio** | Lets the pupil show work they are proud of | A Class 2 child's favourite drawings and stories for parents' day |

The guide applies the list to student teachers themselves: the semester-3 practicum portfolio is a
**growth** portfolio, while the professional portfolio, which is judged against professional
standards, is closer to a **competence** portfolio than a celebration one (HEC, 2012, Unit 3).

### Purposes

Portfolios serve two broad purposes, as the guide notes: **instruction** (pupils learn to set
goals, revise and judge their own work) and **assessment** (the teacher gains rich evidence of
progress and achievement that a single test cannot provide).

### Planning a portfolio: the guide's checklist

Using McMillan, the guide builds a list of what must be decided before a portfolio starts (HEC,
2012, Unit 3):

1. the **purpose and audience** (teacher, parents, next year's teacher, an assessor);
2. the **types of entries** (written work, drawings, test papers, photographs of practical work,
   audio recordings);
3. a **structure** (sections, contents page, dated entries);
4. **who selects** entries: the pupil, the teacher, or both;
5. **evaluation criteria** for individual entries and for the whole portfolio;
6. the pupil's responsibility for **reflection and self-evaluation**;
7. a schedule for **pupil-teacher conferences**;
8. an understanding that entries are made and dated **continuously** until the portfolio is
   complete.

### Reflection and conferences

Each entry should carry a short reflection: why the pupil chose it, what it shows, and what they
would do differently. For young pupils this can be a sentence starter ("I chose this because...",
"Next time I will..."), written in Urdu, Sindhi or English. **Portfolio conferences** are short
meetings in which teacher and pupil review the portfolio together, agree on progress and set the
next goal. They turn the portfolio into feedback.

### How portfolios are assessed

Portfolios are judged against criteria set in advance, usually with a rubric (see
[D09](/licence/pedagogy/d-assessment-and-test-dev/rubrics)). Teachers can:

- score **individual entries** with the rubric for that task;
- score the **whole portfolio** holistically, for example on evidence of growth, range of work,
  quality of reflection and completeness;
- combine both, weighting the parts.

For the scores to be trusted, the criteria must be clear and, where stakes are high, a second
teacher should score a sample so that agreement can be checked. The Test Development and
Evaluation guide returns to portfolios among its essential assessment strategies, listing the
steps of the portfolio assessment process as content to be taught (HEC, 2012, TDE Unit 4); the
planning checklist above is a practical version of those steps.

### A Sindh example

A Class 3 teacher in a Shikarpur government school keeps an Urdu writing **growth portfolio** for
each pupil in a plastic sleeve. In the first week of each month pupils choose one piece of writing
from their working folder, date it, and complete a two-line reflection. Every term the teacher
holds a five-minute conference with each pupil, using a three-criterion rubric (ideas,
handwriting, sentence accuracy). At the parent-teacher meeting, parents see the September and
March pieces side by side, which shows progress far more clearly than two term marks.

### Advantages

- Shows **progress over time**, not just a single performance.
- Involves pupils in **self-assessment** and goal setting, which the NPST Standard 5.4 rubric
  values at its highest level.
- Captures skills and products that tests miss.
- Gives parents concrete evidence.
- Links assessment closely to daily teaching.

### Disadvantages

- **Time**: organising, conferencing and scoring take much longer than testing.
- **Storage and management**: a problem in crowded classrooms with no cupboards.
- **Reliability**: scoring can vary between teachers without clear criteria and moderation.
- **Comparability**: portfolios differ in content, so comparing pupils is harder.
- **Risk of becoming a scrapbook** if purpose, selection and reflection are neglected.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

**Define** portfolio assessment and **describe** four types of portfolio, stating the purpose of
each. *(15 marks)*

### CRQ 2

**Plan** a growth portfolio for Class 4 mathematics for one term. **Explain** what will be included,
who selects entries, how pupils will reflect, and how the portfolio will be assessed. *(15 marks)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Definition includes purposeful collection over time that tells a story of effort, progress or achievement | 3 |
| Mentions pupil selection and reflection as defining features | 2 |
| Four types correctly described (working folder, project, growth, achievement, competence, celebration), each with its purpose, 2.5 marks each | 10 |

Full marks require four different types with a distinct purpose and, ideally, a brief example for
each. A definition such as "a file of best work" earns at most 1 of the definition marks.

### CRQ 2

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| States the purpose and audience of the growth portfolio | 2 |
| Specifies suitable entries showing change over time (e.g. first, mid and end-of-term problem solving) | 3 |
| Explains who selects entries and when | 2 |
| Describes a manageable reflection routine suitable for Class 4 | 3 |
| Describes assessment criteria or a rubric, and how growth will be judged | 3 |
| Addresses feasibility (storage, conferences, time) in a real classroom | 2 |

A full-mark plan is realistic for a large class and shows how the portfolio will reveal growth,
not only final achievement.
