# D16. Test development and review

## What the test asks

This objective is examined through process and calculation. Expect to **describe** the steps of test
construction, **explain** the purpose of a table of specifications, **calculate** difficulty and
discrimination indices from given data and **interpret** them, or **decide** which items to keep,
revise or discard. The calculation questions are straightforward if you know the two formulas and
state what each number means.

Marks are usually lost by:

- listing steps without saying what is done at each one;
- calculating an index but not interpreting it, or reading a high difficulty index as "hard" (it
  means easy);
- recommending that every item with low discrimination be discarded without checking the key or the
  teaching first.

## Explanation

### The steps of test construction

The Test Development and Evaluation guide's unit on test construction and the Classroom Assessment
guide's unit on teacher-made tests describe the same sequence (HEC, 2012, TDE Unit 3; Unit 4).

**1. Determine the purpose and the behaviours to be assessed.** Is the test diagnostic, formative,
summative or for placement? Which SLOs, and which domain and level of thinking, will it cover?

**2. Plan the test with a table of specifications.** The guide calls the table of specifications the
"blueprint" of a test: a two-way grid of content against learning objectives or taxonomy levels,
which assures that the teacher "has adequately covered the subject matter" and matched items to
objectives (HEC, 2012, Unit 4). The guide's procedure is:

- list the learning objectives (or taxonomy levels) across the top and the content categories down the
  side;
- decide the total number of items from the content, its complexity and the time available;
- distribute items to each cell by professional judgement about the importance of each topic and
  objective and the teaching time spent on it;
- total the rows and columns and convert them to percentages;
- check that the distribution gives adequate coverage, and revise it if not;
- write the items to fill the cells.

A worked table for a Class 5 science test is shown in
[D14](/licence/pedagogy/d-assessment-and-test-dev/domains-standards-taxonomies). The guide stresses
that building one is an act of **professional judgement**: two teachers with the same unit may
produce different, equally defensible tables.

**3. Write the items.** Choose item types to fit each cell and follow the writing guidelines (see
[D07](/licence/pedagogy/d-assessment-and-test-dev/types-of-test-questions)). For multiple-choice items,
identify the **stem**, the **key** and the **distractors**. Write a few more items than needed so that
weak ones can be dropped. At the same time, prepare the key for objective items and **model answers
with marking guides** for short-answer and essay items (HEC, 2012, TDE Unit 3).

**4. Review the items.** Before the test is used, check each item, ideally with a colleague; the guide
builds in reviewing peers' tests (HEC, 2012, TDE Unit 3). A review checklist:

- Does the item match its cell in the table of specifications?
- Is it clear, at the pupils' reading level, and free of clues?
- Does it have exactly one defensible key (or a clear marking guide)?
- Is it free of bias against any group of pupils?

**5. Assemble the test.** Group items of the same type together, arrange them roughly from easier to
harder, number them clearly, vary the position of keys, keep each item on one page, and write clear
directions stating the time, marks and how to respond (HEC, 2012, Unit 4).

**6. Administer the test** under standard conditions, with directions read aloud for young pupils.

**7. Score the test** using the prepared key and marking guides, marking one question across all scripts
at a time and hiding names where possible, to minimise bias.

**8. Analyse the items** (below).

**9. Review, revise and report.** Keep good items (ideally in an item bank for reuse), revise or discard
weak ones, re-teach content the class missed, and give pupils feedback.

### Item analysis

**Item analysis** uses pupils' responses to judge how well each item worked. The guide names three
elements: item **difficulty**, **discrimination** and **plausibility** of distractors (HEC, 2012, TDE,
drawing on Ebel and on Nitko, 1996); its unit outcomes also mention **fairness**.

**Forming the groups.** Rank the scripts by total score. Take the top 27 per cent as the **upper group**
and the bottom 27 per cent as the **lower group**, a proportion Kelley (1939) showed to be efficient.
In a class of 36, 0.27 × 36 = 9.7, so take 10 pupils in each group.

**Difficulty index (p)** is the proportion of pupils who answered the item right:

p = (RU + RL) / (NU + NL)

where RU and RL are the numbers right in the upper and lower groups and NU and NL are the group sizes.
p runs from 0 to 1. **A high p means an easy item.**

**Discrimination index (D)** shows whether the item separates stronger from weaker pupils:

D = (RU - RL) / N, where N is the number of pupils in one group.

D runs from -1 to +1. A positive D means more upper-group pupils got it right; a negative D means the
weaker pupils did better, which signals a flawed item or a miskeyed one. Ebel and Frisbie (1991)
suggest: 0.40 and above very good; 0.30 to 0.39 reasonably good; 0.20 to 0.29 marginal, usually needing
improvement; below 0.20 poor, to be rejected or revised.

### A worked example

<Figure id="fig-D-3" kind="table" src="/img/figures/licence/d-assessment-and-test-dev/fig-D-3.svg" alt="Item analysis table for five items with upper and lower groups of 10 from a class of 36, showing the number right in each group, difficulty p, discrimination D and a decision for each item: item 2 keep, item 5 keep, items 1 and 4 marginal, item 3 negative and to be checked and rewritten." width={780} height={420} />

A Class 7 teacher in Jacobabad analyses five items of a science test. Take **item 2**: 8 of the upper
group and 3 of the lower group were right. p = (8 + 3) / 20 = 0.55, a moderate item. D = (8 - 3) / 10 =
0.50, very good discrimination: keep it.

**Item 1**: p = (9 + 7) / 20 = 0.80 (easy) and D = (9 - 7) / 10 = 0.20 (marginal). If the item tests an
essential objective that most pupils have mastered, an easy item is acceptable, especially in a
criterion-referenced test; otherwise, sharpen its distractors.

**Item 4**: p = (3 + 1) / 20 = 0.20 (hard) and D = 0.20. Before blaming the item, the teacher should ask
whether the topic was taught well enough.

**Item 3**: p = (4 + 5) / 20 = 0.45 but D = (4 - 5) / 10 = -0.10. The lower group did better. A
**distractor analysis** shows why. The key is A.

| Option | A (key) | B | C | D |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Upper group (10) | 4 | 5 | 1 | 0 |
| Lower group (10) | 5 | 2 | 2 | 1 |

Half of the upper group chose B. Either B is also defensible (an ambiguous item), or the key is wrong.
Option D attracted only one pupil, so it is not a plausible distractor. The teacher should check the key,
rewrite the stem or option B to remove the ambiguity, and replace option D.

### A simpler classroom tool: the assessment tracker

The Classroom Assessment guide offers a manageable version for everyday use: an **assessment tracker**,
a grid with pupils in rows and items in columns, entering 1 for a right response and 0 otherwise (HEC,
2012, Unit 4). In the guide's example the teacher, using her professional judgement, treats an item as
too easy if 15 per cent or fewer pupils got it wrong, and too hard if 15 per cent or fewer got it right.
The guide is careful to note that there is no research-based rule for these cut-offs. Column totals give
item difficulty; row totals give pupil scores; and patterns across a row show each pupil which
objectives to revisit, which turns the tracker into a feedback tool.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

**Describe** the steps a teacher should follow to develop a classroom achievement test, and **explain**
the role of the table of specifications. *(15 marks)*

### CRQ 2

In a class of 40, the upper and lower groups each contain 11 pupils. For an item, 9 pupils in the
upper group and 4 in the lower group answered right. **Calculate** the difficulty and discrimination
indices, **interpret** each, and **recommend** what the teacher should do with the item. *(15 marks)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Purpose and behaviours/SLOs to be assessed | 2 |
| Planning with a table of specifications, with its purpose explained (content validity, balanced coverage of content and levels, guide for item writing) | 4 |
| Writing items with keys, model answers and marking guides | 2 |
| Reviewing items against a checklist, ideally with a colleague | 2 |
| Assembling (grouping, ordering, directions) and administering under standard conditions | 2 |
| Scoring objectively, item analysis, and revision/reporting | 3 |

A full-mark answer presents the steps in a logical order, says what is done at each, and gives the table
of specifications a clear role in ensuring that the test represents what was taught.

### CRQ 2

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Correct groups: 27 per cent of 40 is 10.8, so 11 per group (as given) | 1 |
| Difficulty: p = (9 + 4) / 22 = 13 / 22 = 0.59 | 3 |
| Interprets p as moderate difficulty (about 59 per cent of the two groups right) | 2 |
| Discrimination: D = (9 - 4) / 11 = 5 / 11 = 0.45 | 3 |
| Interprets D as very good discrimination (0.40 and above) | 3 |
| Recommends keeping the item (and banking it), with a brief check of distractor performance | 3 |

Full marks need both formulas, correct values (0.59 and 0.45), correct interpretations and a justified
recommendation. Reading p = 0.59 as "hard" loses the interpretation marks.
