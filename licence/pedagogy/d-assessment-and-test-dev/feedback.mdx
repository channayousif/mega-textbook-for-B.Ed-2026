# D04. Feedback: characteristics and benefits

## What the test asks

CRQs on feedback ask you to **define** feedback, **describe** the characteristics of effective
feedback, **explain** its benefits, or **critique** a piece of teacher feedback and **rewrite**
it. The critique-and-rewrite format is common because it shows whether you can apply the
characteristics, not just list them.

Marks are usually lost by:

- equating feedback with praise or with a mark;
- listing characteristics ("specific", "timely") without saying why each helps learning;
- rewriting feedback so that the teacher still does all the work (for example, writing in the
  answers).

## Where your degree teaches this

- [EFMP-301 Topic 1.4 - Shaping teaching, assessment and reflection](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-01/topic-04)
  gives the core principle: feedback works when it tells the pupil what to do next, and once a
  mark is attached most pupils stop reading the comment.
- [EFMP-301 Topic 8.2 - Creating positive learning environments](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-08/topic-02)
  shows feedback that names the next step as the centre of a positive classroom climate.

The degree states the principle in a paragraph. The definitions, the research behind it, the
full list of characteristics, the types of feedback and its benefits are taught below.

## Explanation

### What feedback is

The HEC guide contrasts three meanings. In biology and engineering, feedback is a signal that
returns to a system to keep it stable, like the float valve that stops a water tank filling. In
education, feedback aims at **change**, not stability (HEC, 2012, Unit 1). Two definitions the
guide uses are worth knowing:

- Feedback is information about how a person is doing in their efforts to reach a goal
  (Wiggins, 2012).
- Feedback is information provided by an agent (teacher, peer, book, parent, self or
  experience) about aspects of one's performance or understanding (Hattie and Timperley, 2007).

Hattie and Timperley add a useful frame: good feedback answers three questions for the learner.
*Where am I going?* (the goal), *How am I going?* (progress towards it) and *Where to next?*
(the step that closes the gap).

### What the research says: comments versus grades

Ruth Butler's experiments, which the guide places at the centre of its feedback week, compared
pupils who received comments only, grades only, or grades plus comments on their work. Only the
comments-only group improved on the next task. A grade alone did not help, and a grade with a
comment did no better than a grade alone (Butler, 1988; Wiliam, 2011). The guide's explanation
is practical: pupils with high grades do not need to read the comment, and pupils with low
grades do not want to. It also notes that research shows feedback has a powerful effect on
achievement, but that the effect can be negative as well as positive (HEC, 2012, Unit 1).

The lesson is not "never grade". It is that grading every piece of work may crowd out the
feedback that actually moves learning forward.

### Characteristics of effective feedback

Drawing on Wiliam (2011), the guide gives guidelines that you can use directly in a CRQ. Effective
feedback is:

1. **Linked to the learning objective.** It comments on what the lesson was trying to teach,
   not on neatness or length unless those were the objective.
2. **Thought-provoking.** "Feedback isn't feedback unless it leads to thinking and action by the
   student" (HEC, 2012, Unit 1). It gives cues and questions, not ready-made answers.
3. **Actionable.** It names a step the pupil can take.
4. **Focused.** It addresses two or three of the most important points, so the pupil is not
   overwhelmed.
5. **Timely.** Immediate for spoken feedback and, as the guide suggests, within about two days
   for written feedback.
6. **Acted on in class.** Pupils are given time to respond to it.
7. **About the task, not the person.** "You have used evidence from the passage in paragraph 2;
   do the same in paragraph 3" is about the task. "You are clever" is about the person.

Wiggins (2012) adds that feedback should be **goal-referenced**, **tangible and transparent**,
**user-friendly**, **ongoing** and **consistent**, which overlap well with the list above.

### Types of feedback

- **By mode:** oral, written, or non-verbal (a nod, a gesture).
- **By audience:** individual, group or whole-class.
- **By timing:** immediate or delayed.
- **By source:** teacher, peer, or self.
- **By level** (Hattie and Timperley, 2007): about the **task** (is it right?), the **process**
  (how was it done?), **self-regulation** (how can the pupil check their own work?), and the
  **self** (personal praise). The first three help learning; praise directed at the person
  usually carries no information about how to improve.

### Encouragement versus praise, and the growth mindset

The guide links feedback to motivation through the idea of a **growth mindset**: the belief
that ability can increase with effort and good strategies, as opposed to a fixed mindset, which
treats ability as a set amount (Dweck, 2006). Feedback that praises intelligence ("You are so
bright") can feed a fixed mindset. Feedback that recognises effort and strategy ("You checked
each step, and that is why this answer is right") encourages pupils to keep working when tasks
become hard.

### A before-and-after example

A Class 5 pupil in Thatta answers a science test on the solar system. Her teacher's first
feedback, in the style of the guide's case, simply writes the right answer next to each wrong
one. The teacher did all the thinking; the pupil only has to copy. After advice, the teacher
groups the pupil's errors, and writes: "Questions 3 and 7 are both about why we have day and
night. Look again at the diagram on page 42 and explain it to your partner, then correct both
answers." Now the pupil does the work, and the feedback is focused, linked to the objective and
actionable.

### Benefits of feedback

- It closes the gap between current and desired performance.
- It tells the teacher what to re-teach, so it improves instruction as well as learning.
- It builds pupils' ability to assess themselves, which is the highest level of NPST Standard 5
  strand 5.4: a teacher who "coaches children to engage in objective self-assessment" (HEC, 2012,
  NPSTP Standard 5 rubric).
- When task-focused, it supports motivation and persistence.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

**Describe** five characteristics of effective feedback and **explain** how each one helps a pupil
learn. *(15 marks)*

### CRQ 2

A teacher writes "Good work, 7/10" on every Class 6 essay. **Critique** this practice using
research on feedback, and **rewrite** the feedback for one essay so that it meets the
characteristics of effective feedback. *(15 marks)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Five distinct characteristics correctly named (e.g. linked to objective, specific/focused, actionable, timely, task-focused, prompts thinking, acted on in class), 1 mark each | 5 |
| For each characteristic, a clear explanation of how it helps learning, 2 marks each | 10 |

Full marks require five different characteristics, each with a reason grounded in learning (for
example, "focused, because a pupil can act on two points but not ten"). A bare list earns at
most 5.

### CRQ 2

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Identifies that "Good work" is vague praise and gives no information about how to improve | 2 |
| Explains the effect of the mark, citing Butler's comments-versus-grades finding | 3 |
| Notes that identical feedback on every essay is not specific to the pupil or the objective | 2 |
| Rewritten feedback is linked to a stated objective and identifies a strength precisely | 3 |
| Rewritten feedback gives one or two actionable next steps that make the pupil think and act | 3 |
| Rewritten feedback is about the task, not the person, and gives time or a task for response | 2 |

A full-mark answer names the research, explains why the mark blocks the comment, and produces
feedback such as: "Your opening sentence tells the reader clearly what the essay is about. In
paragraph 2 you list three reasons without examples. Add one example from your own village to
each reason, then underline them." Rewrites that supply the corrections for the pupil lose the
last two criteria.
