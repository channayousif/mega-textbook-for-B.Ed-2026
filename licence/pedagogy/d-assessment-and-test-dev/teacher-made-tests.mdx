# D11. Significance of teacher-made tests

## What the test asks

CRQs ask you to **explain** the significance of teacher-made tests, to **compare** teacher-made
and standardized tests, or to **differentiate** norm-referenced from criterion-referenced
interpretation and **decide** which suits a given situation. Scenario questions are common: "A
school wants to select ten pupils for a science olympiad team. Which interpretation should it use,
and why?"

Marks are usually lost by:

- treating "teacher-made" and "standardized" as good and bad rather than as tools for different
  jobs;
- confusing a test's **type** with how its **scores are interpreted** (the same raw score can be
  read either way);
- listing significance points without linking them to what a classroom teacher actually needs.

## Explanation

### Teacher-made and standardized tests

A **teacher-made test** is written by the classroom teacher for her own pupils, on what she has
taught, usually for a unit, month or term. A **standardized test** is "administered under standard
conditions that are the same for all test takers"; its directions, scoring standards, score
interpretations and test-taking conditions are predetermined and identical for everyone (HEC,
2012, Unit 4). Standardized tests are usually written by specialists, tried out on large samples
before use, and come with a manual.

| Point | Teacher-made test | Standardized test |
|---|---|---|
| Who writes it | The class teacher | Test specialists and subject experts |
| Content | Exactly what was taught to this class | A common curriculum or domain across many schools |
| Try-out | Rarely tried out in advance | Piloted and revised on large samples |
| Conditions and scoring | Set by the teacher; may vary | Fixed and identical for all |
| Interpretation | Usually against the teacher's criteria | Against norms or fixed standards |
| Best use | Day-to-day and unit-level decisions in one class | Comparing across classes, schools, districts |

### Why teacher-made tests are significant

1. **They match instruction.** The HEC guide's definition of a fair test is one that covers the
   material taught "in its entirety" and includes nothing that was not taught (HEC, 2012, Unit 4).
   Only the teacher knows exactly what was taught.
2. **They are timely.** A teacher can test at the end of a topic, when the results can still change
   teaching.
3. **They serve formative and diagnostic purposes.** Item-by-item results show which objectives the
   class has missed. The guide's "assessment tracker" (a grid of pupils against items, with 1 for
   a right response and 0 otherwise) turns a teacher-made test into a diagnostic tool.
4. **They fit local conditions.** Language, examples and difficulty can be adapted to the pupils,
   which matters in multilingual Sindh classrooms.
5. **They are flexible and cheap.** No purchase, no external schedule.
6. **They are the basis of school grades and promotion** in most Pakistani schools, so their quality
   directly affects pupils' futures.
7. **They motivate and guide study.** Pupils learn what the teacher values from what she tests.
8. **Writing them improves teaching.** Planning a test forces the teacher to clarify objectives.

### Their limitations, and those of tests in general

The guide asks student teachers to recognise that tests are one assessment method among many and,
like any tool, have pros and cons (HEC, 2012, Unit 4). Teacher-made tests in particular:

- may have **low reliability**, because items are not tried out and marking may be inconsistent;
- may lack **content validity** if the teacher over-tests favourite topics or only recall;
- cannot be **compared** across classes or schools;
- often contain **item-writing flaws** (see [D07](/licence/pedagogy/d-assessment-and-test-dev/types-of-test-questions)).

The remedies are the tools of this heading: a table of specifications, item-writing guidelines, and
simple item analysis ([D16](/licence/pedagogy/d-assessment-and-test-dev/test-development-and-review)).

### Three frames for interpreting a score

A raw score such as 28 out of 40 means nothing until it is compared with something. The guide
names three frames of reference (HEC, 2012, Unit 2):

- **Norm-referenced** (also called **relative**) interpretation compares a pupil's score with the
  scores of others who took the same test: "Ayesha did better than 80 per cent of the class."
- **Criterion-referenced** (also called **absolute**) interpretation compares the score with a fixed
  standard or a defined body of skills: "Ayesha answered 9 of the 10 items on fractions correctly and
  has met the mastery standard."
- **Self-referenced** interpretation compares a pupil's score with her own earlier performance:
  "Ayesha scored 12 more marks than on the September test."

<Figure id="fig-D-1" kind="table" src="/img/figures/licence/d-assessment-and-test-dev/fig-D-1.svg" alt="Table comparing norm-referenced and criterion-referenced interpretation on the question each answers, the reference point, how results are reported, how items are chosen, and a typical use, with a selection test for limited places and a unit or promotion examination as examples." width={780} height={490} />

The guide's own summary of the classroom rule is worth remembering: most tests given in school
settings, such as unit tests and examinations, are criterion-referenced, while most competitive
examinations, such as admission tests with limited places, are norm-referenced (HEC, 2012,
Unit 4). The Test Development and Evaluation guide adds that criterion-referenced tests report
achievement against a specific set of abilities, which is why a promotion examination is the most
common example: failing it means the pupil has not met the goals set for the grade (HEC, 2012,
TDE). Norm-referenced interpretation is needed when the decision is a ranking, because only a
comparison with others can tell you who the top ten are (Kubiszyn and Borich, 2003).

Two cautions. First, the same test can be interpreted in more than one frame; the frame is a
choice about meaning, not a property of the paper. Second, a norm-referenced ranking can hide the
fact that the whole class has, or has not, learned the material, which is why classroom decisions
about learning usually need a criterion-referenced reading.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

**Explain** five reasons why teacher-made tests are significant in the classroom, and **identify** two
of their limitations with a remedy for each. *(15 marks)*

### CRQ 2

**Differentiate** between norm-referenced and criterion-referenced interpretation of test scores.
For each of these situations, **justify** which interpretation is appropriate: (a) choosing five pupils
for a district quiz team; (b) deciding which pupils have mastered two-digit subtraction. *(15 marks)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Five distinct, explained reasons (match to instruction, timeliness, diagnostic/formative use, fit to local context, flexibility and cost, basis of grades, guidance for study, clarifies objectives), 2 marks each | 10 |
| Two valid limitations (e.g. low reliability, weak content coverage, no comparability, item flaws) | 2 |
| A workable remedy for each (e.g. table of specifications, item-writing guidelines, item analysis, rubric) | 3 |

Full marks need explained reasons linked to classroom decisions, not a list of adjectives.

### CRQ 2

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Norm-referenced interpretation defined (relative standing compared with others) | 3 |
| Criterion-referenced interpretation defined (performance against a fixed standard or skill set) | 3 |
| At least two further contrasts (reporting as ranks/percentiles versus mastery/percent correct; item selection to spread scores versus to cover objectives; typical uses) | 3 |
| (a) Norm-referenced, justified: the decision is a ranking for limited places | 3 |
| (b) Criterion-referenced, justified: the decision is whether each pupil has met a defined skill | 3 |

A full-mark answer notes that the frame is a choice about interpreting scores and justifies each
situation by the kind of decision to be made.
