# D05. Assessment policy and practice in Pakistan

## What the test asks

CRQs here ask you to **describe** the assessment system in Pakistani schools, **explain** what
national policy expects of teachers, or **critically analyse** the gap between policy and
practice and **suggest** improvements. The ERQ case study often sets a Sindh school scenario
and asks what the teacher and head teacher should do within existing policy.

Marks are usually lost by:

- writing general complaints about "the exam system" with no reference to an actual policy;
- inventing statistics or policy clauses (examiners know the documents; cite only what you can
  name);
- ignoring the classroom teacher's own responsibility, which is what the guide asks you to
  analyse: "Who is responsible for what?" (HEC, 2012, Unit 1).

## Explanation

### Who governs assessment

Since the 18th Constitutional Amendment (2010) abolished the Concurrent Legislative List,
education has been fully devolved to the provinces (Ministry of Federal Education and
Professional Training, 2017). In practice this means that curriculum, textbooks and school
examinations below the board level are provincial matters, while national bodies coordinate
standards. For a Sindh teacher, the relevant authorities are the provincial education department
and its directorates, the examination boards, and the school itself.

### The layers of assessment a pupil meets

It helps to picture four layers, from the classroom outward.

1. **Classroom assessment by the teacher.** Questioning, classwork, homework, monthly tests,
   and the school's own term and annual (promotion) examinations. This is the only layer the
   teacher fully controls, and it is the focus of the whole Classroom Assessment course.
2. **Public examinations by Boards of Intermediate and Secondary Education** at the secondary
   and higher secondary stages. These are high-stakes, summative and certify pupils for the
   next stage.
3. **Large-scale achievement surveys.** These are not about individual pupils; they tell the
   system how well it is doing. Nationally, the National Education Assessment System (NEAS)
   is named in the National Education Policy 2009. In Sindh, the **Standardized Achievement Test
   (SAT)** was commissioned by the Reform Support Unit of the Education and Literacy Department
   and conducted by Sukkur IBA. Its third round (SAT III, 2014-15) tested 285,624 pupils of
   Classes V and VIII in government schools in language, mathematics and science. Its stated aim
   was to identify gaps in achievement and inform reforms at district and provincial level (Chang
   and Jilani, 2015).
4. **Private and alternative systems.** Private schools set their own tests, and some pupils sit
   examinations of other boards. The result is that pupils in different systems are judged by
   different yardsticks, a concern the National Curriculum Framework raises when it notes that
   public, private, madrassa and non-formal systems follow different curricula and assessment
   systems (Ministry of Federal Education and Professional Training, 2017).

### What national policy says

**National Education Policy 2009, Section 6.4 (Improving Student Assessment).** The policy
singles out rote learning as the deficiency with the most harmful outcomes and calls for
assessment that taps "analytical thinking and critical reflections". Its policy actions include
(Government of Pakistan, 2009):

- exploring multiple assessment tools in addition to traditional examinations, to balance
  formative approaches with the summative approach of high-stakes examinations;
- developing national standards and standardising assessment processes across boards so that
  pupils are judged against common benchmarks;
- making examination boards responsible for building the capacity of paper setters and
  examiners;
- preparing a plan to eliminate cheating and other unfair means;
- linking examinations, national assessment and continuous assessment in a quality cycle that
  feeds back into curriculum, textbooks and teacher education;
- participating in international assessments such as TIMSS.

**National Curriculum 2006.** The curriculum expresses what pupils should learn as competencies,
standards, benchmarks and **Student Learning Outcomes (SLOs)**. The Test Development and
Evaluation guide shows how this changes testing: items are written against SLOs, and marks are
allocated in proportion to standards and benchmarks (HEC, 2012, TDE). A teacher's test that
ignores the SLOs is out of line with the curriculum it is supposed to assess.

**National Professional Standards for Teachers (2009), Standard 5: Assessment.** The guide
reproduces its rubric, which breaks the standard into four strands (HEC, 2012, Unit 1):

| Strand | What the highest level (Level 4) looks like, in summary |
|---|---|
| 5.1 Linked to learning outcomes | Assessment is linked to planned outcomes, uses a range of strategies and is integrated into teaching |
| 5.2 Formative assessment | Plans a variety of formative checks and uses them to change teaching during the lesson |
| 5.3 Summative assessment | Uses a variety of fair, objective tools, including self-made ones, and documents and reports achievement to parents and staff |
| 5.4 Providing feedback | Gives consistently specific, constructive feedback and coaches pupils in self-assessment |

At Level 1, by contrast, assessment is "conducted in traditional 'test taking' manner" with little
variety. The distance between Level 1 and Level 4 is a fair summary of the distance between common
practice and policy.

### Practice: the gap

The HEC guide describes Pakistani schools as being in a slow transition from a **test-based
culture** to an **assessment-based culture** (HEC, 2012, Unit 1). In a test-based culture, marks
on periodic tests are the main evidence, results are used mostly to rank and promote, and
pupils learn to reproduce textbook answers. In an assessment-based culture, evidence is gathered
continuously by many methods, and its main use is to improve learning. Policy (NEP 2009, NPST
Standard 5) points clearly towards the second; much classroom practice remains in the first.

### What this means for the teacher

Policy sets direction; the classroom teacher decides what actually happens on Monday morning.
Within existing rules a Sindh primary teacher can still: write tests against SLOs rather than
textbook sentences; add short formative checks to every lesson; give comment-based feedback on
some work instead of marks on all of it; and keep simple records that make the term grade rest
on more than one test. Each of these moves a classroom along the NPST Standard 5 rubric.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

**Describe** the main layers of assessment in Pakistani schools and **explain** the purpose of
each. Refer to one example from Sindh. *(15 marks)*

### CRQ 2

**Analyse** the gap between assessment policy and classroom practice in Pakistan, referring to the
National Education Policy 2009 and NPST Standard 5, and **suggest** three actions a primary
teacher can take within existing rules. *(15 marks)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Classroom/school-based assessment described with its purpose (improve learning, promotion) | 3 |
| Board examinations described with their purpose (certification, high stakes) | 3 |
| Large-scale assessment described with its purpose (system monitoring, not individual pupils) | 3 |
| Accurate Sindh example (SAT: Classes V and VIII, government schools, commissioned by RSU, conducted by Sukkur IBA, aimed at identifying gaps and informing reform) | 3 |
| Notes provincial responsibility after devolution, or the effect of parallel private/other systems | 3 |

Full marks need at least three layers with distinct purposes and an accurate SAT description.
Invented figures or dates lose the example marks.

### CRQ 2

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Accurately states at least two NEP 2009 assessment provisions (e.g. concern over rote learning, multiple tools balancing formative and summative, standardised benchmarks across boards) | 3 |
| Accurately describes NPST Standard 5 and at least two of its strands | 3 |
| Analyses practice: test-based culture, rote reproduction, marks used mainly to rank | 3 |
| Three practical, policy-consistent teacher actions (e.g. SLO-based tests, formative checks, comment-only feedback, varied summative tools, records) | 4.5 |
| Coherent conclusion linking actions to closing the gap | 1.5 |

A full-mark answer names the policies correctly, shows the contrast between NPST Level 1 and
Level 4, and proposes actions that a teacher can take without new resources.
