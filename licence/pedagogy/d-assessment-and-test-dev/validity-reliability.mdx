# D15. Validity, reliability and characteristics of tests

## What the test asks

CRQs ask you to **define** validity and reliability, **explain** their types, **calculate** a
reliability coefficient (split-half with the Spearman-Brown correction is a favourite),
**explain** the relationship between the two, or **describe** the characteristics of a good test.
A calculation question gives you data; marks go to the method as well as the final number.

Marks are usually lost by:

- saying "a test is valid" as if validity were a fixed property of the paper;
- claiming that a reliable test must be valid;
- applying Spearman-Brown to a whole-test correlation, or forgetting it altogether in split-half.

## Where your degree teaches this

- [EFMP-301 Topic 9.2 - Psychological tests and measurement](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-09/topic-02)
  defines validity (the adequacy of interpretations of scores) and reliability (consistency), shows
  with a target diagram that they are different properties, and adds absence of bias as a third
  requirement.

That is the concept. The degree does not teach the **types** of validity and reliability, how to
**calculate** reliability, the factors that weaken each, or the other characteristics of a good test
listed in the Test Development and Evaluation guide. That residue follows.

## Explanation

### A reminder: both belong to interpretations

The Classroom Assessment guide makes the modern point clearly: validity and reliability are not
properties of tests but of the **conclusions** drawn from test scores (HEC, 2012, Unit 2). "This is a
valid test" is shorthand for "the interpretations we make from these scores, for this purpose and
these pupils, are well supported."

### Types of validity evidence

The Test Development and Evaluation guide lists content, construct, concurrent and predictive
validity (HEC, 2012, TDE). Concurrent and predictive are the two forms of criterion-related validity
(Miller, Linn and Gronlund, 2013).

- **Content validity**: do the items represent the content and objectives taught, in the right
  proportions? This is the classroom teacher's main concern, and the table of specifications is its
  main tool. The guide's unit on test construction speaks of course coverage, concept coverage and
  learning outcomes coverage (HEC, 2012, TDE Unit 3).
- **Criterion-related validity**: do scores agree with another, trusted measure?
  - **Concurrent**: with a measure taken at the same time (a new short reading test against a
    teacher's full reading assessment in the same week).
  - **Predictive**: with a later outcome (Class 8 results predicting success in Class 9 science).
- **Construct validity**: does the test really measure the underlying quality it names (for example,
  "reading comprehension" rather than vocabulary or speed)? Evidence comes from many sources,
  including whether scores behave as the theory predicts.
- **Face validity**: whether the test *looks* suitable to pupils and teachers. It matters for
  motivation but is not evidence that the interpretations are sound.

**Factors that weaken validity**: unclear directions; reading level too hard for the pupils; items
that test only recall when the objectives demand application; poor sampling of content; ambiguous
items; too little time; clues that let pupils guess; cheating; and bias against a group of pupils.

### Types of reliability

The guide's key idea is that every form of reliability is a **correlation between repeated attempts
or repeated judgements**: each one asks whether the result stays consistent (HEC, 2012, TDE).

| Type | How it is obtained | What it checks |
|---|---|---|
| **Test-retest** | Same test, same pupils, two occasions; correlate the scores | Stability over time |
| **Parallel (alternate) forms** | Two equivalent forms given to the same pupils; correlate | Equivalence of forms |
| **Split-half** | One test divided into two halves (e.g. odd and even items); correlate the halves, then correct with Spearman-Brown | Internal consistency |
| **Kuder-Richardson / coefficient alpha** | Computed from item statistics on one administration | Internal consistency across all items |
| **Inter-rater (scorer)** | Two markers score the same scripts; correlate or compare agreement | Consistency of scoring |

### A worked split-half calculation

Six pupils take a 20-item test. Each pupil's score on the 10 odd-numbered items (X) and the 10
even-numbered items (Y) is recorded. (Six pupils are far too few for a real estimate; the small
number keeps the arithmetic visible.)

| Pupil | Odd (X) | Even (Y) | x = X - 5.5 | y = Y - 5.5 | xy | x² | y² |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 9 | 8 | 3.5 | 2.5 | 8.75 | 12.25 | 6.25 |
| B | 7 | 8 | 1.5 | 2.5 | 3.75 | 2.25 | 6.25 |
| C | 6 | 5 | 0.5 | -0.5 | -0.25 | 0.25 | 0.25 |
| D | 5 | 6 | -0.5 | 0.5 | -0.25 | 0.25 | 0.25 |
| E | 4 | 3 | -1.5 | -2.5 | 3.75 | 2.25 | 6.25 |
| F | 2 | 3 | -3.5 | -2.5 | 8.75 | 12.25 | 6.25 |
| **Sum** | 33 | 33 | 0 | 0 | **24.5** | **29.5** | **25.5** |

Both halves have a mean of 33 / 6 = 5.5.

**Step 1: correlate the halves** (Pearson r):

r(half) = Σxy / √(Σx² × Σy²) = 24.5 / √(29.5 × 25.5) = 24.5 / √752.25 = 24.5 / 27.43 = **0.89**

**Step 2: correct for length with the Spearman-Brown formula.** The correlation of 0.89 describes a
10-item test, but the pupils took 20 items. Longer tests are more reliable, so the half-test value
underestimates the whole. The Spearman-Brown formula for doubling the length is:

r(full) = 2 × r(half) / (1 + r(half)) = 2 × 0.893 / (1 + 0.893) = 1.786 / 1.893 = **0.94**

The general form, for a test made k times longer, is r(new) = k × r / (1 + (k - 1) × r). The Test
Development and Evaluation guide uses the same formula for combining several tests or several
markers' judgements (HEC, 2012, TDE Unit 4). For example, if two independent markers' scores
correlate at 0.60, the reliability of their averaged mark is 2 × 0.60 / 1.60 = 0.75.

**Interpreting the result.** A coefficient of 0.94 would indicate high internal consistency. As a
rough guide, the closer to 1.00 the better; classroom tests are commonly well below the reliabilities
of carefully built standardised tests.

**Factors affecting reliability**: test length (more good items, higher reliability); a very narrow
spread of ability in the group (lowers the coefficient); items that are too easy or too hard; ambiguous
items; guessing; inconsistent scoring of open answers; and poor testing conditions.

### Reliability and validity together

Reliability is **necessary but not sufficient** for validity. An inconsistent measure cannot support
sound interpretations, but a perfectly consistent test can still measure the wrong thing, as the
target diagram in Topic 9.2 shows. A teacher's response is different for each: improve an unreliable
test with more items and clearer scoring; improve an invalid one by changing what the items measure.

### Other characteristics of a good test

The Test Development and Evaluation guide's unit on the good test names these alongside validity and
reliability (HEC, 2012, TDE Unit 2):

- **Objectivity**: the score does not depend on who marks it. Objective items and clear marking guides
  raise it.
- **Adequacy**: the test is long and broad enough to sample the content fairly.
- **Differentiability** (discrimination): the test separates pupils who have learned from those who
  have not. Item discrimination indices measure it (see
  [D16](/licence/pedagogy/d-assessment-and-test-dev/test-development-and-review)).
- **Usability (practicality)**: reasonable time, cost, ease of administration, scoring and
  interpretation. The guide's session on the "utility of a test" covers this.
- **Authenticity**: tasks resemble real uses of the knowledge or skill.
- **Comparability**: results can be compared across forms or occasions, as the unit's outcomes
  require.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

In a split-half analysis of a 40-item Class 6 mathematics test, the correlation between scores on the
odd and even items is 0.70. **Calculate** the reliability of the full test using the Spearman-Brown
formula, showing each step, and **explain** why the correction is needed. *(15 marks)*

### CRQ 2

**Explain** four types of validity evidence and **describe** four factors that can lower the validity
of a teacher-made test, with a remedy for each. *(15 marks)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| States the Spearman-Brown formula correctly: r(full) = 2r / (1 + r) | 3 |
| Substitutes correctly: 2 × 0.70 / (1 + 0.70) = 1.40 / 1.70 | 3 |
| Correct result: 0.82 (0.8235 rounded) | 3 |
| Explains that the half-test correlation describes a 20-item test and underestimates the reliability of the 40-item whole, because reliability rises with length | 4 |
| Interprets the value (reasonably high internal consistency for a classroom test) | 2 |

Full marks require all steps shown and the result 0.82. An arithmetic slip with correct method loses
the result marks only.

### CRQ 2

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Content validity explained (representative coverage of taught content and objectives) | 2 |
| Criterion-related validity explained, distinguishing concurrent and predictive | 2 |
| Construct validity explained (measures the intended underlying quality) | 2 |
| Fourth type explained accurately (face validity, with its limits, or concurrent/predictive treated separately) | 1 |

A full-mark answer explains rather than lists the types, and links each factor to a specific
classroom remedy such as a table of specifications, simpler wording, or item review.
