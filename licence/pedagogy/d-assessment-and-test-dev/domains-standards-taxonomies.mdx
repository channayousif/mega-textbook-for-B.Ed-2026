# D14. Domains, standards, taxonomies and frameworks

## What the test asks

This objective joins four ideas. CRQs may ask you to **describe** the three domains of learning with
examples, **differentiate** content standards from performance standards, **explain** how a taxonomy
is used to plan a test, **compare** Bloom's taxonomy with SOLO, or **construct** a simple table of
specifications. The examiner is looking for the link to testing each time: *how does this idea
change the test a teacher writes?*

Marks are usually lost by:

- reciting Bloom's six levels with no connection to test items (Bloom's taxonomy as a whole is
  covered under objective A05; here it matters only as a blueprint tool);
- confusing a standard (broad) with an SLO (specific);
- describing SOLO levels without an example of pupil responses at each level.

## Explanation

### Domains of learning

The Test Development and Evaluation guide introduces three types of learning outcome (HEC, 2012, TDE):

- **Cognitive**: knowledge and mental skills (Bloom, 1956). Example: "explains why the Moon appears
  to change shape".
- **Affective**: feelings, attitudes and values. Krathwohl, Bloom and Masia (1964) arranged it in five
  levels: receiving, responding, valuing, organisation and characterisation by a value. Example:
  "volunteers to help keep the school garden clean".
- **Psychomotor**: physical and manual skills. Simpson (1972) proposed seven levels, from perception
  and set, through guided response and mechanism, to complex overt response, adaptation and
  origination. Example: "uses a measuring cylinder to measure 50 ml of water accurately".

**Why this matters for testing.** Paper-and-pencil tests reach the cognitive domain well and the
other two poorly. Affective outcomes are better assessed by observation, attitude scales and
self-report; psychomotor outcomes need performance tasks with checklists or rubrics (see
[D08](/licence/pedagogy/d-assessment-and-test-dev/performance-based-assessment)). A test plan that
lists only cognitive items has silently dropped two domains of the curriculum.

### Standards, benchmarks and SLOs

Pakistan's National Curriculum 2006 expresses expected learning in layers: **competencies**,
**standards**, **benchmarks** and **Student Learning Outcomes (SLOs)**. As the guide reproduces it,
the curriculum defines standards as specifying, broadly, the knowledge, skills and attitudes that
students will acquire in a key learning area over twelve years of schooling (HEC, 2012, TDE, citing
the National Curriculum for English Language, 2006). Benchmarks describe expected achievement at
particular grade levels, and SLOs state what a pupil should be able to do at the end of a lesson or
unit, using a **command word** and a **content area** (for example, "*identify* the parts of a
flower").

The guide then separates two kinds of standard (HEC, 2012, TDE):

- **Content standards** say **what** should be taught and assessed.
- **Performance standards** say **how well** the content standards must be met. In testing, a
  performance standard is often a **cut score** or a range of scores that places a pupil in a level
  such as pass/fail or a grade band. **Performance descriptors** describe the knowledge and skill
  expected at each level.

A **standards-referenced test** reports performance against these standards. Its advantage, the
guide notes, is precision: different forms of a test built on the same standards can be compared,
and progress can be tracked across cohorts.

### Taxonomies as tools for test design

A **taxonomy** is a classification arranged in levels. In testing, taxonomies help a teacher decide
**at what level of thinking** each item works, so that a test does not end up asking only for recall.

**Bloom's cognitive taxonomy** (1956) has six levels (knowledge, comprehension, application,
analysis, synthesis, evaluation); the revised version (Anderson and Krathwohl, 2001) uses verbs:
remember, understand, apply, analyse, evaluate, create. For test design only two points matter here.
First, the levels become the **columns** of a table of specifications. Second, the Classroom
Assessment guide advises teachers building their first table to start with the first three levels
(knowledge, comprehension, application), and it warns that some items are hard to place and may fit
more than one level (HEC, 2012, Unit 4).

**The SOLO taxonomy** (Structure of the Observed Learning Outcome; Biggs and Collis, 1982) classifies
the **structure of a pupil's answer**, not the question. It has five levels. Take the question "Why
did the 2022 floods cause so much damage in parts of Sindh?":

| SOLO level | What the answer shows | Example response |
|---|---|---|
| Prestructural | Misses the point | "Because it was a bad year." |
| Unistructural | One relevant idea | "Because there was a lot of rain." |
| Multistructural | Several relevant ideas, not connected | "Heavy rain. Low land. Weak houses." |
| Relational | Ideas linked into an explanation | "Very heavy rain fell on flat, low land where water could not drain, so it stood for weeks and weakened mud houses." |
| Extended abstract | Goes beyond, generalises to new cases | "...so any flat, low-lying area with weak drainage and mud housing is at risk, which suggests planning should focus on drainage and building methods." |

The guide asks student teachers to compare the two taxonomies (HEC, 2012, TDE). A fair summary:
**Bloom** helps you plan **questions** at different levels; **SOLO** helps you judge the **quality of
answers**, which makes it especially useful for building marking guides and rubrics for short-answer
and essay items. The Test Development and Evaluation guide expects tables of specifications to be
built on either taxonomy (HEC, 2012, TDE Unit 3).

### Frameworks: putting it together

An **assessment framework** is the plan that connects what is to be assessed (domains, standards and
SLOs), at what level (taxonomy), in what proportions, and by what methods. The National Curriculum
2006 includes such a plan for English: a table allocating marks for Grades IX-X across standards and
benchmarks, and marking some outcomes for classroom or formative assessment instead of the paper
(HEC, 2012, TDE). At classroom level, the framework shrinks to a **table of specifications**: a
two-way grid with content areas as rows and taxonomy levels as columns.

<Figure id="fig-D-2" kind="table" src="/img/figures/licence/d-assessment-and-test-dev/fig-D-2.svg" alt="A two-way table of specifications for a 20-item Class 5 science test: four content areas weighted by teaching periods (plants 8 items, the water cycle 6, weather 4, soil 2) against Remember 6 items, Understand 8 items and Apply 6 items, with row and column totals." width={780} height={388} />

In the example, the teacher spent 10 periods on four topics, so each period is worth 2 of the 20
items: plants (4 periods) get 8 items, the water cycle (3) gets 6, weather (2) gets 4 and soil (1)
gets 2. She decides that 30 per cent of items should test remembering, 40 per cent understanding and
30 per cent application, then fills the cells so that every row and column adds up. How the table is
built step by step, and how items are then written from it, is covered in
[D16](/licence/pedagogy/d-assessment-and-test-dev/test-development-and-review).

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

**Describe** the three domains of learning with one classroom example each, and **explain** what each
implies for the choice of assessment method. *(15 marks)*

### CRQ 2

**Compare** Bloom's taxonomy and the SOLO taxonomy as tools for test development. **Illustrate** the
five SOLO levels with pupil responses to one question from a primary school subject. *(15 marks)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Cognitive domain described with an example | 3 |
| Affective domain described (with at least a mention of its levels) and an example | 3 |
| Psychomotor domain described with an example | 3 |
| Assessment implication for each domain (paper tests for cognitive; observation, scales, self-report for affective; performance tasks and checklists for psychomotor), 2 marks each | 6 |

Full marks need an accurate example and a matched assessment method for each domain. Answers that
only define the domains earn at most 9.

### CRQ 2

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Bloom described as a classification of levels of thinking used to plan questions and ToS columns | 3 |
| SOLO described as a classification of the structure of answers (five levels named) | 3 |
| At least two points of comparison (question versus answer focus; use in planning versus marking; hierarchy of processes versus complexity of response) | 3 |
| Five pupil responses, one per SOLO level, correctly ordered for one question | 5 |
| Conclusion on when each is more useful | 1 |

A full-mark answer uses one question throughout and shows responses that differ in structure (one
idea, several ideas, linked ideas, generalised ideas), not simply in length.
