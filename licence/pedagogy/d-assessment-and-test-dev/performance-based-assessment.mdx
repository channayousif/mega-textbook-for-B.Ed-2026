# D08. Performance-based assessment

## What the test asks

A CRQ may ask you to **define** performance-based assessment and **explain** its features, to
**compare** it with a paper-and-pencil test, to **design** a performance task for a given class
and topic, or to **evaluate** its usefulness in a Pakistani classroom with large classes and few
resources. Design questions reward a task that has a clear prompt, a link to learning objectives,
and criteria for judging the work.

Marks are usually lost by:

- describing an activity but not how it will be assessed (no criteria or rubric);
- calling any group work "performance assessment" even when nothing is observed or judged;
- listing only advantages when the question asks you to evaluate.

## Explanation

### What it is

The HEC guide offers three definitions for student teachers to compare (HEC, 2012, Unit 3).
Together they give a clear picture:

- Performance assessment requires students to **demonstrate** knowledge and skills, including the
  **process** by which they solve problems.
- It is any strategy designed to estimate a child's knowledge, understanding, ability, skill or
  attitudes by methods **other than** standardised multiple-choice tests; typical forms include
  exhibitions, investigations, demonstrations, written or oral responses, journals and portfolios.
- It is a set of strategies for the **application** of knowledge, skills and work habits through
  tasks that are meaningful and engaging to students.

A working definition for the exam: *performance-based assessment asks pupils to carry out a task
that shows what they can do with what they know, and judges both the process and the product
against criteria set in advance.*

The guide's handout puts it simply: this type of assessment examines pupils' competencies and
skills by engaging them in hands-on tasks, and it can last from one period (a **short**
assessment) to several weeks (an **extended** assessment).

### Defining features

1. **Doing, not choosing.** The pupil produces or performs something: measures, builds, writes a
   letter, conducts an experiment, gives a presentation.
2. **Meaningful context.** The task is framed in a realistic situation, which is why these tasks
   are sometimes called authentic.
3. **Process and product.** The teacher can observe how the pupil works, not only the end result.
4. **Integrated skills.** One task often draws on several subjects at once.
5. **Criteria known in advance.** Judgement is made with a rubric or checklist that pupils see
   before they start.
6. **Assessment during and after the task.** Different parts of the rubric may be used on
   different days.

The Test Development and Evaluation guide draws the same contrast as **tasks versus tests**: a
test samples what pupils know under fixed conditions; a task shows what they can do over time
(HEC, 2012, TDE Unit 4).

### Common forms

Demonstrations (using a balance correctly), experiments and investigations, projects,
presentations and oral reports, exhibitions of work, role plays and debates, written products such
as letters or reports, and portfolios (see
[D10](/licence/pedagogy/d-assessment-and-test-dev/portfolio-assessment)).

### A short task from the guide, adapted for Sindh

The guide's short example, "Who has the best apples?", is written for Classes 4 to 5 and takes
three to four periods (HEC, 2012, Unit 3). Pupils play the role of judges at an agricultural fair.
They measure the mass of four apples with a balance and their circumference with a measuring tape,
record the results in a data chart, draw two bar graphs, and write a one-page letter to the winning
farmer explaining the decision. The objectives are to measure mass accurately, measure
circumference accurately, analyse experimental data and draw a bar graph. Assessment happens
during the task (the data chart and the measuring) and at the end (the graphs and the letter), and
a four-level rubric scores each objective.

The same design works with local produce. A Class 5 teacher in Mirpurkhas could use four mangoes
from different growers, and add a science objective about comparing masses and a language
objective about writing a formal letter in Urdu or Sindhi.

### Designing a performance task: the steps

1. **Start with the objectives.** Choose objectives that genuinely need performance to show (for
   example, "uses a balance accurately", not "names the parts of a balance").
2. **Design the task and context.** A situation that gives the task a purpose: a fair, a school
   garden, a letter to the district office.
3. **Write the prompt.** What pupils must do, what they must produce, the materials, the time
   allowed, and whether they work alone or in groups.
4. **Plan the assessment points.** Decide which evidence is collected during the task and which at
   the end.
5. **Build the rubric.** One row per objective, with levels described in observable terms (see
   [D09](/licence/pedagogy/d-assessment-and-test-dev/rubrics)).
6. **Try it and revise.** Check that the task can be done with available materials and time, and
   that two teachers using the rubric would give similar scores.

### Advantages

- It measures skills that paper tests cannot reach: practical, communicative, investigative.
- It links assessment closely to instruction; the task is itself a learning experience.
- Pupils see the purpose of what they learn, which supports motivation.
- It allows pupils with different strengths to show learning, in line with NPST Standard 5.3,
  which asks for a variety of tools that let children "display a variety of strengths" (HEC, 2012,
  NPSTP Standard 5 rubric).

### Challenges

- **Time**: tasks take longer to run and to score than a test.
- **Resources**: balances, tapes or other materials may not be available for every group.
- **Reliability of scoring**: different teachers may judge the same work differently unless the
  rubric is clear and teachers practise with it.
- **Sampling**: a few long tasks cover less content than many short items, so generalising from one
  task is risky.
- **Group work**: it can be hard to separate individual contributions.

The guide asks student teachers to weigh exactly these usefulness-versus-challenge questions after
they try a task in class (HEC, 2012, Unit 3). In large Pakistani classes, practical responses
include running the task in rotating groups, using locally available materials, scoring only two
or three rubric rows per session, and combining performance tasks with shorter tests so that
content coverage is not lost.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

**Define** performance-based assessment and **explain** four of its features. **Compare** it with a
conventional paper-and-pencil test. *(15 marks)*

### CRQ 2

**Design** a short performance-based task for Class 4 on measuring length. Include the objectives,
the pupil prompt, the evidence you will collect during and after the task, and the criteria you
will use. *(15 marks)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Clear definition (pupils demonstrate knowledge and skills through a task; process and product judged against criteria) | 3 |
| Four distinct features explained (e.g. doing not choosing, realistic context, process and product, integrated skills, criteria in advance, short or extended), 1.5 marks each | 6 |
| Comparison with a paper test on at least three points (what is measured, time, scoring, content coverage, reliability) | 6 |

Full marks need four features that are explained, not just listed, and a balanced comparison that
recognises what tests do better (coverage, speed, consistency).

### CRQ 2

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Two or three objectives that require performance (e.g. measures accurately in centimetres) | 3 |
| A pupil prompt with a realistic context, task, product, materials and time | 3 |
| Evidence collected during the task (e.g. observation checklist) | 2 |
| Evidence collected at the end (e.g. recorded table, labelled drawing, short explanation) | 2 |
| Criteria or rubric rows linked to each objective, with levels described | 4 |
| Feasibility in a typical Sindh classroom addressed | 1 |

A full-mark answer is aligned throughout: every objective is seen in the evidence and judged by a
criterion. Tasks without criteria earn at most 8.
