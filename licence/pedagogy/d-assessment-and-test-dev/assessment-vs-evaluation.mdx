# D02. Assessment and evaluation: the difference

## What the test asks

This objective is almost always examined with the command word **differentiate** or
**compare**, sometimes followed by **illustrate with an example**. The examiner wants
several clear points of contrast, not one. A table is acceptable in a CRQ if each row is a
real point of difference and you add a short example.

Common ways to lose marks:

- saying only "assessment is formative and evaluation is summative", which is an
  oversimplification (a summative test can be an assessment too);
- listing differences that are really the same point in different words;
- giving no example, or an example that does not actually show a judgement of value.

## Explanation

### Why the two words get confused

In everyday staff-room talk, "assessment" and "evaluation" are used as if they meant the
same thing, and some textbooks do the same. The HEC Classroom Assessment guide admits this
openly: evaluation and interpretation "are often interchanged", and definitions of
evaluation and assessment vary from expert to expert (HEC, 2012, Unit 1). For the licence
exam you need a working distinction that you can defend. The one below follows the guide's
logic and the standard measurement texts it recommends.

### The core distinction

**Assessment** is the process of gathering and interpreting evidence about learning in order
to understand it and improve it. Its question is *"What does this pupil know and what does
she need next?"*

**Evaluation** is the process of making a **judgement of worth, merit or quality** on the
basis of evidence. Its question is *"How good is this, and what should we decide about it?"*
Evaluation uses assessment evidence, but it adds a standard to judge against and a decision.

In short: assessment describes and diagnoses; evaluation judges and decides.

### Points of difference

| Point | Assessment | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Main purpose | To understand and improve learning | To judge the worth or quality of learning, a programme or a product |
| Orientation | Process-oriented: how learning is going | Product-oriented: what has been achieved |
| Timing | Continuous, during teaching | Usually at set points: end of a unit, term, year or project |
| Feedback | Diagnostic: shows strengths and gaps, points to next steps | Judgemental: gives a grade, rank, pass/fail or rating |
| Relationship between teacher and learner | Collaborative; the pupil can take part (self- and peer-assessment) | More often done to the learner by an authority (teacher, board, inspector) |
| Scope | Usually an individual pupil or class | Can extend to teachers, schools, textbooks and courses |
| Use of results | Adjust tomorrow's teaching and the pupil's effort | Certify, promote, select, report, or keep or drop a programme |

The scope row matters for this syllabus. The Test Development and Evaluation guide devotes a
whole unit to evaluation of teachers, textbooks and courses, and to school accountability
(HEC, 2012, TDE Unit 5). You can assess a pupil's reading, but you **evaluate** a reading
programme, a textbook or a teacher's performance. Assessment is not normally used for those.

### The relationship, not just the contrast

A good answer shows the two are linked. Evaluation depends on assessment: a judgement with no
evidence behind it is an opinion. And assessment information often ends in an evaluation: a
term's worth of classwork, quizzes and observations is summarised into a report card grade.

The guide adds a point worth quoting in a CRQ: the real difference between formative and
summative work lies in the purpose and in how the results are used. A summative test, once its
first purpose is served, can be turned into a formative assessment, for example by going
through the errors with the class, but a formative assessment cannot be turned back into a
summative test (HEC, 2012, Unit 1). The same logic applies here: the evidence can be identical;
what makes it assessment or evaluation is the question you ask of it.

### A Sindh classroom example

In a Class 6 English class in a Larkana government school, Ms Shazia asks pupils to write a
short paragraph about their village every Friday. Each week she reads the paragraphs, notes
that many pupils cannot yet use the past tense consistently, writes one question on each script
("Which three verbs here should be in the past?"), and plans a ten-minute past-tense activity
for Monday. That is **assessment**: continuous, diagnostic, used to improve learning.

At the end of the term the school must report a grade for "writing" to parents. Ms Shazia
reviews the term's paragraphs against the school's criteria and awards each pupil a grade. At
the same time, the head teacher uses the term results of all Class 6 sections to judge whether
the new weekly writing routine is worth continuing next year. Both of those are **evaluation**:
one judges a pupil's achievement, the other judges a programme.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

**Differentiate** between assessment and evaluation on at least five points, and **illustrate**
each with reference to one classroom situation. *(15 marks)*

### CRQ 2

A head teacher says: "We already evaluate pupils at the end of every term, so teachers do not
need to assess during the term." **Evaluate** this claim. *(15 marks)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Working definitions: assessment gathers and interprets evidence to improve learning; evaluation judges worth or quality | 3 |
| At least five genuine, distinct points of difference (purpose, orientation, timing, feedback, scope, use of results, learner role), 1.5 marks each | 7.5 |
| One classroom situation used consistently to illustrate the points | 3 |
| Notes the link: evaluation depends on assessment evidence | 1.5 |

A full-mark answer defines both terms, gives five or more non-overlapping differences, and uses
one realistic example (such as weekly writing tasks versus a term grade) throughout. Points that
repeat each other in different words are credited once.

### CRQ 2

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Identifies the confusion: the claim treats evaluation as a substitute for assessment | 2 |
| Explains that end-of-term evaluation arrives too late to change teaching during the term | 3 |
| Explains what continuous assessment adds (diagnosis, feedback, adjusting instruction, pupil involvement) | 4 |
| Explains that evaluation itself becomes more valid when it rests on a term's worth of assessment evidence | 3 |
| Reaches a reasoned judgement, ideally linked to the shift from a test-based to an assessment-based culture or to NPST Standard 5 | 3 |

Full marks need a clear verdict (the claim is mistaken) supported by at least two reasons and a
practical illustration. A one-sided list of the benefits of assessment without addressing the
head teacher's reasoning earns at most 9.
