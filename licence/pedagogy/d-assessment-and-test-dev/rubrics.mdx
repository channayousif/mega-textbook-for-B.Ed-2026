# D09. Assessment rubrics and their importance

## What the test asks

A CRQ may ask you to **define** a rubric and **explain** its importance, to **differentiate** analytic
from holistic rubrics, or to **construct** a rubric for a given task. Construction questions are
marked on whether the criteria match the task's objectives and whether the level descriptors are
clear enough for two markers to agree.

Marks are usually lost by:

- writing levels that differ only by vague words ("good", "very good", "excellent");
- including criteria that have nothing to do with the objective (neatness in a science
  explanation);
- confusing a rubric with a mark total ("out of 10") that has no descriptions.

## Explanation

### What a rubric is

A **rubric** is a scoring guide that sets out the **criteria** by which a piece of work will be
judged and **describes** what performance looks like at each **level** of quality. Moskal (2000)
describes scoring rubrics as descriptive scoring schemes developed to guide the analysis of
products or processes of students' efforts. The key word is *descriptive*: a rubric says what a
level looks like, not only how many marks it earns.

In the HEC Classroom Assessment course, rubrics appear wherever the answer cannot be marked right
or wrong: essays, projects and performance tasks. The guide's essay handout ends with the plain
instruction that "essay questions are scored using a rubric", and its performance-task examples
each come with a four-level rubric whose rows are the learning standards being assessed (HEC,
2012, Units 3 and 4).

### The parts of a rubric

1. **Criteria** (the rows): the qualities that matter, drawn from the learning objectives.
2. **Performance levels** (the columns): usually three to five, labelled with numbers (4, 3, 2, 1)
   or words (exemplary, proficient, developing, beginning).
3. **Descriptors** (the cells): observable descriptions of work at each level on each criterion.
4. **Scoring rule**: how levels convert to marks, and whether some criteria carry more weight.

### Analytic and holistic rubrics

| Point | Analytic rubric | Holistic rubric |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Each criterion scored separately, then totalled | One overall judgement of the whole work |
| Feedback | Detailed: shows strengths and weaknesses by criterion | General: one level with one description |
| Speed | Slower to use | Faster to use |
| Best for | Formative use, diagnosing specific skills, complex tasks | Quick summative judgements, large numbers of scripts, overall impression tasks |
| Risk | Can fragment a piece of work into parts | Hides uneven performance (strong ideas, weak organisation) |

The HEC course asks student teachers to recognise that essays can be graded by either method (HEC,
2012, Unit 4). A useful rule: choose **analytic** when you want pupils to know exactly what to
improve, and **holistic** when you need a fair overall judgement quickly.

A second distinction is between **general** rubrics, which can be reused across many similar tasks
(for example, any persuasive paragraph), and **task-specific** rubrics, which describe the content
of one task (for example, "names three conditions for plant growth"). General rubrics are better
for teaching because pupils can learn the criteria and apply them again; task-specific rubrics
give more reliable scoring for one assessment (Brookhart, 2013).

### An example: analytic rubric for a Class 6 paragraph

Task: write a paragraph of 80 to 100 words describing your village or neighbourhood.

| Criterion | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ideas and detail | Clear main idea; three or more specific details that a reader can picture | Clear main idea; two specific details | Main idea present but details are general | No clear main idea; little detail |
| Organisation | Topic sentence, details in a logical order, closing sentence | Topic sentence and logical order; weak ending | Some order, but no clear topic sentence | Sentences in no clear order |
| Language accuracy | Almost no errors in tense, spelling and punctuation | A few errors that do not affect meaning | Frequent errors that sometimes affect meaning | Errors make the paragraph hard to follow |

A **holistic** version of the same rubric would merge these into one scale, for example: "4: a
clear, well-organised paragraph with vivid detail and few errors ... 1: unclear, disorganised,
with errors that block meaning."

### How to build a rubric

1. **List the objectives** the task assesses. Each becomes a criterion. Keep to three to five
   criteria.
2. **Decide the number of levels.** Four levels avoid a "safe middle" and are common in the HEC
   guide's examples.
3. **Write the top and bottom levels first**, describing what you would actually see.
4. **Fill in the middle levels** so each step is a real, observable difference, not just a change of
   adjective.
5. **Test the rubric on real work.** Score a few samples, ideally with a colleague, and revise
   descriptors where you disagree.
6. **Share it with pupils before the task**, with examples of work at different levels.

### Why rubrics matter

- **Fairness and consistency.** Every pupil is judged by the same criteria. This reduces the effect
  of the marker's mood, handwriting bias or the order in which scripts are read.
- **Reliability between markers.** The Test Development and Evaluation guide pairs rubrics with
  **double marking** and **inter-rater reliability**: two teachers score the same work with the same
  rubric and check how closely they agree (HEC, 2012, TDE Unit 4). A clear rubric is what makes
  agreement possible.
- **Validity.** Because criteria are drawn from objectives, the score reflects what was meant to be
  learned rather than irrelevant features.
- **Transparency.** Pupils and parents can see why a mark was given.
- **Feedback.** An analytic rubric tells a pupil exactly which criterion to work on next, turning a
  grade into feedback.
- **Self- and peer-assessment.** Pupils who understand the criteria can judge their own and each
  other's work, which is the highest level of NPST Standard 5.4 (HEC, 2012, NPSTP Standard 5
  rubric).
- **Efficiency.** Once built, a rubric speeds up marking of complex work.

### Common mistakes

- Descriptors that count rather than describe quality ("uses 5 adjectives").
- Criteria that overlap, so one weakness is penalised twice.
- Too many criteria, making the rubric slow and confusing.
- Rubrics written after the work is marked, which then justify a mark rather than guide it.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

**Differentiate** between analytic and holistic rubrics, and **explain** three reasons why rubrics
are important in classroom assessment. *(15 marks)*

### CRQ 2

**Construct** an analytic rubric with three criteria and four levels for a Class 5 science task in
which pupils explain, with a labelled drawing, how a plant takes in water. *(15 marks)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Accurate definition of a rubric (criteria plus level descriptors) | 2 |
| Analytic rubric described (criteria scored separately; detailed feedback) | 2 |
| Holistic rubric described (single overall judgement; faster) | 2 |
| At least two points of contrast with appropriate uses of each | 3 |
| Three distinct reasons for importance (fairness/consistency, reliability between markers, validity, transparency, feedback, self-assessment), each explained, 2 marks each | 6 |

Full marks need a clear contrast with a stated use for each type and three explained reasons.
Reasons that are only named earn 1 mark each.

### CRQ 2

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Three relevant criteria drawn from the task (e.g. scientific explanation of water movement, accuracy and labelling of the drawing, use of scientific vocabulary) | 4.5 |
| Four levels clearly ordered for each criterion | 3 |
| Descriptors observable and distinct between levels (not only "good/better/best") | 4.5 |
| Criteria do not overlap and exclude irrelevant features | 1.5 |
| Scoring rule or total stated | 1.5 |

A full-mark rubric would, for example, describe level 4 on the explanation criterion as "explains
that water enters through root hairs and moves up the stem to the leaves, in the correct order",
and level 1 as "names a plant part but does not explain how water moves".
