# D07. Types of test questions

## What the test asks

CRQs on this objective ask you to **classify** question types, **compare** selected-response with
constructed-response items, **explain** the advantages and limitations of a type, or **identify
the flaws** in a badly written item and **rewrite** it. The flaw-spotting format is popular
because a well-prepared candidate can apply the writing guidelines rather than just recite them.

Marks are usually lost by:

- naming types without saying what each is good for;
- rewriting a flawed item but leaving a second flaw in place;
- claiming that multiple-choice items can only test recall (well-written interpretive items can
  test analysis and reasoning).

## Explanation

### Two families of questions

Every test question asks the pupil either to **select** a response from those offered or to
**construct** one. The HEC guide organises its handout on test questions around exactly this
division (HEC, 2012, Unit 4); the handout is adapted from standard measurement texts, including
McMillan (2011) and Miller, Linn and Gronlund (2013).

- **Selected-response items**: true-false, matching and multiple-choice (including interpretive
  exercises). They are quick to answer and to score, and scoring is objective: two markers will
  agree. Their weakness is that pupils can guess, and poorly written items test only recall.
- **Constructed-response items**: sentence completion, short answer, and essays (restricted and
  extended). The pupil must produce the answer, so guessing is less of a problem and complex
  thinking can be shown. They take longer to mark, and marking can be inconsistent unless a
  rubric is used.

A good classroom test usually mixes the two, choosing each item type for what it measures best.

### True-false items

A statement that the pupil marks as true or false. They sample a lot of content quickly, but a
pupil who guesses has a 50 per cent chance on each item. The guide's guidelines include
(HEC, 2012, Unit 4):

- base statements on content taught as definitely true or definitely false, never on opinion;
- keep true and false statements roughly equal in number and length;
- avoid double negatives, and avoid words such as *always*, *never*, *only* (absolutes) and
  *frequently*, *briefly* (vague quantities), because they give clues;
- do not arrange items in a pattern (T, T, F, F);
- write your own statements rather than lifting sentences from the textbook.

*Weak:* "Plants never grow without sunlight." (Absolute word; the pupil can guess "false".)
*Better:* "Green plants make their own food using energy from sunlight." (True, clear, taught.)

### Matching items

Two lists: descriptions (numbered) and options (lettered). Pupils match each description with an
option. Matching is efficient for testing associations: terms and definitions, events and dates,
authors and works. Guidelines (HEC, 2012, Unit 4):

- keep both lists short and **homogeneous** (all dates, or all terms, not a mixture);
- include **more options than descriptions**, so the last match cannot be found by elimination;
- state in the directions how to show the match and whether an option can be used more than once.

### Multiple-choice items

A **stem** (the question or incomplete statement), a **key** (the right option) and several
**distractors** (plausible wrong options). The Test Development and Evaluation guide expects you
to name these three parts (HEC, 2012, TDE Unit 3). Multiple-choice is the most flexible
selected-response type: it can test knowledge, understanding and, with care, application and
analysis. Guidelines from the guide's handout (HEC, 2012, Unit 4):

- the stem alone should make the question clear;
- every distractor must be plausible yet wrong;
- keep options of similar length and form, so the longest option is not a giveaway;
- avoid negative stems ("Which of these is **not**...");
- remove grammatical clues (an article such as "an" that fits only one option);
- vary the position of the key at random;
- avoid "all of the above" and "none of the above".

*Weak:* "Quaid-e-Azam Muhammad Ali Jinnah was a: (a) doctor (b) engineer (c) farmer (d) an
lawyer" (grammatical clue and an implausible pattern).
*Better:* "Before independence, Muhammad Ali Jinnah practised which profession? (a) medicine
(b) law (c) engineering (d) journalism."

### Interpretive exercises

The guide explains that true-false and multiple-choice items can test higher-order thinking if
the pupil is first given new material (a short passage, table, map, graph or picture) and then
asked questions that can only be answered by interpreting it (HEC, 2012, Unit 4). The material
must be new, brief, accurate and readable, and the questions must be impossible to answer without
reading it. This is how a selected-response test reaches analysis and evaluation.

### Sentence completion and short answer

**Completion** items leave a blank for a word or short phrase: "The instrument used to measure air
pressure is a ________." Put the blank near the end, make all blanks the same length, and make
sure only one answer fits. **Short-answer** items ask a direct question answered in one to three
sentences: "What is the main purpose of formative assessment in the classroom?" Both mainly test
knowledge and comprehension. The guide adds that the answer space should match the length of the
expected answer and the wording should use vocabulary pupils know (HEC, 2012, Unit 4).

### Essay questions

Essays measure what objective items cannot: organising ideas, building an argument, explaining a
process, evaluating evidence. The guide distinguishes two kinds (HEC, 2012, Unit 4):

- **Restricted-response** essays set clear limits on content and length. "In one page, explain the
  role of the large intestine in digestion" is better than "Explain the digestive system", because
  it tells the pupil the boundaries.
- **Extended-response** essays give more freedom, but they should still supply a scenario or
  background, a clear task, and the points to address, for example: "In two pages, defend the
  importance of conserving natural resources. Include a definition, three arguments and three
  ways to conserve."

Essay guidelines worth quoting: use essays only for outcomes that objective items cannot measure
well; tell pupils the approximate time for each question; avoid offering a choice of essay
questions (pupils answering different questions have not taken the same test); and score with a
rubric. The main limitations are that essays sample less content, take long to mark, and scores
depend partly on writing ability and on the marker.

### Choosing the right type

| If you want to measure... | Suitable item types |
|---|---|
| Recall of many facts, quickly | True-false, matching, completion, multiple-choice |
| Understanding of a concept | Multiple-choice, short answer |
| Application to a new situation | Multiple-choice with a scenario, short answer, interpretive exercise |
| Reasoning from given information | Interpretive exercise |
| Organising and communicating ideas, argument, evaluation | Restricted or extended essay |

The table follows the guide's principle that the item type should be chosen to match the learning
objective, which is also the basis of the table of specifications (see
[D16](/licence/pedagogy/d-assessment-and-test-dev/test-development-and-review)).

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

**Compare** selected-response and constructed-response test questions. For each family, give two
examples of item types and **explain** one advantage and one limitation. *(15 marks)*

### CRQ 2

**Identify** the flaws in the following item and **rewrite** it to remove them. Then **state** which
level of thinking your rewritten item measures.

"Which of the following is not a cause of flooding in Sindh? (a) heavy monsoon rain (b) river
overflow (c) all of the above (d) an earthquake" *(15 marks)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Defines selected response (pupil chooses from given options) and constructed response (pupil produces the answer) | 3 |
| Two correctly classified examples of each family | 4 |
| One valid advantage and one valid limitation of selected response (e.g. objective and quick to score; guessing, tends to test recall if poorly written) | 4 |
| One valid advantage and one valid limitation of constructed response (e.g. shows complex thinking; slow and less consistent to mark) | 4 |

Full marks need both families defined, four correct examples, and paired advantages and
limitations that are specific rather than generic.

### CRQ 2

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Negative stem identified ("not") | 2 |
| "All of the above" identified as a flawed option (it also contradicts the negative stem) | 2 |
| Grammatical clue identified ("an earthquake" is the only option with an article) | 2 |
| Implausible or inconsistent options noted; options not parallel in form | 2 |
| Rewritten item with a positive stem, four plausible, parallel options and one defensible key | 5 |
| States the thinking level measured by the rewritten item (e.g. knowledge or understanding) with a reason | 2 |

A strong rewrite: "Which of the following most often causes river flooding in Sindh during July and
August? (a) monsoon rainfall upstream (b) winter snowfall (c) coastal cyclones (d) canal
maintenance", keyed (a), measuring understanding of cause. Accept any rewrite that meets the
guidelines and has a single defensible key.
