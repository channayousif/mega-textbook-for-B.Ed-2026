# D12. Tests and measurement

## What the test asks

CRQs ask you to **define** test and measurement, **classify** tests on different bases with
examples, **explain** the scales of measurement, or **discuss** the benefits and limitations of
testing. A frequent question asks why a single test score should not be treated as an exact
measure of a pupil's ability.

Marks are usually lost by:

- defining measurement as "giving a test" (measurement is assigning numbers by a rule, and it
  can happen without a test);
- listing kinds of tests without saying on what basis they are classified;
- treating scores as error-free.

## Explanation

### Test, testing and measurement

A **test** is a systematic procedure for obtaining a sample of a person's behaviour, usually
through a set of questions or tasks, under set conditions and scored by set rules. **Testing** is
the process of giving it. **Measurement** is the assignment of numbers to a characteristic
according to a rule (Miller, Linn and Gronlund, 2013). A test is one instrument of measurement;
rating scales, checklists and counts of observed behaviour are others. The Test Development and
Evaluation guide begins with exactly these ideas: the concept of testing, kinds of tests, and the
benefits and limits of tests (HEC, 2012, TDE Unit 1).

Testing has a long history. The guide's teaching notes trace it from the imperial examinations of
China, through the "payment by results" system introduced in England in 1863 (where school funding
depended partly on pupils' examination performance, until it collapsed in 1890), to the first
successful intelligence test by Alfred Binet and Theodore Simon in 1905 (HEC, 2012, TDE). The same
tension runs through the whole story: tests are useful for fair, objective decisions, but they can
narrow what schools value.

### The four scales of measurement

Numbers carry different amounts of information depending on the scale (Stevens, 1946):

| Scale | What the numbers tell you | Classroom example |
|---|---|---|
| **Nominal** | Categories only; numbers are labels | Roll numbers; house groups 1, 2, 3 |
| **Ordinal** | Order, but not equal distances | Class positions (1st, 2nd, 3rd); grades A, B, C |
| **Interval** | Order and equal distances, but no true zero | Standard scores on a well-built test |
| **Ratio** | Equal distances and a true zero | Time taken to read a passage; number of words read per minute |

Why it matters: you can say one pupil read **twice as many** words per minute as another (ratio),
but you cannot say a pupil in 2nd position is "twice as good" as one in 4th (ordinal). Most classroom
test scores are treated as if they were interval, which is a reasonable working assumption only when
the test is well constructed.

### Kinds of tests

Tests are classified in several ways, and a good answer names the **basis** of each classification:

- **By purpose in instruction** (Miller, Linn and Gronlund, 2013): **placement** (before
  instruction, to find the starting point), **formative** (during, to guide teaching), **diagnostic**
  (to find the causes of persistent difficulties) and **summative** (after, to certify or grade).
- **By what is measured:** **achievement** tests (what has been learned), **aptitude** tests (potential
  to learn), **intelligence** tests (general mental ability), and measures of **attitude, interest and
  personality**.
- **By method of interpreting scores:** **norm-referenced** and **criterion-referenced** (see
  [D11](/licence/pedagogy/d-assessment-and-test-dev/teacher-made-tests)).
- **By who constructs it:** **teacher-made** and **standardized**.
- **By item format:** **objective** (selected-response or short supply items, scored the same way by
  any marker) and **subjective** (essays and extended responses, which need judgement).
- **By administration:** **individual** (one pupil at a time, as in many intelligence tests) and
  **group** (a whole class at once).
- **By time conditions:** **speed** tests (easy items, strict time; the score reflects how many are
  done) and **power** tests (items of increasing difficulty, generous time; the score reflects how
  hard a problem the pupil can solve).
- **By mode of response:** **written**, **oral** and **performance** (practical) tests.

### Measurement error: why a score is an estimate

The Classroom Assessment guide makes a point that every candidate should be able to explain: all
assessments are subject to **error** (HEC, 2012, Unit 2). A pupil's obtained score is made up of
their "true" level plus error, which may come from the pupil (illness, anxiety, guessing), the test
(a small or unrepresentative sample of items, unclear wording), the conditions (noise, heat, poor
light, a crowded room) or the scoring (inconsistent markers).

The Test Development and Evaluation guide introduces the **standard error of measurement** (SEM),
calculated as SD multiplied by the square root of (1 minus the reliability coefficient) (HEC, 2012,
TDE). For example, on a test with a standard deviation of 8 marks and a reliability of 0.84,
SEM = 8 x √(1 - 0.84) = 8 x 0.4 = 3.2 marks. A pupil who scored 30 is therefore best described as
scoring "about 30, give or take roughly 3", and two pupils scoring 30 and 32 cannot be confidently
separated. This is why important decisions should never rest on one test score.

### Benefits of testing

- **Objectivity and fairness**: everyone answers the same questions under the same conditions.
- **Efficiency**: many pupils can be assessed on much content in a short time.
- **Diagnosis**: item results show which objectives were not learned.
- **Motivation and direction**: pupils study what they know will be tested.
- **Accountability and reporting**: results can be summarised for parents, schools and the system.
- **Improving teaching**: results show the teacher what needs re-teaching.

### Limitations of testing

- A test measures only a **sample** of behaviour on one occasion, with error.
- Paper tests reach recall more easily than practical, social and creative abilities.
- High-stakes testing encourages **rote learning** and teaching to the test, a problem the National
  Education Policy 2009 names directly (see [D05](/licence/pedagogy/d-assessment-and-test-dev/assessment-policy-pakistan)).
- Language load can make a test measure reading rather than the subject.
- Test anxiety can depress the scores of some pupils.
- Scores are easily over-interpreted as fixed labels.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

**Classify** tests on four different bases, giving an example of each kind, and **explain** why the
basis of classification matters. *(15 marks)*

### CRQ 2

A Class 7 pupil scored 30 on one test with a standard error of measurement of 3 marks. **Explain**
what this score means, identify three possible sources of measurement error, and **justify** why a
promotion decision should not rest on this score alone. *(15 marks)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Four valid bases of classification named (purpose, what is measured, interpretation, constructor, format, administration, time conditions, response mode), 1 mark each | 4 |
| Correct kinds under each basis with an accurate example, 2 marks per basis | 8 |
| Explains that different bases answer different questions about a test (who made it, what it measures, how scores are read), so one test can belong to several categories | 3 |

Full marks need four genuinely different bases, correctly populated, and the point that one test can
be classified in several ways at once (for example, a teacher-made, objective, criterion-referenced,
summative achievement test).

### CRQ 2

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Explains that the obtained score is an estimate: true score plus error | 3 |
| Interprets the SEM: the pupil's level is best described as about 27 to 33 | 3 |
| Three distinct sources of error (pupil, test, conditions, scoring), 1 mark each | 3 |
| Justifies not relying on one score: error, limited sample, one occasion, high stakes | 4 |
| Recommends combining several sources of evidence (classwork, other tests, observation) | 2 |

A full-mark answer uses the SEM correctly and ties the promotion decision to the need for multiple
evidence.
