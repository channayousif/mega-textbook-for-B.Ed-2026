# E01. Structures and functions of community and schools

## What the test asks

A CRQ on this objective asks you to **describe** how communities and schools are organised in
Pakistan, to **explain** the functions a school performs for its community, or to **analyse** how a
body such as a school management committee (SMC) links the two. Some questions give a short
scenario (a village school with falling enrolment, an SMC that never meets) and ask you to
**suggest** what the structures could do.

Marks are usually lost by:

- defining "community" and "society" as the same thing;
- listing school types without saying how they differ in ownership, funding or culture;
- describing the SMC only as a fund-approving body and missing its duties on attendance and the
  school improvement plan;
- writing only about the classroom when the question is about the school as an institution.

## Explanation

### Society, community and school

The HEC course guide opens with three words written on the board: **society**, **community** and
**education**, and asks how each is connected to the other two (HEC, 2012, Unit 1, session 1).

- A **society** is a large group of people who share a culture. OpenStax separates the two ideas
  neatly: culture is the values, beliefs, norms, language and practices of a group, while society is
  the people who share that culture (Conerly et al., 2021, Ch. 3). Pakistan as a whole is a society.
- A **community** is smaller and closer. It is a group of people linked by a shared place or a shared
  sense of belonging: a village, a neighbourhood (mohalla), a goth in rural Sindh, or the families
  who send their children to the same school. People in a community know one another, meet often and
  depend on the same local services.
- A **school** is a formal institution that a society creates to educate its children. It sits inside
  a community, draws its pupils and many of its staff from that community, and is shaped by it.

The guide stresses two further distinctions. **School** (the institution) is not the same as
**schooling** (the pattern of attending school that families follow), and communities living in the
same region can have quite different schooling patterns (HEC, 2012, Unit 1, session 1). Two
villages a few kilometres apart may differ in whether girls continue after Class 5, whether children
go to a government school or a low-fee private school, and whether parents ever visit.

### How schooling is organised in Pakistan

**By stage.** Schooling moves through pre-primary, primary, middle, secondary and higher secondary
stages. Article 25A of the Constitution, inserted in 2010, says that "the State shall provide free and
compulsory education to all children of the age of five to sixteen years" in the manner the law
decides. Education is now mainly a provincial matter, so in Sindh this right is given effect by the
Sindh Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2013.

**By sector.** The course guide asks student teachers to compare the culture of three types of
school, "public schools, private schools, and madrassas", and elsewhere adds community-based and NGO
schools (HEC, 2012, Unit 4, session 17; Unit 5, session 22). Their main differences can be set out as
follows.

| Sector | Who runs and funds it | What shapes its culture |
|---|---|---|
| Public (government) | Provincial education department, from public funds; free for pupils | Government curriculum and rules; teachers are civil servants; the community's voice comes mainly through the SMC |
| Private | Individuals, trusts or companies, from fees; fee levels vary from low to very high | Parents as fee-payers; competition for enrolment; wide variation in quality |
| Madrassa (deeni madrasa) | Religious organisations, often from donations | Religious scholarship, memorisation of the Quran and Islamic studies; strong ties to a local masjid and its congregation |
| Community-based and NGO schools | Non-government organisations or community groups, sometimes with government support | Local ownership; often set up where no other school is within reach |

The guide's session question "can a masjid be a school by itself?" is a reminder that in many
Pakistani communities religious and educational institutions overlap (HEC, 2012, Unit 5,
session 22). A good answer does not rank the sectors. It describes how each is owned, funded and
governed, and what that means for its relationship with the community.

### Structures that link the school and the community

The school and the community are joined by formal structures as well as by everyday contact. The
UNESCO review of primary education reproduced in the guide lists "PTAs, school councils, village
education committees, women village education committees, parent education councils" as the kinds of
bodies that can be set up "according to the local needs and context" (HEC, 2012, Faculty Resources,
Unit 1).

In Sindh, the legally required body is the **school management committee**. Under the Sindh Right
of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2013:

- every school must constitute an SMC with **equal representation** from government, teachers,
  parents of children in the school, and the school's management, headed by the head teacher;
- the SMC must meet at least **three times** in an academic year;
- its functions include monitoring the general working of the school, making sure prescribed norms
  and standards are observed, making sure government education policy is implemented, preparing and
  recommending the **School Improvement Plan**, and monitoring the use of grants and aid;
- it must **ensure the attendance of every child**, and it may direct a parent whose child is not
  attending to send the child to school.

This makes the SMC more than a committee that signs cheques. It is the point where parents and the
community share responsibility for enrolment, attendance and improvement.

### Functions of a school for its community

Sociologists describe what schools do in terms of **manifest functions** (openly intended) and
**latent functions** (unstated, sometimes unintended). OpenStax lists socialization, transmission of
culture, social control, social placement and cultural innovation as manifest functions, and social
networks, group work and political and social integration among the latent ones (Conerly et al.,
2021, Section 16.2). Applied to a Pakistani community:

1. **Socialization and transmission of culture.** Children learn the language, values and norms of
   their society, including national identity and shared religious and civic values.
2. **Social control.** Pupils learn to follow rules and respect legitimate authority.
3. **Social placement.** Certificates open routes to further study and work, so the school affects
   who in the community moves up.
4. **Cultural innovation.** New knowledge and skills (science, digital skills, health awareness)
   enter the community through its children.
5. **A community resource.** The school building, its teachers and its events serve the wider
   community, for example as a place for meetings, adult literacy classes or health awareness sessions. Unit 4 of the guide develops this
   idea of the school as a "hub for community services" (see E06).

### How the community shapes the school

The relationship runs both ways. The guide's Unit 1 goal is for student teachers to "identify social
factors that influence teaching and learning in Pakistan". Its readings name poverty, children's need
to earn, harsh school discipline, a shortage of teachers and cultural attitudes to girls' education as
reasons children do not enrol or do not stay (HEC, 2012, Unit 1, Faculty Resources). It also records
that the most successful quality projects were those "developed with the community and parents'
support", which became programmes "owned and managed by the community".

**An illustration from Sindh.** Imagine a government primary school near Mirpurkhas where attendance
falls every cotton picking season. The head teacher raises it at the SMC. The parent members explain which families
depend on children's labour in those weeks; the teacher members propose a catch-up timetable; the
committee writes the problem and the response into the School Improvement Plan and agrees to visit
the families of children absent for more than a week. Every part of that response uses a structure
the law already provides.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

**Differentiate** between society and community, and **explain** three functions a school performs
for the community around it, with a Pakistani example of each. *(15 marks)*

### CRQ 2

The SMC of a government primary school in rural Sindh has not met for a year and enrolment of girls
after Class 3 is falling. **Describe** the composition and functions of an SMC under Sindh law and
**suggest** how an active SMC could respond to this problem. *(15 marks)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Society defined as a large group sharing a culture; community as a smaller group tied by place or belonging, with face-to-face relations | 3 |
| Clear point of difference (scale, closeness of relations, or shared locality) | 1.5 |
| Three distinct functions (e.g. socialization, transmission of culture, social control, social placement, cultural innovation, community resource), each explained, 2 marks each | 6 |
| A relevant Pakistani example for each function, 1.5 marks each | 4.5 |

A full-mark answer separates the two terms with a reason, not just a synonym, and ties each function
to a concrete local example (for example, the school building used for an evening adult literacy class for
the community-resource function). Functions that are only named earn 1 mark each.

### CRQ 2

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Composition: equal representation of government, teachers, parents and school management; headed by the head teacher | 3 |
| At least three legal functions (monitor working of school, observe norms and standards, implement policy, prepare School Improvement Plan, monitor grants, ensure attendance) | 4.5 |
| Meeting requirement (at least three times a year) and the SMC's power over attendance noted | 1.5 |
| Response to falling girls' enrolment: at least three realistic actions (identify families, visit homes, involve women members or mothers, address a school-side cause such as safety or facilities, write targets into the School Improvement Plan) | 4.5 |
| Answer linked to the community context rather than generic | 1.5 |

Full marks need correct composition and functions plus a response that uses the SMC's actual powers.
Answers that describe the SMC only as approving funds cannot score above 7.
