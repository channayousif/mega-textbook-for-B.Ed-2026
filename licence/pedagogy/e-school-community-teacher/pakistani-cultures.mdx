# E05. Culture and cultural elements of Pakistani communities

## What the test asks

A CRQ on this objective asks you to **describe** the cultural elements of Pakistani communities, to
**explain** the role of the school in protecting and transmitting culture, or to **evaluate** the
impact of media and technology on school and culture. A frequent format is a scenario: a class with
pupils from several language communities, a school that ignores local festivals, or parents worried
about what children watch on phones.

Marks are usually lost by:

- describing a whole province or community with a stereotype ("people of X are ...");
- listing festivals and dress with no link to schooling;
- treating media as wholly good or wholly bad;
- forgetting that the question is about a teacher's response, not only a description.

## Explanation

### Unity and diversity

Pakistani culture has two layers that a good answer holds together.

**Shared elements** bind communities across the country: Islam, which Article 2 of the Constitution makes the State religion, and the
values of courtesy, hospitality, respect for elders and care for family that are widely held; the
national language, Urdu, which the Constitution names as "the National language of Pakistan"
(Article 251); national symbols and days such as Independence Day on 14 August; and religious
festivals such as Eid ul Fitr and Eid ul Adha.

**Diverse elements** differ between and within communities: mother tongues, local dress and crafts,
music and poetry, food, the rhythm of rural and urban life, and the religious festivals of minority
communities, such as Christmas and Easter for Christians and Holi and Diwali for Hindus.

The **languages** show the diversity most clearly. The 2023 census tables of the Pakistan Bureau of
Statistics record mother tongues including Punjabi, Pashto, Sindhi, Saraiki, Urdu, Balochi, Hindko
and Brahui, alongside many smaller languages (PBS, 2024). In a city such as Karachi or Hyderabad, one classroom
may contain speakers of several of these languages.

### The constitutional basis

Three articles are worth knowing for the test.

- **Article 28** (Preservation of language, script and culture): "any section of citizens having a
  distinct language, script or culture shall have the right to preserve and promote the same", and
  to establish institutions for that purpose, subject to law.
- **Article 251**: Urdu is the national language, and "a Provincial Assembly may by law prescribe
  measures for the teaching, promotion and use of a Provincial language in addition to the National
  language". This is the basis on which Sindhi is taught in Sindh's schools.
- **Article 22** provides safeguards in educational institutions in respect of religion, so that
  pupils of one faith are not compelled into the religious instruction or worship of another.

These articles tell a teacher that cultural and linguistic diversity is protected, not merely
tolerated.

### Speaking about culture without stereotyping

The guide's own session asks student teachers to "name one cultural trait, belief, or practice of
their own community" and discuss how regional practices differ (HEC, 2012, Unit 3, session 13). The
point is to describe practices, not to label peoples. Three rules help:

1. **Describe a practice, not a people.** "Many families in this village hold a gathering on the
   birth of a child" is a description; "Sindhis are ..." is a stereotype.
2. **Expect variation inside every group**, by family, class, gender, religion and urban or rural
   setting. The guide's Unit 5 asks teachers to consider "differences within society and within
   families living in the same regions".
3. **Avoid ethnocentrism**, judging another culture by one's own norms; use cultural relativism,
   understanding a practice in its own terms (Conerly et al., 2021, Section 3.1). This does not mean
   accepting every practice; the teacher still upholds children's rights and school rules, but
   starts from understanding.

### Role of education and the school in protecting and transmitting culture

Schools are among the main **agents of socialization** and transmit culture through what they teach
and how they run (Conerly et al., 2021, Section 5.3). The guide's session 14 suggests two lenses.

**Festivals and holidays.** Student teachers list local and public holidays and festivals and ask
whether schools acknowledge, teach about and value them, and what the impact is when schools do or do
not. When a school recognises the annual urs of Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai (for which the Sindh government
declared a public holiday in 2026; Dawn, 2026) by reading his poetry in class, it transmits a regional heritage; when it
treats local occasions as mere absences, it signals that the community's culture does not belong
at school.

**Uniforms and daily practice.** The guide asks whether school uniforms represent local dress and
whether their material or design reflects social distinctions. A school's rituals, language rules
and dress codes all carry cultural messages.

So the school can:

- **protect** culture: teach mother tongue and provincial language, local literature and history,
  crafts and music;
- **transmit** shared national values and a common language, so that children from different
  communities can live and work together;
- **renew** culture: examine customs critically, keeping what serves children and questioning what
  harms them (for example, practices that keep girls out of school).

### Impact of media on school and culture

The guide's reading "Is Media Changing Society and Culture in Pakistan?" presents both sides (HEC,
2012, Faculty Resources, Unit 3).

- **Concerns**: violence, consumerism and exploitation in some content; fear that values will erode;
  people believing everything they read online; extremists exploiting misinformation; a divide
  between those with and without access; and more individualised lives.
- **Benefits**: information "at our fingertips", awareness of a larger world, flexible work, and the
  potential to "create a bond between the many ethnic groups within Pakistan" and to reach remote
  areas "with needed services, including education".
- **A caution**: the reading quotes Khan (2010) that there is no empirical evidence that media alone
  make people behave in a certain way. Media interact with family, school and community.

The guide also asks whether media present children from diverse backgrounds positively and whether
they use gender stereotypes, and has student teachers plan an inclusive media campaign for
children's right to education (Unit 3, session 15).

### Impact of technology on school and culture

Session 16 asks whether technology "produces homogeneous cultures" and explores **globalization**.
Technology brings new material culture into homes and schools faster than norms adjust (cultural lag,
see E04). It can spread a global youth culture that weakens local languages and crafts, but it can
also record and share them: a class can make a short audio collection of grandparents' folk tales in
their mother tongues.

**A Sindh illustration.** A Class 6 teacher in a Sanghar school finds that pupils know film songs
but not the poetry of their own region. She sets a project: each group interviews an elder about a
festival or craft, records it on a parent's phone, and presents it in Sindhi or Urdu with a summary
in the other language. Media and technology become tools for protecting culture, and the class
discusses which online content respects their communities and which mocks them.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

**Explain** how a school can both protect the diverse cultures of its pupils and transmit a shared
national culture, with two examples of each. *(15 marks)*

### CRQ 2

"Media are destroying the culture of Pakistani children." **Evaluate** this statement and **suggest**
three ways a teacher can use media to strengthen school and community ties. *(15 marks)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Protecting diverse cultures explained (mother tongue and provincial language, local literature, festivals, crafts; respect without stereotyping) | 3 |
| Two concrete examples of protection | 3 |
| Transmitting shared national culture explained (national language, shared values, civic identity, common festivals) | 3 |
| Two concrete examples of transmission | 3 |
| Shows the two aims can be balanced (e.g. reference to Article 28 or 251, or unity in diversity) and avoids stereotypes | 3 |

A full-mark answer gives specific classroom examples and shows why the school needs both aims. An
answer that describes a province with a stereotype loses the final criterion.

### CRQ 2

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Arguments supporting the concern (harmful content, erosion of values, misinformation, access divide) | 3 |
| Arguments against (information access, awareness, national cohesion, reaching remote areas) | 3 |
| Recognises that media interact with family and school (no single cause) and reaches a reasoned judgement | 3 |
| Three practical teacher strategies, 2 marks each (e.g. media literacy lessons; class social-media or WhatsApp group for parents; recording local stories; using educational broadcasts) | 6 |

One-sided answers cannot score above 9. Strategies must connect school and community, not only
classroom use of technology.
