# E03. Elements and types of social interaction

## What the test asks

A CRQ on this objective asks you to **define** social interaction and socialization, to **describe**
its elements, to **differentiate** the five types of interaction with examples, or to **explain** how a
teacher can use interaction to build cooperation and tolerance. Classroom scenarios are common: two
groups of pupils who will not work together, a new pupil from another province, a sports day that
turned into a quarrel.

Marks are usually lost by:

- treating competition and conflict as the same thing;
- defining accommodation and assimilation with the same words;
- giving examples that are not from a school or community;
- saying cooperation is always good, when the guide's reading points out that cooperation can serve
  a harmful goal.

## Explanation

### Socialization and social interaction

**Socialization** is "the process through which people are taught to be proficient members of a
society" (Conerly et al., 2021, Ch. 5). Through it children come to understand norms, accept beliefs
and become aware of values. The course guide's reading adds that we are socialized to a particular
language, beliefs, values, ways of acting and even food preferences, by parents, siblings, extended
family, the community, the media and the school (HEC, 2012, Faculty Resources, Unit 2).

The main **agents of socialization** are the family (the first agent), peer groups, and institutions
such as the school, the workplace, religion, government and the mass media (Conerly et al., 2021,
Section 5.3). Schools also teach a **hidden curriculum**: the informal lessons about rules, waiting
one's turn, competing and cooperating that are never written in the syllabus.

**Social interaction** is the way people act and react towards one another. In the guide's words,
"one person acts in response to another", and whether an interaction is planned (a teacher arranging
group work) or unplanned (two people meeting by chance), "the people who interact influence each
other in some way" (HEC, 2012, Faculty Resources, Unit 2). Socialization happens through social
interaction.

### Levels of social interaction

A practical way to organise the levels the guide names is by who is interacting:

1. **Individual with individual**: a teacher talking with one pupil, two friends sharing a lunch box.
2. **Individual with group**: a teacher and a class, a new pupil joining a cricket team, a head
   teacher addressing the SMC.
3. **Group with group**: two sections of a class in a quiz, the school and a parents' association,
   two neighbouring communities sharing one school.

The level matters because the teacher's tools differ: a quiet word works at the first level, a
classroom routine at the second, and a school-community agreement at the third.

### Elements of social interaction

The guide lists three elements (HEC, 2012, Unit 2, session 6).

- **Social contact.** Interaction begins when people come into contact, face to face or through a
  medium such as a phone or letter. Contact can be direct (a parent-teacher meeting) or indirect (a
  note in the pupil's diary).
- **Communication.** Contact becomes interaction when meaning passes between people, through words,
  gestures, symbols or writing. The guide asks student teachers to apply models of the communication
  process (Foulger, 2004) to an imaginary PTA or SMC meeting, to see who sends messages, who receives
  them and what gets lost.
- **Social attitudes and values.** How people interact depends on what they believe and value.
  Values are the "principles and standards members of a culture hold in high regard" (Conerly et al.,
  2021, Section 3.2). A pupil taught at home that elders are never contradicted will interact with a
  teacher differently from one encouraged to question.

### Types of social interaction

The guide's reading groups interactions into five types (HEC, 2012, Faculty Resources, Unit 2).

<Figure id="fig-E03-1" kind="table" src="/img/figures/licence/e-school-community-teacher/fig-E03-1.svg" alt="Table of the five types of social interaction, cooperation, competition, conflict, accommodation and assimilation, each with its meaning, a school example, and what the teacher can do." width={780} height={560} />

- **Cooperation**: individuals or groups work together to accomplish a goal. The reading stresses that
  cooperation can be positive or negative; a gang cooperating to rob someone is "cooperation towards
  a negative goal". Example: pupils planting a school garden together.
- **Competition**: people want something that is in limited supply, such as a prize, a position or
  a resource. It is directed at the goal, not at the rival, and can be healthy when rules are fair.
  Example: an inter-house science quiz.
- **Conflict**: people disagree and each tries to prevail over the other. The reading notes that
  conflict can become violent if it cannot be resolved, but also that it "can be stimulating when
  people can agree to disagree". Competition turns into conflict when the prize is of great worth or
  when only one side can win.
- **Accommodation**: people adjust their behaviour and language to get along or to end a conflict,
  without giving up their identity. Sometimes the individual adjusts to the group; sometimes the group
  makes room for the individual. Example: a group that switches between Urdu and Sindhi in discussion so that
  a new pupil can follow.
- **Assimilation**: the gradual process of taking on the language, values and goals of another group,
  until the newcomer can no longer be told apart. Example: a child whose family moved from Khyber
  Pakhtunkhwa to Karachi who, after some years, speaks, dresses and plays like her classmates.

The key contrast for exams: in **accommodation** both sides keep their identities and adjust; in
**assimilation** one side is absorbed. The guide asks student teachers to discuss what assimilation
means "for marginalized communities and the teaching and learning of excluded students" (Unit 2,
session 7). A school that demands assimilation (for example, forbidding a child's home language)
may push a child out rather than include her.

### Social groups

A **group** is two or more people who interact and share a sense of identity. It differs from an
**aggregate** (people in the same place who do not interact, like a bus queue) and a **category**
(people who share a trait, like all Class 5 pupils in Sindh) (Conerly et al., 2021, Section 6.1).

- **Primary groups** are small, face-to-face and long-lasting, with emotional bonds; the family is
  the best example (Cooley, in Conerly et al., 2021).
- **Secondary groups** are larger, more impersonal and task-focused; a classroom or a staff room can
  be one.
- An **in-group** is the group a person feels they belong to; an **out-group** is one they do not, and
  may feel competition with (Sumner, in Conerly et al., 2021).
- A **reference group** is one people compare themselves with.

The guide asks which groups matter most for improving teaching and learning, and points towards
primary groups (family, friends) and secondary groups (colleagues in the school) (Unit 2, session 8).
**Individual behaviour** often changes inside a group: a shy pupil may speak up in a trusted small
group, and a well-behaved one may join in mischief with an in-group.

### Interaction for peace, harmony and tolerance

The unit ends with the role of schools and teachers "in developing social interaction for peace,
harmony, and tolerance in Pakistani communities". The guide's peace education reading says that peace
education means learning **about** peace and learning **for** peace, including learning to deal with
conflict without violence and "to deal with cultural differences in a constructive way" (HEC, 2012,
Faculty Resources, Unit 2).

In a Pakistani classroom this means planning interaction deliberately:

- mixed groups (by language, gender where the school allows, and background) for cooperative tasks;
- fair rules for competition, with rotating winners and recognition of effort;
- a simple conflict routine: stop, let each side speak, restate, agree a fair step;
- turning moments of accommodation into lessons, for example learning a greeting in each home
  language in the class.

**A Sindh illustration.** In a Hyderabad school, Sindhi-speaking and Urdu-speaking pupils in Class 6
sit apart and tease each other. The teacher forms mixed pairs for a science investigation (cooperation
around a shared goal), runs a quiz between mixed teams rather than between the two groups (competition
without in-group rivalry), and asks each pair to teach the class three science words in both languages
(accommodation). She does not require the pupils to become alike; she requires them to work together.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

**Differentiate** between competition and conflict, and between accommodation and assimilation, giving
one school example of each type. *(15 marks)*

### CRQ 2

**Explain** the three elements of social interaction and **suggest** how a teacher can use them to
reduce tension between two groups of pupils from different linguistic backgrounds. *(15 marks)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Competition: striving for something in limited supply, directed at the goal, rule-bound | 2.5 |
| Conflict: disagreement in which each side tries to prevail over the other; may turn hostile | 2.5 |
| Clear point of contrast between competition and conflict (goal vs opponent; when competition becomes conflict) | 1.5 |
| Accommodation: adjusting behaviour to get along while keeping identity | 2.5 |
| Assimilation: gradually taking on another group's language, values and ways until indistinguishable | 2.5 |
| Clear point of contrast (both keep identity vs one absorbed) | 1.5 |
| Four relevant school examples, 0.5 each | 2 |

A full-mark answer states the contrast explicitly for each pair, not only two separate definitions.

### CRQ 2

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Social contact explained (direct or indirect coming together) | 2 |
| Communication explained (passing of meaning through words, gestures, symbols) | 2 |
| Social attitudes and values explained as shaping how people interact | 2 |
| At least three teacher strategies linked to the elements (e.g. structured contact in mixed groups; shared vocabulary or bilingual tasks; modelling respect and discussing values of fairness) | 6 |
| Strategies fitted to a linguistically mixed Pakistani class | 1.5 |
| Mentions cooperation or accommodation as the aim rather than forced assimilation | 1.5 |

Full marks need each strategy tied to a named element. Generic advice ("tell them to be friends")
earns no strategy marks.
