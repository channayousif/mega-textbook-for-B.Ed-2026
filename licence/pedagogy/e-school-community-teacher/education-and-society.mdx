# E02. Impact of education on society

## What the test asks

A CRQ here asks you to **explain** how education affects a society, to **evaluate** whether schooling
reduces or reproduces inequality, or to **analyse** why a particular community is not getting the
benefits of education and **propose** what schools and communities could do. The strongest answers
show that education's impact is not automatically positive: it depends on who gets it, what quality
it is, and how the school relates to the community.

Marks are usually lost by:

- writing a list of benefits ("education removes ignorance, brings development") with no mechanism;
- ignoring the negative or unequal effects that the conflict perspective describes;
- giving no Pakistani evidence, when the HEC guide supplies several studies;
- confusing "dropping out" with a purely individual choice.

## Explanation

### Three ways of seeing education's impact

Sociology offers three lenses. A good CRQ answer uses at least two.

**1. Functionalist: education keeps society working.** Functionalists see schools as meeting
society's needs. OpenStax lists the manifest (intended) functions as socialization, transmission of
culture, social control, social placement and cultural innovation (Conerly et al., 2021, Section
16.2). Education prepares people for work, passes on shared values, teaches respect for rules and
authority, sorts people into occupations, and brings new ideas into society. The course guide's
reading on Balochistan makes the same case in economic terms: "Education is an important factor in
economic growth", and areas "such as health, women's status, and child labour improve when people are
educated" (Jabeen and Malik, 2003, in HEC, 2012, Faculty Resources).

**2. Conflict: education can reproduce inequality.** Conflict theorists argue that school systems
often "reinforce and perpetuate social inequalities that arise from differences in class, gender,
race, and ethnicity" (Conerly et al., 2021, Section 16.2). Children from better-off homes arrive with
more **cultural capital**, the knowledge and manners that school rewards, and so do better. The guide
makes the same point with **social capital**: networks have value, and people who know the language
and manners of a dominant group have more of it (HEC, 2012, Unit 1, session 3). In Pakistan this
lens explains why a high-fee private school and a village government school can produce very
different life chances even when both follow a curriculum.

**3. Symbolic interactionist: labels shape learners.** This lens looks at daily classroom
interaction. A pupil labelled a "low achiever" may come to believe it and perform accordingly, a
**self-fulfilling prophecy** (Conerly et al., 2021, Section 16.2). The impact of education on society
is built up from millions of such small interactions.

### Positive impacts on society

Drawing the lenses together, education can:

- **raise productivity and incomes**, because educated workers can do more complex work;
- **improve health and family welfare**, since educated parents are better able to use health
  information (the Balochistan reading links education to health and women's status);
- **strengthen citizenship**, by teaching rights, duties and how to take part in public life;
- **transmit and renew culture**, passing on language and values while adding new knowledge;
- **allow social mobility**, so that a child's future is not fixed by the family's position;
- **build social cohesion**, by bringing children of different backgrounds into shared institutions.

### Why the impact is uneven in Pakistan

The guide asks student teachers to study why the promised benefits have not reached everyone. Its
readings identify:

- **Poverty and child labour.** Khan (2010) found that poverty, children needing to help earn for the
  family, fear of harsh punishment, and limited means to educate children beyond primary school made
  learning a trade seem preferable to many families.
- **Cultural attitudes.** Khan's handout notes that in some areas "culture and traditions do not
  support girls' education"; Jabeen and Malik note that some tribal settings oppose education,
  especially of women.
- **School-side problems.** Untrained or absent teachers, a curriculum not related to real life, too
  few books, poor buildings and a language of instruction children do not speak at home (Jabeen and
  Malik, 2003).
- **Low public spending**, which the Balochistan authors identify as a major reason for slow
  improvement.

The guide adds a sharp reframing. Some educators prefer to say that children are **pushed out** of
school rather than that they **drop out**, and the guide asks student teachers which term is more
accurate and why (HEC, 2012, Unit 1, session 3). The phrase moves responsibility from the child to
the conditions: a harsh classroom, fees, distance, or a school that does not welcome the child's
language.

### Role of education in strengthening Pakistani communities

The guide's session 4 asks how "education can improve the quality of relationships within and among
Pakistani communities". Its readings suggest several routes:

1. **Community ownership.** The UNESCO review found that the most successful quality projects were
   those "developed with the community and parents' support", which became programmes "owned and
   managed by the community".
2. **Flexible provision.** Teachers in Khan's study suggested evening shifts for children who work in
   the morning, and noted that "role of community" is important for raising enrolment.
3. **Peace and tolerance.** Unit 2 of the guide links schooling to "peace, harmony, and tolerance in
   Pakistani communities" (see E03), because a shared school is one of the few places where children
   of different backgrounds meet daily.
4. **Local problem-solving.** A school that teaches health, environment and civic skills gives the
   community knowledge it can act on.

**A Sindh illustration.** Picture a government girls' primary school in a Thar village. The first
girls to finish Class 5 become the reason other families enrol daughters; a literate mother reads
the medicine label for a neighbour; the older pupils read out a health message sent to a parent's phone.
None of these effects appears on a result card, but each is education changing the community. At the
same time, the conflict lens asks who is still missing: the girls whose families need them for
water collection, and the boys pulled into seasonal work.

### Writing a balanced answer

For an "evaluate" question, structure your answer as: the benefit claimed (functionalist), the
evidence that the benefit is unequally shared (conflict), a Pakistani example of each, and a
conclusion about the conditions under which education does strengthen society (access, quality,
community involvement).

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

"Education always reduces inequality in society." **Evaluate** this statement using the functionalist
and conflict perspectives, with evidence from the Pakistani context. *(15 marks)*

### CRQ 2

**Explain** the difference between saying children "drop out" and saying they are "pushed out" of
school, and **suggest** three actions a school and its community could take to reduce push-out in a
rural Sindh village. *(15 marks)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Functionalist view explained (socialization, social placement, mobility, cultural transmission) | 3 |
| Conflict view explained (reproduction of inequality, cultural or social capital, unequal school quality) | 3 |
| At least two pieces of Pakistani evidence (e.g. poverty and child labour, cultural attitudes to girls' education, public-private quality gap, low spending) | 4 |
| Balanced judgement: states the conditions under which education does or does not reduce inequality | 3 |
| Clear structure and use of the command word "evaluate" (a reasoned conclusion) | 2 |

A full-mark answer rejects the word "always" with reasons, uses both perspectives accurately, and
ends with a conditional conclusion. One-sided answers cannot score above 9.

### CRQ 2

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| "Drop out" places the cause in the child or family; "pushed out" places it in school and social conditions | 3 |
| At least two examples of push-out factors (harsh punishment, fees or hidden costs, distance, language of instruction, unwelcoming school culture) | 3 |
| Three realistic actions, each explained, 2.5 marks each (e.g. end corporal punishment and track absence early; flexible timings or catch-up classes in harvest season; home visits by teachers and SMC members; mother-tongue support in early grades) | 7.5 |
| Actions fitted to a rural Sindh setting | 1.5 |

Full marks need a clear contrast of the two terms and actions that address school-side causes, not
only parents' attitudes.
