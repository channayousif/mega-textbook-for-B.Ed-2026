# E06. Relationship between school and community

## What the test asks

A CRQ on this objective asks you to **explain** how a school and its community affect each other, to
**analyse** why a relationship has broken down, or to **design** a strategy for involving parents and
the community. The 2024 sample paper included a 15-mark CRQ on a **parental involvement strategy**, so
expect to write a practical plan with named activities, the people responsible and a way to judge
whether it worked.

Marks are usually lost by:

- listing "PTA meetings" as the whole strategy;
- ignoring the barriers that keep Pakistani parents away (literacy, work hours, the belief that school
  is for experts);
- describing only what parents should do for the school, not what the school offers parents;
- giving no way to check whether the strategy succeeded.

## Where your degree teaches this

- [EFMP-302 Topic 3.3 - Relationships, and what to do when they go wrong](/semester-1/efmp-302/unit-03/topic-03)
  explains what the teacher's relationships with parents and the wider community are for, why the
  teacher must do the work of making a parent relationship usable, and a four-step sequence for
  handling conflict with an angry parent. Revise it for the conflict part of any question.

That topic treats the relationship from the individual teacher's side. It does not cover the school as
an institution in its community, the two-way effects, the school as a hub, or a structured parental
involvement strategy. Those are taught below.

## Explanation

### The school as a social, cultural and community institution

The guide's Unit 4 begins by insisting that a school is "more than just a place for learning the three
Rs - reading, writing, and arithmetic" (HEC, 2012, Unit 4). It is:

- a **social institution**, with roles (head, teacher, pupil, parent), rules and relationships that
  reproduce themselves year after year;
- a **cultural institution**, which carries and passes on values, language and customs, and develops
  its own school culture (see E04);
- a **community institution**, which belongs to a place, draws on local people and resources, and is
  judged by local expectations.

The guide asks student teachers to compare the cultures of public schools, private schools and
madrassas, looking at the attitudes of people inside and outside, the school's norms, and relationships
within it (session 17). Each type stands in a different relationship to its community (see E01).

### Effects in both directions

**Effects of the school on the community.** Literacy and skills; health, civic and environmental
awareness carried home by children; new ideas and aspirations; shared values across groups; a building
and staff that can serve community needs.

**Effects of the community on the school.** Whether children enrol and attend; what families expect of
teachers; the languages and values children bring; local resources, volunteers and donations; and local
pressures, such as seasonal work or attitudes to girls' schooling.

Henderson and Mapp's review of research, which the guide assigns, concluded that when schools, families
and community groups work together, children "tend to do better in school, stay in school longer, and
like school more", and are more likely to earn higher grades, pass their classes and attend regularly
(Henderson and Mapp, 2002). The guide asks student teachers to judge how far these findings apply in
Pakistan (session 18); much of that research is from the United States, so apply it with care.

### The school as a hub for community services

Session 19 introduces the idea of a public school as a **hub**: a place where the community receives
services beyond children's lessons. The guide notes that "some student teachers may not be very
familiar with these ideas in Pakistan" and asks them to discuss the possibilities. Realistic examples
include adult literacy classes for mothers in the afternoon, health and vaccination awareness sessions
with the local health worker, a small lending library open to families, and the school ground used for
community events. A hub strengthens the relationship because families come to see the school as theirs.

### Why parents stay away

The guide's closing note on NPSTP Standard 8 is blunt: teachers "must take the initiative because
parents and other community members may be anxious about getting involved in school", believing
"incorrectly, that they should leave things to the experts when they too might be experts" (HEC, 2012,
Unit 6, session 27). Other common barriers in Pakistani settings are parents' own limited schooling,
work hours that clash with school hours, travel costs, and a history of being called to school only when
a child is in trouble. A strategy that ignores these barriers will fail.

The standard itself includes "recognizing the role of parents, guardians and other family members as a
child's teacher". The guide lists evidence of a teacher meeting it: referring to learning children have
done at home, meeting parents regularly about progress, and involving family members in learning, "for
example, by asking children to read aloud for 10 minutes every day to someone in their family".

### Designing a parental involvement strategy

Epstein (1995) groups school, family and community involvement into **six types**. They make a
reliable skeleton for a CRQ answer.

| Type | What the school does | A Pakistani example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Parenting | Helps families create home conditions that support learning | Short sessions on routines, sleep and study space, in the local language |
| 2. Communicating | Sets up two-way communication about the programme and progress | Simple progress notes; voice messages for parents who do not read; a fixed weekly time to meet the class teacher |
| 3. Volunteering | Recruits and organises parents' help | Parents help at sports day, share a craft or trade in a lesson |
| 4. Learning at home | Gives families ideas to help with homework and learning | The ten-minute read-aloud to a family member; a question for the child to ask an elder |
| 5. Decision making | Includes parents in school decisions | Active SMC with parent members shaping the School Improvement Plan |
| 6. Collaborating with the community | Links the school with community resources and services | Health worker sessions, the masjid committee or a local NGO supporting attendance drives |

In Sindh, type 5 has a legal backbone: the SMC must include parents in equal representation with
government, teachers and school management, meet at least three times a year, and prepare the School
Improvement Plan (Sindh RTE Act, 2013).

A full strategy answer states:

1. **the aim** (for example, raise Class 3 reading levels or reduce absence);
2. **activities**, chosen from several of the six types and fitted to local barriers;
3. **who is responsible** (class teacher, head, SMC parent members);
4. **timing** (a term calendar, meetings at times working parents can attend);
5. **evidence of success** (attendance registers, number of parents attending, reading checks).

**A Sindh illustration.** A primary school near Badin finds that few mothers ever visit. The staff
move the parents' meeting to late afternoon, invite mothers through the children with a picture
invitation, open with a short health talk from the lady health worker (type 6), show each mother her
child's reading book and teach the ten-minute read-aloud (types 2 and 4), and invite two mothers onto the
SMC (type 5). Attendance at the next meeting is counted and compared.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

**Design** a parental involvement strategy for a government primary school in rural Sindh where most
parents have limited schooling and rarely visit the school. Use at least four of Epstein's six types
and state how you will judge success. *(15 marks)*

### CRQ 2

**Explain** two effects of a school on its community and two effects of a community on its school, and
**suggest** how the school could become a hub for community services. *(15 marks)*

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Clear aim linked to pupils' learning or attendance | 1.5 |
| At least four of Epstein's types used correctly, each with a concrete activity, 2 marks each | 8 |
| Activities fitted to the stated barriers (low literacy, work hours, reluctance), e.g. oral or visual communication, flexible timings | 2 |
| Roles and timing stated (teacher, head, SMC; term calendar) | 1.5 |
| Evidence of success named (attendance, parent turnout, learning checks) | 2 |

A full-mark answer reads like a plan a head teacher could adopt. Lists of types without activities earn
at most 4 of the 8 marks for that criterion.

### CRQ 2

| Criterion | Marks |
|---|---|
| Two effects of the school on the community, each explained, 2 marks each | 4 |
| Two effects of the community on the school, each explained, 2 marks each | 4 |
| Meaning of the school as a community hub explained | 2 |
| At least two realistic hub services for a Pakistani setting, with how they strengthen the relationship | 4 |
| Pakistani context used throughout | 1 |

Full marks need effects in both directions, not only benefits of schooling.
