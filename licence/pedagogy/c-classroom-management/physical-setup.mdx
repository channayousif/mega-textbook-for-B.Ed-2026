# C08. Physical setup of the classroom

## What the test asks

A CRQ here typically asks you to **compare** seating arrangements, **recommend** a layout for a
described activity or room, or **justify** changes to a physical setup. Markers want arrangements
matched to learning purposes, costs acknowledged, and solutions that work in real Pakistani rooms
with fixed benches and large numbers. Common ways to lose marks:

- declaring one arrangement "best" for all purposes;
- ignoring the teacher's movement and sightlines;
- proposing layouts that need furniture or space the described school does not have.

## Where your degree teaches this

- [EFMP-301 Topic 8.2 - Creating positive learning environments](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-08/topic-02) teaches physical space as one part of a positive environment: seating for sight, pupil work on the walls, reachable materials.

That topic gives the principle in a paragraph. This page adds the detail the objective asks for: the
guide's range of seating plans and what each serves, varying the layout by activity, proximity and
circulation, learning areas and displays, and adapting a room you cannot rearrange.

## Explanation

### The layout serves the learning

The guide's Week 4 lecture begins with one principle: "Some seating plans are more suited for
certain classroom activities than others." Teachers can vary the plan with the activity, for
example asking four or five pupils to cluster their desks for group work, and where space allows
they can keep permanent areas, such as a rug in one corner for story time. The goal of any
arrangement is the same as the goal of classroom management: maximum learning.

### Seating arrangements, what they serve and what they cost

The guide asks trainees to sketch seven arrangements: a U-shape; pairs of desks in rows; single
desks in rows; a closed circle on the floor; clusters of four or five chairs around desks; children
on a floor rug close to the teacher; and all desks joined into one long table. It notes that "a
circle or U-shaped seating plan is ideal for whole-class discussions." The three most common
choices are compared below.

<Figure id="fig-C08-1" kind="diagram" src="/img/figures/licence/c-classroom-management/fig-C08-1.svg" alt="Three seating arrangements side by side (rows, clusters, horseshoe), each labelled with the learning experience it serves." width={780} height={470} />

- **Rows** (single or paired) serve whole-class instruction, demonstrations, tests and drills:
  everyone faces the board and the teacher can scan the room. The cost is that group work is
  awkward and pupils at the back tend to get less attention.
- **Clusters** of four to six serve group work, discussion and collaboration: pupils can talk and
  share materials. The cost is that some pupils face away from the board, noise rises, and without
  taught norms a cluster becomes a chat.
- **U-shape or horseshoe** serves discussion and debate: everyone can see everyone, and the teacher
  is part of the group. The cost is space, and it is awkward for sustained individual writing.
- **Floor arrangements** (a rug, a circle) suit younger pupils for stories, demonstrations and
  close attention, and cost nothing but floor space.

No arrangement is universally right. A skilled teacher changes the layout for the activity, and
teaches pupils a quick routine for doing so, so that a change of layout takes a minute rather than
ten.

### Movement, proximity and sightlines

The guide sets two conditions for any layout. First, the teacher must be able "to move freely
between children and to be able to reach each child." It calls proximity "an important element of
classroom management": circulating lets the teacher observe and assess each child's work and give
individual help, and it prevents many problems before they start. Second, the teacher must see
everyone and everyone must see the teacher; pupils with hearing or visual impairments may need to
sit closer to the board.

Where a pupil sits also affects learning socially. The guide advises teachers to consider whether
certain pupils seated together are preventing interaction with others, and to "group and regroup
students to maximize their interaction with a variety of peers."

### The room as a learning area

The guide describes "the physical space of the classroom" as "itself a learning area." Teachers
can fill it with visuals and displays, create an environment rich with images and print, and add
learning centres stocked with stationery, crayons, paper, scissors, blocks or other tools, varying
them to keep pupils engaged. In Unit 3 it lists "working in learning centres" among the routines
pupils must learn, since a centre without a routine for using it becomes a crowd.

The guide also asks trainees to research the **Reggio Emilia** approach to organising space, which
holds that children should have some control over the direction of their learning, learn through
touching, moving, listening, seeing and hearing, explore relationships with other children and with
materials, and have many ways to express themselves. The practical lesson is that the physical setup
should invite activity and expression, not only listening.

### Low-cost setup in a fixed-bench room

The guide asks trainees to brainstorm "low-cost/no-cost classroom materials" and practical ways to
arrange space. In many Sindh government schools the benches are fixed, rooms are crowded and there
is no budget. The design principle becomes: **match the arrangement to the learning, and when you
cannot move the furniture, change how pupils use it.**

- For discussion, pupils on alternate benches turn round to face the bench behind, making groups of
  four within rows.
- Keep one aisle clear so the teacher can reach the back; teach part of each lesson from the middle
  or back of the room.
- Use the floor space at the front for small-group work with the teacher while others work at
  their benches.
- Rotate seating every few weeks so the same pupils are not always at the back.
- Use walls for a learning-target corner, pupil work and reference charts made from chart paper or
  newspaper.
- Store shared materials (bottle-top counters, charts, a class library in a carton) where monitors
  can distribute them quickly.

Mr Hassan in Sanghar has taught Class 6 in fixed rows for two years. He cannot move the benches, but
on discussion days he asks pupils to turn and face the bench behind, keeps a clear aisle to the back,
and moves there when asking questions. The room is the same; how it is used has changed.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Compare** three seating arrangements described in the course guide, stating for each the
learning activity it serves best and one cost. Then **recommend** an arrangement for a Class 5 lesson in
which pupils debate whether their village should plant more trees, and **justify** your choice.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) A Class 7 room in a Sukkur government school has fixed benches in rows, 55 pupils, one
blackboard and bare walls. **Suggest** five practical changes to its physical setup that cost nothing,
and **explain** how each supports learning or prevents a management problem.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Points-based rubric, 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns the marks |
|---|---|---|
| Three arrangements compared | 9 | Each correctly described with a best-fit activity (1.5) and a genuine cost (1.5); e.g. rows, clusters, U-shape, floor circle |
| Recommendation | 3 | U-shape, circle or similar arrangement where all pupils can see and hear each other |
| Justification | 3 | Links to debate: eye contact, turn-taking, teacher as part of the group; notes a routine for setting it up |

A full-mark answer shows that each arrangement has a purpose and a cost, and chooses for the debate
on explicit learning grounds.

### CRQ 2

Points-based rubric, 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns the marks |
|---|---|---|
| Five changes | 10 | Each realistic without money (2 marks each: change 1, explanation of benefit 1); e.g. turn-and-face groups, clear aisle, teaching from the back, seat rotation, wall displays, materials store with monitors, front floor area |
| Management link | 3 | At least two changes explicitly prevent a problem (e.g. proximity reduces off-task talk; monitors speed transitions) |
| Fit to the described room | 2 | Respects fixed benches, 55 pupils and no budget |

A full-mark answer gives five distinct, workable changes, each tied to learning or prevention, and
none requiring new furniture.
