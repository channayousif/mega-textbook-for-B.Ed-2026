# C05. Curriculum and classroom management

## What the test asks

A CRQ on this objective asks you to **explain** the link between what and how a teacher teaches
and how well the class behaves, or to **plan** a lesson so that it prevents management problems.
The key idea markers look for is the guide's claim that disengagement and disruption often start
as curriculum weaknesses. Common ways to lose marks:

- treating curriculum as fixed by the textbook, with no teacher decisions in it;
- describing a lesson plan without saying how each part prevents a management problem;
- writing about motivation only as rewards, with no link to relevance and task level.

## Where your degree teaches this

- [EFMP-301 Topic 10.2 - Instructional design and teacher effectiveness](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-10/topic-02) teaches backward planning: an observable outcome first, the evidence second, the activity third, with a forward check.

That topic teaches how to plan a lesson well. This page adds what it leaves out: the guide's
argument that curriculum decisions **are** management decisions, the four decisions that carry
most management weight, the need for a plan consistent with the teacher's philosophy, and the
management decision inside each stage of the plan-motivate-teach-assess cycle.

## Explanation

### Many management problems are curriculum problems

The guide's Unit 2 introduction is blunt: teachers tend to think of curriculum as separate from
classroom management, yet "many of the managerial issues that surface in classrooms, such as lack
of attentiveness and engagement, incompletion of assignments, and disruption, occur because of
curricular weaknesses." Content that is too easy or too difficult, irrelevant to pupils' lives, or
taught in a way that fails to motivate leaves pupils disengaged, "and disengaged students are more
apt to act in ways that pose managerial problems."

The guide also rejects the idea that curriculum belongs entirely to outside authorities. Policy and
textbooks are set elsewhere, but teachers "exercise tremendous actual control over what is taught
and how it is taught in the classroom." A Class 4 English teacher in Lahore and a colleague next
door can teach the same textbook lesson, one spending the period managing disruption and one not,
because one made curriculum decisions before the lesson that removed the frictions the other met
unprepared.

### Four curriculum decisions that act as management

A useful way to see this is to break curriculum planning into four decisions, each of which changes
how much of the lesson is spent learning.

- **What to teach (selection).** Trying to cover every page of a chapter produces rushed
  explanations, confusion and the disruption that follows confusion. Deciding the essential idea
  and letting the rest wait makes depth possible, and depth is easier to manage.
- **In what order (sequencing).** Hard ideas placed before their foundations lose the class;
  practice placed before explanation becomes guessing. A well-sequenced lesson holds attention
  because each step follows from the last.
- **At what pace (pacing).** Too fast leaves strugglers behind; too slow bores the quick. Reading
  the room and adjusting speed is management in real time.
- **For whom and in what grouping.** Whole-class work is easiest to direct but hardest to
  differentiate; group work serves differentiation but needs routines (see C06 and C10).

The guide's Week 3 list of curricular concerns makes the same point: a prescribed textbook "cannot
mean rote memorization of the content." Children need to understand and internalise it and connect
it with their world, which requires lectures, activities and discussions designed to reach all
learners.

### A plan consistent with your philosophy

The guide asks: *in what ways can teachers create a plan for teaching and learning that is
consistent with their philosophy?* A teacher who believes learning is transfer will plan
explanation, then practice, then checking. A teacher who believes learning is construction will
plan a problem, then group investigation, then drawing out the insight. Both are defensible. What
damages management is inconsistency: a teacher who says pupils must build understanding but then
teaches only by dictation sends mixed signals, and pupils cannot learn what the room expects. Wong
and Wong (1998) put the practical test simply: pupils should be able to rely on a consistent plan.

### The plan-motivate-teach-assess cycle

The guide lists "planning, motivating, teaching, and assessing the curriculum" as one theme. Each
stage contains a management decision.

<Figure id="fig-C05-1" kind="flowchart" src="/img/figures/licence/c-classroom-management/fig-C05-1.svg" alt="Flowchart of the four-stage cycle: plan the lesson, motivate the learners, teach the content, assess understanding, with the management decision named at each stage and a return arrow from assess back to plan." width={780} height={470} />

1. **Plan: clarity of target.** A teacher who can state in one sentence what pupils will be able to
   do by the end has a test for every later decision and can cut whatever does not serve it. The
   plan also fixes the routines the lesson relies on: how materials are handed out, what early
   finishers do, how transitions run.
2. **Motivate: relevance.** The guide's essential question is *how can I plan lessons in a way
   that motivates and engages learners?* A two-minute hook question, a real problem from pupils'
   lives, a short competition, or a choice of task each costs different amounts of time and
   carries different risks (a competition, for instance, creates losers). The management question
   is which strategy gives the most engagement for the least cost with this class.
3. **Teach: pacing and participation.** Short explanation followed by pupil activity, repeated in
   cycles, gives pupils a rhythm to move with. Transitions between activities are where time leaks,
   so each needs a clear signal, a time limit and a check that everyone has started.
4. **Assess: evidence.** A show of hands, an exit slip, a pair-share or a question to a pupil who
   looked unsure tells the teacher whether to move on or reteach. Without this, the next lesson is
   planned on hope. The return arrow from assess to plan is the point of the cycle.

### Multigrade: reorganising the curriculum

The guide raises one curriculum issue that is easy to overlook: textbooks and curricula are written
for single grades, but small schools in remote areas teach several grades in one room. Such
teachers "require the knowledge and skills to reorganize the prescribed curriculum." Its first
principle for multigrade teaching is to "use the curriculum to plan", learning to set objectives for
several class levels at once and to adapt the timetable to match, rather than writing separate plans
for every grade. C06 and C10 take this further.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) The course guide claims that many management problems "occur because of curricular
weaknesses." **Explain** this claim with three examples, and **describe** how a teacher could prevent
each problem through a curriculum decision.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) **Plan** a 35-minute Class 5 science lesson on the water cycle using the
plan-motivate-teach-assess cycle. For each stage, **state** the management decision you make and
**justify** how it prevents disruption.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Points-based rubric, 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns the marks |
|---|---|---|
| Claim explained | 3 | Disengagement from content that is too hard, too easy, irrelevant or poorly taught leads to management problems |
| Three examples | 6 | Each example links a curriculum weakness to a specific management symptom (2 marks each) |
| Three preventive decisions | 6 | Each names a curriculum decision (selection, sequencing, pacing, grouping, relevance) that addresses the example (2 marks each) |

A full-mark answer shows cause and effect clearly for each example and proposes a realistic
teacher decision, not a behaviour rule.

### CRQ 2

Points-based rubric, 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns the marks |
|---|---|---|
| Plan stage | 4 | One-sentence learning target; named routines for materials and transitions |
| Motivate stage | 3 | A relevant hook (e.g. puddles drying after rain in the school yard) with its time cost |
| Teach stage | 4 | Explanation-activity cycles; clear transition signals and time limits |
| Assess stage | 3 | A quick check of understanding and what the teacher will do with the result |
| Overall justification | 1 | The stages link, and the plan returns assessment information to the next plan |

A full-mark answer is a workable lesson, not a list of labels, with a management reason stated at
every stage.
