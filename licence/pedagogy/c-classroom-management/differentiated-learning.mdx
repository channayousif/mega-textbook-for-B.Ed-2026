# C10. Differentiated learning

## What the test asks

The licence sample paper includes a CRQ tagged **instruction differentiation**, so expect this
objective. Questions ask you to **define** differentiation, **distinguish** it from giving weaker pupils
easier work, **plan** one objective for pupils at different levels, or **explain** how to differentiate
in a multigrade or overcrowded room. Common ways to lose marks:

- describing differentiation as separate, easier work for "weak" pupils;
- changing the learning target instead of the route to it;
- claiming differentiation is impossible in large classes instead of showing a workable first step.

Those topics explain learner differences, scaffolding and the multigrade reality. This page adds what
they leave out: a working definition of differentiation as a management strategy, the ways to
differentiate while keeping the target, and the tactics for differentiating in multigrade and
overcrowded classrooms.

## Explanation

### Why differentiation is a management issue

One of the guide's course outcomes is to "differentiate instruction according to student needs,
interests, and levels." Its Week 1 list of what a well-managed classroom looks like ends with
"teaching reaches every child in the room": teachers "who plan only for the 'average' student will
end up ignoring many children," and the best-managed classrooms are those where all children are
engaged in learning appropriate for their level. The link to behaviour is direct. Pupils who find the
work far too hard or far too easy disengage, and, as the guide's Unit 2 introduction says,
disengaged pupils are more likely to cause management problems. A task a pupil can actually do is
one of the best defences against disruption.

### What differentiation is, and is not

One common way to describe differentiation is as adjusting **what** pupils work with, **how** they work, or **how they show**
their learning, so that every pupil works towards the same learning target at a level of challenge
they can meet. It does **not** mean giving some children easier work on a different target. Easier
work lowers expectations, and lower expectations produce less learning. The guide lists "the same
content must be taught to all the students in one class" as a misconception; the correction is not
"different content for different pupils" but "the same target, reached by different routes."

Mr Naveed's Class 4 mathematics group in Sukkur has fifteen pupils: five already add two-digit numbers
with carrying confidently, six are still building the skill, and four are not yet secure with
single-digit addition. One worksheet for all three groups
guarantees boredom at the top and confusion at the bottom.

<Figure id="fig-C10-1" kind="diagram" src="/img/figures/licence/c-classroom-management/fig-C10-1.svg" alt="Diagram showing one curriculum objective differentiated three ways for a mixed-ability class: a core task for all, a scaffolded version for pupils who need support, and an extended version for pupils who are ready." width={780} height={470} />

The figure shows one objective (add two-digit numbers with carrying) planned three ways. Every pupil
works on the same core skill. Pupils who need support get a number line and a worked example, a
scaffold in the sense taught in EFMP-301 Topic 3.4, removed as they gain confidence. Pupils who are
ready apply the skill to a word problem with extra information. The target is the same; the support
and complexity differ.

### Three ways to differentiate

Following the guide's wording, a teacher can differentiate by:

- **need**: adjusting the scaffolding a pupil requires (worked examples, sentence starters,
  manipulatives, a peer partner, extra time);
- **interest**: offering a choice of context or material (a story problem about a cricket match or
  a mango stall) so that the pupil engages;
- **level**: adjusting the complexity of the task while keeping the target (fewer steps, more
  steps, an extension question).

Grouping supports all three: flexible groups that change by task, pairs of a more and a less
confident pupil, or a small group working with the teacher while others work independently. The
guide recommends UNESCO's resource on curriculum differentiation for further reading on these
adjustments in diverse classes.

### Differentiating in a multigrade classroom

The guide insists that "multigrade teaching is not deficit teaching" and should be treated as a
natural learning environment, since children live and play with children of different ages. It names
five pedagogical principles: use the curriculum to plan (objectives across class levels, an adapted
timetable); pupils tend to develop independent work habits and self-study skills; co-operation between
age groups builds collective ethics and responsibility; pupils develop positive attitudes to helping
each other; and "remediation and enrichment activities can be more discreetly arranged than in classes
with a single class level." A multigrade room, in other words, is already a differentiated room, and
the guide's second sample assignment is a 60-minute lesson for Classes 2 to 4 using activity corners
and multiple resources to differentiate.

Three tactics carry the load:

- **Anchor activities**: self-checking tasks one group does independently while the teacher works
  with another (see C06 for the routine).
- **Structured peer tutoring**: an older or more confident pupil explains to a younger one, with a
  clear role for each and a teacher check, so that it does not become one pupil doing the work.
- **Activity corners**: areas of the room with prepared tasks at different levels, visited in
  rotation.

### Differentiating in an overcrowded classroom

The guide lists "managing a large number of learners within a small space" as a core challenge,
requiring a room set up "so that co-operative learning can occur, students can be grouped and
re-grouped, and instruction can be responsive to individual needs." With 60 or more pupils, individual
plans are impossible, so differentiation works through structure:

- **a whole-class anchor** (one shared question, problem or text) with **tiered follow-up**: a core
  question everyone answers, a supported version with a hint on the board, and an extension question
  for those who finish;
- **pair and group work** so that pupils support each other, with peer helpers identified in each
  row;
- **structured routines** for handing in work and asking for help, so the teacher's time goes to the
  pupils who need it most;
- **quick checks** (slates held up, fingers for confidence) that show the teacher which pupils need
  the supported version today.

The guide also recommends UNESCO's practical tips for teaching large classes. The survival mindset,
the common belief that a class of sixty can only be survived, not managed, is itself the main barrier, because it stops a
teacher investing in the structures that make differentiation possible.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Define** differentiated learning and **explain** why giving weaker pupils easier work on a
different topic is not differentiation. **Plan** one Class 3 English objective differentiated by need,
interest and level.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) A teacher of a 65-pupil Class 5 in a Hyderabad government school says: "Differentiation is
impossible with this many children." **Evaluate** the claim and **propose** three realistic strategies,
**justifying** each by the constraint of the overcrowded room.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Points-based rubric, 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns the marks |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | 3 | Adjusting content, process or product so that every pupil works towards the same target at an appropriate level of challenge |
| Why easier work is not differentiation | 3 | It changes and lowers the target; lower expectations reduce learning; the guide's misconception about same content is corrected by same target, different routes |
| By need | 3 | A scaffold for the same objective (e.g. picture cues, sentence frames) |
| By interest | 3 | A choice of context or text that keeps the objective |
| By level | 3 | Simpler and extended versions of the same task |

A full-mark answer names one clear objective and shows three versions that all serve it.

### CRQ 2

Points-based rubric, 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns the marks |
|---|---|---|
| Evaluates the claim | 3 | Accepts that individual plans are impossible at 65 but rejects the conclusion; differentiation through structure is possible and necessary |
| Strategy 1 | 3 | e.g. whole-class anchor with tiered questions, justified by scale |
| Strategy 2 | 3 | e.g. structured pair or peer-helper system, justified by the teacher's limited reach |
| Strategy 3 | 3 | e.g. quick checks and help routines so the teacher targets support, justified by time |
| Realism | 3 | Needs no extra resources; fits fixed benches and a 35-40 minute period |

A full-mark answer takes the teacher's difficulty seriously and offers three strategies that could
start in the next lesson.
