# C09. Strategies for classroom management

## What the test asks

This is the broadest objective in heading C. CRQs ask you to **distinguish** management from
discipline, **suggest** strategies for a described problem, **evaluate** a teacher's response to
misbehaviour, or **outline** a classroom management plan. The sampled CRQ on **classroom challenges**
and many ERQ case studies draw on it. Common ways to lose marks:

- offering only punishments, or only "be kind", instead of a range from prevention to response;
- treating discipline and punishment as the same thing, which the guide explicitly rejects;
- responding to a case with generic advice that ignores the pupils and setting described.

## Where your degree teaches this

- [EFMP-301 Topic 8.1 - What classroom management is, and its principles](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-08/topic-01) teaches the four principles behind every strategy: prevention, routine, engagement and consistency.
- [EFMP-301 Topic 8.3 - Managing discipline and behavior](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-08/topic-03) teaches few rules about responsibility, natural and logical consequences, and the escalation ladder from proximity to the private word, the choice, the consequence and the parent meeting.

Use those topics for the principles, rules, consequences and the escalation ladder. This page adds
what they leave out: the proactive and reactive distinction as the guide draws it, rewards and
positive discipline, a restorative response to a behaviour breakdown, a decision aid for unexpected
events, the guide's misconceptions, and how to assemble a classroom management plan.

## Explanation

### Proactive management and reactive discipline

**Classroom management** is proactive: the work done before and during learning to create the
conditions for it (routines, seating, pacing, clear tasks). **Discipline** is the response after a
norm has been broken. The guide's summary of essential knowledge adds a precise distinction:
"Punishment and discipline are not the same thing. Discipline involves teaching right from wrong,
and it includes methods to prevent or respond to behaviour problems so they do not occur in the
future." Punishment only makes a pupil pay; discipline teaches.

Two Class 5 teachers in Multan show the difference. Mrs Ahsan spends the first week teaching
routines (entering, handing in homework, asking for help) until they are automatic; by October the
class runs itself while she works with a small group. Mrs Bashir spends the first week announcing
rules and consequences and spends October responding to breaches. Both care; only one is managing.

The guide's second Doyle quotation gives the practical rule: teachers must balance activity
management with rule enforcement, because time spent publicly on violations distracts from the main
activity, and if violations are frequent "misbehaviour, rather than academic work can become the
operating curriculum." So experienced teachers "push ahead with activities and try to make
reprimands brief and private."

### Prevention strategies

The guide asks trainees how a proactive teacher would prevent pupils interrupting, pushing in a
queue, missing deadlines, missing class and using rude language, and reminds them that the physical
space can help. A preventive toolkit:

- **taught routines** for recurring moments (C06);
- **a layout** with clear sightlines and a path to every pupil, and **proximity**: moving towards a
  pupil before a problem grows (C08);
- **tasks within reach** and clear instructions, since confusion breeds disruption (C05, C10);
- **withitness**: a term Marzano, Marzano and Pickering (2003) use for the teacher's awareness of
  the whole room and readiness to act early;
- **a short set of clear expectations**, stated positively and taught, so pupils know what is
  expected (C04);
- **relationships** built before they are needed (C07).

### Rewards and positive discipline

The guide distinguishes **extrinsic rewards** (a star, praise, tokens, stickers, sweets) from
**intrinsic rewards** (pride, interest, self-esteem): intrinsic rewards are "more difficult, but
... also more meaningful and longer lasting." It suggests non-material collective rewards such as
extra break time or a class role, and asks trainees to research "positive discipline in the
classroom" and to role-play punishment memories from their own schooling, asking whether each was
positive or negative discipline and how it could be improved. The strategic point: use rewards to
build habits, but aim to move pupils towards the intrinsic satisfaction of work done well.

Among the guide's resources is Lee Canter's assertive discipline, which pairs clear rules with
consistent positive recognition and planned consequences. Whatever system a teacher uses,
consistency matters more than severity.

### Responding to a behaviour breakdown

The guide's Unit 4 asks how a caring classroom builds "responsible actions and personal
accountability," and what happens when behaviour breaks down. Personal accountability is the
internal version of discipline: the pupil owns what they did and makes it right. A caring,
accountable response has six steps.

<Figure id="fig-C09-1" kind="flowchart" src="/img/figures/licence/c-classroom-management/fig-C09-1.svg" alt="Flowchart showing a behaviour breakdown handled through a caring, accountable response: stop the behaviour calmly, check for harm, hear both sides, agree a repair, follow up, and restore the relationship." width={780} height={470} />

1. **Stop the behaviour calmly**, modelling the self-control you are teaching.
2. **Check for harm**: the first question is whether anyone is hurt, not who started it.
3. **Hear both sides**, each pupil without interruption.
4. **Agree a repair**: the pupil who caused harm helps decide how to put it right.
5. **Follow up** later to check that the repair happened.
6. **Restore the relationship**, so no conflict is left to fester.

During group work in a Hyderabad Class 5, Bilal hits Hamid. Mr Tariq does not shout. He stops the
behaviour, checks Hamid is all right, hears both boys, and asks Bilal, "What will you do to make
this right?" Bilal apologises and helps Hamid finish the task, and the lesson continues. Compare
sending Bilal out of the room: the behaviour stops, but Bilal learns nothing about repair, Hamid's
harm is not addressed, and the class learns that exclusion is the answer.

### Dealing with unexpected events

The guide also asks: *how do I deal with unexpected events?* A pupil falls ill, a visitor arrives
unannounced, the electricity fails, a fight breaks out outside. No routine covers everything, so a
simple decision order helps: **safety first** (is anyone in danger? act at once, calmly), **learning
second** (can the lesson continue, adapted, or should the class switch to a backup task?),
**relationship third** (afterwards, what did the event show about the class, and what routine or
conversation is needed?). A well-managed classroom is not one where nothing unexpected happens, but
one with enough routine and trust to absorb surprises.

### Misconceptions the guide wants corrected

The guide lists seven common misconceptions. Each is a trap in exam answers:

1. the best-managed classroom is one where the teacher, not the pupil, controls learning;
2. the quietest classrooms are the best managed;
3. the goal of classroom management is peace and discipline;
4. classroom management relies on threats and punishment;
5. classrooms are homogeneous;
6. each class has fixed content that must be taught;
7. the same content must be taught to all pupils in one class.

### Putting strategies into a classroom management plan

The guide's first sample assignment asks trainees to write a classroom management plan covering:
their management philosophy (what a well-managed classroom is and should look and feel like); the
physical layout and whether it will vary; rules and pupil responsibilities; routines and procedures;
activities to foster community and care; and methods for dealing with disruptions. Unit 5 then asks
trainees to present, critique and revise their plans. A useful review checks **clarity** (can the
philosophy be stated in one sentence?), **consistency** (do the routines and responses match the
philosophy?) and **coverage** (are all the parts present, and is the plan written for a real
constraint such as a class of 60 or a multigrade room?).

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Differentiate** between classroom management and classroom discipline, and between
discipline and punishment, using the course guide. **Give** two proactive strategies and one reactive
strategy a teacher could use for pupils who repeatedly call out answers.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) During a Class 6 lesson in a Shikarpur school, a pupil knocks over a classmate's
materials and the class laughs. **Apply** the six-step caring, accountable response to this incident
and **justify** why it is more effective than sending the pupil out of the room.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Points-based rubric, 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns the marks |
|---|---|---|
| Management vs discipline | 4 | Management is proactive, acts on conditions for learning; discipline responds after a breach; one example each |
| Discipline vs punishment | 3 | Uses the guide: discipline teaches right from wrong and prevents recurrence; punishment only penalises |
| Two proactive strategies | 5 | e.g. taught hands-up or think-pair-share routine, pair rehearsal before answers, clear expectations, proximity, engaging task (2.5 each) |
| One reactive strategy | 3 | Brief, private, consistent response (e.g. a quiet reminder of the routine, then a logical consequence), in line with Doyle's advice |

A full-mark answer draws both distinctions accurately and gives strategies specific to calling out,
with the reactive step kept brief and private.

### CRQ 2

Points-based rubric, 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns the marks |
|---|---|---|
| Six steps applied | 9 | Each step applied to the incident (1.5 each): calm stop; check the classmate and materials; hear both; agree repair (e.g. pick up and replace materials); follow up; restore relationship |
| The laughing class | 2 | Addresses the class's laughter by restating the shared norm without humiliating anyone |
| Justification | 4 | Exclusion stops behaviour but loses learning time, teaches no repair, ignores the harmed pupil and damages relationships; the six steps build accountability |

A full-mark answer walks through all six steps with concrete actions and gives a reasoned comparison
with exclusion.
