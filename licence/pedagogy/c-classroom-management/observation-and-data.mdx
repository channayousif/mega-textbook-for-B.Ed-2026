# C03. Classroom observation and data collection

## What the test asks

A CRQ on this objective asks you to plan or critique an observation: **describe** a focus and a
tool, **explain** how you would record and analyse the data, or **distinguish** observation from
inference in a given record. The strongest answers are concrete: they name what will be counted
or mapped, how, and what the data could and could not show. Common ways to lose marks:

- promising to "observe the whole class" with no focus;
- naming a tool without saying what it records or how it will be analysed;
- writing judgements ("the class was bored") as if they were observations;
- forgetting the ethics of observing a colleague's classroom.

## Where your degree teaches this

- [EFMP-302 Topic 4.3 - Using a standard on yourself](/semester-1/efmp-302/unit-04/topic-03) teaches the improvement cycle of collecting evidence about your own practice, judging it, planning one change and collecting again, and what different kinds of evidence can show.

That topic gives you the evidence-and-improvement cycle for self-appraisal. No degree topic teaches
classroom observation as a management skill: choosing a management focus, designing and testing a
data-collection tool, and keeping observation separate from inference. This page teaches those from
the course guide's Unit 1, Week 2, and summarises Steps 4 and 5, which connect to that cycle.

## Explanation

### Why observation belongs in classroom management

The course guide sends trainees into practicum classrooms in the second week of the course, to
spend six hours observing "including class and out-of-class hours", and asks them, where
possible, to visit at least two classrooms "so that they can compare their data." The reason is
simple. Classroom management cannot be learned only from definitions. A teacher has to be able to
see what is actually happening in a room: where time goes, who participates, how routines run,
and how behaviour is handled. Observation is also how a working teacher improves: by collecting
evidence about their own classroom, trying a change and checking whether it worked.

Without a method, an observer sees what they expect to see. With one, they see what is there. The
method has five steps, shown in the figure below: decide what to look for, record what you saw,
separate observation from inference, ask why it might be happening, and plan one change to try.

### Step 1: decide what to look for

You cannot observe everything, any more than you can photograph a whole city at once. The guide
asks trainees, working in pairs, to "decide which aspect(s) of classroom management they will
collect data about." Its examples are:

- the **physical arrangement** of classroom space;
- the **social climate**, meaning how teacher and pupils address each other and how children
  interact among themselves;
- the **organisation of activities** and the management of time;
- the children's **level of engagement and participation**;
- **behaviour**, for example whether it is positively or negatively reinforced.

Later units add more focused questions. For routines (Unit 3), trainees observe and interview
teachers and children about the routines in use, and the guide warns that "in very
teacher-centred classrooms there will be fewer routines, so choose the classrooms carefully." For
teacher-pupil relationships (Unit 4), it suggests counting how many times the teacher addresses
individual pupils, how many times by name, how the class starts, and what the teacher's body
language and tone convey. Each of these is a focus narrow enough to record.

### Step 2: choose and design a tool

The guide explains that "collecting direct data involves a variety of techniques" and names
**observation schedules, interviews, maps and sketches**, adding that trainees "may need to use
multiple sources and methods." Each tool suits a different focus.

- **Observation schedule.** A prepared sheet listing behaviours to record, with space to tally
  how often each happens or to note what happens in each time block. Good for engagement,
  participation, time use and reinforcement. Example: a sheet with rows for "teacher praises
  effort", "teacher praises correct response", "teacher reprimands publicly", "teacher corrects
  privately", tallied across a 35-minute lesson.
- **Map or sketch.** A floor plan of the room, marked with furniture, the teacher's position and
  movement, traffic paths, displays and resources. A seating map can also carry a tick each time
  a pupil speaks, which shows at a glance whether participation is spread across the room or
  concentrated in the front rows.
- **Interview.** Short, planned questions to the teacher or pupils after the lesson, for example
  "What do you do when you finish your work early?" Interviews reveal routines and intentions that
  an observer cannot see, and they let pupils' views into the data.
- **Field notes.** A running written record, useful when the focus is a process such as a
  transition or the handling of an incident.

The guide asks trainees to think about whether their tools will help: will they collect the data they need,
what type of data will it produce, how will they record it, and how will they analyse it? A tool
that produces pages of notes nobody can summarise has failed that test.

### Step 3: record what you saw, separately from what you think

A usable record keeps two columns apart.

| What I saw (observation) | What I think it means (inference) |
|---|---|
| The teacher stood at the front for the first 12 minutes. | The teacher controlled the pace. |
| Three pupils in the back row talked throughout group work. | They were not engaged by the task. |
| The teacher walked to the back row and stood there. | The teacher used proximity to redirect them. |

The left column is evidence that anyone present could check. The right column is interpretation,
and it may be wrong: the three pupils may have been finishing the task together. Keeping the
columns apart stops an observer from confusing what they saw with what they expected. The first
question is always "what actually happened here?"; judging whether it was good teaching comes only
after the evidence is recorded.

A quick test for the left column: could a second observer, sitting beside you, disagree? "Hina
answered four questions" passes. "Hina was confident" does not; it belongs on the right.

### Step 4: analyse and ask why

In the guide's third session, trainees work in pairs "to analyse the data and to make some initial findings." Analysis
means turning raw records into patterns:

- **Counts and proportions**: 14 of 22 questions went to the same six pupils; three of five
  transitions took more than four minutes.
- **Comparisons**: between two classrooms, between the start and end of a lesson, or between
  what the teacher said in interview and what the schedule recorded.
- **Tentative explanations**: why might the pattern exist? If most questions go to the front rows,
  is it the seating, the teacher's habit of standing in one place, or pupils at the back who have
  learned not to volunteer? Each explanation suggests something to check next time.

Findings should be stated with appropriate caution. One lesson is one lesson; a pattern across
three lessons and two tools is stronger. This is why the guide values comparing two classrooms and
using more than one method.

### Step 5: plan one change, and review the tool

The last step turns data into management. A finding such as "participation is concentrated in the
front three rows" suggests one change to try, for example asking every pair to agree an answer
before any hand goes up, or moving to the back of the room when asking questions. The next
observation then checks whether the change made a difference, which starts the cycle again.

The guide also asks trainees to write about **the process of data collection** itself: whether the
tools were useful, whether it was easy to collect data, and whether they would change the tool if
they used it again. Reflecting on the tool is part of the skill, because a better tool gives better
evidence next time.

### Ethics of observing

Observation happens in someone else's classroom. Ask the teacher's permission, agree the focus in
advance where possible, do not interrupt the lesson, and do not discuss your judgements with pupils.
In any written report, do not name the teacher, the pupils or the school. The purpose is to learn
about classroom management, not to grade a colleague.

### A worked example

A trainee pair in a government girls' primary school in Nawabshah chooses the focus "engagement
and participation in Class 3 mathematics." Their tools are a seating map with a tick for each
pupil contribution and a two-question interview with the teacher. The map shows 31 of 40
contributions came from the first two rows. In interview the teacher says she "tries to ask
everyone". Their inference, written as a possibility, is that the teacher's fixed position at the
board makes the back rows less visible. Their proposed change is for the teacher to ask questions
from the back of the room for one week, and their review notes that the tool missed non-verbal
participation, so next time they will add a column for pupils showing answers on slates.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) You are asked to collect data on how time is used in a Class 5 lesson in a government
school. **Design** a data-collection plan: state your focus, **describe** two tools you would use and
what each records, and **explain** how you would analyse the data and what one finding might lead you
to change.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) A trainee's observation notes read: "The teacher was strict. Pupils were scared and did
not want to answer. The back benches were lazy." **Distinguish** between observation and inference,
**rewrite** the notes as a two-column record with at least three observable entries, and **explain** why
the distinction matters for a trainee's professional learning.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Points-based rubric, 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns the marks |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | 2 | A narrow, observable focus on time use (e.g. minutes lost in transitions, time on task) |
| First tool | 3 | Named (e.g. timed observation schedule) with what it records and how |
| Second tool | 3 | A different method (e.g. interview, map, field notes) and what it adds |
| Analysis | 4 | Converts records to counts or proportions, compares segments or classes, states findings cautiously |
| Link to a change | 3 | One finding leads to one specific management change and a plan to re-observe |

A full-mark answer is concrete throughout: it names what is timed or counted, uses two methods that
complement each other, and closes the loop from finding to change.

### CRQ 2

Points-based rubric, 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns the marks |
|---|---|---|
| Distinction stated | 3 | Observation is checkable evidence of what happened; inference is the observer's interpretation |
| Identifies the notes as inferences | 2 | "Strict", "scared", "lazy" are judgements, not observations |
| Two-column rewrite | 6 | At least three observable entries (2 marks each), each with a cautious matching inference |
| Why it matters | 4 | Prevents the observer seeing only what they expect; allows alternative explanations; supports fair, evidence-based improvement |

A full-mark answer converts every judgement into checkable behaviour (for example "the teacher
asked 12 questions; 2 were answered by pupils in the last three rows") and explains the
professional value of the distinction.
