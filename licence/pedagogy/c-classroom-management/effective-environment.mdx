# C02. Designing an effective classroom environment

## What the test asks

A CRQ here usually gives you a real or described classroom and asks you to **identify** what makes
an environment effective, **design** improvements, and **justify** them against learning. Markers
look for three things: that you start from the learning the room must serve, that you cover more
than furniture (the social and intellectual environment count), and that your proposals are
realistic for a Pakistani government school. Common ways to lose marks:

- listing decorations or buying new materials, with no link to a learning goal;
- describing only the physical room and ignoring norms, relationships and expectations;
- proposing changes a teacher with no budget and fixed benches could never make.

## Where your degree teaches this

- [EFMP-301 Topic 8.2 - Creating positive learning environments](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-08/topic-02) teaches the five designed parts of a positive environment (physical space, routines, relationships, participation norms, feedback) and a cheap first-week move for each.

That topic gives you the parts. This page adds what it leaves out: the guide's seven features of a
well-managed classroom, the guide's design question "What kind of classroom environment do I want?", the resource
audit and the three purposes of displays, and the guide's distinction between the physical, social
and intellectual environment.

## Explanation

### What a well-managed classroom looks like

The course guide asks trainees to test their picture of a well-managed classroom against seven
features. In a well-managed classroom:

1. pupils are clear about the teacher's expectations for rules and behaviour;
2. the teacher uses a variety of teaching methods;
3. pupils want to learn and actively participate, sometimes talking more than the teacher (the
   guide notes such a room "can actually seem chaotic at times when genuine learning is taking
   place");
4. small daily tasks such as queuing, collecting homework and taking attendance happen quickly,
   and the room allows easy movement;
5. time is well managed;
6. the teacher is clear about what to teach, how to teach it and how to check that learning has
   happened;
7. teaching reaches every child, not only the "average" pupil.

Notice that only one of the seven is about the room's layout. An effective environment is a
learning system, and its design has to serve all seven.

### Start from the learning goal

The guide's Week 3 question is *what kind of classroom environment do I want?* This is a question
about learning, not furniture. If you want pupils to practise procedures until they are automatic,
you design for clear sightlines, quick routines for materials and repetition. If you want pupils
to argue about ideas and build understanding together, you design for clusters, a norm of
listening to disagreement, and displays that make pupil thinking visible. The design follows the
goal. A teacher who pushes desks into clusters because it looks modern, without a learning reason,
has changed the furniture but not the learning.

### Audit the resources you already have

The guide lists "identify resources for learning" as the first design act. In a low-resource
school the useful question is not "what do I wish I had?" but "what is already here that I am not
using?" A resource audit lists everything that could support learning: textbooks, exercise
books, the blackboard, walls, floor space, real objects from home and the market, and the pupils
themselves, since a pupil who has understood an explanation can give it to a neighbour. Each item
is then checked for **access**. A science kit locked in the head teacher's cupboard is furniture,
not a resource, until there is a routine for getting it to pupils when needed. The guide also asks
trainees to brainstorm low-cost and no-cost materials, which in Sindh often means newspaper,
bottle tops, seeds, old calendars and the backs of used exam sheets.

### Displays with a job to do

The guide asks teachers to "use displays and visuals to enhance the learning environment" and
describes the room as "itself a learning area", rich with images, print and learning centres. A
display earns its wall space when it keeps teaching after the teacher has moved on. Three kinds do
this:

- **Learning-target displays**: the day's or week's target, where every pupil can see it, so the
  teacher does not have to repeat it.
- **Pupil-work displays**: current work, not only the best, which tells pupils the room is theirs
  and their thinking is valued.
- **Reference displays**: a number line, a word wall, a map, a chart showing the routine for
  asking for help. Pupils use these without asking the teacher, which is part of what lets a room
  run itself.

### Three environments, not one

In Week 4 the guide separates three environments a teacher creates:

- the **physical** environment, "an important resource for learning": space, furniture, displays,
  materials and centres;
- the **social** environment: how pupils resolve conflict, share and collaborate, work in teams,
  and support each other;
- the **intellectual** environment: the expectations the teacher sets for participation in
  discussion, asking questions, developing inquiries, rewarding authentic ideas, and keeping
  content appropriately challenging for every learner.

The three depend on each other. Clusters without a norm of listening are just noisy. A wall of
pupil work without a norm of respectful comment is just a wall. A demanding task without a safe
social climate will not be attempted. Evertson and Emmer (2009) and Wong and Wong (1998) both treat
the first weeks of term as the time to set these norms deliberately, by stating, modelling and
practising them.

### Design is proactive management

The guide closes Week 4 by linking environment to behaviour: "a proactive teacher takes preventive
action against potential problems rather than waiting for the problem to occur and then reacting
to it," and trainees are reminded that the physical space itself can prevent problems such as
pushing in a queue or interrupting. An effective environment is one designed so that the behaviour you want is
the easiest behaviour to do.

### A Sindh example

Miss Rukhsana has 48 pupils in a Class 4 room in Tando Muhammad Khan, with fixed benches, one
blackboard and no budget. Her learning goal for the term is more pupil talk in mathematics. She
cannot buy anything, so she audits: the side wall is bare, the floor at the front is free, and
several pupils bring bottle tops. She turns the wall into a number line made from newspaper, keeps
a sack of bottle tops as counters with two monitors to hand them out, writes the day's target in
one fixed corner of the board, and teaches one norm in the first week: "a wrong answer helps us
find the right way." Nothing about the benches changed, yet all three environments did.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) Using the course guide's features of a well-managed classroom, **explain** why a
classroom can look orderly but still be ineffective. Then **describe** three changes that would make
a described Class 5 room in a government school more effective, one each for the physical, social
and intellectual environment.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) A newly posted teacher in rural Thar has a room with bare walls, one blackboard and no
budget. **Design** a low-cost plan for making the room an effective learning environment in the first
month, and **justify** each element by the learning it serves.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Points-based rubric, 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns the marks |
|---|---|---|
| Orderly but ineffective explained | 4 | Order is not the goal; uses at least two of the guide's seven features (e.g. participation, teaching reaches every child, clarity about assessment) to show what an orderly room can lack |
| Physical change | 3 | Specific, doable, linked to learning (e.g. reference display, materials routine) |
| Social change | 3 | Specific norm or practice (e.g. peer help, conflict resolution routine) with its purpose |
| Intellectual change | 3 | Specific expectation (e.g. every pupil answers in pairs before hands go up; appropriately challenging tasks) |
| Coherence and realism | 2 | Fits a government-school room; the three changes support each other |

A full-mark answer uses the guide's features to separate order from learning, then gives three
concrete, realistic changes, each tied to a learning purpose.

### CRQ 2

Points-based rubric, 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns the marks |
|---|---|---|
| Starts from a learning goal | 3 | States what kind of classroom the teacher wants and why |
| Resource audit | 3 | Identifies unused resources (walls, floor, local materials, pupils) and the access problem |
| Displays | 3 | At least two display types with their teaching job (target, pupil work, reference) |
| Social and intellectual norms | 3 | At least one norm taught and practised in the first weeks |
| Justification and realism | 3 | Each element linked to learning; no purchases assumed; fits Thar conditions |

A full-mark answer builds a first-month plan from goal to audit to displays to norms, with each
step justified and achievable without money.
