# C06. Routines, schedules and time management

## What the test asks

A CRQ here asks you to **distinguish** routines from rules and procedures, **explain** how routines
save time, or **design** routines and a schedule for a named situation: a multigrade room, a class of
sixty, a pupil with a particular need, or group work. Answers score when the routine is specific
(what pupils do, in what order, with what signal) and when you say how it is taught. Common ways to
lose marks:

- announcing a routine and assuming it now exists;
- confusing a rule ("respect others") with a routine or procedure;
- ignoring the "diverse classrooms" part of the objective: multigrade, mixed need, large classes.

## Explanation

### Why small tasks matter

The guide opens Unit 3 with a list of "tiny administrative actions": gaining attention, taking
attendance, collecting assignments, gathering pupils, grouping and regrouping, handing out supplies,
leaving the room, taking tests. "These everyday classroom actions, if not administered efficiently,
can become time-consuming, even downright chaotic." It adds that instruction needs predictable
systems too: listening while the teacher explains, raising a hand, waiting a turn, joining a
discussion, moving between tasks, working in pairs, working silently, working in small groups.
Structure lets a teacher manage learning within limited time, but only if it is "predictable to
students."

The guide's essential question puts the teacher's benefit plainly: *how can I reduce the number of
things I need to think about and organise while I teach?*

### Rules, procedures and routines

The guide asks trainees to distinguish these and gives examples: a **rule** may be "Show respect to
others"; a **procedure** could be taking turns while talking. A useful set of working definitions:

- A **rule** states what is expected or allowed.
- A **procedure** is a step-by-step way of doing a particular task (handing in homework, lining up).
- A **routine** is a procedure practised until pupils do it without being told.
- A **structure** is the framework that holds routines: the timetable, the seating plan, the
  arrangement for group work.

The guide sorts common routines into three types: **class-management routines** (checking
attendance, pupil movement, cleaning the blackboard, record keeping), **lesson-management routines**
(collecting homework, distributing materials, working in learning centres) and **interaction
procedures** (raising hands, taking turns, asking to leave the room).

### How routines are taught

The guide is explicit: routines "need to be taught, demonstrated, practised frequently with
feedback, and re-taught, especially with younger children and during the first two weeks of a
school year, until they become set habits." Wong and Wong (1998) make the first days of school the
time for exactly this work. A routine that has been announced but not practised is only a wish.

Teachers usually design routines, because they organise the work, but the guide suggests seeking
ideas from children and involving them in running routines: older children helping younger ones,
or groups taking turns to tidy the room. It lists the benefits: the teacher has "one less" thing to
do when children can act without instruction, and children like routines because "they are familiar
and they can create a sense of responsibility."

### How routines buy time, and the transition

Consider a transition from explanation to group work without a routine. The teacher gives
instructions, waits for noise to settle, repeats them for those who did not listen, redirects two
pupils who started the wrong task, and only then begins. With a practised routine the teacher gives
a signal, pupils move, and the task starts. Across several transitions a day, five days a week, the
difference is many minutes of teaching recovered. Marzano, Marzano and Pickering (2003) treat
routines and procedures as one of the core components of effective management for this reason.

A good transition has three parts: a clear signal that the current activity is ending, a stated
time for the move, and a prompt that starts the next activity before pupils drift. **Routines do not
make a room rigid.** They remove the need to negotiate every move, which frees the teacher to be
flexible about content and response. The guide quotes Lucy Calkins: creative workplaces are
"deliberately kept predictable so the unpredictable can happen."

### Schedules in the multigrade classroom

In a multigrade room, one teacher with Classes 2 and 4 cannot teach both at once, so the schedule
itself is a management tool. The guide's multigrade principles note that plans and "the school
timetable" must be adapted, and that multigrade pupils "tend to develop independent work habits and
self-study skills." A common pattern alternates direct teaching and independent work between groups,
framed by whole-class time at the start and end.

<Figure id="fig-C06-1" kind="diagram" src="/img/figures/licence/c-classroom-management/fig-C06-1.svg" alt="Diagram of a multigrade timetable showing which group is with the teacher when: Group A works independently while the teacher teaches Group B, then they swap, with a whole-class anchor at the start and end." width={780} height={470} />

Two routines make this schedule work. The **anchor routine** is the task one group does while the
teacher is with the other: pupils start it without being told, know what to do when stuck, check
their own work, and know what to do when finished. The **independent-work routine** governs getting
materials, asking for help without calling out, and what to do next. The guide lists the multigrade
handbook (Vincent, Book 3: Classroom management and discipline) among its resources for this work.
Keep the routine set small and practised, including a clear signal for switching between groups.

### Routines for special needs and situations

The guide asks how routines can help with special needs and situations. The principle is to give
the pupil a routine that provides what they need without constant teacher attention:

- **needs extra time**: a continuation task and a quiet signal for "not finished yet";
- **easily distracted**: a fixed seat away from the door, a task broken into short steps, a check-in
  at set points;
- **finishes early**: a standing next task, a reading corner, or a peer-helper role;
- **needs help but will not ask**: a card on the desk, a place in a help queue, a predictable
  check-in moment.

### Subject-specific routines

The guide asks how routines can support mathematics, science and literacy. Each subject has its own
friction. In **mathematics**, distributing materials and checking answers can create queues at the
teacher's desk; a materials monitor and self- or peer-checking routines remove them. In **science**,
safety and clean-up are the risks; a safety routine before any practical work and a clean-up routine
at the end protect both pupils and the next lesson. In **literacy**, sustained reading and writing
need protection from interruption; routines for choosing a text, reading in pairs and recording a
response keep the block moving.

### Routines for co-operation and collaboration

The guide's final Unit 3 question asks how routines promote co-operation and collaborative learning.
Sitting pupils together is not collaboration. Collaborative work needs routines in three phases:
**set up** (assign roles such as reader, writer, reporter and timekeeper; state the task and time),
**run** (turn-taking, checking every member contributes, what to do when stuck, with the teacher
monitoring participation) and **close** (a whole-class share and a short reflection on how the group
worked). Quiet structures such as think-pair-share suit large classes; noisier ones such as jigsaw
need more space and more practised routines.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Distinguish** between a rule, a procedure and a routine, with a classroom example of
each. Then **explain**, with reference to the course guide, how a teacher should establish routines in
the first weeks of the year and why this saves teaching time.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) A teacher in a rural school in Umerkot teaches Classes 3 and 4 together in one room for a
40-minute mathematics period. **Design** a schedule for the period and **describe** two routines that
make it work, **justifying** each by the constraint of the multigrade room.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Points-based rubric, 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns the marks |
|---|---|---|
| Three terms distinguished | 6 | Rule states expectation; procedure is steps for a task; routine is a procedure practised to habit; one valid example each (2 marks per term) |
| How routines are established | 5 | Taught, demonstrated, practised with feedback, re-taught; first two weeks; may involve children in running them |
| Why this saves time | 4 | Fewer repeated instructions; smooth transitions; teacher freed to teach; example of a transition with and without a routine |

A full-mark answer uses the guide's examples or equivalents, describes the teaching of routines as a
process, and explains the time argument with a concrete transition.

### CRQ 2

Points-based rubric, 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns the marks |
|---|---|---|
| Workable schedule | 5 | Timed blocks with whole-class start and close, and alternating teacher-led and independent work for each class |
| First routine | 4 | e.g. anchor routine: self-starting, self-checking, linked to the topic, with a stuck/finished procedure |
| Second routine | 4 | e.g. switch signal, independent-work help routine, or peer-helper routine |
| Justification | 2 | Each routine linked to the teacher's inability to be with both classes at once |

A full-mark answer gives a realistic 40-minute plan and two routines described precisely enough that
a teacher could teach them to the class.
