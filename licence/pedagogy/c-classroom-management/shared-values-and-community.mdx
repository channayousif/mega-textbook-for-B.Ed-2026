# C07. Creating shared values and community

## What the test asks

CRQs on this objective ask you to **explain** why a sense of community matters for management,
**describe** how a teacher builds shared values, or **distinguish** caring from permissiveness. A
related sampled CRQ asks for a **parental involvement strategy**, which draws on the community part
of this page. Common ways to lose marks:

- describing a friendly teacher rather than deliberate practices that build community;
- treating an ethic of care as having no expectations or consequences;
- confusing parents attending an event with parents being involved in decisions.

## Where your degree teaches this

- [EFMP-302 Topic 3.3 - Relationships, and what to do when they go wrong](/semester-1/efmp-302/unit-03/topic-03) teaches why a teacher builds working relationships with pupils, parents, colleagues and the community, and how to handle conflict professionally.

That topic covers the teacher's relationships as a professional. This page adds what it leaves out:
the classroom as a community with shared values, how the teacher sets and teaches those values,
Noddings' ethic of care and the respectful moves that build it, and the practical routines for
bringing parents and the community into the classroom.

## Explanation

### What a classroom community is

The guide defines it directly: effective teachers "develop a sense of community in the classroom.
This means that students share a feeling of fellowship with each other that results from common
attitudes, experiences, and values. These values should foster respect for each other and each
other's property, opinions, beliefs, and cultures." It warns that a good curriculum and ordered
routines are not enough: if the teacher does not know pupils' names and backgrounds, or pupils are
not sharing their learning with each other, "an extremely valuable resource is being wasted."
Creating a community of learners "means building a feeling of mutual trust, respect, collaboration,
and support," and teaching children "to take care of themselves, their fellow students, their
environment, and their work."

This is a management idea, not only a moral one. Pupils who feel they belong, and who expect each
other to behave well, do much of the room's management themselves.

### How the teacher sets shared values

The guide names three things the teacher does, in order:

1. **Models** the values and behaviours expected of pupils. Pupils learn respect from how the
   teacher speaks to the weakest reader, not from a chart on the wall.
2. **Uses the curriculum** to teach values and behaviours, since some subjects give more room for
   this than others: a story in Urdu or Sindhi, a social studies lesson on neighbours, a science
   group task that needs sharing.
3. **Gives specific time** to teaching these behaviours and invites pupils to take an active part in
   upholding them, for example by agreeing class values together at the start of the year and
   reviewing them.

The guide also suggests reinforcing good behaviour with **collective rewards** that are not objects:
extra minutes at break, a leadership role, a favourite class activity, or praise in front of others.
Its examples of collective behaviour include returning promptly after break, leaving shoes tidily
outside the class, switching off lights and fans, and cleaning up. Because the reward is shared,
pupils encourage each other.

### An ethic of care

The guide asks: *what is "ethic of care" in education as defined by Nel Noddings?* and opens Unit 4
with her words: "To have as our educational goal, the production of caring, competent, loving and
lovable people is not anti-intellectual." For Noddings (2005), caring is a relation: the carer pays
close attention to the cared-for and responds to what they actually need, and the cared-for
recognises and responds to that care. A teacher is caring not because they feel warmly, but because
their attention and response reach the pupil.

Noddings (2005) names four components of moral education from the care perspective, and each
is something a teacher does:

- **modelling**: teachers show pupils how to care, through how they treat every child;
- **dialogue**: teachers talk with pupils, openly and without a fixed conclusion, about how people
  should treat each other;
- **practice**: teachers give pupils real chances to care, such as helping a younger child or a
  classmate who is stuck, and supervise that practice;
- **confirmation**: teachers see and affirm the best in each pupil, including when correcting them.

In a Pakistani classroom this looks like a teacher who greets the weakest reader by name
(modelling), talks with the class after a quarrel about what fairness means (dialogue), pairs
older pupils with younger ones in a reading routine (practice), and says to a pupil who pushed a
friend, "That is not like you; you are someone who helps" (confirmation). Henley (2009) frames
this kind of relationship-based classroom as proactive management.

**Care is not permissiveness.** A caring classroom still has expectations and consequences. The
difference is that consequences aim to repair harm and rebuild relationships rather than to
humiliate (see C09).

### Respectful relations: moves that build them

The guide's Unit 4 observation task asks trainees to count how often a teacher addresses pupils
individually and by name, how the class begins, and what the teacher's body language and tone
convey. These are the small moves that build or damage community. Each sends a message:

- using a pupil's name: *you matter as a person, not a seat*;
- listening without interrupting: *your thinking matters, even unfinished*;
- correcting privately: *your dignity matters more than the correction*;
- acknowledging effort as well as success: *trying counts here*;
- giving choices: *you have a voice in what happens*.

A single respectful act is an incident; the same act repeated daily becomes the culture of the room.

### Bringing families and the community in

The guide's Unit 1 list of challenges includes "the world around the classroom": the classroom
"should involve parents, invite community support, and address the social issues that children
encounter in their daily lives." Its Unit 4 questions ask what community means inside and outside
the school, what participation and involvement are, and what routines are needed to involve the
community.

Two working distinctions help answer the guide's questions. First, community has **circles**: the classroom, the school (head and
colleagues), parents and families, and the wider community (neighbours, local leaders, health
workers, places of worship). Second, **participation** means being present (attending a meeting,
signing a form, giving money), while **involvement** means having a voice or a role (helping plan
the meeting, contributing to a lesson, being consulted and heard). A school with a full hall and no
parental voice has participation without involvement.

Inviting a community member into a lesson works best as a routine: identify a curriculum need first;
find a person who matches it (a lady health worker for hygiene, a carpenter for measurement, a
farmer for crops); make a specific invitation; plan the visit together; host it by staying in the
room and setting expectations; and follow up with thanks and a learning activity. In a multigrade
room a prepared visitor can work with one class while the teacher teaches the other.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Explain** what the course guide means by a sense of community in the classroom, and
**describe** three things a teacher can do to set and teach shared values in a Class 4 room. **Justify**
how each helps classroom management.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) A colleague in Jacobabad says: "Caring teachers let children do what they like; I prefer
to be strict." Using Noddings' ethic of care, **evaluate** this view and **describe** how a caring teacher
would respond when a pupil laughs at a classmate's wrong answer.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Points-based rubric, 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns the marks |
|---|---|---|
| Community explained | 3 | Shared fellowship from common attitudes, experiences and values; respect for others, their property, opinions, beliefs and cultures |
| Three practices | 9 | e.g. modelling, teaching values through the curriculum, dedicated time with pupil participation, collective non-material rewards, respectful moves (3 marks each: described 1, concrete example 1, management benefit 1) |
| Link to management | 3 | Explains that pupils who belong and share expectations help regulate behaviour and learning |

A full-mark answer uses the guide's definition, gives three distinct, concrete practices and shows
how each reduces the need for teacher control.

### CRQ 2

Points-based rubric, 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns the marks |
|---|---|---|
| Ethic of care stated | 3 | Care as a relation: the carer attends and responds to real need, the cared-for recognises and responds; Noddings' components (modelling, dialogue, practice, confirmation) |
| Evaluates the view | 5 | Rejects the care/permissive equation; caring teachers hold expectations; strictness without care relies on fear; accepts that firmness and care can coexist |
| Response to the incident | 5 | Calm stop; protects the pupil's dignity (private word or re-framing the error as useful); restates the class norm; restorative step for the pupil who laughed; follow-up |
| Clarity and example | 2 | Uses the described incident concretely |

A full-mark answer shows that care and high expectations belong together and gives a response that
both protects the pupil who was laughed at and teaches the class the shared value.
