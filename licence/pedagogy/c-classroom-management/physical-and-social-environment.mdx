# C04. Physical and social environment features

## What the test asks

The licence sample paper includes a CRQ on **classroom challenges**, and this objective is where
that material lives. Questions ask you to **identify** features of the physical or social
environment that help or hinder management, **analyse** a described classroom against them, or
**explain** how a teacher can manage a named challenge such as overcrowding or scarce resources.
Common ways to lose marks:

- naming features ("seating", "discipline") without explaining their management effect;
- treating the social environment as the same thing as rules;
- listing challenges without any realistic response a teacher could make this week.

## Where your degree teaches this

- [EFMP-301 Topic 8.2 - Creating positive learning environments](/semester-1/efmp-301/unit-08/topic-02) teaches physical space, routines, relationships, participation norms and feedback as designed parts of a positive environment.

That topic describes the parts to build. This page adds what it leaves out: the specific physical
features that carry management weight (sightlines, traffic paths, proximity, display walls,
resource areas), the four social features to watch, and the guide's list of challenges a teacher
must negotiate, with responses suited to Pakistani government schools.

## Explanation

### The physical environment: features that carry management weight

The physical environment is everything material a teacher can arrange: furniture, displays,
resources, light and the use of wall and floor space. The guide's Week 4 lecture stresses that
whatever the layout, "it ought to enable the teacher to move freely between children and to be
able to reach each child." Five features matter most.

- **Sightlines.** Can the teacher see every pupil, and can every pupil see the board? The guide
  says teachers "must ensure that they can see everyone - and that everyone can see the teacher,"
  and that pupils with hearing or visual impairments may need to sit closer to the board.
- **Proximity and circulation.** The guide calls proximity "an important element of classroom
  management": the teacher must circulate "to observe and assess each child's work and provide
  individual instruction." A room with no path to the back benches is a room where the back
  benches are managed from a distance, if at all.
- **Traffic paths.** How pupils move to collect books, line up or form groups. A badly placed
  cupboard can turn a two-minute transition into five minutes of pushing.
- **Display walls.** What the walls show tells pupils what the room values: their own work, the
  learning target, useful references, or nothing at all.
- **Resource areas.** Where materials live, and whether pupils can reach them without asking. A
  room where every pencil comes from the teacher's table depends on the teacher for everything.

### The social environment: features that carry management weight

The social environment is the set of relationships and norms that govern how people in the room
treat each other. It is harder to see than furniture but has more power over behaviour. The guide
asks how teachers can help pupils with conflict resolution, sharing and collaboration, working in
a team, and supporting each other. Four features are worth watching.

- **Participation patterns.** Who is asked, who answers, who never speaks. A room where the same
  six hands go up for every question is a room where the teacher manages the visible few.
- **Norms of help.** Whether helping a neighbour is expected or treated as cheating. The guide's
  suggested resources include the IRIS Center case study on norms and expectations (Evertson and
  Poole), which is a place to see how classroom norms are established.
- **Responses to error.** Whether a wrong answer is treated as useful or humiliating. Learning
  needs the risk of being wrong in front of others, so a room where error is safe is a room where
  thinking is attempted.
- **Belonging.** Whether every pupil has a visible place: a name the teacher uses, a task that
  matters, a group that expects them.

The guide's summary of essential knowledge adds a feature that crosses both environments: setting
"a protocol for acceptable behaviour and communicating effectively so that students are clear
about what is expected of them."

### How the two environments interact

The physical arrangement shapes how people treat each other, and the social norms decide whether
the arrangement works. Clusters invite talk, but without a norm of listening they produce noise.
Rows make the teacher the focus, and if the teacher only ever stands at the front, the back rows
learn they are unseen. The guide notes that where a pupil sits "can affect learning", and that
teachers may need to "group and regroup students to maximize their interaction with a variety of
peers." Seating is therefore a social decision as well as a physical one.

### Challenges a teacher must negotiate

The guide's Week 3 lecture lists "some of the bigger things that teachers need to keep in mind."
Each is a management challenge with a realistic response.

| Challenge (from the guide) | What makes it hard | A realistic response |
|---|---|---|
| Curricular concerns: what to teach and how | A prescribed textbook to "cover", but pupils must understand and connect it to their world | Decide the essential idea of each lesson and teach it through activity and discussion, not rote (see C05) |
| Disparate levels and backgrounds | Pupils enter with very different prior knowledge | Systems that let each child learn: differentiated tasks, peer support (see C10) |
| A large number of learners in a small space | A room of dozens is "not a natural learning environment" | Arrange space so pupils can be grouped and regrouped; teach movement routines; establish clear rules |
| Routines, schedules and time | Limited time for many tasks | Predictable routines and prior planning save time (see C06) |
| Community and shared values | A class is not automatically a community | Model and teach values of respect for others' property, opinions, beliefs and cultures (see C07) |
| The world around the classroom | Children bring social issues into school | Involve parents, invite community support, address issues pupils face (see C07) |

Two further pressures shape many Sindh classrooms: **multigrade rooms**, where one teacher has two
or more classes at once, and **resource scarcity**, where one textbook may be shared by several
pupils. Neither is a reason to give up on management. Both are reasons to invest more heavily in
routines, peer roles and a clear physical layout, because the teacher cannot be everywhere.

### Reading a described classroom

When a CRQ describes a room, analyse it in two passes. First the physical pass: sightlines,
proximity, traffic, walls, resources. Then the social pass: participation, help, error, belonging.
For each feature, say what you see, what effect it has on learning, and one change within the
teacher's control. Consider a Class 4 room in Hyderabad with 44 pupils on benches built for 30,
one textbook per four pupils, the back benches in shadow, and no pupil speaking unless asked.
Physically, the back rows are out of the teacher's sightline and reach; a first change is for the
teacher to teach from the middle aisle for part of each lesson. Socially, participation is
teacher-controlled and silent; a first change is pair talk before any answer is taken, so every
pupil rehearses a reply.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Explain** four features of the physical environment and four features of the social
environment that affect classroom management, giving one classroom example for each, and show how
the two environments depend on each other.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) A Class 6 teacher in a Karachi government school has 70 pupils in a room built for 40,
and says "management is impossible here." **Analyse** two challenges the teacher faces using the course
guide's list, and **propose** a realistic response to each that covers both the physical and the
social environment.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Points-based rubric, 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns the marks |
|---|---|---|
| Four physical features | 6 | Any four from sightlines, proximity/circulation, traffic paths, display walls, resource areas, seating; 1.5 marks each for the feature plus its management effect and example |
| Four social features | 6 | Any four from participation patterns, norms of help, responses to error, belonging, behaviour protocol; 1.5 marks each |
| Interaction of the two | 3 | Shows how a physical feature depends on a social norm or vice versa |

A full-mark answer explains why each feature matters for learning, not just what it is, and
notes that the two environments work together.

### CRQ 2

Points-based rubric, 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns the marks |
|---|---|---|
| First challenge analysed | 4 | e.g. large numbers in small space: sightlines, proximity, movement and noise explained |
| Second challenge analysed | 4 | e.g. disparate levels, time, or scarce resources, with its specific effect |
| Physical responses | 3 | Realistic changes (teaching from the aisle, movement routine, materials monitors) |
| Social responses | 3 | Realistic norms or roles (pair talk, peer helpers, clear behaviour protocol) |
| Challenges the "impossible" claim | 1 | Argues that such rooms need more deliberate management, not less |

A full-mark answer uses the guide's challenges by name, analyses them concretely for a room of 70,
and proposes responses the teacher could start this week.
