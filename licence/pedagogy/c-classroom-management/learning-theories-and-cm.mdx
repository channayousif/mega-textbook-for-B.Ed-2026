# C01. Learning theories and classroom management

## What the test asks

A CRQ on this objective does not ask you to recite three definitions. It asks you to connect a
theory of learning to a **management decision**: why a teacher who holds one view of learning
arranges routines, seating, talk and responses to error differently from a teacher who holds
another. Expect command words such as **explain**, **compare**, **justify** and **apply to a
described classroom**. Common ways to lose marks:

- defining behaviourism, cognitivism and constructivism correctly but never saying what each
  implies the teacher should *do*;
- treating the theories as rivals ("constructivism replaced behaviourism") instead of as lenses
  suited to different learning targets;
- defining classroom management as keeping pupils quiet, which contradicts the guide's
  definition and costs the whole answer its frame.

Read those four topics for the theories themselves. This page teaches only what they leave out:
the explicit link from each theory to management moves, the guide's own definition of the
well-managed classroom, and how a teacher's philosophy decides what they treat as the problem.

## Explanation

### The guide's starting point: management serves learning

The course guide opens with a deliberate contrast. "In its narrowest sense, classroom management
is defined in terms of disciplining and controlling students," but the course "places the goal of
student learning at the heart of classroom management" and views the best-managed classrooms "as
ones in which each learner is effectively engaged in constructing knowledge." It makes the point
with a business comparison: just as a manager's goal is profit, a teacher's goal is maximum
learning. A class can look orderly and still be badly managed if pupils are not learning; a noisy
class can be well managed if every pupil is engaged and can say what they are doing to learn.

This definition is why learning theory belongs in a classroom management syllabus. If management
exists to produce learning, the teacher must have a view of how learning happens, and that view
shapes every management choice.

### Each theory implies different management moves

The guide's faculty handout sets out the three families simply: behaviourists see learning as a
response to stimuli, repeated when rewarded; cognitivists see learning as invisible mental
organisation built on memory and prior knowledge; constructivists see learners building their own
understanding through interaction with their environment. Here is what each means for managing a
room.

**Behaviourist moves.** Model the target behaviour, practise it, give immediate feedback, and
reinforce the response you want. This is the logic behind taught routines: entering the room,
handing in work, asking for help. Mr Karim, a Class 3 Urdu teacher in Ghotki, introduces a new
letter by saying the sound, having rows repeat it, and praising the rows that say it clearly. The
management weight is predictability: pupils know exactly what is expected and it is practised
until automatic.

**Cognitivist moves.** Activate prior knowledge before new material, make the structure of the
content visible, and keep the load on working memory manageable. On Wednesday Mr Karim asks which
words the class already knows that begin with the sound, writes them on the board and groups them
by pattern. The management weight is clarity: a pupil who can follow the structure of a lesson has
less reason to drift.

**Constructivist moves.** Set a task that makes pupils do the intellectual work, let them talk
through their thinking, and compare one group's reasoning with another's. On Friday Mr Karim gives
pairs picture cards to sort into "starts with the letter" and "does not", then asks each pair to
explain its grouping to another pair. The management weight shifts to task design and social
norms: pupils need to know how to work in pairs, how to disagree, and what to produce.

Ertmer and Newby (1993) place the three on a continuum rather than in competition: behaviourism
explains the acquisition of basic responses and procedures well, cognitivism explains the
organisation and storage of information, and constructivism explains transfer to new situations.
A fluent teacher moves along that continuum as the learning target changes. Drilling
multiplication tables for ten minutes is a defensible behaviourist move; asking pupils to invent a
word problem that needs multiplication is a defensible constructivist one.

### Philosophy decides what counts as the problem

The guide asks trainees in Week 1: *how does personal philosophy about teaching and learning affect
beliefs about classroom management?* The answer is that philosophy decides what the
teacher treats as the problem.

- A teacher who believes learning is **transfer** from teacher to pupil treats attention to the
  teacher as the management problem. A pupil looking away is the thing to correct, and a silent
  room looks like success.
- A teacher who believes learning is **construction** treats the demand of the task as the
  management problem. A quiet pupil may not be learning at all, and purposeful noise may be a sign
  that the work is happening.

Neither teacher is wrong to care about order. The danger is the teacher who has never examined
their beliefs, because they manage by habit, and habit comes from how they were taught rather
than from what their current pupils need. Wong and Wong (1998) make the practical version of the
point: a teacher with a conscious, consistent plan spends the first weeks teaching it and the rest
of the year teaching the curriculum.

### Doyle's point: order comes from how work is organised

The guide asks trainees to discuss a quotation from Walter Doyle: "order in classrooms is not a
consequence of reactions to misbehaviour but a condition established and sustained by the way a
teacher organizes and guides classroom activities and academic work." This is the bridge between
theory and management. Whichever lens you use, order is built into the design of the learning,
not added afterwards by punishment. That is also why the guide lists "the best-managed classroom
is one in which the teacher (instead of the student) is in control of learning" among its common
misconceptions.

### A worked example for the exam

Suppose a CRQ describes a Class 5 teacher in Mirpurkhas who teaches every lesson by lecture and
punishes any talking. A strong answer names the implicit theory (learning as transfer, closest to
a behaviourist reading of the teacher as source and pupils as responders), explains what that
belief makes the teacher manage (attention and silence), shows what it leaves out (checking
understanding, which a cognitivist would add; pupil talk for building meaning, which a
constructivist would add), and proposes one management change for each missing lens, such as a
two-minute prior-knowledge question at the start and a structured pair task in the middle.

## Practice questions

### CRQ 1

(15 marks) **Explain** how a behaviourist view and a constructivist view of learning would lead a
teacher to manage the same Class 4 science lesson on floating and sinking differently. Refer to
routines, seating or grouping, and the teacher's response to a pupil's wrong idea.

### CRQ 2

(15 marks) A head teacher in Larkana says: "The best-managed classroom is the quietest one, where
the teacher is fully in control." **Evaluate** this claim using the course guide's definition of
classroom management and at least two learning theories.

## Answers and marking guidance

### CRQ 1

Points-based rubric, 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns the marks |
|---|---|---|
| Behaviourist view stated accurately | 2 | Learning as observable response shaped by reinforcement |
| Constructivist view stated accurately | 2 | Learning as pupils building meaning through activity and interaction |
| Routines | 3 | Behaviourist: modelled, practised procedure (e.g. for handling water tubs); constructivist: routine for group investigation and reporting |
| Seating or grouping | 3 | Behaviourist: rows facing the demonstration; constructivist: small groups around materials |
| Response to a wrong idea | 3 | Behaviourist: immediate corrective feedback and re-practice; constructivist: ask the pupil to test the idea and compare with a peer |
| Coherence and use of the described lesson | 2 | Both versions are concrete and clearly different, tied to floating and sinking |

A full-mark answer defines both theories briefly, then contrasts the two lessons on all three
management points with concrete classroom detail, and notes that neither version is wrong in
itself.

### CRQ 2

Points-based rubric, 15 marks.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns the marks |
|---|---|---|
| States the guide's definition | 3 | Management centres on student learning; the best-managed class is one where each learner is engaged in constructing knowledge |
| Identifies what the claim assumes | 3 | Silence and teacher control equal learning; names this as a guide misconception |
| Uses a first theory | 3 | e.g. behaviourist order has a place for routines but does not show understanding |
| Uses a second theory | 3 | e.g. constructivist or cognitivist view: purposeful talk, prior knowledge and pupil activity are signs of learning |
| Balanced verdict | 3 | Accepts that order matters as a means, rejects it as the goal, and gives one concrete indicator of a well-managed class |

A full-mark answer takes the head teacher's claim seriously, shows exactly where it conflicts
with the guide, draws on two theories with classroom examples, and reaches a reasoned verdict.
