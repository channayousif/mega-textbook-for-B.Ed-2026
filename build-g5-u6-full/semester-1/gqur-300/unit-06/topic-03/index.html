Topic 6.3 - Decision-making with data in education and society

A real classroom situation​

A father in Naushahro Feroze must choose between two schools for his daughter: Government High School A, with 900 pupils and a 75 percent pass rate, and a private school B, with 300 pupils and a 90 percent pass rate. The private school's brochure quotes only the 90. The father, helped by his daughter's cousin - a B.Ed trainee with this course - asks one more question of each school: "How many pupils sat the exam?" School A entered 300 and passed 225; school B entered 60 and passed 54. The pass rates hold - but the trainee notices two more things: A's rate is built on five times the candidates, and B charges 1,500 rupees a month while A is free. The decision is the father's; the data's job is to make sure he decides on the whole picture, not on the one number someone chose to print.

Explanation​

Decision-making using quantitative data​

A data-informed decision runs the loop in the concept map above: name the question, gather the evidence, list the options with their costs, and weigh the trade-offs before deciding. It is Unit 1's four-step strategy grown up: the "plan" step has become "options and trade-offs", because real decisions are choices between incomparable goods - money against class size, one year's result against five years' trend.

Worked example: the school choice. Listing the evidence for the father's decision: pass rates (75 vs 90 percent), candidates (300 vs 60 - the small base makes B's rate wobblier), fees (0 vs 1,500/month = 18,000/year), distance, and the female teachers question his daughter cares about. The trade-off step names what each choice gives up: A gives up 15 points of pass rate but keeps 18,000 rupees a year in the family; B buys the rate and spends the money. There is no formula that settles this - and that is the point: the numbers inform the decision; they do not replace the decider.

Interpreting quantitative information in education and society​

The indicators in the table above are the ones a teacher will meet in staff meetings, news reports and policy documents. Three of them carry the unit's verified Sindh figures (PBS, 2023): literacy (10 years and above) at 57.54 percent, with its 14-point male-female gap; the out-of-school rate (ages 5 to 16) at 46.29 percent; and behind every school, the pupil-teacher ratio. Each indicator answers one question and hides another - the literacy rate hides its base, the out-of-school rate hides where the children are, and the ratio hides how pupils are distributed among classes. McClure (2020) built a quantitative-literacy unit for high-school STEM teachers around exactly this skill: reading an indicator's construction before using it to argue.

A common misconception to correct. Trainees often believe "the data decides". Data never decides; people decide, using data. The belief is dangerous in both directions - it lets a bad number overrule good judgement, and it lets a decision hide behind a number that was never questioned. The honest statement is quieter: a good decision names its question, shows its evidence, owns its trade-offs, and can say what would change its mind.

Activity: The school choice panel​

Work in groups of four. Each group receives the two-school dossier (pass rates, candidates, fees, distances, teacher numbers) plus one extra fact that differs by group (a new bus route, a sibling already at B, A's five-year rising trend). Groups run the decision loop and present a recommendation in exactly four sentences: question, evidence, options and costs, trade-off and choice. The class votes on which recommendation is best argued, not which school it picks. About 25 minutes.

Check your understanding​

  1. Name the four stages of the decision loop in order. (Remember)
  2. School A passes 225 of 300; school B passes 54 of 60. Compute both rates, and state which rate rests on the firmer base and why. (Apply)
  3. State what a pupil-teacher ratio of 30 can hide, with a concrete example. (Analyze)
  4. Name the indicator behind each figure: (a) 57.54 percent of persons 10 and above; (b) 46.29 percent of ages 5 to 16. (Understand)
  5. A committee says "the data decided". Correct the statement in two sentences. (Analyze)

Summary​

A data-informed decision runs question, evidence, options, trade-offs - the four-step strategy grown up, where the plan step becomes a choice between incomparable goods. The standard indicators (literacy, out-of-school, enrolment, attendance, pupil-teacher ratio) each answer one question and hide another, and the verified Sindh figures give them real weight. Data informs the decision; it never replaces the decider.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can run the decision loop on a real choice and present it in four sentences.
  • I can read the standard education indicators with their bases and their blind spots.
  • I can state, for a given decision, what evidence it used and what would change its mind.

Try this at your practicum school​

Take one decision your cooperating school made this term (a purchase, a timetable change, an event). Reconstruct it as the decision loop: the question, the evidence actually used, the options considered, and the trade-off that settled it. Ask the decision-maker whether your reconstruction is fair, and note their correction.

Summative task​

A district committee must spend one grant of 500,000 rupees either on hiring two new teachers for a school with a pupil-teacher ratio of 50, or on a bus for 40 pupils who walk 5 kilometres, in a district whose out-of-school rate (ages 5 to 16) is 46.29 percent (PBS, 2023). In a short written answer: (a) run the decision loop - state the question, the evidence, the two options with their costs, and the trade-off each choice makes, (b) recommend one option with the computation that supports it, and (c) state one piece of evidence that would change your recommendation.

Mini-rubric (each criterion 0-2 marks):

  • Loop run: question, evidence, options and trade-offs all present and separated.
  • Recommendation with computation: for example, two teachers cut the ratio for 100 pupils from 50 to 25 (100/4: two teachers become four), while the bus moves 40 pupils - the support names which population each option serves. (Analyze)
  • Changing evidence: a concrete item (a survey showing the 40 walkers would drop out without the bus; a second grant arriving) that would genuinely flip the choice.
