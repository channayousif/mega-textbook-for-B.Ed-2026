Topic 6.2 - Quantitative reasoning in media and advertisements

A real classroom situation​

A toothpaste advertisement declares: "88 percent of dentists recommend it." The claim sounds scientific, and it may even be true. But a trainee who has Unit 5 asks the questions: 88 percent of how many dentists? Recommended over what - over other brands, or over not brushing at all? Was the survey run by the company? And what happened to the other 12 percent? None of these questions needs new mathematics; they need the habit of asking where a number came from before repeating it. This topic collects the standard moves that make numbers persuade, and the questions that restore the arithmetic. Steen (2001) built the case for quantitative literacy on exactly this ground: a democracy whose citizens cannot question numbers must simply believe them.

Explanation​

Quantitative reasoning in media and advertisements​

The table above is the working toolkit; the moves it polices are the same ones Unit 5 met inside the classroom, now pointed outward.

The truncated axis. The diagram above shows the classic: a bar chart whose vertical axis starts near the data instead of at zero, turning a 6-point difference into a towering victory. The honest chart beside it carries the same numbers. Advertisers truncate axes; so do news graphics; so, sometimes, do school results boards.

The convenient base. "Now with 50 percent more fruit!" - more than what? Fifty percent more than a rival, or than the same product's previous tiny amount? A percentage without its base is a sentence without its subject. Unit 5's census reading practised the same guard: 57.54 percent meant nothing until "10 years and above" was read with it (PBS, 2023).

The missing comparison. "The fastest-growing brand in Sindh" may be fastest-growing because it started smallest. Growth rates, exam passes, attendance figures - all persuade most when the comparison is chosen by the persuader. The cure is always the same question: compared to what, over what period?

The instalment that hides the rate. From Topic 6.1: a shop advertises "only 2,000 rupees a month" on a 20,000-rupee item. Twelve instalments collect 24,000 rupees; the hidden interest is 4,000 rupees on 20,000 for one year - a 20 percent rate, four times a bank's. The monthly number feels small; the rate is the fact.

A common misconception to correct. Trainees often believe "numbers in the media must be true, because numbers are facts". A number is a fact about a question someone asked, in a way someone chose. The advertisement's arithmetic is usually correct; what is engineered is the question, the base and the comparison. Tout (2020) draws the same lesson from international assessments: what a number says depends on the task that produced it, and cross-comparing figures without reading their construction misleads. The reader's job is not cynicism - it is the five questions, which a truthful claim survives easily.

Activity: The advertisement audit​

Work in pairs. Your tutor displays five real-style advertisements and headlines (a toothpaste survey, a "50 percent more" food claim, a growth-rate boast, a monthly instalment offer, a news chart). Each pair picks two and writes, for each: the claim in one sentence, the two questions that most need answering, and what the honest version of the claim would say. Pairs present one audit to the class. About 25 minutes.

Check your understanding​

  1. Name the move at work: a bar chart's vertical axis starts at 80, not 0. (Remember)
  2. "Now with 50 percent more fruit." State the question the claim hides, and two possible bases that would change its meaning. (Apply)
  3. A 20,000-rupee item at "2,000 a month for 12 months": compute the total paid and the hidden first-year interest as a percentage. (Apply)
  4. "The fastest-growing school in the district." Write the two questions you would ask before believing the headline. (Analyze)
  5. Explain in two sentences why correct arithmetic can still mislead. (Understand)

Summary​

Numbers persuade through construction, not lies: the truncated axis exaggerates, the convenient base hides the denominator, the missing comparison chooses the rival, and the instalment hides the rate. The five questions - source, base, comparison, unit, what is missing - restore the arithmetic, and a truthful claim survives them easily.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can name the four standard moves and spot each in a real advertisement.
  • I can rebuild a hidden interest rate from an instalment offer.
  • I can write the honest version of a persuasive claim.

Try this at your practicum school​

Collect one advertisement or news number from the school's notice board, a newspaper at the staff room, or a shop near the school. Write the claim, the two questions it raises, and what the honest version would say. Bring it to class for the unit assessment.

Summative task​

A canteen supplier's poster reads: "9 out of 10 pupils prefer our samosas - and now 30 percent bigger!" In a short written answer: (a) state the two questions each claim needs answered before a school orders, (b) compute what "30 percent bigger" means if the old samosa was 80 grams, and (c) write the honest one-sentence version of the combined claim that a headteacher could act on.

Mini-rubric (each criterion 0-2 marks):

  • Questions: for the survey, who counted and how the 10 were chosen; for the size, the base of the 30 percent - both named.
  • Computation: 80 x 1.30 = 104 grams.
  • Honest version: a sentence carrying both numbers with their bases, for example "in the supplier's own survey of 10 pupils, 9 preferred its samosas, which now weigh 104 grams against the previous 80". (Analyze)
