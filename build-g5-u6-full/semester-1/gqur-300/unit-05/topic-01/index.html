Topic 5.1 - Collecting and organizing data

A real classroom situation​

Miss Benazir, the headteacher of a girls' primary school in Khairpur, wants to know why attendance dips on Thursdays. She does not guess. She asks the office for last term's attendance registers (records), sends two teachers to note arrivals at the gate for a week (observation), and asks forty pupils one question with three answer choices (survey). In ten days she has three piles of facts - and no conclusion yet, because raw data answers nothing by itself. The conclusion comes from the cycle in the flowchart above: a question, then collection, then organization, then summary, then interpretation. This topic is the cycle's first half: collecting honestly and organizing so the summarizing can begin.

Explanation​

Collection and organization of data​

Data are the raw facts a question collects. The three collection methods each answer a different kind of question, as the table below shows.

Organizing with tallies and frequency tables. A frequency table counts how often each answer occurs. The tally is its working form: strokes grouped in fives (four vertical strokes, the fifth across), so a full page of counts can be totalled at a glance. Suppose the forty-pupil survey asks "What keeps you away on Thursdays?" with choices: household work, transport, illness. The tally columns close at these frequencies:

ReasonFrequency
Household work12
Transport14
Illness9
Total recorded35

And 35 is not 40. Five pupils went unrecorded. That gap is the topic's quiet lesson: an organized table audits its own collection. Miss Benazir's next move is not to summarize but to find the missing five - five blank survey sheets in a drawer - and only then does the table close at 40.

Worked example. A travel-to-school survey of one class of 40 gives: walk 16, van 11, rickshaw 8, bicycle 4, other 1. Organized, that is a frequency table - the same organizing device Marecek, Anthony-Smith and Mathis (2020, Section 5.5) use in their own worked examples on averages; summarized, walk is 16/40 = 40 percent of the class. The organization step is what makes the percentage a one-line computation.

A common misconception to correct. Trainees often believe "data means numbers". Half of Miss Benazir's data are words: reasons, names of transport, register marks. Numbers arrive at the summarize step, when the words are counted. The counting is only as honest as the collection: a survey question with leading choices, or an observation done on an unusual week, poisons every number built on it. The Pakistan Bureau of Statistics runs the national census on the same cycle - question, collection, organization, summary, interpretation - and its Sindh report (2023) is organized as exactly these stages (PBS, 2023).

Activity: The travel-to-school survey​

Work in groups of four. Each group designs one survey question about how the class travels to school, with three or four fixed choices. Groups swap question papers and survey eight classmates each (different classmates, so the whole class is covered). Each group then tallies its own eight responses into a frequency table, checks the total, and pins the table beside the question. The class then combines all tables into one master table and compares group totals. About 25 minutes.

Check your understanding​

  1. Name the five stages of the data cycle in order. (Remember)
  2. For each question, name the best collection method and why: (a) how many pupils arrive after the bell each day this week; (b) which snack the canteen should stock; (c) how many pupils were absent last month. (Apply)
  3. A survey of 40 pupils gives frequencies 12, 14, 9. State what the table tells you about the collection, and what the next move is. (Analyze)
  4. Build a frequency table from these tallies: walk 16, van 11, rickshaw 8, bicycle 4, other 1; and state what fraction walked. (Apply)

Summary​

Data are raw facts collected for a question, by observation, survey or records; the method must match the question. Tallies and frequency tables organize the facts so summary becomes simple counting, and a good table audits its own collection - a total that does not close names a collection problem before any summary is trusted.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can name the five stages of the data cycle and the three collection methods.
  • I can design a survey question with fixed choices and tally responses into a frequency table.
  • I can check a frequency table's total against the sample size and say what a gap means.

Try this at your practicum school​

With permission, take one page of the attendance register and tally the week's absences by day. Organize the counts into a small frequency table, check the total against the class strength, and note one thing the table shows that the register page itself does not.

Summative task​

The headteacher wants to know the school's most common travel mode. Design and run a mini-survey of 20 pupils (fixed choices: walk, van, rickshaw, bicycle, other). In a short written answer: (a) present your frequency table with the total check, (b) state the most common mode and its percentage, and (c) identify one way your collection could be biased (who you asked, when, or how the choices were worded) and how you would fix it in a rerun.

Mini-rubric (each criterion 0-2 marks):

  • Table with check: a correct frequency table totalling exactly 20.
  • Mode and percentage: the most common mode named with a correct percentage.
  • Bias identified: a real collection bias named (for example, surveying only one class) with a concrete fix. (Analyze)
