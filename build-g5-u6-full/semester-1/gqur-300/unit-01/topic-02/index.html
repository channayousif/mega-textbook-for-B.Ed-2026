Topic 1.2 - Number sense, estimation and approximation

A real classroom situation​

It is the annual sports day at a primary school in Sukkur. The headteacher turns to Miss Farzana, the Class 6 teacher: "Roughly how many people are on the ground now? The water contractor wants to know." Miss Farzana cannot count 800 people one by one. She looks at one crowded section, counts about 40 people there, counts 20 similar sections by eye, and says "about 800, maybe a few more". The contractor fills the water cooler accordingly, and there is enough. Nobody needed the exact number 817. What Miss Farzana used is estimation, and the judgement of when it is good enough is part of number sense.

Explanation​

Numeracy and number sense​

Numeracy is the ability to use mathematics in daily life. Number sense is its foundation: a feel for what numbers mean, how big they are, and how they relate to each other. A person with number sense is not surprised that a class of 40 pupils cannot share 5 books fairly, or that a 10 percent fee rise on a 2,000-rupee fee is 200 rupees, not 2,000.

Number sense shows itself in small, fast judgements:

  • Size judgement: knowing that 0.5 kg of tomatoes at 120 rupees a kg costs about 60 rupees, before the shopkeeper weighs it.
  • Relationship sense: knowing that doubling a group doubles its cost, and that a third as many pupils needs a third as many worksheets.
  • Reasonableness checking: looking at any answer and asking "could this possibly be right?" If a trainee computes that a school of 300 pupils needs 3,000 chairs, number sense sounds the alarm.

Gula and Lovric (2025) argue that numeracy tasks are their own category of mathematics task, distinct both from word problems and from mathematical modelling, and that a quality numeracy task "inspire[s] transfer between concrete and abstract thinking spaces". That is exactly the movement this topic asks of you: from a ground full of people (concrete) to a defensible estimate (abstract), and back to the decision the estimate serves.

Estimation and approximation​

An estimation is a deliberate, reasoned guess that is close enough for the purpose. An approximation is a value used in place of an exact one to make work easier, like taking 398 as 400. The two often travel together: Miss Farzana approximated each section as 40 people, then estimated the total.

Gula and Lovric (2025) put exactly this habit at the heart of a quality numeracy task: the estimate is the abstract answer that the concrete situation must be able to carry, and checking it against the situation is the transfer back.

Worked example. A school function needs biscuits for the pupils. A packet serves about 10 pupils. The school has 348 pupils in Classes 1 to 5, plus about 30 parents. Round 348 up to 350 (we do not want to run short), add 30 parents to get 380 people, and divide by 10: about 38 packets. Buy 40 to be safe. Notice the reasoning moves: round for ease, round up when shortage is worse than surplus, and check the size of the answer at the end.

A common misconception to correct. Many trainees believe "estimation is just guessing". A guess has no method; an estimate has a method and a purpose. A good estimate can be defended: "I counted one section and multiplied" is an argument, not a shrug. Equally, estimation has limits: when the headteacher files the official enrolment return, an estimate is wrong even if it is close - the register must be counted exactly. Part of quantitative reasoning is knowing which situations demand exactness and which reward a fast, defensible estimate.

Activity: The one-minute estimate challenge​

Work in groups of four. Your tutor will show five quick prompts one at a time, for exactly one minute each (for example: how many bricks are in the front wall of the classroom, how many litres does the water cooler hold, what is the monthly chalk cost for the school). Each group writes an estimate plus one sentence naming its method. After all five prompts, each group reveals its methods. The tutor awards a point for a sensible method, not for closeness to the true figure. About 25 minutes.

Check your understanding​

  1. In your own words, state the difference between an estimate and an approximation, with one example of each. (Understand)
  2. A trainee estimates 47 x 52 as 50 x 50 = 2,500. Name the strategy used, and say whether the true answer is above or below the estimate, with a reason. (Apply)
  3. For each situation, say whether an estimate or an exact count is appropriate, and why: (a) ordering exam papers for the class, (b) deciding whether the school van can carry all pupils in one trip. (Apply)

Summary​

Number sense is a feel for the size and meaning of numbers; it powers the reasonableness check on every answer. Estimation is a reasoned, defensible guess with a method and a purpose, built from strategies like grouping, rounding, front-end digits, and compatible numbers. Knowing when an estimate is good enough, and when exactness is required, is itself a quantitative judgement.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can explain the difference between a guess, an estimate, and an approximation.
  • I can apply at least two estimation strategies to a school situation and name my method.
  • I can judge whether a given situation needs an estimate or an exact count, with a reason.

Try this at your practicum school​

Estimate one quantity at your practicum school without counting it fully (for example, the number of books on the class shelf, or the pupils on the ground at break). Write down your estimate, your method, and then, with permission, find the true figure. Note how far off you were and what you would change about your method. Bring the note to class.

Summative task​

The Parent Teacher Association can spend 25,000 rupees on prizes for a school of 512 pupils. The president plans to buy 100 prizes at about 250 rupees each. In a short written answer: (a) estimate whether the plan fits the budget, naming your strategy, (b) identify one quantity the plan ignores that could change the conclusion, and (c) recommend the plan or a change to it, with reasons.

Mini-rubric (each criterion 0-2 marks):

  • Estimate with a named strategy: the budget check uses a stated estimation strategy, not a bare number.
  • Ignored quantity identified: the answer names a real factor (prize quality tiers, transport cost, number of prize categories) and shows how it could change the result. (Analyze)
  • Recommendation follows: the final recommendation follows from (a) and (b) together.
