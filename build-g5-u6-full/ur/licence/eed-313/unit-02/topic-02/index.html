Topic 2.2 - Plan, motivate, teach, assess

A real classroom situation​

Miss Saira teaches Class 5 science in a government school in Quetta. Her lessons follow the same four moves every time: she decides what the pupils will learn and what they will do, she opens with a question that makes them want to find out, she teaches the content through a mix of explanation and activity, and she checks before the bell whether they have got it. Her pupils know what to expect, and the room runs on that predictability. This topic names the four moves and the management decision each one depends on.

Explanation​

The guide describes a four-stage cycle that carries a teaching plan from intention to evidence: plan, motivate, teach, assess. The cycle is not a list of tips. It is a sequence in which each stage creates the condition for the next, and the management decision at each stage is the thing that determines whether the lesson stays on track or drifts.

Planning the curriculum​

Planning is the stage in which the teacher decides the learning target, the activities, the materials, and the time each part of the lesson will take. The management decision here is clarity of target: a teacher who can state in one sentence what the pupils will be able to do by the end of the lesson has a compass for every later decision; a teacher who cannot will spend the lesson improvising, and improvisation is the enemy of a well-managed classroom.

Marzano, Marzano and Pickering (2003) found that the clarity of the learning target was one of the strongest predictors of pupil learning, because it lets the teacher cut anything that does not serve the target and keep the lesson focused. A focused lesson is easier to manage than a wandering one.

The plan also decides the routines the lesson will rely on: how pupils will get materials, what they will do when they finish early, how transitions between activities will be handled. These are management decisions, and Topic 1.3's observation protocol is the tool for checking whether the routines you planned are the routines that actually happen.

Motivating learners within the curriculum​

Motivation is the stage in which the teacher creates the want to learn before the teaching begins. The management decision here is relevance: pupils who can see why the lesson matters to them are easier to teach than pupils who cannot.

The figure above maps four common motivation strategies against what each one costs in time and materials. A well-chosen hook question costs almost nothing and takes two minutes. A hands-on investigation costs materials and time but produces deeper engagement. A competition is fast but can produce winners and losers, which is a management cost of its own. A pupil choice is motivating but needs clear boundaries or it becomes noise.

There is no universally best strategy. The management question is: given this class, this content, and the time I have, which strategy gives me the most engagement for the least management cost? Wong and Wong (1998) argue that the first minutes of a lesson determine its management tone for the whole period, so the motivation stage is where the teacher earns or loses the room.

Teaching the curriculum​

Teaching is the stage in which the teacher delivers the content, and the management decision here is pacing and participation. A teacher who talks for thirty minutes manages attention by force of personality; a teacher who mixes explanation with activity manages it by design.

The guide's preferred pattern is short explanation followed by pupil activity, repeated in cycles: tell them what they are going to learn, show them how, let them try, check, move on. Each cycle is a small management unit with a beginning, a middle, and an end, and pupils learn to move with the rhythm.

Evertson and Emmer (2009) stress the transition points between activities as the moments most likely to produce disruption. A teacher who signals the transition clearly, gives a time bound, and checks that every pupil has started the new task is managing the room; a teacher who moves on while half the class is still finishing the last thing is not.

Assessing the curriculum​

Assessment is the stage in which the teacher checks whether the learning target has been met. The management decision here is evidence: a teacher who ends the lesson without checking does not know whether to move on or reteach, and a teacher who does not know is managing on hope.

Assessment does not have to be a test. A show of hands, an exit ticket, a pair share, a question to a pupil who looked uncertain - each is a quick check that tells the teacher what to do next. Marzano, Marzano and Pickering (2003) treat feedback as one of the most powerful management tools available, because it tells the teacher whether the plan worked and what to adjust.

The figure at the start of this topic shows a return arrow from "assess" back to "plan". That arrow is the point: assessment is not the end of the cycle. It is the information the teacher uses to plan the next lesson, and the management habit of closing that loop is what separates a teacher who improves from a teacher who repeats.

Activity: Trace a lesson through the cycle​

Work in pairs. Take a lesson you have observed during practicum (or one from your own schooling). Trace it through the four stages: what was the plan, how did the teacher motivate, how did the teacher teach, and how did the teacher assess? For each stage, name the management decision the teacher made. Allow 20 minutes. Then compare with another pair and identify one stage where a different management decision would have changed the lesson.

Check your understanding​

  1. Name the four stages of the plan-motivate-teach-assess cycle and the management decision at each stage. (Remember)
  2. A teacher says, "I don't need a hook question; I just start teaching." Using this topic's account, explain what that approach costs in management terms. (Analyze)
  3. Explain why the transition between activities is the moment most likely to produce disruption. (Understand)
  4. A teacher ends every lesson with the same question: "Did you all understand?" Assess whether this counts as assessment in the sense this topic uses the term. (Analyze)
  5. Give two examples from your own schooling of a motivation strategy and state what it cost in time or materials. (Apply)

Summary​

The plan-motivate-teach-assess cycle carries a teaching plan from intention to evidence. The management decisions are: a clear target at the planning stage, relevance at the motivation stage, pacing and participation at the teaching stage, and evidence at the assessment stage. The return arrow from assess to plan closes the loop and is what makes the cycle a habit rather than a formula.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can name the four stages of the cycle and the management decision at each one.
  • I can explain why the motivation stage sets the management tone for the lesson.
  • I can name at least three motivation strategies and the cost of each.
  • I can explain why assessment is not the end of the cycle but the information for the next plan.
  • I can trace a described lesson through the four stages and name the management decision at each.

Try this at your practicum school​

Over three lessons taught by the same teacher, keep a tally of the time spent on each of the four stages. At the end, calculate the proportion and write half a page on which stage dominates and whether that dominance fitted the content.

Do not name the school or the teacher in your write-up.

Summative task​

Write 350 to 400 words on the following.

Describe a lesson you have observed (in practicum or your own schooling). Trace it through the four stages of the plan-motivate-teach-assess cycle. Then select the one stage you judge to have been the weakest, propose a specific improvement, and justify it by reference to the pupils or the content.
