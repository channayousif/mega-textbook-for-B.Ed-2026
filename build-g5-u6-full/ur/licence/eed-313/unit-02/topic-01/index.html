Topic 2.1 - Curriculum as a management tool

A real classroom situation​

Two teachers in the same school in Lahore teach the same Class 4 English lesson from the same textbook. One spends the period managing disruption; the other does not. The difference is not personality. It is that the second teacher made, before the lesson, a set of curriculum decisions - what to emphasise, in what order, at what pace - that removed the frictions the first teacher met unprepared. This topic is about those decisions.

Explanation​

The guide treats curriculum not as a body of content to be delivered but as a set of management decisions that shape the classroom before the lesson begins. Get them right and the room runs more smoothly. Get them wrong and you spend the lesson reacting.

How curriculum supports classroom management​

Curriculum, in this course, is the teacher's answer to four questions: what to teach (content selection), in what order (sequencing), at what pace (pacing), and for whom (grouping). Each of these is a management act, because each one determines how much of the lesson's time is spent learning and how much is spent handling the friction that comes from a poor fit between the plan and the pupils.

Choosing content is the first management decision. A teacher who tries to cover everything in the chapter sets the class up for rushed explanations, confused pupils, and the disruptions that follow confusion. A teacher who decides what the essential idea is and lets the rest go has created the conditions for depth, and depth is easier to manage than superficial coverage.

Sequencing determines whether the lesson builds or stalls. Put the hard idea before the foundation and the class loses the thread; put practice before the explanation and the practice becomes guesswork. A well-sequenced lesson manages attention because each step follows naturally from the last.

Pacing is the most common management failure. A pace that leaves the strong pupils bored creates one kind of disruption; a pace that leaves the struggling pupils behind creates another. Evertson and Emmer (2009) treat pacing as a core management skill: the teacher who reads the room and adjusts the speed of the lesson is managing in real time.

Grouping decides whether pupils work as a whole class, in pairs, or in small groups, and the management demand of each is different. Whole-class work is the easiest to direct but the hardest to differentiate; group work serves differentiation but requires clear routines. The curriculum decision is not just what pupils learn, but how they are arranged to learn it.

Creating a teaching and learning plan consistent with your philosophy​

Topic 1.2 asked how a personal philosophy of teaching shapes management beliefs. This topic takes that a step further: it asks you to build a plan that is consistent with your philosophy, because a plan that contradicts the teacher's own beliefs produces a classroom that feels incoherent to everyone in it.

The figure above traces two short teaching plans back to two philosophies. The first plan assumes that learning is the transfer of knowledge from teacher to pupil: the teacher explains, the pupils practise, the teacher checks. The second plan assumes that learning is the pupil's active construction: the teacher poses a problem, pupils investigate in groups, the teacher draws out the insight. Both are defensible. What is not defensible is a teacher who holds the second philosophy but teaches the first plan, because the pupils experience the contradiction and the management suffers for it.

Wong and Wong (1998) put it as a practical test: a well-managed classroom has a consistent plan that the pupils can learn to rely on. The plan does not have to be elaborate. It has to be yours, and it has to be consistent.

Building such a plan starts with three questions:

  1. What do I believe learning is? (Unit 1's three theories are your options.)
  2. What does that belief imply I should do first, second, third in a lesson?
  3. What does it imply I should look like - at the board, moving among groups, listening?

The answers are your plan's spine. Everything else - the activity, the timing, the materials - hangs off it.

Activity: One objective, three plans​

Work in groups of three. Take the following curriculum objective:

Class 3 mathematics: pupils will be able to add two-digit numbers with carrying.

Now plan this objective three ways: for a well-resourced single-grade class, for a multigrade class (Classes 2 to 4 in one room), and for a class of sixty. For each version, decide: what you would do first, what the pupils would do, and what you would need. Allow 25 minutes. Then present your three plans and identify one decision that changed because of the room, not because of the mathematics.

Check your understanding​

  1. Name the four curriculum decisions this topic identifies as management acts, and give one example of each. (Remember)
  2. A teacher says, "I just follow the textbook, so I don't make curriculum decisions." Using this topic's account, explain what that claim overlooks. (Analyze)
  3. Explain why a fast pace can be a management failure even when the content is correct. (Understand)
  4. A friend's lesson plan has pupils investigating in groups, but she believes learning is the teacher transferring knowledge. Identify the inconsistency and explain what it costs in management terms. (Analyze)
  5. For the objective in the Activity, state one way your plan for a multigrade class would differ from your plan for a single-grade class, and explain why. (Apply)

Summary​

Curriculum decisions - what to teach, in what order, at what pace, for whom - are management acts that shape the classroom before the lesson begins. A teaching plan that is consistent with the teacher's philosophy produces a coherent, manageable classroom; a plan that contradicts the teacher's beliefs produces friction the teacher will spend the lesson resolving.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can name the four curriculum decisions that function as management acts and give an example of each.
  • I can explain why curriculum planning is not separate from classroom management.
  • I can build a short teaching plan that is consistent with a stated philosophy.
  • I can identify the inconsistency between a described teacher's philosophy and their lesson plan.
  • I can plan one objective for three different classroom realities.

Try this at your practicum school​

Over one week, keep a brief note after each lesson: one curriculum decision you made (content, sequence, pace, or grouping) and what happened as a result. At the end of the week, write half a page on which decision made the most difference to how the lesson ran, and why.

Do not name the school or the teacher in your write-up.

Summative task​

Write 350 to 400 words on the following.

A teacher in your practicum school says: "I don't have time to plan for every child. I just teach the lesson and manage the class."

Write a reply that takes the claim seriously, uses this topic's account of curriculum as a management act, and argues which single curriculum decision would most improve the management of a described lesson. Justify the choice by reference to the pupils or the setting.

Mini-rubric

CriterionWhat a strong response does

Further reading​

  • Wong, H. K., & Wong, R. T. (1998). The first days of school: How to be an effective teacher (rev. ed.). Harry K. Wong Publications. Chapter 4 describes the philosophy-consistent plan and its role in a manageable classroom.
