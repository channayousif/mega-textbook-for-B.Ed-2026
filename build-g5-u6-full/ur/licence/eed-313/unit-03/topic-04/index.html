Topic 3.4 - Routines for co-operation and collaboration

A real classroom situation​

Mr Sajid teaches Class 4 social studies in a government school in Quetta. His pupils work in groups of four. Each group has a reader, a writer, a reporter and a timekeeper. The pupils know what each role does, how to begin the task, how to check that everyone is contributing, and how to close the activity with a share. This topic is about the routines that make group work into collaborative learning, and about the difference between the two.

Explanation​

Topic 3.3 ended with a distinction: group work is pupils sitting together; collaborative learning is pupils working together toward a shared goal with individual accountability. This topic takes that distinction seriously and asks what routines are needed to make it real.

Co-operation and collaborative learning distinguished​

The figure above maps the structures this topic describes. The distinction matters because the routines for each are different.

Co-operation means pupils work in the same space, toward a shared task, helping each other as needed. The routine manages seating, noise and mutual help. Think-pair-share, round-robin and numbered heads are co-operative structures.

Collaborative learning adds individual accountability: every pupil has a role, every role is necessary, and the group cannot succeed unless every member contributes. The routine manages roles, task structure, participation checking and closure. The jigsaw method is a collaborative structure.

Common misconception. "Group work and collaborative learning are the same thing." They are not. Group work without individual accountability produces one pupil doing the work while the others watch. Collaborative learning requires a routine that makes every role necessary and every contribution visible.

The collaborative routine: set up, run, close​

The figure above shows the three phases of a collaborative task. Each phase has its own routine.

The set-up routine assigns roles, states the task, checks that every pupil understands their role, and sets a time bound. A strong set-up routine uses a roles card (reader, writer, reporter, timekeeper) and a quick check: "Readers, point to your text. Writers, show your clipboard." Without this routine, the first five minutes of group work are spent negotiating who does what.

The run routine governs how the group works: how to take turns, how to check that everyone is contributing, what to do when the group is stuck. The teacher's role during the run phase is to monitor participation, not to teach content - a group that is off task needs a management response, not a content explanation. Evertson and Emmer (2009) stress that the run phase is where most collaborative learning fails, because the teacher confuses "pupils are talking" with "pupils are learning" and does not check that every role is being filled.

The close routine governs how the activity ends: a whole-class share, a reflection on how the group worked, a pack-away sequence. A strong close routine includes a reflection question: "What did your group do well? What would you change next time?" Without it, the learning stays in the group and does not transfer to the whole class.

Matching structure to group size and noise cost​

The figure above maps four co-operative structures against the group size and noise cost each one carries. Think-pair-share is quiet and works in any size of class; the jigsaw is louder and needs groups of four to six; round-robin is structured and works when the task has a clear sequence. The management question is: given this class, this content, and the noise I can afford, which structure gives me the most collaboration for the least management cost?

Marzano, Marzano and Pickering (2003) found that the effectiveness of group work depends less on the structure chosen and more on the routines that carry it. A think-pair-share with a practised routine produces more learning than a jigsaw with a routine that has not been taught.

Activity: Design a collaborative routine​

Work in groups of four. Take the following task:

Class 5 science: pupils will investigate which material conducts electricity, in groups of four.

Now design the full routine: the set-up (roles, task statement, time bound), the run (how to check participation, what to do when stuck), and the close (share, reflection, pack-away). Allow 25 minutes. Then present your routine and identify one moment where a group might slip from "collaborative" to "one person doing the work", and explain how your routine prevents it.

Check your understanding​

  1. State the difference between co-operation and collaborative learning, and give one example of each. (Remember)
  2. A teacher puts pupils in groups and gives them a task, but one pupil does all the work while the others watch. Using this topic's account, explain what routine is missing. (Analyze)
  3. Name the three phases of a collaborative task and the routine each one requires. (Remember)
  4. A teacher says, "I cannot do group work; there are sixty pupils." Using one idea from this topic, propose a workable first step. (Apply)
  5. Evaluate the claim that "think-pair-share is the best group-work structure." Refer to the match between structure and group size in your answer. (Evaluate)

Summary​

Co-operation and collaborative learning are different, and the routines for each are different. A collaborative task has three phases - set up, run and close - and each phase needs its own routine. The effectiveness of group work depends less on the structure chosen and more on the routines that carry it.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can distinguish co-operation from collaborative learning and give an example of each.
  • I can describe the set-up, run and close routines for a collaborative task.
  • I can explain why individual accountability is what makes group work into collaborative learning.
  • I can match a co-operative structure to a described group size and noise constraint.
  • I can design a collaborative routine for a described lesson.

Try this at your practicum school​

Over two lessons that use group work, keep a tally of the time spent on each phase: set up, run and close. At the end, calculate the proportion and write half a page on which phase dominates and whether that dominance fitted the task.

Do not name the school or the teacher in your write-up.

Summative task​

Write 400 to 450 words on the following.

A teacher in a multigrade classroom (Classes 3 and 4 in one room) wants to use collaborative learning but says, "I do not have time to teach routines for group work." Using this topic's account of the set-up, run and close routines, argue which single routine would most improve the quality of group work in the described room, and justify the choice by reference to the constraint a multigrade room imposes.
