Topic 4.1 - What community means for a school

A real classroom situation​

Miss Farzana teaches Class 3 in a government school in Ghotki. Her classroom does not end at the door. Parents visit once a term. A local shopkeeper lends materials for science lessons. The imam at the nearby mosque talks to the class about respect. This topic is about what community means for a school, and about the difference between asking the community to help and genuinely involving it.

Explanation​

The guide treats community not as a vague aspiration but as a set of relationships that a school can build, neglect or damage. The management question is: who is part of the community that supports learning, and how does the school relate to them?

The circles of community​

The figure above maps the circles of community around one classroom. The innermost circle is the classroom itself - the relationships between teacher and pupils and among pupils. The next circle is the school - the headteacher, other teachers, the support staff. The next is the parents and families. The outermost is the local community - neighbours, local leaders, businesses, places of worship.

Each circle has a different relationship with the classroom, and the management demand of each is different. The classroom circle is managed directly; the school circle through collaboration with colleagues; the parent circle through communication and invitation; the community circle through partnership and reciprocity. Wong and Wong (1998) argue that a well-managed classroom is one where all four circles are deliberately tended, not just the innermost one.

Participation and involvement distinguished​

The figure above maps the distinction this topic makes. The terms are often used interchangeably, but they mean different things, and the difference matters for management.

Participation means being present: attending a school event, signing a form, contributing money, showing up. A parent who attends a parent-teacher meeting is participating.

Involvement means having a voice in what happens: helping to decide the agenda, contributing to a classroom activity, being asked for advice and acting on it. A parent who helps plan the meeting is involved.

Marzano, Marzano and Pickering (2003) found that the quality of a school's relationship with its community depends less on how many people participate and more on how many are genuinely involved. A school with fifty parents at a meeting but no voice in what happens has participation without involvement. A school with ten parents who help make decisions has involvement, and involvement is what changes the classroom.

Common misconception. "Community involvement means asking parents for money." It does not. Asking for money is a transaction; involvement is a relationship. A parent who raises funds is participating; a parent who helps decide how the funds are spent is involved.

Typical practices of community participation​

The guide describes several typical practices, and the management demand of each:

  • Parent-teacher meetings - the most common practice, and the one most likely to become participation without involvement if the agenda is set entirely by the school.
  • Home visits - the teacher visits the family, which builds a relationship that no meeting can replace. The management demand is time and cultural sensitivity.
  • Community events - the school hosts an event and the community attends. The management demand is planning, and the risk is that the event becomes a performance rather than a partnership.
  • Community members in the classroom - a parent or local professional contributes to a lesson. The management demand is preparation: the visitor needs to know what to do, and the pupils need to know why they are there.

Activity: Participation or involvement?​

Work in pairs. Read the following five scenarios and classify each as participation or involvement. For each, explain your reasoning in one sentence.

  1. A parent attends a parent-teacher meeting where the teacher reports on pupil progress.
  2. A parent helps design the science fair and judges the entries.
  3. The school asks each family to contribute towards a new water tap.
  4. A local farmer talks to the class about how crops grow, at the teacher's invitation.
  5. A parent sits on the school management committee and votes on the budget.

Allow 15 minutes. Then compare with another pair and identify one scenario where the boundary is unclear, and explain why.

Check your understanding​

  1. Name the four circles of community around a classroom, starting from the innermost. (Remember)
  2. A teacher says, "We had a great community event; two hundred parents came." Using this topic's account, explain what that claim might overlook. (Analyze)
  3. State the difference between participation and involvement, and give one example of each. (Remember)
  4. A headteacher says, "We do not need community involvement; we need the parents to pay their fees." Evaluate that claim using this topic's account of what involvement achieves. (Evaluate)
  5. For a described school that wants to move from participation to involvement, propose one specific first step and explain why it is realistic. (Apply)

Summary​

Community is not a single thing but a set of circles around a classroom, each with a different relationship to the school. Participation is being present; involvement is having a voice. The quality of a school's community relationships depends less on how many people participate and more on how many are genuinely involved.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can name the four circles of community around a classroom.
  • I can distinguish participation from involvement and give an example of each.
  • I can explain why involvement matters more than participation for learning.
  • I can classify a described practice as participation or involvement.
  • I can propose a realistic first step towards greater community involvement.

Try this at your practicum school​

Over one week, note every interaction between the school and the community (parents, local leaders, visitors). Classify each as participation or involvement, and write half a page on which dominates and what the cost is for the school's relationship with its community.

Do not name the school or the teacher in your write-up.

Summative task​

Write 350 to 400 words on the following.

A teacher in your practicum school says, "I do not have time for community involvement; I just teach the children." Using this topic's account of what community means and the distinction between participation and involvement, argue which single practice would most improve the relationship between the described school and its community, and justify the choice by reference to the pupils or the setting.

Further reading​

  • Wong, H. K., & Wong, R. T. (1998). The first days of school: How to be an effective teacher (rev. edn.). Harry K. Wong Publications. Chapter 8 describes building relationships with parents and the wider community.
  • Marzano, R. J., Marzano, J. S., & Pickering, D. J. (2003). Classroom management that works: Research-based strategies for every teacher. ASCD. Chapter 7 covers parent and community involvement.
  • Henley, M. R. (2009). Introduction to proactive classroom management. In Classroom management: A proactive approach. Pearson. Chapter 5 frames community relationships as a proactive management strategy.
