Topic 1.2 - What classroom management is, and is not

A real classroom situation​

Two Class 5 teachers in the same school in Multan manage their classrooms very differently. Mrs Ahsan spends the first week of term establishing routines: how to enter, where to put homework, what to do when you have a question. She practises them until they are automatic. By October, the class runs itself for long stretches while she works with a small group. Mrs Bashir spends the first week on rules and consequences, and spends October responding to the breaches. Both care about their pupils. Only one of them is managing the classroom in the sense this course uses the term.

Explanation​

The guide defines the best-managed classroom as one "in which each learner is effectively engaged in constructing knowledge" (ClassroomMgmt_Sept13.txt:128-136). That definition is the spine of this whole course, and it is narrower and more demanding than most people assume.

Classroom management as maximising learning​

Classroom management, as this course uses the phrase, is the set of teacher actions whose purpose is to create the conditions in which student learning can happen and to keep those conditions in place. Notice what that definition puts at the centre: not order, not quiet, not compliance, but learning. Order and quiet are sometimes useful means to that end; they are not the end itself.

Marzano, Marzano and Pickering (2003) found that the strongest single predictor of pupil learning was not the severity of the rules but the quality of the teacher's management of time, space, transitions and engagement. In other words, the teacher who manages the classroom well is the teacher who has arranged things so that more pupils are actually doing more of the intellectual work for more of the time.

This is a demanding standard. A silent class in which every pupil is copying from the board is orderly but not necessarily well managed, because copying is not the same as learning. A class in which pupils are arguing about a problem in small groups is noisy but may be very well managed, because the noise is the sound of the intellectual work happening.

How a personal philosophy of teaching shapes management beliefs​

The guide asks a question most training programmes skip: how does your personal philosophy about teaching and learning affect your beliefs about classroom management? (ClassroomMgmt_Sept13.txt:170-172). The answer is that it determines what you treat as the problem.

If you believe learning is the transfer of knowledge from teacher to pupil, then the management problem is keeping attention on the teacher, and a pupil looking away is the thing to correct. If you believe learning is the pupil's active construction, then the management problem is keeping the task demanding enough that construction can happen, and a pupil sitting quietly is not necessarily a pupil learning.

Neither philosophy is wrong, but each produces a different classroom. The guide's point is not that you must pick one. It is that you must know which one is driving your management at any given moment, because a teacher who has not chosen a philosophy manages by habit, and habit is shaped by how they were taught, not by what their current pupils need.

Wong and Wong (1998) put this practically: a teacher with a consistent, conscious management plan spends the first weeks of term teaching the plan, and the rest of the year teaching the curriculum. A teacher without one spends the whole year re-establishing control.

What happens in a well-managed classroom​

A well-managed classroom has a handful of observable features, and they are about learning, not about silence:

  • Routines are in place and practised. Pupils know what to do when they enter, when they need help, when they finish early, and the teacher does not have to give the instruction more than once. Evertson and Emmer (2009) treat this as the single most productive management investment a teacher makes in the first weeks.
  • The intellectual demand is high and the disruption is low. Pupils are doing work that requires thought, and the transitions between activities are smooth enough that little time is lost.
  • The teacher is watching. Marzano, Marzano and Pickering (2003) use the term "withitness" for a teacher's accurate awareness of what is happening across the whole room and their willingness to act on it early.
  • The environment supports the learning. The physical and social arrangements, which Topic 1.4 examines, are set up to make the intended learning easier, not harder.

Common misconception. "A well-managed classroom is one where nothing unexpected happens." The opposite is closer to the truth. A well-managed classroom is one in which the teacher has built enough routine and enough trust that unexpected things can be handled without the learning falling apart. Surprises are a feature of classrooms; a teacher's management is measured by how they respond, not by whether they prevent every one.

How classroom discipline and classroom management differ​

Discipline and classroom management are not the same thing, and confusing them is one of the most common mistakes a new teacher makes. The figure above maps the difference.

Classroom management is proactive. It is the teacher's work before and during learning to create the conditions in which learning can happen: the routines, the seating, the pacing, the clarity of the task. It acts on the environment and on the structure of the lesson.

Classroom discipline is reactive. It is the teacher's response after a pupil has breached a norm: the reminder, the consequence, the conversation. It acts on the individual pupil's behaviour.

The purpose differs too. The purpose of management is to maximise learning time. The purpose of discipline is to restore a norm that has been broken. Success in management looks like a class that runs itself for long stretches. Success in discipline looks like a pupil who understands what went wrong and is trusted to do better.

This matters because a teacher who relies almost entirely on discipline is a teacher who spends their day reacting. A teacher who invests in management spends the first weeks building routines and the rest of the year teaching. The guide is explicit: "In its narrowest sense, classroom management is defined in terms of disciplining and controlling students. However, this course places the goal of student learning at the heart of classroom management" (ClassroomMgmt_Sept13.txt:128-136).

Common misconception. "Discipline and management are the same thing." They are not. Discipline is a small and necessary part of management, but a classroom can be tightly disciplined and poorly managed if the pupils are quiet but not learning.

Activity: Management or discipline?​

Work in pairs. Below are eight short descriptions of a teacher's action. For each one, decide whether it is primarily an act of management (proactive, acts on the learning environment) or of discipline (reactive, acts on a pupil's breach), and be ready to defend the placement.

  1. The teacher practises the "hands up" routine until every pupil can do it without being asked.
  2. The teacher moves a pupil to a seat near the front after talking during group work.
  3. The teacher arranges the desks in clusters before a discussion lesson.
  4. The teacher stays behind to talk to a pupil who refused to hand in homework.
  5. The teacher writes the lesson's learning target on the board before starting.
  6. The teacher revokes break time after shouting in the corridor.
  7. The teacher prepares a "finished early" task for pupils who work quickly.
  8. The teacher sends a note home after a pupil is repeatedly late.

Allow 15 minutes. Then compare with another pair and find one placement you disagree on. Spend your last five minutes on that disagreement.

Check your understanding​

  1. In your own words, state the difference between classroom management and classroom discipline, and give one example of each. (Understand)
  2. A teacher says, "My class is silent and on task, so my management is excellent." Explain what that claim assumes about the purpose of management, and what it leaves out. (Analyze)
  3. Name the four observable features of a well-managed classroom described in this topic. (Remember)
  4. A new teacher spends most of every lesson responding to misbehaviour. Using the management/discipline distinction, explain what that teacher is likely not investing in. (Apply)
  5. Explain how a teacher who believes "learning is the teacher transferring knowledge" and a teacher who believe "learning is the pupil constructing meaning" would manage the same transition between activities differently. (Analyze)

Summary​

Classroom management is the proactive work of creating and maintaining the conditions in which student learning happens. It is not the same as discipline, which is the reactive response to a breach of norms. A teacher's personal philosophy of teaching shapes what they treat as the management problem. A well-managed classroom is marked by practised routines, high intellectual demand, teacher withitness, and an environment that supports the learning, not by silence or by the absence of surprises.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can define classroom management as maximising student learning and distinguish it from discipline.
  • I can explain how a teacher's philosophy of teaching shapes their management choices.
  • I can name the observable features of a well-managed classroom.
  • I can classify a described teacher action as management or as discipline and justify the placement.
  • I can reject the claim that a silent classroom is necessarily a well-managed one.

Try this at your practicum school​

Over two full lessons taught by the same teacher, keep a tally of every management or discipline move you observe. Mark each one M or D. At the end, calculate the ratio and write half a page on what that ratio suggests about where the teacher invests their management effort, and whether the balance fitted the lessons you watched.

Do not name the teacher or the school in your write-up.

Summative task​

Write 350 to 400 words on the following.

A teacher in your practicum school says: "I don't have a management problem. When someone misbehaves I deal with it, and they stop."

Write a reply that takes the claim seriously, explains what the management/discipline distinction reveals about it, and uses one concrete classroom example of your own to show what a greater investment in proactive management would change.

Further reading​

  • Marzano, R. J., Marzano, J. S., & Pickering, D. J. (2003). Classroom management that works: Research-based strategies for every teacher. ASCD. Chapters 1 and 2 set out the research case for management centred on learning and the role of teacher "withitness".
  • Wong, H. K., & Wong, R. T. (1998). The first days of school: How to be an effective teacher (rev. ed.). Harry K. Wong Publications. Chapters 1 to 3 describe the management-plan approach and the investment of the first weeks.
  • Evertson, C. M., & Emmer, E. T. (2009). Classroom management for elementary teachers (8th ed.). Pearson. Chapter 2 covers the distinction between proactive management and reactive discipline.
