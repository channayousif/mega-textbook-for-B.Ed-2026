Topic 1.3 - Reading a real classroom

A real classroom situation​

You have been asked to observe a Class 4 lesson in a government school in Hyderabad. You have a notebook, a chair at the back, and forty minutes. The teacher has not told you what to look for. If you try to write down everything, you will capture nothing useful. If you write down only your impressions, you will capture your own assumptions, not the classroom. This topic gives you a simple protocol for seeing what is actually there.

Explanation​

The guide asks you to spend six hours observing a classroom, in and out of class (ClassroomMgmt_Sept13.txt:174-175). That observation is only as good as the discipline you bring to it. Without a protocol, an observer sees what they expect. With one, they see what is there.

Classroom observation: what to look for​

Classroom observation, in this course, is the structured act of watching a classroom with a specific focus, recording what you see in a consistent format, and keeping a hard line between what you saw and what you inferred from it.

The focus comes first. You cannot observe "the whole classroom" any more than you can photograph a city by pointing the camera in every direction at once. Before you sit down, decide what you are looking for. This topic gives you four lenses, and a sound observation uses at least two in a single visit:

  1. The physical environment. Where the furniture is, where the teacher usually stands, what is on the walls, where the resources are, how the pupils are grouped, and what the traffic paths look like when the class moves.
  2. The social environment. Who talks to whom, how the teacher addresses the class, what happens when a pupil disagrees, whether every pupil is visibly part of the room's life.
  3. Routines in action. How the lesson starts and ends, what pupils do when they need help, how transitions between activities are handled, and whether the teacher has to give the same instruction twice.
  4. Teacher withitness. Where the teacher's eyes are, whether they notice off-task behaviour early, and what they do when they do.

Evertson and Poole (IRIS Center) stress that the first question in any observation is not "was this a good lesson?" but "what was actually happening here?" Judgement comes later, and only after the evidence has been recorded.

Collecting and recording classroom data​

A usable observation record has two columns, and the discipline is to keep them separate:

What I saw (observation)What I think it means (inference)
The teacher stood at the front for the first 12 minutes.The teacher was in control of the pace.
Three pupils in the back row talked throughout group work.Those pupils were not engaged by the task.
The teacher moved to the back row and stood there.The teacher used proximity to redirect them.

The left column is evidence anyone could verify. The right column is your interpretation, and it may be wrong. The three pupils in the back row may have been finishing the task together; you cannot know until you ask. Keeping the two columns apart stops you from confusing what you saw with what you hope it means.

Marzano, Marzano and Pickering (2003) describe this as the difference between data and conclusion, and treat the discipline of recording both as a core professional skill. An observer who skips the left column and writes only conclusions is an observer who will not learn from the visit.

Features of classroom management: the physical environment​

The physical environment is the set of material features a teacher can arrange: furniture, displays, resources, lighting, and the use of wall and floor space. The figure at the start of this topic maps them on a floor plan.

Each of these carries management weight. Sightlines determine whether the teacher can see every pupil and every pupil can see the board. Traffic paths determine how smoothly the class moves between activities, and a badly placed bookshelf can turn a two-minute transition into a five-minute disruption. Display walls signal what the room values: a wall of pupils' work says something different from a wall of commercial posters, and a bare wall says something different again. Resource corners determine whether pupils can get what they need without asking the teacher, which is the difference between a room that runs itself and one that depends on the teacher for every material.

In a low-resource Pakistani government school, many of these features cannot be changed: the furniture is fixed, the walls are cracked, there is no budget for new displays. The management question is not "how do I get the ideal room?" but "given this room, what can I change this week?" Topic 1.4 takes that question up in detail.

Features of classroom management: the social environment​

The social environment is the set of relationships and norms that govern how people in the room treat each other: between teacher and pupils, among pupils, and between the class and any outsider who enters.

The features that carry management weight are harder to see than the physical ones, but they are more powerful:

  • Participation patterns. Who gets called on, who never speaks, and whether the teacher knows the difference. A room in which the same six hands go up for every question is a room in which the teacher is managing the visible few and losing the invisible many.
  • Norms of help. Whether pupils are permitted and expected to help each other, or whether helping is treated as cheating. Evertson and Poole (IRIS Center) treat the establishment of clear norms and expectations as the foundation of the social environment.
  • Responses to error. Whether a wrong answer is treated as a useful event or as a humiliation. A room in which it is safe to be wrong is a room in which learning can happen, because learning requires the risk of being wrong in front of someone.
  • Belonging. Whether every pupil has a visible place in the room's life: a name the teacher uses, a task that matters, a peer group that expects them.

Challenges a teacher must negotiate​

Classroom challenges are the recurring frictions that make management harder than the clean routines in a textbook suggest. The guide names the ones a teacher in the Pakistani system meets often:

  • Multigrade and overcrowded rooms. A teacher managing two grades in one room, or sixty pupils in a room built for thirty, is managing a different problem from the one a well-resourced single-grade textbook assumes.
  • Resource scarcity. When there is one textbook between three pupils, the management of the textbook, not just the teaching of the content, becomes the teacher's problem.
  • Diverse preparation. Pupils entering the same class with very different prior knowledge require the teacher to manage several levels of engagement at once.
  • Unpredictability. The event no plan anticipates: a pupil who arrives distressed, a surprise inspection, a power cut in the middle of a carefully prepared activity.

The point of naming these is not to discourage you. It is to make the case that management is not a set of tricks that work everywhere. It is the capacity to read a specific classroom and respond to what is actually happening in it, which is exactly what this topic's observation protocol is designed to build.

Activity: Observe the described classroom​

Work in groups of three. Read the following description of a Class 5 room, then complete the two-column record the topic describes. Aim for at least four rows of evidence in the left column and a genuine inference in the right column for each.

The room holds 44 pupils at benches meant for 30. The teacher, Miss Naveed, stands at the front near a worn blackboard for most of the lesson. There is one textbook for every four pupils; she reads from hers while they follow. The back two benches are in shadow because the one working tube light is at the front. The walls are bare except for a faded map of Pakistan. When a pupil at the back asks a question, Miss Naveed does not hear it. When two pupils in the middle begin to talk, she pauses, looks at them, and continues. No pupil speaks unless asked.

Allow 20 minutes. Then compare your record with another group and find one row where your left columns agree but your right columns differ. That disagreement is the most useful thing in the room, so spend your last five minutes on it.

Check your understanding​

  1. Name the four observation lenses this topic gives, and explain why an observer should use at least two in a single visit. (Remember)
  2. A classmate writes in her observation notebook: "The teacher clearly does not care about the back benches." Explain why that sentence belongs in the right column, not the left, and rewrite it as a defensible inference. (Analyze)
  3. List three features of the physical environment that carry management weight, and for each, state one thing a teacher in a low-resource government school could change this week. (Understand)
  4. The description in the Activity says no pupil speaks unless asked. Give two different inferences that observation could support, and state which further evidence would help you choose between them. (Analyze)
  5. Explain what "withitness" means, and describe one concrete behaviour from the Activity description that shows its absence. (Apply)

Summary​

A classroom observation is only as good as its protocol. Decide what to look for before you sit down, record what you actually saw in a left column and what you think it means in a right column, and keep the two apart. The physical environment (sightlines, traffic paths, displays, resources) and the social environment (participation patterns, norms of help, responses to error, belonging) are the two landscapes a managed classroom shapes. The recurring challenges, from overcrowding to unpredictability, are the reason management is the capacity to read a specific room and respond to what is actually happening in it.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can explain why an observation protocol matters and name the four lenses.
  • I can keep observation and inference apart in a two-column record.
  • I can identify the physical and social features of a described classroom that carry management weight.
  • I can complete the five-step observation cycle on a described room.
  • I can name at least three recurring classroom challenges a Pakistani government-school teacher is likely to face.

Try this at your practicum school​

Carry out one full observation using the five-step cycle in the figure. Choose the physical environment as your focus for the first visit. Before you sit down, decide on two specific things to look for (for example, sightlines and traffic paths). Record a two-column record with at least six rows of evidence. After the lesson, write half a page on one change the teacher could make this week, given the room they actually have.

Do not approach the teacher during the lesson, and do not name the school or the teacher in your write-up.

Summative task​

Write 400 to 450 words on the following.

Take the classroom described in the Activity (or, if you prefer, a classroom you have observed during practicum). Write a two-column record with at least four rows of evidence and a genuine inference for each. Then select the single feature of that classroom that you judge to carry the most management weight, and argue for one concrete change a teacher could make this week. Justify the change by reference to either the physical or the social environment.
