Topic 1.1 - How humans learn: three theories

A real classroom situation​

Mr Karim teaches Class 3 Urdu in a government primary school in Ghotki. On Monday he introduces a new letter. He says the sound, the class repeats it, he praises the rows that say it clearly, and the rows that falter try again. On Wednesday he introduces the same letter, but this time he asks the class what words they already know that begin with its sound, writes their suggestions on the board, and groups them by pattern. On Friday he gives each pair a set of picture cards and asks them to sort the pictures into "starts with the letter" and "does not start with the letter", then explain their grouping to another pair.

Three lessons, three different ideas about what learning is. This topic names those ideas.

Explanation​

Before we name the theories, notice something about Mr Karim's week. He did not contradict himself. Each lesson described a real part of how learning happens: sometimes a pupil needs to practise a response until it sticks, sometimes they need to connect new material to what they already know, and sometimes they need to build the idea for themselves. The three theories below describe those three parts. They compete only if you insist that learning is one single thing.

Surfacing what you already believe​

You arrived in this classroom with years of experience of being taught. That experience has given you beliefs about how learning works, and most of them are half-true. Common ones: "children learn by doing", "practice makes perfect", "a quiet class is a learning class". None of these is wrong. None is the whole story.

The reason to surface your beliefs now is that a teacher who has not examined them teaches on autopilot: they manage the classroom for the kind of learning they assume is happening, and miss the rest. Each theory below gives you a lens. The skill is knowing which lens a moment needs.

Behaviourism: learning as a change in visible behaviour​

Behaviourism says that learning is a change in observable behaviour produced by the interaction between a stimulus and a response, shaped by what follows the response. What happens inside the mind is set aside as unobservable; what matters is what the learner does, and whether that doing is reinforced or not.

In the classroom this looks like: clear modelling of a target response, repetition, immediate feedback, and reinforcement that makes the wanted response more likely to recur. Mr Karim's Monday lesson is a clean example: he models the sound, the class repeats it, he praises the correct response, and the rows that falter try again until they get it right.

The teacher's job, on this account, is to arrange the environment so the right responses are practised and reinforced and the wrong ones are not. Evertson and Emmer (2009) describe this as the backbone of classroom routines and procedures, where consistent practice of expected behaviour builds the automatic responses that keep a classroom running.

Behaviourism is strongest where the learning target is a repeatable skill or a clear procedure: letter formation, multiplication tables, lab safety steps, the routine for entering a classroom. It is weakest where the target is understanding, interpretation, or the ability to apply an idea in a new setting.

Cognitivism: learning as the building and reorganising of mental structures​

Cognitivism says that learning is not a change in behaviour but a change in the way information is organised inside the mind. The learner receives information, processes it against what is already stored, and reorganises those internal structures. Behaviour may or may not change as a result; the learning itself is the reorganisation.

In the classroom this looks like: activating what pupils already know before introducing something new, organising material so its structure is visible, managing the limited space of working memory, and giving learners strategies for storing and retrieving what they learn. Mr Karim's Wednesday lesson does this: he activates prior knowledge ("what words begin with this sound?"), makes the letter-sound pattern visible on the board, and groups examples so the structure can be seen.

The teacher's job, on this account, is to present material in a way the mind can actually process: not too much at once, clearly structured, and connected to existing understanding. Ertmer and Newby (1993) describe the shift from behaviourism to cognitivism as a shift in focus from what the teacher does to the learner to what the learner does with what is presented.

Cognitivism is strongest where the learning target is understanding a concept, seeing a relationship, or applying a known procedure to a familiar type of problem. It is weakest where the target requires the learner to build a genuinely new idea that does not yet connect to anything in their experience.

Constructivism: learning as the building of personal meaning​

Constructivism says that learning is the active construction of meaning by the learner. Knowledge is not transferred intact from teacher to pupil; each learner builds their own understanding by acting on material, testing it against experience, and revising it when it does not hold. Other people, including the teacher, are a resource in that process, not its source.

In the classroom this looks like: a task that requires the learner to do the intellectual work, opportunities to talk through emerging understanding, comparison of one learner's construction with another's, and revision in light of evidence. Mr Karim's Friday lesson does this: the pairs sort picture cards, build a grouping rule, and explain it to another pair, who may have grouped differently.

The teacher's job, on this account, is to design the task and the social conditions in which construction happens, then listen carefully enough to hear what the learner is actually building, which is not always what you intended. Ertmer and Newby (1993) stress that on this account the learner's prior knowledge and the social context of the activity are not add-ons; they are the mechanism by which learning occurs.

Constructivism is strongest where the learning target is deep understanding, the ability to transfer an idea to an unfamiliar context, or the capacity to work with others on an open task. It is weakest where time is short, the target is a fixed procedure, or the learner does not yet have the prior knowledge the construction requires.

Why all three have classroom validity​

The single most important idea in this topic is that the three theories describe different parts of the same process, not competing claims about the whole of it. Ertmer and Newby (1993) map them along a continuum: behaviourism best explains the acquisition of foundational responses and procedures, cognitivism best explains the organisation and storage of information, and constructivism best explains the transfer and application of understanding to new situations.

A teacher who relies on behaviourism alone produces pupils who can perform a procedure but cannot explain when to use it. A teacher who relies on constructivism alone asks pupils to "discover" things that are more efficiently told. A fluent teacher moves between the three as the learning target demands: drills a routine until it is automatic (behaviourism), makes the structure of a new topic visible (cognitivism), and then sets a task that requires pupils to use that structure in a context the teacher did not fully specify (constructivism).

Common misconception. "Behaviourism was disproved and constructivism replaced it." It was not, and it did not. What happened is that researchers kept finding kinds of learning the earlier account could not explain well, and added a new lens. The earlier lens still works for what it was built for. A well-managed classroom uses all three.

Activity: One lesson, three ways​

Work in groups of three. Take the following short lesson description:

The teacher introduces the concept of "halves" to Class 2. She has a chart, a set of paper shapes, and a basket of real fruit.

Now plan this lesson three ways, one from each theoretical stance. For each version, decide: what the teacher does first, what the pupils do, and what success looks like. Allow 25 minutes. Then, as a group, identify one thing that all three versions have in common and one thing that genuinely differs.

Check your understanding​

  1. In one sentence each, state what behaviourism, cognitivism and constructivism say learning is. (Remember)
  2. A friend says, "Constructivism is just the newest theory, so it is the best one." Using the idea of a continuum, explain what is wrong with that claim. (Understand)
  3. A teacher drills multiplication tables every morning for ten minutes. Name which theory this serves best, and explain why it is a defensible use of that theory. (Apply)
  4. Look at Mr Karim's Friday lesson. Identify one moment that is best read through a constructivist lens, and explain what the teacher would have to do differently to teach the same content from a behaviourist stance. (Analyze)
  5. Give one example from your own schooling of a task that asked you to build understanding yourself rather than repeat a modelled response. Name the theory it served. (Apply)

Summary​

Behaviourism treats learning as a change in visible behaviour shaped by reinforcement; it is the right lens for building automatic routines and practised skills. Cognitivism treats learning as the reorganisation of mental structures; it is the right lens for making new information processable and connectable. Constructivism treats learning as the active construction of personal meaning; it is the right lens for deep understanding and transfer. The three describe different parts of one process, and a well-managed classroom draws on all three as the learning target demands.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can state what each of the three theories says learning is, without mixing them up.
  • I can explain why the three theories complement rather than replace each other.
  • I can recognise which theory a described classroom moment is drawing on.
  • I can reject the claim that constructivism has simply made the other two obsolete.
  • I can give a classroom example where each theory is the most useful lens.

Try this at your practicum school​

Over one full lesson, keep a tally of every management move the teacher makes. For each one, mark it B (it shapes a visible response), C (it makes the structure of the material visible or connects to prior knowledge), or K (it asks pupils to build understanding themselves). At the end, note which letter dominates and write two sentences on whether that dominance fitted the lesson's learning target.

Do not mark anything the teacher does that is not a management move. And do not discuss your tally with the teacher unless they ask.

Summative task​

Write 350 to 400 words on the following.

Describe a single topic you might teach in a lower-primary class. Explain how you would teach it once from a behaviourist stance and once from a constructivist stance. Then argue which of two would be more appropriate for a class you know, giving one concrete reason drawn from the pupils or the setting.
