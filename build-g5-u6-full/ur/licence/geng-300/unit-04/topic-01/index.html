Topic 4.1 - Professional writing in practice

A real classroom situation​

Miss Hira has just started her first teaching job at a government high school in Hyderabad. In her first week she is asked to write four things: an email to the headteacher requesting leave, a memo to staff about a changed timetable, a short report on a school-cleanliness drive, and a formal letter to a parents' association. She knows English well, but she has never had to choose between an email, a memo, a report and a letter. This topic is about those choices.

Explanation​

Professional writing is not about using long words or complex sentences. It is about choosing the right format for the right purpose and audience, and then following the conventions that readers expect. Hutchinson and Waters (1987) describe English for professional purposes as a set of genres: each genre has a typical structure, a typical tone and a typical set of phrases, and learning the genre means learning to produce writing that fits the reader's expectations.

The figure above maps the writing process you will use for every format this topic covers. It is rare to get a professional document right in a first draft. The process moves from planning (what is this for, who is it for) through drafting, revising (rethinking structure and content) and editing (fixing grammar, punctuation and tone) to a final version.

Business emails​

A business email is the most common professional writing format today. It is used to request, inform, confirm or follow up, usually within an organisation or between organisations.

The structure of a business email is simple:

  • Subject line: short and specific. "Leave request for 23 September" is better than "Request".
  • Salutation: "Dear Mr Rashidi," or "Dear Colleagues," depending on the relationship.
  • Opening: state your purpose in the first sentence. "I am writing to request leave on 23 September for a medical appointment."
  • Body: give only the information the reader needs. One short paragraph is usually enough.
  • Closing: "Thank you for your time," or "I look forward to your response," followed by a sign-off ("Yours sincerely," for someone you do not know well; "Kind regards," for someone you do).
  • Signature: your name, role and contact details.

The tone of a business email is polite and direct. It is less formal than a letter but more formal than a text message. Avoid slang, emoticons and all-capital writing. Common phrases include "I am writing to…", "Could you please…", "I would appreciate it if…", and "Please find attached…".

Worked example before you write one. A poor version of Miss Hira's leave email reads: "Hi, I want leave on 23rd because I have a doctor appointment. Thanks, Hira." A better version reads: "Subject: Leave request for 23 September. Dear Mr Rashidi, I am writing to request leave on 23 September for a medical appointment. I have arranged for Mrs Kazi to cover my Classes 9 and 10 during my absence. Thank you for your consideration. Yours sincerely, Hira Ansari, Class 9 English." The second version is not longer because it tries to sound formal; it is longer because it gives the reader the information they need.

Memos and short reports​

A memo (short for memorandum) is an internal document used to inform or instruct people within the same organisation. A short report is used to present findings, often to a supervisor or a small committee. Both follow a clear structure.

The memo structure:

  • To, From, Date, Subject at the top.
  • Purpose: one sentence on why you are writing.
  • Background: the context the reader needs.
  • Action or information: what happened, what you found, or what you want the reader to do.
  • Next steps or deadline, if any.

The short report structure adds a little more:

  • Title and date.
  • Introduction: what the report is about and why it was written.
  • Findings: what you observed or discovered, often in short paragraphs or bullet points.
  • Conclusion and recommendation: what should happen next.

Hutchinson and Waters (1987) note that both genres reward clarity over decoration: short paragraphs, plain headings, and concrete detail. A memo that says "The cleanliness drive took place on 15 September. Sixty pupils and eight teachers participated. Three areas of the school were cleaned. The committee recommends a follow-up drive in November" is more useful than a vague, wordy paragraph.

Formal letters and applications​

A formal letter is used for communication outside the organisation or for official communication within it: letters of complaint, request, application or recommendation. Downes (2010) describes the formal letter as the format that still carries the most weight in professional settings in South Asia, where a printed and signed letter is often expected for job applications and official requests.

The formal letter structure:

  • Your address (top right), then the date.
  • The recipient's name and address (left).
  • Salutation: "Dear Sir/Madam," (when you do not know the name) or "Dear Mr Khan,".
  • Subject line (optional but common in Pakistani formal letters): "Subject: Application for the post of…"
  • Opening paragraph: state your purpose.
  • Body paragraphs: develop your point.
  • Closing paragraph: state what you hope will happen next.
  • Sign-off: "Yours faithfully," (when you wrote "Dear Sir/Madam") or "Yours sincerely," (when you used the person's name), then your signature and printed name.

A job application letter (cover letter) follows the same structure. Downes (2010) advises that a strong cover letter does not repeat the CV; it picks two or three points from the CV and explains why they matter for this specific post. "I have three years of teaching experience in a government school in Sindh, including two years managing a multi-grade classroom" is more convincing than "I am hardworking and honest."

Common misconception. "Professional writing means using complex vocabulary." It does not. Professional writing means matching the format, tone and level of detail to the reader and the purpose. A short, clear email is better professional writing than a long, vague letter. As Hutchinson and Waters (1987) put it, the reader is the judge of whether a piece of writing works, and the reader judges it by how easily they can find what they need.

Activity: Match the format to the situation​

Work in pairs. Read the following five situations and decide which format (email, memo, short report or formal letter) is most appropriate for each. For each situation, explain your choice in one sentence and draft the subject line or opening sentence. Allow 20 minutes. Then compare your choices with another pair and discuss any disagreements.

  1. You want to tell all staff that the staff meeting has moved from Monday to Wednesday.
  2. You are applying for a teaching post at a private school in Sukkur.
  3. You need to tell the headteacher that the science laboratory has no running water.
  4. The education district officer asks you to summarise enrolment trends in your school over the last three years.
  5. You want to request a meeting with the parents' association chair to discuss attendance.

Check your understanding​

  1. Name the four professional writing formats this topic covers and state the main use of each. (Remember)
  2. A colleague writes a business email with the subject line "Urgent!!! Please read now!!!!" and no clear opening sentence. Using this topic's account, explain what is wrong and how to fix it. (Analyze)
  3. Arrange these writing-process stages in the correct order and explain what happens at each: edit, draft, plan, revise, final version. (Understand)
  4. A pupil writes a job application letter that repeats every point from their CV. Using Downes (2010), explain what a stronger letter would do instead. (Apply)

Summary​

Professional writing is not one skill but several. A business email is short, direct and polite, with a clear subject line and opening. A memo informs or instructs internally. A short report presents findings with a clear structure. A formal letter is the most formal of the four and is used for official and external communication. All four rely on the same writing process: plan, draft, revise, edit, final version. The reader's needs, not the writer's wish to sound impressive, decide what good professional writing looks like.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can choose the right professional writing format for a described situation and explain why.
  • I can draft a business email with a clear subject line, opening, body and closing.
  • I can distinguish a memo from a short report and a formal letter.
  • I can move a piece of writing through the writing process from plan to final version.
  • I can identify and correct tone and structure problems in a sample professional document.

Try this at your practicum school​

Collect one example of professional writing used in the school: a letter, a notice, a report or an email (with permission, and with names removed). Analyse it using this topic's account: which format is it, does it follow the expected structure, is the tone right for the audience, and what is one thing you would change to make it clearer? Write half a page.

Do not name the school, the staff or the pupils in your write-up.

Summative task​

Write 350 to 400 words on the following.

You are a teacher at a government school in rural Sindh. The school has no computer lab, and the headteacher has asked you to write to the district education officer requesting two computers for the school. Choose the most appropriate professional writing format, draft the document, and explain in one paragraph why you chose that format and how your draft follows its conventions.
