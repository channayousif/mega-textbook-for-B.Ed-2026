Unit 4 teacher notes

These notes are for the instructor. They set out teaching strategies, sequencing, the misconceptions this unit is likely to meet, and the practical work that gives trainees something to do rather than only something to read. This unit carries no assessment items of its own; the question bank lives in unit-assessment.mdx.

Teaching strategies​

The guide lists four teaching strategies for this course: lecture, discussion, question-answer and brain storming. This unit uses all four, but leans heavily on discussion and brain storming because the content (register, tone, cultural awareness) is best learned by comparing examples and arguing about choices, not by memorising rules.

  • Lecture works best for the structural knowledge in Topic 4.1: the parts of a business email, a memo, a short report and a formal letter. A short lecture that names and shows the structure of each format, followed by a worked example, gives trainees the scaffold they need before they write.
  • Discussion works best for Topics 4.2 and 4.3, where there is rarely one right answer. Trainees learn more from arguing about whether a message is "too direct" or "too vague" and why, than from being told the answer.
  • Question-answer is useful for checking understanding of key terms (high-context vs low-context, positive vs negative face, register vs tone). Use it after a reading or an activity, not as the main event.
  • Brain storming is useful at the start of an adaptation task: before trainees rewrite a message, have the whole group list everything they know about the new audience. This makes the audience analysis visible and shared.

The guide also lists group work, group assignments, individual assignment and presentations as practical work. This unit's activities are designed for pairs and small groups, with one individual transfer task ("Try this at your practicum school") per topic.

sequencing​

The three topics are designed to be taught in order across Weeks 13 to 16 (derived):

  • Week 13 to 14 (Topic 4.1): professional writing formats and the writing process. Start with the formats trainees are most likely to use (business email, formal letter) before the less common ones (memo, short report). Build in at least one writing task where trainees draft, revise and edit a real document.
  • Week 15 (Topic 4.2): intercultural communication. This is the most abstract topic. Ground it in concrete examples from the trainees' own experience (staff meetings, parent interactions, differences between schools they have worked in). The frameworks (Hall, Brown and Levinson) are tools for interpreting experience, not facts to be memorised in isolation.
  • Week 16 (Topic 4.3): adapting communication. This topic depends on both previous topics, so it should come last. The summative task here is the unit's most integrative piece of writing: trainees analyse an audience, choose a register and tone, and rewrite a message.

If time is short, Topic 4.3 can be combined with the revision of Topic 4.2, but do not teach 4.3 before 4.2: the adaptation tasks assume trainees already understand high-/low-context communication and face-saving strategies.

Common misconceptions and how to handle them​

The content-spec names three misconceptions for this unit. All three are durable and worth addressing directly.

  1. "Professional writing means using complex vocabulary." Trainees often believe that formal = impressive = long words. Handle it by showing two versions of the same message, one over-formal and one plain, and asking trainees which a real reader would prefer. The note to parents in Topic 4.3 is a good example: the over-formal version is grammatically correct but fails with its audience.

  2. "One communication style works everywhere." Trainees may default to the formal-official style they associate with school. Handle it by giving them the same message to rewrite for three audiences and having them compare the results. The Activity in Topic 4.3 is designed for exactly this.

  3. "Cultural awareness is about memorising facts about other cultures." Trainees may try to learn a list of "Pakistani traits" or "British traits". Handle it by stressing, as Hall (1976) does, that these are tendencies on a spectrum, not fixed labels, and that the value of the framework is to notice and interpret differences, not to stereotype people.

Practical work​

The unit's practical work is built into each topic's Activity and Summative task. For a cohesive four-week block, consider the following sequence of assessed tasks:

  • Week 13: trainees draft a business email and a formal letter on a given scenario; peer review using a simple checklist (structure, tone, clarity).
  • Week 14: trainees write a short report on a described school activity; revise it after feedback.
  • Week 15: in small groups, trainees analyse a cross-cultural communication breakdown (the Activity in Topic 4.2) and present their analysis to another group.
  • Week 16: the Summative task from Topic 4.3 (analyse and rewrite a message for a new audience) serves as the unit's integrative practical assessment.

Across all four weeks, encourage trainees to collect real examples of professional writing and real cross-cultural interactions from their practicum schools. These real examples are the best material for discussion, and they make the frameworks concrete.
