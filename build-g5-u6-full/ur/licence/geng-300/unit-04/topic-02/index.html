Topic 4.2 - Intercultural communication

A real classroom situation​

Mr Omar teaches in a government school in Lahore. A visiting teacher from rural Sindh, Miss Zahra, joins the staff. In a staff meeting, the headteacher asks Miss Zahra to share her teaching experience. She speaks softly, begins with a long account of her village and her training, and never directly states her main point. Some colleagues look impatient. Later, Mr Omar asks a British-trained volunteer teacher the same question. The volunteer answers in two sentences: "I focus on group work. My pupils' test scores rose last term." Same question, two very different answers. This topic explains why.

Explanation​

Intercultural communication is the study of how people from different cultural backgrounds communicate with one another, and how cultural assumptions shape the way messages are sent, received and interpreted. English is a global language: the English spoken in Karachi, in London, in Lahore and in New York is not used in exactly the same way. Learning to communicate across these differences is a core skill for a teacher, who works with pupils, parents and colleagues from many backgrounds.

High-context and low-context communication​

The most influential account of cultural difference in communication comes from Hall (1976), who distinguished high-context and low-context communication.

In a high-context culture, much of the meaning is carried by the context: the relationship between the speakers, the shared history, the setting, what is left unsaid. The words themselves may be indirect and understated. The listener is expected to "read between the lines". Many South Asian, East Asian and Middle Eastern communication styles are relatively high-context. Miss Zahra's long, story-like answer in the staff meeting is a high-context style: the meaning is embedded in the account, and a listener who knows the style will hear humility, respect for the group and a gradual build to the point.

In a low-context culture, meaning is carried mainly by the words themselves. The speaker is expected to be explicit, direct and efficient, and the listener should not have to guess. Many Northern European, North Australian and North American communication styles are relatively low-context. The volunteer's two-sentence answer is a low-context style: the meaning is in the words, and nothing is left to inference.

Neither style is better. Hall (1976) is clear that the distinction is a tendency, not a rule: every person and every conversation falls somewhere on the spectrum between the two extremes. A Pakistani doctor explaining a diagnosis to an elderly patient may use a high-context style (gentle, indirect, relationship-focused), while the same doctor writing a referral letter to a specialist uses a low-context style (precise, technical, direct).

Why this matters for you. Pakistani classrooms are often high-context environments, but the English textbooks, exams and official documents pupils encounter are written in a relatively low-context style. A teacher who understands both can help pupils navigate between them.

Directness, politeness, and face-saving strategies​

A second dimension of intercultural variation is directness. In some cultures, saying "no" directly is honest and efficient. In other cultures, a direct "no" threatens the relationship and is avoided. Brown and Levinson (1987) describe face as the public self-image every person wants to maintain, and they distinguish two kinds:

  • Positive face: the want to be liked, appreciated and approved of.
  • Negative face: the want to be unimpeded, to have freedom of action without pressure.

A face-saving strategy is a way of speaking that protects either your own face or the other person's face. Common face-saving strategies in English include:

  • Hedging: softening a statement. "I wonder if you might…", "Perhaps we could…", "It might be a good idea to…".
  • Giving reasons before a refusal: "I would love to help, but I have a prior commitment" instead of "No, I cannot".
  • Using questions instead of commands: "Could you share the notes?" instead of "Give me the notes".
  • Apologising: "I'm sorry, but I won't be able to attend" signals respect for the other person's invitation.

These strategies are not weakness. Brown and Levinson (1987) argue that politeness is a fundamental part of human language, and every culture has its own system of it. The difference is in which face is prioritised and which strategies are considered polite. In many Pakistani settings, protecting the other person's positive face (showing warmth, respect, hospitality) is central. In some low-context Western settings, respecting negative face (not imposing) is more central.

Worked example before you analyse one. Consider a parent who arrives late to a meeting with a teacher. A relatively direct (low-context) response: "You are fifteen minutes late. We started without you." A face-saving (higher-context) response: "Thank you for coming. We have just started; please join us." Both communicate the same fact (the meeting has started), but the second protects the parent's positive face and preserves the relationship. A skilled teacher chooses the response the situation and the relationship call for.

Common misconception. "Cultural awareness is about memorising facts about other cultures." It is not. Facts are a starting point, but cultural awareness is the habit of noticing that your own way of communicating is not the only way, and of adjusting your style when the situation demands it. Hall (1976) warns against turning cultural tendencies into stereotypes: not every Pakistani is high-context, and not every British person is low-context. The value of the framework is that it gives you a way to notice and interpret differences, not a set of labels to apply to people.

Activity: Analyse a communication breakdown​

Work in groups of three. Read the following scenario and answer the questions below. Allow 25 minutes. Then present your analysis to another group.

A Pakistani teacher on an exchange programme in England emails her mentor: "I was wondering if it might be possible for you to observe my lesson next week, if it is not too much trouble. I completely understand if you are too busy. Perhaps another time would be better." The mentor reads the email as lacking confidence and assumes the teacher does not really want the observation.

  1. Using Hall's high-context / low-context framework, explain what is happening.
  2. Using Brown and Levinson's face framework, identify the face-saving strategies the teacher uses and how the mentor might interpret them.
  3. Rewrite the email so it is clearer for a low-context reader while still sounding polite.

Check your understanding​

  1. Explain the difference between high-context and low-context communication in your own words, and give one example of each. (Remember)
  2. A colleague says, "Low-context communication is just rude." Using Hall (1976), explain what is wrong with that claim. (Understand)
  3. A pupil writes in an essay, "Tell me the answer." Rewrite the sentence using two different face-saving strategies from this topic and explain which face each one protects. (Apply)
  4. Analyse the staff-meeting scenario at the start of this topic. Identify one high-context feature of Miss Zahra's answer and one low-context feature of the volunteer's answer, and explain how each fits their likely cultural framing. (Analyze)

Summary​

Intercultural communication is shaped by at least two major dimensions. Hall's (1976) high-context / low-context spectrum describes where meaning lives: in the shared context or in the words themselves. Brown and Levinson's (1987) face framework describes how politeness works: speakers use strategies to protect their own and others' positive and negative face. Neither framework ranks one style above the other; both give you tools to notice, interpret and adapt to the communication patterns you meet as a teacher.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can explain high-context and low-context communication and recognise each in a described example.
  • I can identify at least three face-saving strategies in a piece of spoken or written English.
  • I can analyse a described cross-cultural communication breakdown using Hall's and Brown and Levinson's frameworks.
  • I can rewrite a direct message in a more face-saving way without losing its meaning.
  • I can reject the claim that one communication style is simply better than another.

Try this at your practicum school​

Over one week, collect three examples of communication between the school and parents or the community (a letter home, a verbal request, a notice at the gate). For each, note whether the style is closer to high-context or low-context, and identify any face-saving strategies used. Write half a page on what you notice and whether the style fits the audience.

Do not name the school, the staff or the families in your write-up.

Summative task​

Write 400 to 450 words on the following.

A teacher in a government school in Punjab sends the following message to a non-governmental organisation (NGO) that works in a low-context, direct communication style: "We are very grateful for your continued support. If it is not too much trouble, and only if it suits your schedule, we were hoping you might consider visiting our school one day. We would not want to impose on your valuable time." The NGO replies that they are unsure whether the school actually wants the visit. Analyse the breakdown using this topic's two frameworks (Hall and Brown and Levinson), and rewrite the message so the request is clear and polite for a low-context reader.

Further reading​

  • Hall, E. T. (1976). Beyond Culture. Anchor Press. Chapters 6 and 7 set out the high-context / low-context distinction this topic draws on.
  • Brown, P., & Levinson, S. C. (1987). Politeness: Some Universals in Language Usage. Cambridge University Press. Chapter 1 introduces the face framework and the politeness strategies this topic describes.
