Topic 3.3 - Presentations and informal communication

A real classroom situation​

Miss Rashida is asked to give a fifteen-minute presentation on reading strategies to a group of primary-school teachers at a training in Larkana. She has never given a formal presentation before. She wonders how to choose what to say, whether to use slides, what to do if someone asks a question she cannot answer, and how to keep to fifteen minutes. This topic walks through the whole process, from preparation to follow-up. It then covers the informal side of communication: the small talk, networking and conversational moves that make professional relationships work.

Explanation​

Presentations and informal communication are two sides of a teacher's professional life. A presentation is planned, structured and delivered to a group. Informal communication is unplanned, responsive and happens in hallways, staffrooms and workshops. Both require skill, and both can be learned.

Planning and delivering a presentation​

Presentation skills are the abilities that let you deliver a clear, engaging talk to a group. The figure above maps the stages from preparation to follow-up (Hutchinson & Waters, 1987; Verma & Raman).

1. Define your purpose and audience. Before you decide what to say, decide why you are speaking and who is listening. Are you informing (explaining a new strategy), persuading (arguing for a change in assessment) or training (teaching a skill)? What does your audience already know? A presentation on reading strategies for experienced teachers looks different from one for first-year trainees.

2. Research and organise your content. Gather your material and organise it into the three-part structure from Topic 3.1: introduction (state your purpose and preview your points), body (two or three main points, each with an example) and conclusion (summarise and give a takeaway). Limit yourself to two or three main points. A fifteen-minute presentation cannot cover everything; choose what matters most.

3. Design visual aids. Visual aids (slides, charts, handouts) support your talk; they do not replace it. Each slide should carry one idea, use large fonts, and avoid long paragraphs. A simple diagram or a short bullet list is easier to follow than a page of text. Hutchinson and Waters note that the best visual aids are ones the audience can grasp in seconds, leaving their attention free for you.

4. Rehearse. Practise out loud, ideally in the room or a similar space. Time yourself. Rehearsal reveals points that are unclear, sections that run too long, and transitions that need work. It is the single most effective way to reduce the anxiety described in Topic 3.2.

5. Deliver. On the day, arrive early, check the equipment, and take a breath before you begin. Open with your purpose, make eye contact, and modulate your voice (Topic 3.2). Keep an eye on the clock. If you are running short, skip a minor example; do not rush the conclusion.

6. Handle Q&A. Handling Q&A means responding to audience questions confidently. Listen to the whole question before answering. If you know the answer, give it briefly. If you do not, say so honestly: "I do not have that information with me; I will find out and follow up." Audiences respect honesty more than guessing.

7. Follow up. After the presentation, share your slides or a summary if promised. Thank the organiser. Reflect on what went well and what you would change next time. This reflection is what turns one presentation into a skill you build over time.

Managing time. Managing time in a presentation means planning how long each section will take and sticking to it. A useful rule of thumb: spend about ten per cent of your time on the introduction, eighty per cent on the body, and ten per cent on the conclusion and Q&A. For a fifteen-minute talk, that is roughly ninety seconds, twelve minutes and ninety seconds. Practise with a timer so you learn what twelve minutes of speaking feels like.

Informal communication​

Informal communication is the unplanned, everyday talk that builds professional relationships: conversations in the staffroom, introductions at a workshop, a quick exchange with a parent after school. It may feel unstructured, but it follows conventions you can learn (Downes, n.d.; Verma & Raman).

Small talk. Small talk is brief, light conversation about non-controversial topics: the weather, travel, a recent school event. Its purpose is not to exchange information but to build rapport. In Pakistani professional settings, small talk often includes polite enquiries about health or family before moving to business. Starting a workshop interaction with "How was your journey?" or "Is this your first visit to this city?" signals courtesy and consideration (the six Cs from Topic 3.1).

Networking. Networking is building and maintaining professional relationships that support your work. Downes describes it as the skill of making a genuine connection in a short exchange: introducing yourself clearly, asking a question that shows interest, and ending with a concrete next step ("I would like to hear more about your reading programme; could we talk after the session?"). Networking is not collecting contacts; it is starting relationships.

Turn-taking. Turn-taking is the convention by which speakers alternate in a conversation. Smooth turn-taking uses cues: a slight pause signals that you are finished, a raised intonation can hold your turn, and a brief "mmm" or nod signals that you are listening and the other person should continue. Interrupting or dominating a conversation breaks the norm and signals disrespect.

Conversational repair. Conversational repair is what speakers do when something goes wrong: a misunderstanding, a word that does not come, or a point that was not clear. Repair strategies include rephrasing ("Let me put it differently"), asking for clarification ("Could you say more about what you mean by ...?"), and checking understanding ("So you are saying that ...?"). Repair is a sign of a skilled communicator, not a weak one.

Politeness strategies. Politeness strategies are the ways speakers protect each other's dignity in interaction. They include indirectness ("Would it be possible to ...?" instead of "Do this"), hedging ("I think perhaps ..."), and showing appreciation ("I really value your point on ..."). In Pakistani professional culture, politeness often carries additional weight: a direct refusal can feel rude, so softening your language is both courteous and effective.

Common misconception. "Informal communication requires no structure." It does. Small talk has topics that are safe and topics that are not. Networking has an arc (introduce, connect, follow up). Turn-taking, repair and politeness all follow patterns. The difference from formal communication is not the absence of structure but the flexibility with which you apply it.

Activity: Plan and deliver a mini-presentation​

Work in groups of three.

Part 1 (20 minutes). Choose a topic from your B.Ed course (for example, a teaching strategy, a classroom management technique, or a reading activity). Plan a five-minute presentation using the seven stages in the figure above. Prepare no more than three slides (hand-drawn on paper is fine). Decide who will present, who will time, and who will ask a question.

Part 2 (15 minutes). Each group delivers their five-minute presentation to another group. The audience asks one question during Q&A and gives one piece of feedback on voice, body language or visual aids. The presenting group notes what they would change next time.

Check your understanding​

  1. Name the seven stages of planning and delivering a presentation, in order. (Remember)
  2. A friend says, "I will put all my notes on my slides so I do not forget anything." Using this topic's account of visual aids, explain what that approach gets wrong. (Analyze)
  3. Explain the difference between small talk and networking, and give one purpose for each. (Understand)
  4. A colleague interrupts you three times in a five-minute conversation. Identify the turn-taking problem and describe two strategies you could use to handle it politely. (Apply)
  5. A presenter is asked a question they cannot answer. Describe the recommended response and explain why it is better than guessing. (Apply)

Summary​

A presentation follows seven stages: define purpose and audience, research and organise content, design visual aids, rehearse, deliver, handle Q&A and follow up. Time management and rehearsal are what separate a smooth presentation from a rushed one. Informal communication, far from being structureless, follows conventions: small talk builds rapport, networking builds relationships, and turn-taking, conversational repair and politeness strategies keep interactions respectful and productive. Both formal and informal communication are skills that improve with deliberate practice.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can plan a short presentation using the seven stages from preparation to follow up.
  • I can design visual aids that support, rather than replace, my spoken message.
  • I can explain the difference between small talk, networking and professional dialogue.
  • I can use turn-taking, conversational repair and politeness strategies in a described interaction.

Try this at your practicum school​

Attend one staff meeting or workshop break. Note one example of small talk, one example of networking and one example of conversational repair or politeness. Write half a page on how each move functioned: what it achieved and what might have happened without it.

Do not name the school or any person in your write-up.

Summative task​

Write 350 to 400 words on the following.

You are asked to give a ten-minute presentation on inclusive language (Topic 3.2) to a group of trainee teachers at your practicum school. Using this topic's seven-stage planning process, outline your presentation: state your purpose and audience, list your two or three main points, describe your visual aids, and explain how you will handle Q&A and manage your time. Then explain one strategy you will use to manage your own nerves, drawing on Topic 3.2.
