Topic 1.4 - Designing the environment on purpose

A real classroom situation​

Mr Hassan teaches Class 6 in a rural school in Sanghar. His room has fixed benches in rows, a cracked blackboard, one locked cupboard, and no budget. He has been teaching in the same layout for two years and has begun to feel that the room is working against him. He cannot move the benches, but he can do more than he thinks. This topic is about what a teacher can change on purpose, even in a room that looks unchangeable.

Explanation​

Topic 1.3 taught you to read a classroom. This topic asks you to design one, or at least to design the small part of it that is within your power. The guide names four design acts for Week 4 (ClassroomMgmt_Sept13.txt:182-189): identify resources, use displays and visuals, arrange seating for different kinds of learning, and employ the physical and social facilities. This topic takes them in order, then adds the question that holds them together: what kind of classroom do I want?

Deciding what kind of classroom environment you want​

Before you change a single thing, you need an answer to the guide's question: what kind of classroom environment do I want? (ClassroomMgmt_Sept13.txt:181). That question is not about furniture. It is about the learning you are trying to make possible.

If your answer is "I want a room in which pupils can practise procedures until they are automatic", then the environment you design will look one way: clear sightlines to the board, a routine for retrieving and returning materials, a seating arrangement that makes repetition easy. If your answer is "I want a room in which pupils argue about ideas and build understanding together", the environment will look different: clusters, a norm of listening to disagreement, displays that make pupil thinking visible.

The point is that the design follows the learning goal, not the other way around. A teacher who rearranges the desks because clusters look modern, without a learning reason, has changed the furniture but not the learning. Start with the goal, then choose the environment that serves it.

Identifying resources for learning​

Learning resources are anything in the room a pupil can use to support learning: textbooks, exercise books, the blackboard, posters, real objects, the floor, the wall, and other pupils. The design question is not "what do I wish I had?" but "what is already here that I am not using?"

In a low-resource government school, the answer is often more than the teacher assumes. The walls can hold pupil-made displays. The floor can be a sorting space. The pupils themselves are a resource: a pupil who has understood an explanation can explain it to a peer, and both learn from the act. Wong and Wong (1998) treat the teacher's first design task as a resource audit: list everything in the room that could support learning, then decide what is going unused.

The management weight of resources is about access. A resource no pupil can reach is not a resource; it is furniture. If there is one set of science materials in a locked cupboard and the key is with the headteacher, the design problem is not the cupboard but the routine for getting the materials to the pupils when they are needed.

Using displays and visuals to enhance the learning environment​

A display is not decoration. A well-chosen display is a teaching tool that keeps teaching after you have moved on. The design question is what the display is for.

Three kinds of display carry management weight:

  • Learning-target displays. The day's or week's learning target, written where every pupil can see it, answers the question "what are we trying to learn?" without the teacher having to repeat it.
  • Pupil-work displays. A wall of pupils' current work signals that the room belongs to them, that their thinking is visible and valued, and that the standard is "the best we can do this week", not "perfect".
  • Reference displays. A number line, a vocabulary wall, a map, a chart of the routine for asking help. These are tools pupils can use without asking the teacher, which is the difference between a room that depends on the teacher and one that runs itself.

In a room with cracked walls and no budget, displays can be made from newspaper, from scrap paper, from the backs of old examination sheets. The constraint is not money; it is the teacher's willingness to make the work visible.

Arranging seating for different kinds of learning experiences​

The figure at the start of this topic shows three common arrangements, and the point of the figure is that none of them is universally right. Each serves a kind of learning and costs something.

  • Rows serve whole-class instruction well: every pupil can see the board, the teacher can see every pupil, and the arrangement signals that the teacher is the focus. The cost is that group work is awkward and the pupils nearest the teacher get more attention than the pupils at the back.
  • Clusters (groups of four to six) serve collaboration well: pupils can talk, share materials, and build understanding together. The cost is that the teacher cannot see every pupil at once, noise rises, and without a clear routine the cluster becomes a chat.
  • Horseshoe (a open rectangle or semicircle) serves discussion and debate well: every pupil can see every other pupil, the teacher is part of the circle rather than outside it, and the arrangement signals that the thinking is shared. The cost is that it needs more space and is awkward for individual written work.

Mr Hassan's benches are fixed in rows, and he cannot change that. But he can, on discussion days, ask pupils to turn to face the side or the back, creating temporary clusters within a row layout. The design principle is: match the arrangement to the learning, and when you cannot change the furniture, change how the pupils orient within it.

Employing physical facilities and building the social environment​

The physical and the social are not separate design acts. The room's physical arrangement shapes how people treat each other, and the social norms determine whether the physical arrangement works. A cluster layout without a norm of listening to disagreement is just noisy. A display of pupil work without a norm of respectful feedback is just a wall.

Building the social environment on purpose means establishing, practising and maintaining the norms the learning requires:

  • A norm that a wrong answer is useful, not shameful, if you want pupils to take the risk of thinking in front of others.
  • A norm that pupils help each other, if you want the cluster arrangement to produce learning rather than copying.
  • A norm that every voice matters, if you want the back benches to participate as fully as the front.

Evertson and Emmer (2009) are clear that these norms do not establish themselves. The teacher states them, models them, practises them in the first weeks, and responds consistently when them are breached. That is management work, and it is the work that makes the physical design actually function.

Activity: The room you have, the room you want​

Work in pairs. Take the description of Mr Hassan's room from the start of this topic (fixed benches, cracked blackboard, one locked cupboard, no budget). Decide on one learning goal he might have for a single lesson: either "practise procedures" or "build understanding together".

For your chosen goal, complete the audit table from the figure: what to look for, why it matters, what he can change this week, and a first step. Then present your audit to another pair that chose the other goal, and explain why your environment differs from theirs.

Allow 25 minutes.

Check your understanding​

  1. Name the four design acts the guide lists for Week 4. (Remember)
  2. A teacher puts up a colourful commercial poster of a film star. Using this topic's account of what a display is for, explain whether that counts as a learning display. (Understand)
  3. A teacher always uses clusters "because group work is good". Explain what that reasoning leaves out, and name the learning goal that would make clusters the right choice. (Analyze)
  4. For the same fixed-bench room as the Activity, propose one change to the social environment that would make a discussion lesson more workable, and explain why the change is social rather than physical. (Apply)
  5. A friend says, "I cannot design my classroom because the benches are fixed and I have no budget." Using two ideas from this topic, explain what she can still change this week. (Apply)

Summary​

Designing the environment on purpose starts with a learning goal, not with furniture. The four design acts are: identify the resources already in the room, make displays that keep teaching, match the seating arrangement to the learning it needs to serve, and build the social norms that make the physical design actually work. In a low-resource government school, the constraint is real but narrower than it first appears: the teacher can change how the room is used, how the walls are read, how pupils orient, and how they treat each other, even when the benches do not move.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can explain why the learning goal must come before the physical design.
  • I can conduct a simple resource audit of a described classroom.
  • I can match a seating arrangement to the learning experience it serves and name what it costs.
  • I can distinguish a learning display from a decoration.
  • I can name at least three social norms a teacher would need to establish on purpose.

Try this at your practicum school​

Carry out a resource audit of one classroom you have access to. List every resource currently in the room, mark which ones pupils can actually reach and use during a lesson, and identify one resource that is going unused. Then propose one display the teacher could create this week from materials already available, and write half a page on what it would teach and how it would need to be introduced.

Do not rearrange any furniture without the teacher's permission.

Summative task​

Write 400 to 450 words on the following.

Take a classroom you know (your practicum school, the Activity room, or your own schooling). Describe one physical feature and one social feature of that room. Then argue for one purposeful change to each, and justify both changes by reference to a specific learning goal and, for at least one of them, to a learning theory from Topic 1.1.

CriterionWhat a strong response does

Further reading​

  • Wong, H. K., & Wong, R. T. (1998). The first days of school: How to be an effective teacher (rev. ed.). Harry K. Wong Publications. Chapters 15 to 17 cover the design of the physical environment, displays, and seating in low-resource settings.
  • Evertson, C. M., & Emmer, E. T. (2009). Classroom management for elementary teachers (8th ed.). Pearson. Chapter 3 sets out the design of the physical and social environment as a management act, including the establishment of norms.
  • Henley, M. R. (2009). Introduction to proactive classroom management. In Classroom management: A proactive approach. Pearson. Chapter 1 frames environmental design as a proactive management strategy.
