Topic 4.1 - What professional standards are for

A real classroom situation​

A district education office has to decide whether to confirm two teachers at the end of their probation year.

About the first, the head reports: "She is good. The parents like her, the class is quiet, and I have no complaints." About the second: "He is difficult. He argues in meetings. His results are fine."

On that basis the office confirms the first and hesitates over the second. Nothing here is corrupt. Everyone involved is acting in good faith. But notice what the decision actually rests on: one head teacher's impression, expressed in terms that cannot be checked, compared with nothing.

Now imagine the same two cases with a written standard on the table, and a requirement that the head's report cite evidence against named parts of it. The decision might come out the same way. It would at least be a different kind of decision, and one the second teacher could contest.

Explanation​

The concept of a professional standard, and what it is for​

A professional standard is a documented statement of the knowledge, values and practice expected of a competent member of a profession, written in terms specific enough that someone can judge whether it has been met.

Three features do the work.

It is written. An unwritten expectation cannot be taught to a trainee, cannot be appealed against, and changes silently depending on who holds it. Writing it down is not bureaucracy; it is what converts an opinion into something that can be argued with.

It is shared. The same statement applies across schools and districts, so quality does not depend on the accident of which head teacher you were assigned.

It is specific enough to be evidenced. "Be a good teacher" is not a standard. "Plans lessons with clear learning objectives that take account of what pupils already know" is close to one, because you could look for evidence either way.

The first thing to fix is the confusion the figure above addresses, because it defeats a lot of trainees:

  • A code of conduct (Unit 2) answers how should I behave? It governs conduct, and the consequence of breaching it is disciplinary.
  • A curriculum answers what should pupils learn? It governs content.
  • A professional standard answers what does competent teaching look like, in a form that can be checked? It governs practice, and the consequence of not meeting it is developmental before it is disciplinary.

They overlap. Most standards frameworks include conduct among their expectations, which is why Pakistan's standards contain a code-of-conduct element. But a standard is not primarily a rule about behaviour; it is a description of competent work.

A misconception worth naming now: a standard tells you how to teach. It does not, and a standard that did would be a bad one. A standard describes what competent practice achieves and leaves the method to the teacher's judgement. That space is deliberate, and Topic 3.5's facilitator discussion is what fills it.

Global perspectives on teacher standards​

Standards frameworks are not a Pakistani invention or a donor imposition; they are close to universal in school systems that have tried to raise teaching quality at scale over the last thirty years. UNESCO and the International Task Force on Teachers published a global framework of professional teaching standards precisely to give countries a common reference point.

What the international picture shows is a family resemblance rather than one model. Frameworks differ on:

  • Scope. Some cover only classroom practice; others include professional conduct, collegial contribution and continuing development.
  • Granularity. Some are a page of principles; others run to hundreds of indicators.
  • Stakes. In some systems a standard governs entry to the profession and nothing else; in others it drives annual appraisal, pay and promotion.

The differences matter more than the similarities when you are reading your own system's framework, because they tell you what the framework is for, and therefore how it will be used on you.

National perspectives on teacher standards​

Pakistan's National Professional Standards for Teachers were developed by the Ministry of Education in 2009, with support from UNESCO and USAID, and they are the framework this country's teacher education is built to. Topic 4.2 deals with them in detail.

The national dimension matters for a reason beyond administration. A standard imported unchanged from a system with small classes, reliable electricity and a well-stocked library will contain indicators that a teacher in interior Sindh cannot meet for reasons that have nothing to do with their competence. A national framework is an attempt to state what competent teaching looks like here, under the conditions that actually obtain.

That attempt can succeed or fail, and it is fair to examine a framework on exactly that question. When you read Pakistan's standards in the next topic, one useful test is to ask, of each expectation, whether a committed teacher in an under-resourced government school could demonstrate it.

Using standards to ensure teacher quality and accountability​

A standard is put to four distinct uses, shown in the figure above, and a great deal of confusion about standards comes from not noticing which use is in play.

Guiding initial training. The standard tells a teacher education programme what it is preparing people for. This is why your own degree is designed around it.

Guiding continuing development. A serving teacher uses the standard to find the gap between current and competent practice, and to choose what to work on. Topic 4.3 is this use.

Informing appraisal. A school or system uses the standard as the reference for judging performance. Topic 4.4 is this use.

Public accountability. The profession uses the standard to make a claim to society: this is what we hold ourselves to. Unit 1's account of professionalism depends on being able to make such a claim.

The four uses pull against each other, which the figure marks and which is the analytical point of this topic. A standard good for development is generous, developmental in tone, and safe to admit failure against. A standard good for accountability is precise, evidenced and consequential. Serving both fully in one document is correspondingly hard, and Isoré (2009), reviewing teacher evaluation across OECD countries, found this tension to be the central design problem of teacher evaluation systems rather than an incidental flaw in any one of them. She is careful to call the purposes conflicting rather than incompatible: systems that hold both exist, and they do it by deciding the trade-off openly instead of leaving it to chance.

This gives you the answer to a common and reasonable complaint: standards are a bureaucratic import with no bearing on a real classroom. Sometimes true, and when it is true, the usual cause is that a framework built for one of the four uses is being applied for another. A development tool used for promotion decisions will be experienced as surveillance, and teachers will respond by managing their evidence instead of improving their practice. That is not cynicism about teachers; it is what any sensible person does when a tool for reflection is repurposed into a tool for judgement.

Activity: Which use is this?​

Work in groups of three. Below are six situations in which a teaching standard is invoked. For each, identify which of the four uses is in play, and say what the standard would need to look like for that use to work well.

  1. A university revises its B.Ed curriculum.
  2. A head teacher completes an annual report that feeds into a promotion decision.
  3. A teacher chooses which of three training courses to attend.
  4. A provincial minister answers a journalist's question about teaching quality.
  5. A mentor and a trainee agree what to focus on during a practicum.
  6. A district draws up a list of teachers to be transferred.

Then identify the two situations in the list where a standard designed for a different use is most likely to be borrowed, and say what goes wrong when it is.

Allow 25 minutes.

Check your understanding​

  1. Define a professional standard and name the three features that make it usable. (Remember)
  2. Distinguish a professional standard from a code of conduct and from a curriculum by the question each answers. (Understand)
  3. Name the four uses a standard is put to, and state what each use requires of the standard. (Remember)
  4. Explain why "be a good teacher" fails as a standard while "plans lessons with clear learning objectives that take account of what pupils already know" comes closer. (Apply)
  5. Explain why a standard good for development and one good for accountability pull against each other, and what a system must settle in advance to serve both. (Analyze)

Summary​

A professional standard is a written, shared, evidence-specific statement of what competent practice in a profession looks like. It differs from a code of conduct, which governs behaviour, and from a curriculum, which governs content: a standard governs practice and answers what competent teaching looks like in a checkable form. It does not prescribe method, and one that did would be a poor standard.

Standards frameworks are near-universal internationally and differ in scope, granularity and stakes; those differences tell you what a given framework is for. Pakistan's national framework, developed in 2009 with UNESCO and USAID support, is the reference this country's teacher education is built to, and a fair test of any national framework is whether a committed teacher under local conditions could actually demonstrate it.

Standards serve four uses: initial training, continuing development, appraisal, and public accountability. The four pull against each other, and most complaints that standards are irrelevant bureaucracy trace to a framework built for one use being applied for another.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can define a professional standard and say what makes one usable.
  • I can distinguish a standard from a code of conduct and a curriculum by the question each answers.
  • I can name the four uses and identify which is in play in a described situation.
  • I can explain why a development standard and an accountability standard pull against each other, and what has to be decided in advance for one document to serve both.
  • I can test a framework by asking whether a teacher in local conditions could demonstrate it.

Try this at your practicum school​

Ask your mentor teacher two questions: whether they have ever read the National Professional Standards, and what happens in their annual appraisal.

Write half a page on the relationship between the two answers. The common finding is that the standards exist and the appraisal does not refer to them, or refers to them only formally. If that is what you find, identify which of the four uses is actually operating in that school, and which are absent.

Summative task​

Write 400 to 450 words.

Return to the two probationary teachers at the start of this topic. Argue whether introducing a written standard into that decision would improve it.

Your answer must say specifically what the standard would change about the head teacher's report, identify which of the four uses is in play, and engage with the strongest objection: that a standard would let a compliant but mediocre teacher be confirmed while a difficult, effective one is not.

Mini-rubric

CriterionWhat a strong response does
