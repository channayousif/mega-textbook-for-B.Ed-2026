Unit 4 assessment

Unit summary​

Professional writing is the skill of choosing the right format (email, memo, report, formal letter) for the right purpose and audience, and following the conventions each format expects. Every professional document moves through the same writing process: plan, draft, revise, edit, final version. Intercultural communication is shaped by Hall's (1976) high-context / low-context spectrum (where meaning lives) and by Brown and Levinson's (1987) face framework (how politeness works). Adapting communication means analysing a new audience and purpose, then reshaping the register, tone and content of a message so it works for that reader. One style does not work everywhere; the skill is to read the situation and adapt.

Summative assessment​

Multiple-choice questions (MCQs)​

  1. In this unit's account, a business email's subject line should be: (Remember) a) left blank so the reader opens the email to find out what it is about b) short and specific, telling the reader what the email concerns c) a full sentence that explains the whole request d) the word "Urgent" followed by as many exclamation marks as possible

  2. Which writing format is most appropriate for informing all staff about a changed timetable? (Understand) a) A formal letter b) A short report c) A memo d) A job application letter

  3. In Hall's (1976) account, a high-context communication style carries most of its meaning in: (Remember) a) the exact words the speaker uses b) the grammatical structures of the sentences c) the shared context, relationship and what is left unsaid d) the written record of the conversation

  4. A teacher writes, "Perhaps we could consider revising the homework policy, if that is all right with everyone." This is an example of: (Apply) a) a face-saving hedge b) a low-context demand c) a formal letter closing d) a report recommendation

  5. In the writing process this unit describes, which stage comes immediately after drafting? (Remember) a) Final version b) Editing c) Revising d) Planning

  6. Brown and Levinson (1987) describe "negative face" as the want to: (Understand) a) be liked and appreciated b) have freedom of action without pressure c) avoid all forms of politeness d) communicate only in writing

  7. Which of the following is a feature of a formal letter but NOT of a business email? (Understand) a) A subject line b) The sender's address and the date at the top c) A polite closing d) A clear opening sentence

  8. A teacher adapts a formal-official message for a parent audience by changing "Guardians are hereby notified" to "Dear parents, please note". This is primarily a shift of: (Apply) a) format b) grammar rules c) register and tone d) the writing process

  9. A face-saving strategy that gives a reason before refusing is used to protect the listener's: (Apply) a) physical safety b) positive or negative face c) knowledge of grammar d) understanding of register

  10. A short report differs from a memo primarily because it: (Remember) a) is always handwritten b) presents findings with an introduction, findings and conclusion c) has no heading d) is sent to people outside the organisation

Restricted-response questions (RRQs)​

  1. Explain the difference between a memo and a short report, and give one situation where you would choose each. (Remember)

  2. A colleague writes a business email that begins with three paragraphs of background before stating the purpose. Using this unit's account of professional email structure, explain what is wrong and how to fix it. (Analyze)

  3. Explain the difference between high-context and low-context communication, and give one example of each. (Remember)

  4. Identify two face-saving strategies in the following sentence and explain which face each one protects: "I'm really sorry to bother you, but I was wondering if you might possibly be able to share your notes, if it's not too much trouble." (Analyze)

  5. A teacher writes the same message about a school event to a district officer and to a group of parents with limited schooling. Explain two specific changes the teacher should make for the parent audience and why. (Apply)

  6. Explain the difference between register and tone, and give one example of each. (Remember)

  7. A pupil says, "Face-saving strategies are just dishonest ways of avoiding the truth." Using Brown and Levinson (1987), explain what that claim overlooks. (Evaluate)

  8. Arrange the five stages of the writing process in order and explain what happens at each stage. (Understand)

  9. A message written in a high-context style is read by a low-context reader. Explain one specific misunderstanding that is likely to happen and why, using Hall (1976). (Apply)

  10. Explain why "one communication style works everywhere" is a misconception, and give one example of a situation where adapting your style matters. (Understand)

Extended-response questions (ERQs)​

  1. A teacher needs to write three documents in one week: a request to the headteacher for classroom supplies, an announcement to staff about a changed exam date, and a letter to the district education officer reporting on a school event. For each document, choose the most appropriate professional writing format, explain your choice, and outline the structure and tone you would use. (Analyze)

  2. A Pakistani teacher in a multicultural school writes the following to a low-context British colleague: "If it is not too much trouble, and only if you are free, I was hoping you might not mind observing my lesson sometime, but please don't feel you have to." The colleague later says they did not realise the teacher actually wanted the observation. Analyse the breakdown using Hall's and Brown and Levinson's frameworks, and rewrite the request so the meaning is clear and the politeness is preserved. (Analyze)

  3. Take a formal notice from a school or office (for example, a fee notice, a holiday announcement or a meeting invitation). Analyse it for register, tone and likely audience. Then rewrite it for a different audience (for example, parents with limited formal schooling, or a group of colleagues). Explain every change you make. Write 400 to 450 words. (Evaluate)

  4. Evaluate the claim that "professional writing means using complex vocabulary and long sentences." Use this unit's account of professional writing, register and tone to argue for or against the claim. (Evaluate)

  5. Integrative. You are the headteacher of a government school in Sindh. You need to communicate a sudden school closure (due to flooding) to two audiences: (a) the district education officer and (b) the parents of your pupils, many of whom have limited formal schooling. Plan both messages, choose the right format and register for each audience, and justify your choices using this unit's account of professional writing, intercultural communication and audience adaptation. (Create)

Answers and marking guidance​

MCQ answer key​

  1. b - a subject line should be short and specific, telling the reader what the email is about.
  2. c - a memo is the format for informing people within the same organisation about a change.
  3. c - high-context communication carries meaning in the shared context, relationship and what is unsaid.
  4. a - "Perhaps we could consider" is a hedge that softens the suggestion and protects face.
  5. c - the order is plan, draft, revise, edit, final version; revising follows drafting.
  6. b - negative face is the want to be unimpeded, to have freedom of action without pressure.
  7. b - the sender's address and date at the top are features of a formal letter, not of a business email.
  8. c - changing formal-official phrasing to plain, warm phrasing is a register and tone shift.
  9. b - giving a reason before refusing is a face-saving strategy that protects the listener's positive or negative face.
  10. b - a short report presents findings with an introduction, findings and conclusion/recommendation.

RRQ model answers and mark schemes​

1. (4 marks) A memo informs or instructs people within the same organisation (1); a short report presents findings, often to a supervisor, with an introduction, findings and conclusion (1); the memo situation is genuinely internal (1); the report situation genuinely presents findings or recommendations (1).

2. (5 marks) Identifies the problem: the purpose comes too late (1); explains the principle: a business email states its purpose in the opening sentence so the reader knows immediately why they are reading (1); advises moving the purpose to the first sentence and keeping background brief (1); the reasoning draws on Topic 4.1 (1); the reasoning is coherent (1).

3. (4 marks) High-context communication carries meaning in the shared context, relationship and what is left unsaid (1); low-context communication carries meaning mainly in the words themselves (1); the high-context example is genuinely indirect or context-dependent (1); the low-context example is genuinely explicit (1).

4. (5 marks) Identifies at least two strategies from: apologising ("I'm really sorry to bother you"), hedging ("I was wondering if", "might possibly"), and the conditional softening ("if it's not too much trouble") (2 for two correct); explains that these protect the listener's negative face (freedom from imposition) (2); the reasoning is coherent (1).

5. (5 marks) Names two specific changes (e.g. plainer language, warmer tone, shorter sentences, adding a reason the parents should care) (1 each, up to 2); explains why each change serves the parent audience (e.g. limited schooling, high-context expectations) (1 each, up to 2); the reasoning is coherent (1).

6. (4 marks) Register is the level of formality a situation demands (1); tone is the writer's attitude toward the subject and the audience (1); the register example is genuinely about formality level (1); the tone example is genuinely about attitude (1).

7. (5 marks) Takes the claim at its strongest (1); explains that Brown and Levinson treat politeness as a fundamental, universal part of language, not as dishonesty (1); notes that face-saving strategies protect relationships and reduce threat, which honest directness can damage (1); argues a verdict with reasons (1); the reasoning is coherent (1).

8. (5 marks) Correct order: plan, draft, revise, edit, final version (1); explains planning (purpose, audience) and drafting (first version) (1); explains revising (rethinking structure and content) and editing (fixing grammar, punctuation, tone) (1); explains final version (the polished document) (1); the reasoning is coherent (1).

9. (5 marks) Identifies a likely misunderstanding (e.g. the low-context reader takes indirect phrasing literally and misses the point, or interprets excessive politeness as lack of interest) (1); explains it using Hall's framework (the reader expects meaning in the words, but the message embeds it in context) (2); the reasoning draws on Topic 4.2 (1); the reasoning is coherent (1).

10. (4 marks) Explains that different audiences and purposes demand different register, tone and content (1); explains that a style that works for one reader can confuse or offend another (1); gives a genuine example (e.g. formal-official language for parents, or indirect language for a low-context reader) (1); the reasoning is coherent (1).
