Topic 3.2 - Routines in multigrade and mixed-need classes

A real classroom situation​

Mr Karim teaches Classes 2 and 4 in one room in a government school in Sanghar. He has one blackboard, forty-two pupils, and no assistant. When he works with Class 4 mathematics, Class 2 must be able to work without him. When he moves to Class 2, Class 4 must not sit idle. This topic is about the routines that make that possible.

Explanation​

Topic 3.1 described routines as time-recovery tools. This topic asks what happens when the room makes a one-routine-fits-all approach impossible: two grades in the same room, or pupils with different needs in the same class. The answer is not to abandon routines but to redesign them for the constraint.

Routines in the multigrade classroom​

The figure above maps the routines this topic describes. The two workhorses of a multigrade classroom are the anchor routine and the independent-work routine.

The anchor routine is the task one grade does while the teacher works with the other. Topic 2.3 introduced the anchor activity; this topic adds the routine that makes it work: how pupils start the anchor without being told, what they do when they are stuck, how they check their own work, and what they do when they finish. A good anchor routine is self-checking, connected to the current topic, and familiar enough that the teacher is not interrupted to explain it. Without the routine, the anchor activity is just a task sheet; with the routine, it is a management system that runs itself.

The independent-work routine governs what pupils do when they are working alone: how to get materials, how to ask for help without calling out, what to do when finished. Evertson and Emmer (2009) stress that the independent-work routine is the one most multigrade teachers neglect, because it is easier to keep the pupils busy than to teach them to work independently. But a busy pupil who is not learning is just a quiet disruption, and the teacher's time is still being spent on management rather than teaching.

Vincent (The Multigrade Classroom: Book 3) describes the multigrade teacher's routine set as a small, practised collection: how to signal a grade change, how to start an anchor, how to end a peer-tutoring pair, how to close the lesson so both grades stop at the same time. The routines are few, but they must be taught and rehearsed as deliberately as in a single-grade classroom.

Routines for special needs and situations​

The figure above also maps routines matched to specific special needs. The guide treats these as routine adaptations, not as separate management systems, because the underlying principle is the same: the pupil who needs something different needs a routine that provides it without requiring the teacher's constant attention.

  • A pupil who needs extra time needs a routine for what to do when the rest of the class moves on: a continuation task, a self-checking extension, a way to signal "I am not finished yet" without disruption.
  • A pupil who is easily distracted needs a routine for where to sit, how to use a timer, how to break a task into smaller steps - the routine provides the structure that the pupil cannot yet provide alone.
  • A pupil who finishes early needs a routine for what to do next: a reading corner, a peer-tutoring role, a self-checking extension. Without this routine, the early finisher becomes the source of disruption.
  • A pupil who needs help but will not ask needs a routine for how to signal for assistance: a coloured card on the desk, a place in the question queue, a check-in point with the teacher at a predictable moment.

Common misconception. "Routines are for young children only." They are for every age. The content of the routine changes - older pupils do not need a routine for how to line up - but the principle does not. A well-designed routine frees the teacher to teach, and that is as valuable in a lower-secondary classroom as in a primary one.

Activity: Design an anchor routine​

Work in groups of three. Take the following scenario:

Class 2 and Class 4 share one room. The teacher is working with Class 4 on two-digit addition. Class 2 is working independently on a reading task.

Now design the anchor routine: how Class 2 starts the task, what they do when they are stuck, how they check their work, and what they do when they finish. Allow 25 minutes. Then present your routine and identify one moment where a pupil might drift from "working" to "just busy", and explain how your routine prevents it.

Check your understanding​

  1. State the difference between an anchor routine and an independent-work routine, and explain the management purpose of each. (Remember)
  2. A multigrade teacher says, "I just give the other grade a worksheet while I teach." Using this topic's account, explain what that approach costs in management terms. (Analyze)
  3. Name the two workhorse routines of a multigrade classroom and describe one feature each must have. (Remember)
  4. A pupil finishes every task early and then disrupts the class. Propose a routine that addresses the problem and explain why it is realistic in a class of sixty. (Apply)
  5. Assess whether a routine designed for a single-grade class can be used unchanged in a multigrade classroom. Refer to the anchor routine in your answer. (Analyze)

Summary​

Multigrade and mixed-need classrooms require routines that are deliberately redesigned for the constraint. The anchor routine and the independent-work routine are the two workhorses of a multigrade room; routines for special needs provide the structure that allows a pupil to work without constant teacher attention. In each case the principle is the same: the routine removes the need for the teacher to manage every moment.

Self-assessment checklist​

  • I can explain why a routine that works in a single-grade class may need to be redesigned for a multigrade room.
  • I can describe the anchor routine and the independent-work routine and the purpose of each.
  • I can design an anchor routine that is self-checking, connected to the topic, and familiar.
  • I can propose a routine for at least two different special needs.
  • I can explain why routines are not only for young children.

Try this at your practicum school​

Identify one multigrade classroom. Over two lessons, note the routines the teacher uses when one group is working independently, and write half a page on which routine makes the most difference to the teacher's ability to teach the other group.

Do not name the school or the teacher in your write-up.

Summative task​

Write 350 to 400 words on the following.

A teacher in a multigrade classroom (Classes 3 and 4 in one room) says, "I cannot use routines; every day is different." Using this topic's account of anchor routines and independent-work routines, argue which single routine would most improve the management of the described room, and justify the choice by reference to the constraint a multigrade room imposes.

Further reading​

  • Evertson, C. M., & Emmer, E. T. (2009). Classroom management for elementary teachers (8th edn.). Pearson. Chapter 5 covers independent-work routines and managing group work.
